--- DISORDER ---
Major Depressive Disorder
--- CASE STUDY ---
Andrew Quinn was a 60-year-old white man who returned to see his longtime psychiatrist two weeks after the suicide of his 24-year-old son.
 His son had struggled with major depressive disorder and substance use disorder and was found surrounded by empty pill bottles and an incoherent suicide note.
Mr. Quinn had been very close to his son. In the initial weeks after the death, he described feeling as if his life had lost all meaning.
 He experienced persistent sadness, poor concentration, social withdrawal, and preoccupying thoughts about how he might have prevented the suicide.
 He blamed himself for prioritizing his career over his son and began drinking half a bottle of wine nightly—despite never having had a history of alcohol misuse.
 At that stage, his psychiatrist recognized these symptoms as consistent with a normal grief reaction and recommended weekly follow-up.
By the sixth week, Mr. Quinn's symptoms had worsened significantly. He was no longer simply ruminating on past actions but stated that he believed he should have died instead of his son.
 He continued to suffer from insomnia and experienced early-morning awakenings (around 4:30 AM), followed by long periods lying in bed with feelings of worthlessness, sadness, and fatigue.
 He reported diminished self-confidence, reduced sexual interest, and an absence of enthusiasm for previously enjoyed activities.
 He questioned whether what he was experiencing was still grief or a recurrence of major depressive disorder.
Mr. Quinn had a history of two prior episodes of MDD in his 30s that had responded to psychotherapy and medication.
 There was no personal history of alcohol or substance use disorders. Both of his parents were described as “depressive” but untreated. No family history of suicide was reported.
Mental status: cooperative, emotionally withdrawn, described persistent sadness and feelings of worthlessness, denied hallucinations or delusions, no signs of psychosis. No manic symptoms.
Discussion: Mr. Quinn meets diagnostic criteria for Major Depressive Disorder. Though his initial presentation was consistent with normal bereavement, his worsening mood, early-morning insomnia, feelings of worthlessness, suicidal ideation, and cognitive symptoms after six weeks—combined with his prior history of MDD—indicate the onset of a new major depressive episode.
