--- DISORDER ---
Persistent Depressive Disorder (Dysthymia)

--- CASE STUDY ---

Diane Taylor, a 35-year-old laboratory technician, was referred to outpatient psychiatry by her employer’s employee assistance program after becoming tearful during a mild criticism in her annual performance review. She reported feeling “low for years” and described persistent feelings of inadequacy, guilt, and frustration with her work, which she viewed as a “dead end.” Diane’s symptoms have waxed and waned but remained consistent over the past 3 years. She denied active suicidal ideation, psychotic or manic symptoms, and significant neurovegetative changes.

Ms. Taylor’s social interactions are limited; she feels shy and awkward around unfamiliar people, has few female friends, and a history of dysfunctional and occasionally abusive romantic relationships. She enjoys jogging and outdoor activities.

Her personal history includes chronic mild depression beginning in high school, initially triggered by her father’s leukemia. She responded well to psychotherapy at that time. She disclosed childhood sexual abuse by a family friend, which contributes to her interpersonal difficulties. Her family history includes a father who is a pharmacist and a mother who was emotionally distant and depressed.

She began combination therapy with citalopram and supportive psychotherapy, which is appropriate given the interplay of chronic depression, personality traits (withdrawal, restricted affectivity, intimacy avoidance, sensitivity to criticism), and trauma history.

Discussion: Diane presents with a chronic depressive syndrome of mild to moderate severity, impairing occupational and social functioning without meeting criteria for a major depressive episode at the time of evaluation. Her persistent depressive symptoms, personality traits, and history of trauma make her presentation consistent with DSM-5-TR criteria for persistent depressive disorder.

--- DSM-5 SYMPTOMS ---

Depressed mood for most of the day, more days than not, for ≥2 years

Chronic low self-esteem and feelings of inadequacy

Impairment in occupational and interpersonal functioning

Absence of hypomanic, manic, or psychotic episodes

Intermittent periods of relatively normal mood may occur but do not exceed 2 months

Psychological symptoms predominate over neurovegetative symptoms
