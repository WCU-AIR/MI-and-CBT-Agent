--- DISORDER ---
Personality Disorders

--- CASE STUDY ---

Frazier Archer was a 34-year-old single white man who contacted a mood and personality disorders research program after an ex-friend called him "borderline," wanting to learn more about his personality.

During the diagnostic interview, Mr. Archer was initially reluctant to share personal information, fearing misunderstanding or misuse of his words. He described regular, almost daily situations in which he believed he was being lied to or deceived, particularly by people in leadership positions or those with psychology training. He felt he could detect manipulation while others "drank the Kool-Aid."

Employed as an assistant manager in a grocery store chain, Mr. Archer was highly detail-oriented, had trouble delegating tasks, and frequently worked long hours correcting coworkers’ mistakes. Colleagues and employers reported he focused excessively on rules, lists, and small details and needed to be more friendly. He had held numerous jobs, quitting as often as being fired.

Mr. Archer emphasized valuing quality over productivity, attributing his high standards to a "bad temper" and emotional ups and downs. Socially, he engaged only superficially with a few acquaintances and spent most of his time alone. He could recall exact moments when friends and lovers had "betrayed" him.

He denied trauma, substance use, head injuries, and prior psychiatric diagnoses or treatment. He reported social anxiety and childhood academic difficulties, which he attributed to his high standards and "unreliable" friends.

Mental status examination: well-groomed, cooperative, oriented; speech varied between slow, deliberate responses and long, digressive, angry answers; coherent; no perceptual disturbances; affect constricted and occasionally inappropriate; reported apathy about living but no active suicidal or homicidal ideation. He became irritated when denied copies of research questionnaires.

Discussion: Mr. Archer demonstrates long-standing, inflexible, dysfunctional patterns of interpersonal behavior, including distrust and suspiciousness (paranoid traits) and preoccupation with perfectionism and control (obsessive-compulsive traits). He meets DSM-5 criteria for both paranoid personality disorder (PPD) and obsessive-compulsive personality disorder (OCPD). Comorbidity of personality disorders is common with PPD. Distinctions were made from schizophrenia and OCD.
