--- DISORDER ---
Delirium

--- CASE STUDY ---

Wesley Brown, a 63-year-old married white businessman, was found down in the road by police and brought to the emergency room for evaluation of "psychotic behavior." His family reported a 2-week history of strange behavior, including running around the kitchen with knives, sending paranoid emails about the justice system, displaying guns to neighbors, seeing people in walls, and suspecting his wife of infidelity. He had been missing for three days prior to admission, and his car was found with numerous prescription and over-the-counter medication bottles, as well as items from his gun collection.

Medical history included coronary artery disease with bypass surgery 5 years prior, chronic back pain, and multiple spinal surgeries with associated daily opioid use. Outpatient medications included metoprolol, oxycodone, cyclobenzaprine as needed, and diazepam as needed.

On examination, Mr. Brown was confused, combative, yet somnolent. Vital signs included temperature 39.9°C, heart rate 115 bpm, and blood pressure 172/74. Physical findings included dry skin and oral mucosa, mydriasis, hypoactive bowel sounds, urinary retention, epistaxis, and depressed reflexes. CT head was negative; cervical spine CT showed degeneration. Blood alcohol screen was negative; labs were within normal limits. Urine toxicology was positive for benzodiazepines and tricyclics (likely cyclobenzaprine cross-reactivity).

Mental status examination revealed fluctuating attention and awareness, unkempt appearance, lack of cooperation, slurred speech, psychomotor retardation, and alternating subdued and agitated affect. He exhibited paranoid ideation, visual and auditory misperceptions, tangential thought process, and impaired judgment and insight. Mini-Mental State Exam scored 16/30 (orientation 7/10, attention/calculation 3/5, recall 2/3, sentence writing 1/1, design copying 1/1).

Discussion: Mr. Brown’s acute onset, fluctuating attention and awareness, cognitive deficits, perceptual disturbances, and evidence of a physiological trigger (medications, possible opioid effects) fulfill DSM-5-TR criteria for delirium. The anticholinergic properties of cyclobenzaprine, combined with benzodiazepine and opioid use, likely precipitated his condition. Other contributing factors include chronic pain and sleep disruption.
