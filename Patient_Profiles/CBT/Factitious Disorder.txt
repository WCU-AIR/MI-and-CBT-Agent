--- DISORDER ---
Factitious Disorder

--- CASE STUDY ---

Rebecca Ehrlich was a 24-year-old woman who had been hospitalized 2 days earlier for severe abdominal pain related to Crohn's disease. She was admitted through the emergency room for a flare-up. The psychiatric consultation-liaison service was called after nurses expressed concern that she was sad and lonely and having difficulty adjusting to her medical condition.

During interview, Ms. Ehrlich stated that her pain was excruciating but denied sadness or loneliness, noting that she was visiting from out of town and that no one knew she was in the hospital. Her only prior therapy had been in college for anxiety related to test-taking and career choices, successfully treated with cognitive-behavioral therapy. She denied other psychiatric history or prior psychiatric medications. She had studied psychology in college and worked part-time as a hospital orderly, considering a career in medicine or nursing.

Ms. Ehrlich reported that recurrent abdominal pain had disrupted her social life and work, causing job loss and missed interviews. She communicated daily with an online bowel disorders support group and said her aunt, also with Crohn's disease, was the only family member who understood her. Collateral information from her mother indicated a history of at least six hospitalizations, though Ms. Ehrlich reported fewer. Attempts to contact prior providers were initially unsuccessful.

On examination, she was cooperative, conversant, and calm, with fluent speech and linear thought processes. She denied paranoia, hallucinations, or suicidality. Attention and memory were intact. Although she appeared sad initially, she became more engaged and euthymic as the interview progressed. She was frustrated when pressed for details about prior care. Following normal endoscopy and colonoscopy results, she removed her IV and left the hospital before discharge.

Discussion: Ms. Ehrlich’s behavior meets DSM-5 criteria for factitious disorder: presenting as ill through falsification of symptoms, without obvious external rewards or alternative diagnoses. The recurrent pattern of deceit, including fleeing the hospital after normal results, supports the diagnosis. Motivation is unclear but may include assuming the sick role or gaining attention. Factitious disorder differs from malingering, which involves concrete external incentives.
