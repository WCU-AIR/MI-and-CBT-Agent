--- CASE STUDY ---
Yvonne Perez was a 23‑year‑old Hispanic woman who presented for an outpatient psychiatric evaluation 3 weeks after giving birth to her second child. She was referred by her breastfeeding nurse with concerns about her “feeling hopeless and exhausted” and displaying a flat affect and significant fatigue.
Ms. Perez reported that she had felt worried and unenthusiastic since discovering the pregnancy, which she and her husband had not planned. Her husband had expressed a preference that she terminate—a choice she refused on religious grounds—and grew increasingly critical when she felt too tired to continue her part‑time work at a local café. After delivery, breastfeeding proved difficult: her son refused her breast, spit up frequently, and remained colicky, forcing her to hold him most of each day. She began to believe her baby was “rejecting me” and wondered whether she deserved this hardship because she had not wanted the pregnancy.
With her husband working long hours away from home and no family nearby, Ms. Perez struggled to care for her newborn and her demanding 16‑month‑old daughter. Over the past several weeks, she slept very little, felt constantly fatigued, cried daily, and worried about how she would manage each day. Her mother‑in‑law, who had just arrived to help, attributed her mood to typical postpartum “hormonal blues.”
On mental status examination, Ms. Perez was casually dressed and cooperative but tearful. She made minimal eye contact, her speech was fluent yet slowed with increased latency, and her affect was constricted. She denied thoughts of suicide or self‑harm and reported no hallucinations or true delusions, though she described overvalued ideas of punishment. She was fully oriented, registered three objects but recalled only one after five minutes, and demonstrated average intelligence. Her insight and judgment were fair to good.
