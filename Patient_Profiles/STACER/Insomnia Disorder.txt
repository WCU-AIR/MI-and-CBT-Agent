--- DISORDER ---
Insomnia Disorder

--- CASE STUDY ---

Aidan Johns, a 30-year-old graduate student in English, visited a psychiatrist for difficulty staying asleep. The problem began 4 months earlier, with early morning awakenings at 3:00 AM and inability to return to sleep. Daytime fatigue and difficulty concentrating led him to worry about completing his doctoral dissertation and teaching responsibilities.

He initially had no particular thoughts on waking, but the persistent sleep disturbance caused anticipatory anxiety about the day ahead. Some mornings he lay awake next to his fiancée, other mornings he got up early to work.

After one month of interrupted sleep, he consulted a physician's assistant who prescribed a sedative-hypnotic, which was ineffective. Falling asleep was never the issue; the problem was staying asleep. He avoids caffeine after 2:00 PM, limits tennis to mornings, and consumes one to two glasses of wine nightly.

Mental status examination: Aidan appeared slender, fit, pleasant, and open. He was not visibly tired and dressed as a young academic. He described his low-level anxiety regarding sleep and reported stress related to work and relationship strain. He had previously completed 3 years of psychodynamic psychotherapy in college. He has historically been an “easy sleeper.”

Discussion: Aidan reports 4 months of dissatisfaction with sleep, difficulty maintaining sleep, early morning awakenings, daytime fatigue, impaired concentration, mild anxiety, and interpersonal and vocational impairment. No other medical, psychiatric, or substance use disorders are indicated. DSM-5 criteria for insomnia disorder are met. Behavioral patterns, including worry about sleep and use of wine and caffeine, may perpetuate the problem. Asthma and occasional use of β2-adrenergic agonists may contribute to arousal.
