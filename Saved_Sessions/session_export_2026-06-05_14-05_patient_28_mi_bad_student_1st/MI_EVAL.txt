Most damaging are explicit rejections: "No, you don't get to negotiate here," "No, there's no middle ground here," and "No, I'm not here to work on anything together. ### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist actively suppresses autonomy through controlling language, false binaries, and explicit rejection of client choice. Prescriptive statements dominate: "You have to be strict—no excuses," "You need to stop worrying," "You're either going to check your blood sugar daily...or you're going to pay the price." The question "Do you want to be healthy or not? Yes or no?" is a false binary that eliminates genuine choice and frames health behavior as the only legitimate option. When Daniela attempts to assert autonomy or propose graduated approaches, the psychiatrist responds with "No, you don't get to negotiate here" and "No, there's no middle ground here." The psychiatrist also removes himself from collaborative ownership while simultaneously controlling behavior—a contradictory and harmful stance that violates core MI principles and is likely to increase resistance and decrease intrinsic motivation.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no evocative skill. Questions asked are closed, leading, or rhetorical rather than open and exploratory: "Have you tried to quit smoking before?" (closed, based on false premise), "Have you been checking your blood sugar regularly?" (closed, leading, implies judgment), "Do you want to be healthy or not? Yes or no?" (false binary, rhetorical). Daniela offers multiple instances of change talk ("I do care about my health," "part of me wants to do better," "Maybe I could try checking more often"), but the psychiatrist does not amplify or reinforce any of it. Instead, he dismisses it as insufficient ("No, you can't just 'try'"). This represents a complete missed opportunity to evoke and strengthen her own motivation for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist actively assumes the expert role and explicitly denies that the client has any relevant wisdom. The opening statement ("I'll be telling you what you need to do") establishes this immediately. Throughout the session, the psychiatrist positions himself as the sole source of knowledge and authority, never acknowledging that Daniela knows her own life, fears, social context, and capacity for change better than anyone else. The psychiatrist does not ask questions that would elicit client expertise: "What do you know about yourself that might help us figure this out?" or "You know your friends better than I do—what do you think would happen if you told them?" True partnership is actively rejected through explicit statements: "No, I'm not here to work on anything together." This represents a fundamental violation of MI's core principle that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist systematically suppresses every instance of client change talk rather than cultivating it. Daniela offers multiple expressions of willingness and motivation: "I do care about my health" (ignored), "part of me wants to do better" (dismissed with "No, you can't just 'try'"), "Maybe I could try checking more often" (rejected with "No, there's no middle ground"), and "Maybe I can commit to doing it today" (rejected with "No, I'm not here to work on anything together"). Not only does the psychiatrist fail to evoke change talk; he actively punishes it when it emerges, using language like "No," "you can't," and "there's no middle ground" to shut down every expression of willingness. This is the opposite of cultivating change talk and directly undermines the client's intrinsic motivation for change. A skilled MI practitioner would recognize these offers of commitment and respond with encouragement and collaborative problem-solving.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist's approach to sustain talk is confrontational rather than skillfully softening. When Daniela expresses reasons for not changing ("It's hard," "I don't want my friends to think I'm different," "I'm scared"), the psychiatrist confronts directly and aggressively: "No, you can't just 'try,'" "You need to stop worrying," "Feeling scared is just holding you back." While the psychiatrist does not reinforce sustain talk (which is slightly better than amplifying resistance), he does not use MI techniques like double-sided reflection, reframing, or strategic redirection to reduce it. For example, he does not acknowledge validity ("It makes sense that you're worried about how your friends will react") or offer reframes ("What if telling your friends actually made it easier to manage your diabetes?"). His confrontational approach is likely to increase rather than decrease client resistance. The score reflects that he avoids reinforcing resistance but fails to employ skillful softening techniques.

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall Score: 1.1/5**

### Critical Findings

This session represents a **severe failure of Motivational Interviewing practice** and a violation of core principles of client-centered care. The psychiatrist demonstrates a confrontational, controlling, and judgmental approach that is fundamentally inconsistent with MI spirit and is likely to **decrease** rather than increase client motivation for change.

**Key Deficits:**
- Explicit rejection of collaboration, empathy, and autonomy support
- Systematic suppression of client change talk and motivation
- Dismissal of the client's actual presenting concern (peer disclosure and identity)
- Misidentification at session beginning and end, suggesting severe inattention
- Coercive language, ultimatums, and false binaries
- Developmental insensitivity to adolescent needs for peer belonging and identity

### Clinical Impact

Daniela expresses clear ambivalence and some willingness to change ("I do care about my health," "Maybe I can commit to doing it today"). A skilled MI practitioner would amplify this motivation. Instead, this psychiatrist suppresses it, leaving the client feeling isolated and hopeless ("Feeling like I'm completely alone in this...makes me want to just give up sometimes"). This approach is clinically harmful and likely to increase resistance and decrease sustained behavior change.

### Immediate Recommendations

1. **Foundational Training:** The psychiatrist must receive formal MI training focused on spirit and values before continuing clinical work with this client.
2. **Apology and Reset:** At the next session, explicitly acknowledge the previous session's failures and commit to a collaborative approach.
3. **Supervision:** Seek ongoing MI supervision with an MI-trained clinician to review recordings and develop core competencies.
4. **Engagement with Presenting Concern:** Recognize that peer disclosure is foundational to diabetes management for this adolescent and explore it collaboratively rather than dismissing it.
5. **Language Shift:** Replace confrontational language ("No," "you have to," "you can't") with collaborative language ("I hear," "let's explore," "what do you think").
