### Empathy

When Adrian expresses fear ("it's tough to picture my weekends without it"), psychiatrist pivots to confrontation rather than exploring ambivalence. Psychiatrist lectures about danger without demonstrating understanding of Adrian's internal world—his fear, ambivalence, sense of control, or social connections tied to use.

Direction is imposed unilaterally rather than co-created. Repeated demands for complete abstinence with immediate quit date lack exploration of Adrian's own goals, values, or reasons for change. Never asks Adrian what he thinks would help, never solicits his ideas about change strategies, never explores his preferences or concerns. Language reinforces hierarchy ("You need to," "You must," "You have to," "This is not optional"). Adrian's input is overruled rather than valued. No shared decision-making occurs. Adrian positioned as passive recipient of clinician's expertise rather than active partner.

#### **Score (1–5):** 1

Multiple opportunities for evocation are missed: Adrian's change talk ("I do want to avoid long-term damage," acknowledgment of infection connection) is not explored or amplified. No open-ended questions asked to draw out Adrian's own motivations, values, or resources. Closed, leading questions designed to elicit agreement with psychiatrist's position ("Don't you realize the damage?" "Don't you want to be healthy?") replace genuine evocation. Confrontational approach suppresses rather than evokes Adrian's motivation. Adrian's change talk remains minimal and underdeveloped by session end.

## Summary Assessment

### Strengths
- Psychiatrist maintains clear focus on target behavior (meth cessation)
- Genuine concern for Adrian's health and safety is evident
- Does not inadvertently reinforce or encourage continued use

### Critical Areas for Improvement
- **Fundamental MI Spirit Violation:** Entire session violates core MI principles (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation). Authoritarian, expert-driven model is fundamentally incompatible with MI approach.
- **Absence of Acceptance:** Repeated judgmental language communicates conditional regard and disapproval rather than unconditional positive regard.
- **Minimal Empathy:** Psychiatrist rarely reflects Adrian's perspective, emotions, or ambivalence. Adrian's internal experience is dismissed rather than understood.
- **Suppression of Change Talk:** Adrian's change talk is missed, ignored, or actively suppressed through confrontation. Confrontational approach suppresses rather than evokes motivation.
- **Coercive Pressure:** Repeated demands ("you must," "you have to," "this is not optional") create coercive dynamic that violates autonomy support and increases resistance.
- **Failed Collaboration:** Adrian's ideas, concerns, and requests for collaborative problem-solving are dismissed. Power held exclusively by psychiatrist.
- **No Genuine Evocation:** Psychiatrist tells rather than asks, imposes rather than explores, lectures rather than listens.
- **Defensive Escalation:** As session progresses, Adrian becomes increasingly defensive and withdrawn. By final exchange, Adrian expresses hesitation and ambivalence rather than growing motivation.

### Impact on Client Engagement
This session is likely to **decrease** rather than increase Adrian's motivation for change. Research demonstrates confrontational, pressure-based approaches increase client defensiveness and resistance. Adrian's progressive entrenchment and withdrawal indicate therapeutic rupture. Psychiatrist's approach violates fundamental MI principle that motivation resides within client and must be evoked, not imposed.

### Recommendation
Psychiatrist should seek formal MI training and supervision to develop genuine competence in this approach. Current session demonstrates clinician is not yet ready to conduct MI sessions without significant skill development and fundamental attitude shift regarding client autonomy and collaborative partnership.
