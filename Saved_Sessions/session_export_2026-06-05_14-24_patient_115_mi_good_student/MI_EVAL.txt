## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths

This session demonstrates solid motivational interviewing practice with several exemplary elements. The psychiatrist establishes a genuinely collaborative partnership from the opening, consistently inviting Ryan to set the agenda and shape the conversation. **Autonomy support is particularly strong**—the clinician never prescribes solutions, instead asking Ryan to identify what might help, which powerfully communicates respect for his right to choose. The psychiatrist's reflective listening is generally accurate and often sophisticated, capturing Ryan's core conflict between valuing social connection and wanting to avoid negative consequences. The handling of sustain talk is skillful, using double-sided reflections and values-based redirection without confrontation. The session maintains clear focus on the target behavior (drinking) and its relationship to Ryan's stated values, with logical progression toward concrete first steps. Most importantly, the psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental stance throughout, avoiding labeling and instead inviting Ryan to explore what his drinking means for him.

### Areas for Improvement

The most significant gap is **insufficient cultivation and amplification of change talk**. While Ryan produces several instances of change talk, the psychiatrist reflects these statements but rarely follows up with strategic questions designed to strengthen or elaborate on them. When Ryan rates importance at 7/10, for example, the psychiatrist validates but doesn't ask "What would need to happen for it to be an 8?" that would evoke more detailed change talk. The session also misses opportunities for **deeper emotional exploration**—when Ryan expresses fear about being "the guy sitting on the sidelines," the clinician reflects but doesn't explore the shame, identity threat, or deeper fears embedded in that statement. Additionally, the session relies heavily on reflections rather than **strategic open-ended questions** designed to evoke specific dimensions of change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment). The psychiatrist also makes subtle moves that slightly undermine the collaborative stance—statements like "I'm really impressed" position the clinician as evaluator rather than partner. Finally, while the psychiatrist respects Ryan's autonomy around moderation, there's limited **explicit exploration of whether moderation is actually Ryan's authentic goal** or a default assumption.

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change

This session successfully engages Ryan and establishes a strong therapeutic alliance, which is foundational for MI. Ryan feels heard, respected, and genuinely collaborative—he's not defensive or resistant. However, the session's impact on Ryan's actual motivation for change is likely moderate rather than strong. While Ryan identifies concrete first steps, his underlying motivation for change hasn't been deeply explored or significantly strengthened. His change talk remains somewhat tentative ("I guess," "I think," "probably"), and the psychiatrist hasn't systematically amplified his reasons for change or his confidence in his ability to change. Ryan may struggle to maintain commitment when encountering obstacles (social pressure, difficulty talking to friends, temptation to drink). A more strategic approach to evoking and amplifying change talk would likely increase the likelihood that Ryan follows through on his stated intentions and sustains change over time.

## Key Recommendations

1. **Systematically evoke and amplify change talk** using the DARN-CAT framework (Desire, Ability, Reasons, Need, Commitment, Taking steps, Activation). After each reflection of change talk, follow up with strategic questions that strengthen and elaborate on it.

2. **Deepen exploration of emotional fears and identity concerns** rather than staying at surface level. When Ryan expresses concerns about isolation or being different, explore the deeper emotional experience—the shame, fear, or identity threat.

3. **Use scaling questions as a gateway to deeper exploration**. Always follow up scaling questions with evocative questions that explore what's driving the rating and what would move it higher.

4. **Explore Ryan's actual change goals more explicitly** while respecting autonomy. Ask open-ended questions that invite him to consider different possibilities without pressure regarding whether moderation or abstinence fits better for him.

5. **Strengthen partnership by reducing clinician-as-evaluator positioning**. Replace evaluative statements with collaborative acknowledgments that emphasize Ryan's agency and problem-solving capacity.

6. **Explicitly explore specific fears** about opening up to friends. When Ryan expresses hesitation (6/10 confidence), don't just validate—explore what specifically he fears and what the worst-case and best-case scenarios actually look like.

7. **Ask more open-ended follow-up questions** about Ryan's own problem-solving. When he identifies strategies, ask follow-up questions that deepen his thinking and strengthen his commitment to his own ideas.

---

**Overall Assessment:** This clinician demonstrates competent MI practice with strong foundational elements but would benefit from developing greater skill in the strategic amplification of motivation for change. With these refinements, this clinician could move from solid (3.9) to excellent (4.5+) MI practice.
