### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance throughout. Strong validation of ambivalence ("both a source of comfort and a bit of a challenge") and normalization of phone use. Warm, affirming language maintained consistently. Minor missed opportunities for deeper affirmation of self-awareness and explicit normalization of ambivalence as healthy part of change process. Could have more explicitly affirmed courage in self-referring and willingness to experiment.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Solid reflective listening with accurate capture of competing values and emotional tension. Strong example: reflection about being "caught between wanting to keep that comforting routine and the desire to improve your daytime functioning." However, reflections tend toward surface level and lack emotional complexity. Misses opportunity to explore deeper emotional needs (e.g., underlying need for immediate reward or escape). When client expresses worry about stress/boredom without scrolling, clinician doesn't deeply explore what that fear represents about coping capacity. Reflections remain somewhat formulaic rather than capturing nuanced emotional landscape.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Maintains some focus on target behavior (phone use at night and sleep impact) but direction becomes increasingly diffuse. Early collaborative establishment of direction is appropriate, but session prematurely shifts toward problem-solving and action planning before fully exploring and resolving ambivalence. Lacks strategic direction toward deepening motivation for change. Moves quickly to "What would be some reasons or benefits?" before thoroughly exploring ambivalence. No clear direction regarding what happens if music strategy fails or if client continues to struggle. Accepts low-effort approach without gently redirecting toward deeper exploration of readiness or commitment.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong collaborative stance demonstrated through opening question positioning client as expert on priorities. Frequent invitations for input ("What do you think would be most important?", "What has helped you in the past?", "What might be a good first step?"). Language consistently inclusive and non-directive. However, collaboration becomes slightly unbalanced in latter half of session. Once music alternative emerges, psychiatrist gently steers toward this solution with phrases like "It seems like music could offer that calming presence." Represents subtle expert guidance rather than true co-construction. Could have asked more open questions to maintain equal partnership in solution development.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently respects and supports client autonomy throughout session. Avoids prescriptive language and frames suggestions as options for consideration. Honors ambivalence without pressuring toward change (validates 6/10 importance rating). Affirms right to choose approach that fits ("respects what you want and what feels manageable, rather than forcing something too rigid"). However, subtle moments where autonomy support could strengthen: doesn't explicitly remind client of right to *not* change, and phrase "still open to experimenting" gently implies experimenting is preferred direction. True autonomy support would include explicit acknowledgment that deciding against change is client's choice.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates some evocation of client's own motivations and resources, but inconsistent and often superficial. Early open questions appropriately draw out client thinking. However, evocation becomes weak in critical moments. When client mentions grades slipping, clinician doesn't deeply explore what that means, academic values, or vision for future—all powerful sources of intrinsic motivation. Asks leading question "What would be some reasons or benefits?" that presupposes client should set boundaries rather than genuinely evoking own reasons for change. Session lacks deep exploration of values, strengths, and self-efficacy. Doesn't ask about times client successfully managed impulses, what she's proud of, or what matters most beyond academics. Represents missed opportunity for evoking intrinsic motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong partnership stance demonstrated by consistently positioning client as expert on own life and experience. Uses inclusive language and asks for input before offering suggestions. Opening question immediately establishes client's priorities shape session agenda. Avoids expert role by asking questions rather than providing answers. However, partnership becomes slightly compromised in solution-building phase. Phrase "It seems like music could offer that calming presence" represents clinician subtly positioning themselves as having insight into what will work, rather than remaining equal partners. Progression from exploring alternatives to "Music sounds like a good fit" represents gentle shift toward clinician-guided direction. Doesn't explicitly invite co-creation of plan or ask about next session focus.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Significant weakness in session. Minimal skill in evoking, recognizing, and strategically reinforcing change talk. While client produces spontaneous change talk ("getting better sleep would help me feel less foggy," "I want to actually do well"), psychiatrist largely fails to amplify or deepen this language. When client expresses motivations, clinician simply reflects back without strategic reinforcement or exploration. Asks leading questions that presuppose change rather than evoking genuine change talk. Misses opportunities to evoke change talk related to ability, commitment, and need. Session focuses narrowly on desire talk without developing full spectrum of change language. When client expresses doubt, clinician moves to solution-building rather than evoking own problem-solving or confidence.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Adequate but inconsistent skill in handling sustain talk. Handles some sustain talk reasonably well through reflective listening and gentle reframing (e.g., double-sided reflection about competing values). However, misses critical opportunities to soften sustain talk more skillfully. When client says "maybe just not late at night?" clinician doesn't explore whether this represents genuine preference or ambivalence about commitment. When client expresses worry about feeling "more stressed or bored" without scrolling, psychiatrist doesn't adequately address concern or explore client's own coping resources. Instead offers music as solution, representing problem-solving rather than softening sustain talk through exploration. When client says "I'm not gonna stress too much about it," clinician doesn't gently explore whether this represents healthy optimism or avoidance of commitment.

### Softening Sustain Talk (continued)
**Comments (continued):** More skillful response would explore what would help client follow through if reaching for phone at night. Represents missed opportunity to deepen exploration and build genuine commitment.

## Summary Assessment

**Strengths:**
- ✓ Consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance
- ✓ Strong collaborative approach with genuine power-sharing in early exploration
- ✓ Solid reflective listening capturing ambivalence and competing values
- ✓ Respectful autonomy support with non-prescriptive language
- ✓ Appropriate use of scaling questions to assess readiness and confidence
- ✓ Warm, supportive tone building strong rapport
- ✓ Avoids confrontation and maintains alliance despite client ambivalence
- ✓ Good session pacing and structure

**Areas for Improvement:**
- ✗ **Weak evocation of change talk:** Minimal strategic amplification of client's own motivations; leading questions presuppose change
- ✗ **Premature problem-solving:** Shifts to solution-building before fully exploring and resolving ambivalence
- ✗ **Superficial reflections:** While accurate, lack emotional depth and complexity; miss underlying needs and fears
- ✗ **Incomplete exploration of values and meaning:** Doesn't deeply explore what better sleep, academic success, or health mean to client
- ✗ **Inadequate softening of sustain talk:** Accepts sustain talk at face value without exploring client's own resources
- ✗ **Limited cultivation of full change talk spectrum:** Focuses narrowly on desire talk without developing ability, need, and commitment talk
- ✗ **Missed affirmation opportunities:** Could more explicitly affirm self-awareness, courage, and willingness to experiment
- ✗ **Subtle shift toward expert guidance:** In solution phase, clinician gently steers toward music alternative rather than maintaining true co-construction

---

## Clinical Recommendation

This session demonstrates **adequate foundational MI competence** (3.4/5) with solid rapport and collaboration, but falls short of good practice (4/5+) due to **premature problem-solving that undermines genuine evocation of intrinsic motivation**. To improve, the psychiatrist should: (1) slow the exploration-to-action timeline, (2) spend more time exploring client values and meaning before problem-solving, (3) strategically amplify change talk when it emerges, (4) explore sustain talk more deeply rather than accepting it at face value, and (5) maintain genuine collaboration throughout solution-building. The critical insight is that MI prioritizes helping clients develop their own compelling reasons for change, not finding the right solution quickly.
