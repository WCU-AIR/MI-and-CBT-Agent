--- DISORDER ---
Brief Psychotic Disorder with Marked Stressors

--- CASE STUDY ---

Lara Gonzalez was a 51-year-old divorced freelance journalist who presented to the ER requesting evaluation for a suspected flea infestation. She believed tiny insects were burrowing into her skin, walls, rugs, and mattress. Over the 36 hours prior to presentation, she engaged in frantic cleaning, repeated showering, and consulted multiple exterminators, but no evidence of infestation was found. She threw away clothing and purchased new items, worsening her financial strain.

Ms. Gonzalez had no recent drug or alcohol use and no family history of psychiatric illness. Her history included depression in college, briefly treated with antidepressants. Her symptoms emerged in the context of multiple stressors, including her sister’s cancer diagnosis, menopause, financial strain, and a recent breakup. She described herself as obsessive with contamination concerns, historically worse during stress.

Mental status examination revealed a calm, cooperative, and engaged patient. She was tearful at times but smiled reactively. Thoughts were preoccupied with fleas, with firm delusional beliefs (e.g., hair turning into larvae, eggs coming from tears). She denied hallucinations and suicidal or homicidal ideation. Cognition was intact; insight was impaired, but judgment was reasonably intact. Dermatological, neurological, laboratory, and imaging evaluations were normal.

After a brief inpatient stay with low-dose antipsychotic medication and supportive psychotherapy, her preoccupation resolved within 2 weeks. She gained insight, recognizing her beliefs as “crazy thoughts” and attributing them to stress. Family corroborated her rapid return to baseline functioning.

Discussion: The rapid onset and resolution of delusional beliefs in the context of acute stressors support a DSM-5 diagnosis of brief psychotic disorder with marked stressors. Differential diagnoses such as delusional disorder, bipolar disorder, or personality disorders were considered but ruled out based on symptom duration, context, and rapid return to premorbid functioning. Favorable prognosis is supported by good premorbid functioning, identifiable acute stressors, and absence of prior psychiatric or family history.
