# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal acceptance throughout the session. Surface-level warmth is immediately undermined by judgmental language ("you really need to take this seriously," "You have to quit now," "you're risking your health unnecessarily"). No affirmations of Ahmed's strengths are provided, and his perspective is consistently invalidated. The clinician fails to normalize Ahmed's ambivalence or communicate unconditional positive regard. When Ahmed explains he works hard and takes care of himself otherwise, this strength is not acknowledged. The phrase "you're risking your health unnecessarily" conveys disapproval rather than acceptance of Ahmed's current readiness level.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Empathy is largely absent. The psychiatrist makes minimal effort to understand Ahmed's perspective or the meaning behind his behavior. When Ahmed states mini-cigars help him relax after work, this is not explored. When Ahmed distinguishes mini-cigars from cigarettes, the clinician dismisses rather than seeks to understand this differentiation. Pseudo-reflections ("I understand you feel that way, but...") are used as rhetorical devices to negate Ahmed's perspective rather than demonstrate genuine understanding. No complex reflections demonstrating deep emotional attunement are present. Ahmed's internal conflict and resistance are not explored or validated. The psychiatrist does not view resistance as information signaling misalignment with the client's readiness.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains clear, consistent direction—Ahmed should quit mini-cigars completely and immediately. However, direction is maintained authoritatively rather than collaboratively. The approach is rigid, with the same directive message repeated across all exchanges regardless of Ahmed's responses. The clinician does not strategically guide Ahmed through stages of change or recognize that Ahmed is in precontemplation. Direction is used to impose the clinician's agenda rather than to guide Ahmed toward self-discovery. No attempt is made to evoke Ahmed's own motivations or progressively deepen understanding of his ambivalence. The psychiatrist fails to ask questions that would help Ahmed discover his own reasons for change.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration is virtually absent. The psychiatrist adopts an expert stance throughout, positioning himself as the authority on what Ahmed should do. Repeated directives ("You have to quit now," "You need to quit completely," "Can you commit to not using any mini-cigars starting today?") demonstrate unilateral control. True collaboration involving joint problem-solving and shared decision-making does not occur. Ahmed's input does not substantively influence the session's direction. When Ahmed expresses concerns or resistance, these are dismissed rather than explored as valid contributions. Power-sharing is entirely absent. The clinician maintains control throughout, using hierarchical language that reinforces expert authority rather than partnership.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is fundamentally undermined. The psychiatrist repeatedly pressures Ahmed to make a specific choice (complete cessation starting today) rather than supporting his right to choose. Directives like "You have to quit now" and "You need to quit completely" minimize Ahmed's perception of choice and communicate that his autonomy is not respected. The clinician fails to acknowledge Ahmed's right to make choices differing from the recommendation. No exploration of Ahmed's own values or goals occurs to help him connect behavior change to his priorities. Fear-based language ("you're risking your health unnecessarily," "serious health problems") is coercive rather than autonomy-supportive. The approach is fundamentally coercive, not autonomy-supportive.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation is almost entirely absent. The psychiatrist tells Ahmed why he should change rather than asking Ahmed to explore his own reasons for change. Closed questions ("Have you thought about cutting back or quitting them?") do not evoke Ahmed's thinking. No questions invite Ahmed to generate his own reasons for change, connect to values, or discover his own solutions. Ahmed's strengths (works hard, takes care of himself otherwise) are not explored or amplified. His ambivalence and internal conflict are not explored despite being evident in his statements. The psychiatrist does not ask about Ahmed's ideas for change; instead, a solution is imposed. The approach is entirely didactic, with the clinician providing reasons for change rather than evoking Ahmed's own motivation.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership is fundamentally absent. The psychiatrist actively assumes the expert role and maintains this stance throughout with no meaningful effort to foster collaboration or power-sharing. Language like "you really need to," "you have to," and "you're risking your health unnecessarily" reinforces hierarchy rather than partnership. Ahmed's contributions do not substantively influence the session. When Ahmed explains his perspective, the clinician dismisses it and reiterates the same directive, demonstrating that Ahmed's input is not valued. True partnership would involve statements like "You know your body and your life better than I do" or "What's your sense of what might work for you?" These are absent. Ahmed's wisdom about his own situation is not valued or incorporated into the clinical approach.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Change talk is not cultivated. Ahmed does not spontaneously generate significant change talk; instead, he expresses sustain talk and resistance throughout. The psychiatrist does not ask strategic questions designed to evoke Ahmed's own reasons for change, such as "What concerns do you have about continuing?" or "How might your health be different if you stopped?" No questions invite Ahmed to explore his own pathway to change. When Ahmed expresses any ambivalence or hint of concern ("I don't want to put my health at risk"), this is not amplified or reinforced. The clinician provides reasons for change rather than evoking Ahmed's own reasons. No reinforcement of commitment language occurs. The psychiatrist fails to ask "What would need to happen for you to feel ready?" when Ahmed expresses reluctance, missing opportunities to cultivate change talk.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Sustain talk is not softened; it is reinforced through confrontation. Ahmed's sustain talk includes minimization ("They're just little, mini cigars"), rationalization ("I don't see how that's really bad for me"), and resistance ("I'm not ready to stop right away"). When Ahmed expresses sustain talk, the psychiatrist argues directly against it ("Mini-cigars still expose you to harmful chemicals"), which research shows strengthens rather than weakens resistance. No double-sided reflections, reframing, or strategic agreement techniques are used. The clinician does not redirect Ahmed's focus toward change talk or explore the function of mini-cigar use (stress relief). Instead of curiosity and strategic redirection, confrontation is used, which amplifies Ahmed's defensiveness and strengthens his resistance to change.

### Summary of Key Findings

#### **Strengths:**
- Clear identification and consistent focus on target behavior (mini-cigar use)
- Genuine concern for Ahmed's health evident throughout
- Factually accurate information about health risks associated with mini-cigars

#### **Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Fundamental misalignment with MI principles** across nearly all domains
- **Absence of evocation**: Clinician tells Ahmed why to change rather than asking Ahmed to explore his own reasons
- **Poor acceptance and empathy**: Ahmed's perspective is dismissed rather than understood; ambivalence is not explored or validated
- **Confrontational approach to sustain talk**: Direct argumentation strengthens rather than weakens resistance
- **Authoritarian expert stance**: Unilateral control maintained throughout; Ahmed's input does not shape the session
- **Autonomy undermined**: Pressure and directives used rather than support for Ahmed's right to choose

#### **Impact on Client Engagement:**
This session is **likely to harm rather than help** Ahmed's motivation for change. Ahmed ends the session more entrenched in his position ("I'm not convinced this mini-cigar thing is the big problem you're making it out to be") than he began. The psychiatrist has created an adversarial dynamic rather than a collaborative partnership. If behavior change occurs, it would likely be due to external pressure rather than internal motivation, making relapse highly probable.

---

### Primary Recommendations for Improvement

1. **Shift from telling to asking**: Replace directives with open-ended questions designed to evoke Ahmed's own reasons for change
2. **Develop genuine acceptance**: Actively affirm strengths, normalize ambivalence, and communicate unconditional positive regard
3. **Soften sustain talk**: Use double-sided reflections and reframing instead of direct argumentation
4. **Build partnership**: Seek client input and demonstrate that his perspective shapes the session
5. **Support autonomy**: Help Ahmed connect behavior change to his own values rather than external pressure
6. **Seek formal MI training**: This session demonstrates fundamental misalignment with evidence-based MI practice; formal training is essential

---

**Recommendation:** The psychiatrist should pursue formal Motivational Interviewing training (2-3 day workshop with ongoing supervision) immediately. Current approach, while well-intentioned, is counterproductive and likely to increase client resistance and reduce likelihood of sustained behavior change.
