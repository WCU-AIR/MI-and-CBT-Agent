**Comments:** Almost entirely absent in first six exchanges. Clinician tells Andrea why she should change ("Drinking is dangerous," "you're going to end up with serious health problems") rather than evoking her own motivations, values, and resources. No exploration of Andrea's reasons for potentially changing, her values, or her concerns. ### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Fundamentally absent throughout first six exchanges. Clinician actively assumes expert role and conveys that clinician, not Andrea, possesses knowledge and authority to determine what is best. Expert positioning evident in "I'm telling you," "you need to understand," "I'm going to give you a strict plan." Dismissal of client expertise—when Andrea states her experience ("I'm careful about it," "it's manageable," "I don't see it as a problem"), clinician responds with contradiction rather than curiosity. Power imbalance established through directive framing ("you have to," "you must," "you need to") rather than collaborative exploration. Unilateral decision-making violates partnership principle. Andrea's explicit resistance ("I don't want to say yes just because you're telling me to") indicates she experiences clinician as controlling authority, not collaborative partner. Final two exchanges show improved partnership through positioning Andrea as expert on her own experience and inviting her contributions to shape session direction. ### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually absent throughout most of session. Clinician makes no skillful effort to evoke change talk; instead, argumentative approach actively suppresses it. No questions designed to elicit change talk (concerns about drinking, how life might be different, values, importance of health). Clinician tells Andrea why she should change—opposite of cultivating her own change talk. Andrea's responses remain focused on sustain talk and status quo maintenance ("I don't think it's a big deal," "I don't see why I should stop completely," "I don't feel ready"). Critical moment occurs in final exchange when clinician asks evocative question: "what do you think might be some benefits if you decided to cut back?" Andrea generates first explicit change talk: "maybe I'd feel less tired the next day or not have to worry about getting in trouble or anything. So, that could be better." However, clinician fails to recognize, reinforce, or amplify this emerging change talk. Andrea immediately minimizes her own articulation ("But honestly, I don't think those benefits are big enough"), and clinician does not respond by reflecting back, exploring further, or reinforcing Andrea's own articulation of reasons to change. Represents missed opportunity to cultivate and strengthen critical moment of client-generated motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk abundant throughout session and actively amplified rather than softened by clinician's approach. Andrea's sustain talk includes: "I don't think it's a big deal," "It's not like I'm doing it all the time," "Everyone does it sometimes," "I'm careful about it," "I don't really think it's something I need to seriously stop," "I don't feel like it's hurting my health," "I don't think I've had a reason to quit," "I don't think it's that big of a problem yet." Clinician responds to sustain talk with direct argument ("you're making excuses," "that's exactly what I'm talking about," "you need to take responsibility"), which is opposite of softening—reinforces sustain talk by creating defensive reactions. No use of MI techniques for softening sustain talk: no double-sided reflections acknowledging both perspectives, no reframing that shifts perspective without confrontation, no strategic redirection toward change talk, no exploration of ambivalence. Final exchanges show improved approach—collaborative stance reduces need for Andrea to defend position—but no explicit softening of sustain talk through reflections, reframing, or strategic redirection. Overall reflects poor MI practice in managing sustain talk.

### **SUMMARY OF CLINICAL FEEDBACK**

#### **Strengths:**
- **Late recognition of ineffective approach:** Psychiatrist demonstrates capacity to shift from directive to collaborative stance in final exchanges, suggesting some ability for self-correction
- **Basic reflective listening in final exchanges:** Simple reflections ("It sounds like drinking is mainly a social thing for you") demonstrate foundational empathic listening
- **Open-ended questions in final portion:** Questions like "Can you tell me what role alcohol plays for you?" represent appropriate MI technique
- **Successful elicitation of change talk:** Despite poor overall technique, clinician eventually asks question that successfully evokes Andrea's own articulation of potential benefits

#### **Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- **Authoritarian, expert-driven stance:** First 75% of session characterized by directive language, unilateral decision-making, and power imbalance violating fundamental MI principles
- **Argumentative approach:** Clinician argues with Andrea's perspective rather than exploring it, predictably increasing resistance and sustain talk
- **Failure to evoke intrinsic motivation:** Clinician tells Andrea why she should change rather than inviting her to generate own reasons
- **Dismissal of client autonomy:** Repeated minimization of Andrea's right to choose and attempts to impose solutions
- **Missed opportunities to build on strengths:** Andrea's protective factors (careful, no major problems, values school and music) never acknowledged or leveraged
- **Lack of ambivalence exploration:** No exploration of Andrea's mixed feelings about drinking or her own concerns
- **Failure to reinforce emerging change talk:** When Andrea articulates potential benefits of change, clinician does not amplify or reinforce this critical moment
- **Inconsistent approach:** Dramatic shift in technique in final exchanges suggests lack of coherent MI framework; clinician responding reactively rather than strategically

#### **Impact on Client Engagement and Change:**
This session is likely to **decrease Andrea's motivation for change** and **damage the therapeutic alliance**. Andrea enters in precontemplation (normal, expected stage), but clinician argues with her, dismisses her experience, and attempts to force compliance. This predictably generates resistance and defensive sustain talk. Andrea explicitly states she does not feel heard or respected: "I don't want to say yes just because you're telling me to, you know?" Therapeutic relationship is compromised. If Andrea returns, she is likely to be more defensive and less willing to engage authentically. Clinician has positioned herself as adversary rather than collaborative partner—opposite of what is needed to support behavior change.

#### **Current MI Competency Assessment:**
**Pre-competent level.** Requires significant training and practice to achieve basic MI competency. Inconsistency between early and late portions suggests lack of coherent MI framework rather than isolated skill deficits.

#### **Most Critical Improvements Needed:**
1. Adopt collaborative, partnership-based stance
2. Eliminate argumentative responses
3. Evoke rather than tell
4. Recognize and reinforce change talk
5. Support autonomy explicitly

### **RECOMMENDATIONS FOR SUPERVISION AND TRAINING**

**Priority 1: Adopt Collaborative Partnership Stance**
- Shift from expert-driven to collaborative approach
- Position Andrea as expert on her own experience
- Practice open-ended questions that invite perspective sharing without judgment
- Example: Instead of "you really need to stop drinking," try "I'm curious about your drinking. Can you help me understand what role it plays in your life right now?"

**Priority 2: Eliminate Argumentative Responses**
- Replace all argumentative statements with reflective listening
- When client expresses sustain talk, reflect back with genuine curiosity rather than correcting
- Commit to zero argumentative statements in next session
- Record sessions and review for argumentative patterns

**Priority 3: Evoke Rather Than Tell**
- Replace statements about why client should change with questions inviting exploration of own motivations
- Develop set of evocative questions specific to alcohol use
- Practice asking with genuine curiosity rather than as leading questions
- Examples: "What concerns do you have about your drinking?" "How does drinking fit with your priorities around school and music?" "What would be the benefits of cutting back?"

**Priority 4: Recognize and Reinforce Change Talk**
- Listen specifically for change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment to change)
- When heard, pause and reflect back immediately to amplify
- Use complex reflections that deepen meaning
- Example: When Andrea said "maybe I'd feel less tired," clinician should have responded: "So you're noticing that cutting back could help you feel better physically. Tell me more about that."

**Priority 5: Support Autonomy Explicitly**
- Replace directive language ("you need to," "you have to," "you must") with language emphasizing client choice and control
- Example: Instead of "I'm going to give you a strict plan: no alcohol at all, no exceptions," try "There are different approaches we could explore together. What feels right to you?"
- Practice phrases: "you might consider," "some people find it helpful to," "what do you think about," "what feels right to you?"

**Priority 6: Seek Formal MI Training and Supervision**
- Enroll in formal MI training (e.g., through MINT—Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers)
- Work with supervisor or MI trainer to review session recordings
- Set specific, measurable MI competency goals
- Track progress over time

**Recommended Next Steps:**
1. Record next session with client permission
2. Review recording with supervisor focusing on: argumentative statements, use of reflective listening, evocative questions, recognition of change talk
3. Complete formal MI training module
4. Practice MI role-plays with feedback
5. Establish regular supervision schedule to monitor progress toward competency
