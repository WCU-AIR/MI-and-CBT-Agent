
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

This document outlines a structured framework for assessing therapist competence in Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT). 
It is used for training, supervision, and evaluation. Each item is scored on a 0–6 scale:

0 = Poor
2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Conduct Comprehensive Case Conceptualization Including All Maintaining Factors**
- **Action:** In the next session, explicitly map out all factors maintaining the hoarding disorder: (1) difficulty discarding due to anxiety about loss/regret, (2) excessive acquisition behavior, (3) depressive symptoms and hopelessness, (4) social isolation and shame, (5) underlying beliefs about possessions (e.g., "I need to be prepared," "possessions = security," "throwing things away = waste"). Create a visual diagram showing how these factors interact and maintain each other.
- **Example:** "Wendy, I'd like us to step back and look at the bigger picture. We've identified that anxiety about discarding is a big part of the problem. But I'm also noticing that you mentioned bringing home more stuff, feeling hopeless, and being isolated for 15 years. These things are all connected. Let me draw this out so we can see how they work together and what we need to address."
- **Practice:** Use the case conceptualization framework to guide all future interventions. Before each session, review the diagram and ask: "What part of this cycle are we addressing today? What parts are we missing?"

### 2. **Develop a Multi-Session Treatment Plan Addressing Acquisition Behavior**
- **Action:** Create explicit homework and in-session work to reduce acquisition. This might include: (1) stimulus control (avoid going to thrift shop, unsubscribe from promotional emails), (2) identifying triggers for acquisition (boredom, anxiety, habit), (3) developing alternative behaviors (call a friend, go for a walk, read), (4) examining beliefs about acquisition ("What am I hoping this item will do for me?").
- **Example:** "I notice you mentioned bringing home more stuff from work and other places. That's actually as important to address as the decluttering. Let's think about what's driving that. When you bring something home, what are you thinking or feeling? What do you hope it will do for you? And what could you do instead when you feel that urge?"
- **Practice:** Assign homework to track acquisition: "For the next week, notice each time you bring something home. Write down what it was, where it came from, and what you were thinking or feeling. We'll look at the patterns together."

### 3. **Integrate Depressive Symptoms into Treatment Through Behavioral Activation**
- **Action:** Explicitly address the patient's low mood (4/10), loneliness, and 15-year social isolation. Use behavioral activation to schedule valued activities and social contact. Explore the relationship between depression and hoarding (depression reduces motivation for change; hoarding increases shame and isolation).
- **Example:** "Wendy, I'm noticing that your mood is pretty low today, and you mentioned feeling lonely and isolated. I'm wondering if that's connected to the hoarding—like, the clutter keeps you from inviting people over, which increases the loneliness, which makes you feel more hopeless about changing things. Does that resonate? I think we need to work on both the hoarding and the isolation together."
- **Practice:** Assign behavioral activation homework: "This week, I'd like you to do one thing that you enjoy or that connects you with someone else. It could be calling a friend, going for a walk, visiting a café—something that gets you out and engaged. We'll track how it affects your mood."

### 4. **Deepen Cognitive Work Using Formal Thought Records and Behavioral Experiments**
- **Action:** Move beyond basic cognitive restructuring to systematic thought records that include: (1) situation, (2) automatic thought, (3) evidence for, (4) evidence against, (5) alternative thought, (6) belief strength before/after, (7) behavioral experiment to test the thought. Design specific behavioral experiments to test anxious predictions.
- **Example:** "Let's use a thought record to really examine this thought: 'What if I throw something away that I'll need later?' We'll look at all the evidence, and then we'll design an experiment. For example, you could throw away one item that you're pretty sure you don't need, and we'll see what actually happens. Does the catastrophe occur? What do you actually feel?"
- **Practice:** Have the patient complete a thought record for homework each week. Review it in session and identify patterns in her thinking (catastrophizing, overestimation of threat, underestimation of ability to cope).

### 5. **Explore and Modify Core Beliefs About Possessions**
- **Action:** Move beyond surface-level thoughts to identify and modify core beliefs. Use techniques like the "downward arrow" to identify underlying assumptions. Common core beliefs in hoarding include: "I need to be prepared for any scenario," "Possessions represent my worth," "Throwing things away means I'm wasteful," "I might need this someday."
- **Example:** "When you think about throwing something away, what's the worst thing that could happen? [Patient: 'I might need it later.'] And if you did need it later and didn't have it, what would that mean about you? [Patient: 'That I wasn't prepared, that I failed.'] So it sounds like there's a belief that you need to be prepared for anything, and if you're not, that means something bad about you. Is that right? Let's explore where that belief comes from and whether it's really true."
- **Practice:** Use the "pie chart" technique to examine the evidence for and against core beliefs. For example: "What percentage of the time do you actually need something you threw away? What percentage of the time do you throw something away and never need it?"

### 6. **Address the "Maybe" Box as a Potential Safety Behavior**
- **Action:** Explicitly discuss the "maybe" box as a temporary tool, not a permanent solution. Plan for how items will be reviewed and ultimately discarded. Set a specific timeline (e.g., "We'll review the maybe box in 2 weeks and decide what to do with items").
- **Example:** "The 'maybe' box is a great way to reduce pressure in the moment, but I want to make sure it doesn't become a way to avoid making decisions forever. Let's plan that in two weeks, we'll review what's in the box together and make decisions about each item. Does that feel okay?"
- **Practice:** In future sessions, review the "maybe" box and help the patient make decisions about items using decision-making criteria (e.g., "Have you used this in the past year? Do you have a specific plan to use it? Does it align with your values?").

### 7. **Implement Prior Homework Review as Standard Practice**
- **Action:** At the beginning of each session, explicitly review the previous homework. Ask: "How did the homework go? What happened? What did you learn? What got in the way?" Use this information to adjust the treatment plan.
- **Example:** "Before we start today, let's talk about the homework from last week. You were going to sort through a small pile on Saturday morning. How did that go? Were you able to do it? What happened?"
- **Practice:** Create a homework tracking sheet that documents what was assigned, whether it was completed, what the patient learned, and any barriers. Review this sheet at the beginning of each session.

### 8. **Develop Explicit Relapse Prevention and Obstacle Planning**
- **Action:** Before assigning homework, explicitly discuss potential obstacles and develop specific plans to address them. Include: (1) what to do if anxiety becomes overwhelming, (2) what to do if the patient reverts to avoidance, (3) how to recognize early warning signs of relapse, (4) how to get back on track.
- **Example:** "We've talked about what might get in the way—like feeling overwhelmed and wanting to give up. Let's make a specific plan for that. If you start feeling really anxious, what will you do? [Patient: 'Take breaks, remind myself it's okay.'] Great. And if you do give up and put everything back, what will you do then? Will you try again the next day? Will you call me? Let's have a plan so you don't get stuck."
- **Practice:** Create a "relapse prevention plan" document that the patient can refer to if she encounters difficulties between sessions.

### 9. **Increase Mid-Session Feedback Elicitation to Ensure Ongoing Collaboration**
