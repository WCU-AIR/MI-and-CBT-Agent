
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrates partial understanding of the patient's experience but misses critical nuances and fails to demonstrate deep comprehension of the patient's "internal reality."

**What the therapist understood:**
- The basic behavioral pattern: hair pulling occurs after work, involves ritualistic behavior, and is followed by relief
- The emotional aftermath: shame, self-directed anger, frustration
- The functional impairment: social avoidance and withdrawal
- The automatic nature of the behavior: "I don't even think about it until afterward"

**Critical gaps in understanding:**

1. **Tension-relief cycle mischaracterization:** The patient explicitly states, "I feel this tension building up all day, and then when I pull a hair out...it feels like this little moment of relief." The therapist acknowledges this ("when you get home and feel tired or bored, the hair pulling kind of comes up automatically and gives you some relief") but never explores what this tension is, where it originates, or how it functions as a maintaining factor. In trichotillomania, the tension-relief cycle is central to the disorder's maintenance. The therapist does not investigate whether this is primarily affect regulation, sensory seeking, or habit-driven.

2. **Automatic vs. intentional behavior:** The patient describes pulling as "automatic, like I don't even think about it until afterward," yet the case profile explicitly states Zoe is "aware and intentional in her hair-pulling and skin-picking behaviors." The therapist does not clarify this apparent contradiction or explore the distinction between automatic urges and deliberate pulling. This is crucial for treatment planning—if behavior is intentional, exposure and response prevention (ERP) is indicated; if automatic, habit reversal training may be primary.

3. **The "loop" the patient describes:** The patient states, "It's like a loop — I feel bad, but then later I end up doing it again because I don't know how to deal with the urge." This suggests a negative reinforcement cycle (pulling to escape negative affect) and possible avoidance of distress tolerance. The therapist does not explore this mechanism or ask what "dealing with the urge" means to the patient.

4. **Shame and self-directed anger:** While the therapist acknowledges these emotions, there is no exploration of their function. Are they maintaining factors? Do they trigger subsequent pulling episodes? The patient's statement, "I feel bad about it, but I do it anyway," suggests possible emotion regulation difficulties, but the therapist does not investigate.

5. **Social impairment specificity:** The patient mentions avoiding social situations due to visible scarring and hair loss, but the therapist never asks about specific situations avoided, the severity of avoidance, or whether this represents social anxiety or purely appearance-based avoidance. This distinction affects treatment.

6. **Previous treatment attempts:** The case profile notes "attempted to stop the behaviors for months at a time but relapsed." The therapist never asks about these attempts—what strategies were used? Why did they fail? What was the relapse like? This information is essential for understanding what has and hasn't worked.

In the second session, the therapist again demonstrates surface-level understanding. The patient says, "Even when I try to stop or distract myself, it feels like the urge is always there, creeping up when I least expect it." The therapist does not explore what "always there" means—is this constant, or episodic? Does the urge have a quality (visual, tactile, emotional)? The therapist does not ask clarifying questions.

**Summary:**

The therapist grasps explicit statements and basic emotional content but misses critical mechanisms underlying the disorder. Understanding remains at a surface level without exploration of maintaining factors, functional relationships, or nuanced emotional/behavioral patterns.

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
### **Score: 4/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrates adequate warmth, empathy, and professionalism without significant interpersonal problems, but lacks the optimal tailoring and depth expected of excellent therapeutic presence.

**Strengths:**
- Warm, welcoming tone: "Hi there! It's good to see you today"

**Limitations:**

1. **Lack of depth in empathic attunement:** While the therapist is warm, there is minimal demonstration of truly understanding the patient's internal experience. Empathy requires not just validation but evidence of grasping the patient's perspective. 2. **Premature reassurance:** The therapist frequently offers reassurance ("Yeah, that's totally okay," "You're already doing a good job") before fully understanding the patient's concerns. While supportive, this can inadvertently minimize the patient's struggle or suggest the therapist is uncomfortable with the patient's distress.

3. **Lack of genuine curiosity:** The therapist asks questions but often in a leading or suggestive manner ("Maybe you were feeling stressed or bored?") rather than with genuine curiosity about the patient's unique experience. True interpersonal effectiveness includes conveying authentic interest in understanding the patient's world.

4. **Missed opportunities for connection:** When the patient expresses vulnerability ("I just don't want to feel like I'm failing if I don't stop right away"), the therapist responds supportively but does not deepen the connection by exploring what "failure" means to the patient or acknowledging the courage it takes to try despite fear of failure.

A more attuned therapist would recognize the patient's frustration and offer more structure.

**Summary:**

The therapist is warm, supportive, and professional with no significant interpersonal deficits. However, empathic depth is limited, and the therapist relies on reassurance and validation rather than genuine curiosity and attunement. Interpersonal effectiveness is adequate but not optimized.
