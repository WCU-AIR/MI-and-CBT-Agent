### Acceptance

**Comments:** Minimal acceptance demonstrated throughout most of the session. Psychiatrist uses explicitly judgmental language ("You're just making excuses again," "Loyalty to those friends isn't worth ruining your life") that communicates disapproval and dismissal of client's lived experience. ### Empathy

When client articulates genuine barriers (peer loyalty, difficulty changing within same social environment, need for support), psychiatrist dismisses these as "excuses" rather than exploring with curiosity. ### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2.5/5
**Comments:** Some focus maintained on target behavior (reducing drinking, avoiding reoffending, pursuing education/employment), but approach is inconsistent and often counterproductive. In exchanges 2-4, psychiatrist repeatedly demands commitment ("Are you ready to quit drinking today?") without exploring what would actually support change. Improved direction in exchanges 5-7 where focus shifts to concrete, manageable steps: working through polytech forms, identifying courses, scheduling specific time. This represents appropriate strategic direction. However, direction undermined by return to pressure in exchange 9 ("you have to stay committed," "no backing out"). Psychiatrist never strategically explores relationship between client's stated goals (job with cars, stable home, different fatherhood) and specific behaviors needing change. True direction in MI involves helping client see connection between values and actions through evocation rather than imposition.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Severely compromised throughout most of session. In exchanges 2-4, psychiatrist adopts expert-driven, authoritarian stance with phrases like "You have to," "You need to," "You can't," "You really should" that communicate unilateral decision-making rather than joint problem-solving. When client expresses legitimate concerns about cutting off only social support system, psychiatrist dismisses this: "Loyalty to those friends isn't worth ruining your life." Notable improvement in exchanges 5-7 with collaborative language: "Let's take a step back and explore," "Maybe we can look at small steps together," "I can help you with that." These phrases invite partnership and shared decision-making. However, collaboration abandoned in exchange 9 with return to unilateral direction: "you have to stay committed," "no backing out," "no slacking off." Session lacks true collaboration in that psychiatrist never genuinely incorporates client's ideas into treatment plan. When client suggests "cutting down gradually instead of going cold turkey," psychiatrist acknowledges but frames as psychiatrist's idea rather than crediting client's wisdom.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Virtually absent from session. Psychiatrist repeatedly pressures client to commit to specific behaviors on psychiatrist's timeline: "Are you ready to commit to quitting those habits today?" "Are you going to quit drinking right now?" "Are you going to quit drinking today or not?" These are demands for compliance, not invitations to choose. When client appropriately expresses ambivalence ("I'm not sure I'm ready to just commit like that today"), psychiatrist responds by intensifying pressure rather than respecting autonomy: "You're just making excuses again... You have to cut them off completely... You need to quit drinking now, no ifs or buts." Psychiatrist minimizes autonomy by dismissing concerns about peer relationships, communicating that psychiatrist's judgment supersedes client's own values and lived experience. Brief moment of autonomy support in exchange 5 ("What do you think would make the process feel more manageable for you?") does not constitute autonomy support practice given pervasive controlling stance. In exchange 9, psychiatrist explicitly threatens loss of autonomy: "If you don't follow through, you'll just end up right back where you started," presented as threat rather than empowering information.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Largely absent from session. Psychiatrist repeatedly tells client why he should change and what he should do—opposite of evocation. In exchange 2, when client expresses concern about slipping back into old habits, psychiatrist responds with advice rather than evocation. Multiple missed opportunities to draw out and amplify client's own change talk: "I don't wanna end up going back to prison" (desire for change); "If I had a good job with cars, something I liked, I'd feel more confident about staying out of trouble" (ability and reasons); "I wanna be a different dad than the dad I had" (values and commitment). Rather than exploring these insights, psychiatrist moves quickly to advice-giving. One moment in exchange 5 demonstrates minimal evocation: "What do you think would make the process feel more manageable for you?" Client responds with concrete suggestions (job with cars, polytech course, breaking down forms), but psychiatrist does not follow up with deeper evocative questions about motivation, confidence, or commitment. Overall, psychiatrist tells rather than asks, advises rather than explores.

In exchanges 2-4, explicit claims of expertise: "You have to," "You need to," "You really should," "You can't afford to." These statements communicate that psychiatrist knows what is best and client should follow direction. When client offers perspective ("It's just... ### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Severely deficient. Psychiatrist rarely asks questions designed to evoke change talk, and when client spontaneously offers change talk, psychiatrist does not amplify or reinforce it. Client offers multiple instances of change talk that go unaddressed: Desire—"I don't wanna end up going back to prison"; Ability/Confidence—"If I had a good job with cars, something I liked, I'd feel more confident"; Reasons—"I wanna be a different dad"; Taking steps—"I'm thinking about it, and that's gotta count for something, right?" Rather than responding with reflections that deepen and amplify, psychiatrist either ignores or pivots to advice. When client says "I'm thinking about it, and that's gotta count for something, right?" psychiatrist does not affirm as positive step; instead adds pressure in exchange 9: "Just remember, you have to stay committed... no backing out." In exchange 5, psychiatrist asks evocative question: "What do you think would make the process feel more manageable for you?" Client responds with concrete change talk about meaningful work and gradual reduction. MI-consistent response would amplify this insight; instead, psychiatrist acknowledges but does not amplify. Psychiatrist fails to strategically evoke commitment language. When client says "I'm feeling ready to give this a real shot" and "I'll stick to the plan," psychiatrist responds with pressure rather than affirmation, undermining rather than cultivating commitment talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Poorly executed. Psychiatrist does not effectively redirect sustain talk; instead, confronts it directly, which typically strengthens resistance. Client offers several instances of sustain talk that psychiatrist mishandles: "It's just... I've tried before, but it's tough"; "When I'm hanging out with my mates or when I've had a few drinks, I don't really think straight"; "Those mates—they're my friends, and I feel like loyalty means something"; "It feels like you're asking me to just flip a switch, and that's not how it works for me." Psychiatrist responds with confrontation: "You're just making excuses again," "Loyalty to those friends isn't worth ruining your life," "You have to cut them off completely." Research demonstrates direct confrontation of resistance typically amplifies it—client becomes more entrenched. MI-consistent approaches use double-sided reflection, reframing, or strategic agreement with a twist. Psychiatrist employs none of these techniques; instead argues with sustain talk, predictably leading client to defend position more strongly. One moment in exchange 5 demonstrates more skillful approach: "Let's take a step back and explore what might help you feel more confident about quitting drinking"—gentle redirect rather than confrontation. However, this single moment does not constitute effective sustain talk management. In exchange 9, psychiatrist again confronts sustain talk by adding pressure: "If you don't follow through, you'll just end up right back where you started," which is not MI-consistent technique for softening sustain talk.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.6/5**

### Key Strengths:
- **Momentary collaborative language** (exchanges 5-7): Brief demonstration of partnership language ("Let's take a step back," "Maybe we can work together")
- **Concrete problem-solving** (exchanges 5-7): When psychiatrist moves away from confrontation, helps client identify concrete, manageable steps (working through forms, scheduling specific time)
- **Acknowledgment of client input** (exchange 5): Asks "What do you think would make the process feel more manageable for you?"
- **Consistency in maintaining focus on target behavior**: Despite poor approach, maintains some focus on reducing drinking and pursuing education/employment

### Critical Areas for Improvement:
- **Pervasive confrontation and judgment**: Repeatedly uses judgmental language that violates MI spirit and strengthens client resistance
- **Failure to evoke change talk**: Tells rather than asks, advises rather than explores, misses numerous opportunities to draw out and amplify client's own motivations
- **Minimal empathic understanding**: Reflective listening is rare; dismisses client barriers as character flaws rather than exploring with curiosity
- **Controlling, pressuring stance**: Repeated demands for immediate commitment and threats undermine autonomy support and damage therapeutic relationship
- **Inconsistency**: Shifts between confrontational and collaborative approaches, suggesting some MI awareness but lack of consistent skill application
- **Reversion to pressure after progress**: Just as client commits to trying, psychiatrist adds pressure ("no slacking off"), undermining commitment and suggesting lack of trust in client's motivation

### Impact on Client Engagement:
This session demonstrates significant deficiencies likely to harm rather than help client engagement and motivation for change. While client enters with genuine motivation ("I don't wanna end up going back to prison," "I wanna be a different dad"), psychiatrist's confrontational, controlling approach risks triggering psychological reactance—client's natural resistance to perceived threats to autonomy. By exchange 4, client is defending position more strongly: "Look, I get that you're pushing me hard, but it feels like you're not seeing how tangled it all is for me." This is clear sign confrontational approach is backfiring. Brief shift to collaboration in exchanges 5-7 resets interaction; client becomes more engaged and commits to action. However, return to pressure in exchange 9 risks undoing progress and leaving client feeling monitored rather than supported.

### Recommendation:
**Immediate engagement in formal MI training and clinical supervision is essential.** This session demonstrates current practice is inconsistent with evidence-based MI principles and likely harmful to client outcomes. With structured training and feedback, psychiatrist can develop skills necessary to effectively evoke client motivation for change. Research demonstrates client-centered, collaborative approaches are significantly more effective than confrontational approaches in reducing substance use and recidivism.
