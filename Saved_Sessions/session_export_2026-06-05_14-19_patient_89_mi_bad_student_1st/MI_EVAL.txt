### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist fails to identify, evoke, or amplify any emerging change talk. Client provides multiple instances of contemplation and ambivalence that should be amplified: "I guess I've thought about it a little," "Maybe it could get me in trouble," "I've thought about cutting back a little." Skilled MI clinician would respond with reflections deepening these statements. Instead, psychiatrist ignores or contradicts them. When client expresses "I've thought about cutting back a little," psychiatrist responds "You need to quit completely," negating client's more modest change talk. Ambivalence is dismissed rather than explored ("You're just brushing off the risks"). Confrontational approach suppresses change talk and hardens client's position. By session end, client has moved further into precontemplation: "I guess I'm not ready to say yes right now." Fundamental failure in MI's most powerful technique for evoking motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist directly confronts every sustain talk statement, which predictably strengthens it. Client's sustain talk includes: "It's not like it's a big deal, everyone does it," "We're just having fun, it's not hurting anyone," "It's not like it's ruining my life or anything," "It feels pretty far off, like something that's not really gonna happen to me." Rather than using double-sided reflections or strategic reframing, psychiatrist responds with direct contradiction: "That's just wrong," "You're just making excuses," "You're just brushing off the risks," "That's exactly the kind of attitude that's holding you back," "You're just being careless and selfish with your body." This confrontational approach is documented in MI research to strengthen sustain talk and increase defensiveness. Marcus's responses demonstrate this: sustain talk becomes more entrenched throughout session. No skillful softening techniques employed. Sustain talk is amplified rather than softened, representing fundamental failure in MI technique.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.1/5**

**Session Outcome:** This session represents a near-complete failure to implement Motivational Interviewing principles and actively violates MI's core spirit of partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation. The psychiatrist assumes an authoritarian expert role, uses confrontation and coercion, suppresses emerging change talk, amplifies sustain talk through direct contradiction, and explicitly rejects client autonomy and collaboration.

**Critical Finding:** By session end, Marcus has moved further away from contemplating change. He explicitly states the psychiatrist's approach makes him "wanna shut down even more" and that he will "figure this out on my own, not by someone ordering me around." This represents documented harm of confrontational approaches with resistant adolescent clients.

**Likelihood of Behavior Change:** Extremely low. Marcus will likely not follow through on imposed commitment to abstain for one week. More critically, he is now less likely to engage with healthcare providers about drinking in the future, with serious implications for health and safety.

**Recommendation:** This psychiatrist requires comprehensive MI training and supervision before continuing to use MI as an intervention approach. Current approach is not MI and is contraindicated for this population. Consider formal MI training (minimum 20 hours with supervision), recorded session review with MI trainer, and competency assessment using validated measures (MITI) before resuming MI-based interventions.
