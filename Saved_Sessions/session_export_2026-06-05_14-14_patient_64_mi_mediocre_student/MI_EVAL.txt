### Collaboration

Repeated prescriptive language ("It would probably help if you...," "You might want to...," "Maybe you should...") appears 10+ times, positioning clinician as expert with solutions and client as recipient of advice. ### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** This is perhaps the most critical failure. Clinician demonstrates virtually no evocation—the core MI skill of drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources. No exploration of Jordan's values ("What matters most to you?", "What kind of person do you want to be?", "What would your life look like if you weren't shoplifting?"). No exploration of past successes or existing resources. No exploration of consequences or deeper harms. No exploration of ambivalence despite clear evidence of it. No exploration of strengths or coping strategies. Closed questions constrain rather than evoke. Strategic amplification of change talk is absent—when Jordan says "I want to stop, I really do," clinician doesn't follow up with "Tell me more about that." No systematic exploration of desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment. Session is characterized by telling rather than asking.

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change

This session is unlikely to increase Jordan's intrinsic motivation for change. While Jordan leaves with a plan, it is the clinician's plan, not Jordan's. Research on MI demonstrates that when clients feel directed or controlled, they experience reactance and are less likely to sustain change. Jordan's closing statement ("I'll try to keep at it and see if it helps... It's hard, but if I don't try, nothing changes, right?") reflects passive acceptance rather than genuine commitment. His earlier statement ("I mean, I don't know if I'll stick with it") suggests low confidence in the plan.

The session fails to address Jordan's core ambivalence—tension between desire to stop and fear of losing friends. Without exploring and resolving this ambivalence, Jordan is likely to return to shoplifting when peer pressure increases. The session does not build Jordan's self-efficacy or sense of agency. He leaves uncertain whether he can implement the plan rather than feeling he has resources and capability to change.

---

### Primary Recommendations for Improvement

1. **Shift from Expert to Collaborative Partner (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**: Eliminate prescriptive language and replace with genuine collaborative inquiry. Position yourself as partner exploring options together, not expert dispensing solutions.

2. **Evoke Rather Than Tell**: Replace closed questions and advice-giving with open questions designed to evoke Jordan's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources. Use OARS framework strategically.

3. **Cultivate Change Talk Systematically**: When Jordan produces change talk, follow up with strategic questions to amplify and deepen it. Use DARN-CAT framework to explore desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment.

4. **Address Ambivalence with Double-Sided Reflections**: Acknowledge both sides of tension between wanting to stop and fearing loss of friends. This validates experience and paradoxically often reduces sustain talk.

5. **Explore Values, Meaning, and Intrinsic Motivation**: Ask questions helping Jordan connect change to deeper values and what matters most. This builds sustainable intrinsic motivation.

6. **Build Self-Efficacy and Autonomy Support**: Explicitly affirm Jordan's right to choose, his capability to change, and his ownership of change process. Use language emphasizing his agency and control.

7. **Use Complex Reflective Listening**: Move beyond basic reflections to capture emotional nuance and deepen client's insight. Reflect feeling, meaning, and ambivalence.

8. **Affirm Strengths and Build on Resources**: Identify and affirm Jordan's strengths, past successes, and existing resources as foundation for building confidence and self-efficacy.

9. **Maintain Collaborative Stance—Co-Create the Plan**: Don't present plan to client. Ask what plan he thinks would work and build from there. Ensure client's ideas substantially influence final plan.

10. **Develop Comfort with Silence and Uncertainty**: Practice tolerating silence and not rushing to fill it with advice. Allow client space to think, feel, and discover own answers.

---

### Conclusion

This session demonstrates fundamental misunderstanding of Motivational Interviewing. While the psychiatrist is respectful and maintains focus, the session is characterized by expert-driven advice-giving rather than collaborative, evocative partnership. **The most critical improvement needed is a fundamental shift in stance—from expert to partner, from telling to asking, from prescribing to evoking.** This requires not just learning new techniques but developing genuine belief that the client has wisdom and capability to solve his own problems, and that the clinician's role is to help him access that wisdom, not impose solutions from outside.

Without these changes, this clinician will continue to deliver supportive counseling that feels helpful in the moment but is unlikely to evoke sustained intrinsic motivation for change. The client will comply with the plan but is at high risk of returning to problematic behavior when external pressure increases.
