
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Feedback: Therapist Evaluation

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made no attempt to establish a collaborative agenda at any point in the session. A proper CBT agenda would involve: (1) explicitly identifying the session's target problems, (2) prioritizing among multiple concerns, (3) allocating time to specific topics, and (4) obtaining patient agreement on what to focus on. Instead, the therapist opened with a vague, unstructured invitation ("Hey there! How's it going today?") and never returned to structure the session around specific, measurable goals.

The patient presented multiple critical clinical issues: chronic fatigue, headaches, significantly low weight (BMI 14.7), restricted caloric intake (~600 cal/day), amenorrhea, poor concentration, and body-related distress. A competent CBT therapist would have explicitly prioritized these concerns collaboratively. For example: "I hear you're struggling with fatigue, headaches, and concerns about your weight. Given our time today, I'd like to focus on understanding your eating patterns and the thoughts that make it difficult to eat more. Does that sound like a good use of our time?" No such structure emerged.

The session repeated this pattern across two separate encounters, suggesting this is not an isolated oversight but a fundamental gap in the therapist's approach. The lack of agenda is particularly problematic in eating disorder treatment, where clear behavioral targets and measurable goals are essential for progress.

**Summary:**

The therapist completely failed to establish a collaborative agenda, leaving the session without clear direction, prioritized targets, or measurable goals—a foundational CBT requirement.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made minimal, inadequate attempts to elicit or respond to patient feedback. Genuine feedback-seeking in CBT involves: (1) asking whether the patient understands the therapist's reasoning, (2) checking if interventions are helpful, (3) soliciting reactions to the session, and (4) adjusting based on patient input.

The therapist asked only surface-level questions like "Have you been able to get enough rest lately?" and "Have you noticed if any particular time of day feels a bit easier or harder for you?" These are exploratory questions, not feedback-seeking. True feedback questions would sound like: "Does this explanation make sense to you?" "Is this approach feeling helpful?" "What's your reaction to what we've discussed?" "Do you feel like we're addressing what matters most to you?"

Most critically, the therapist never checked whether the patient actually understood or agreed with the suggestions being offered. The therapist repeatedly recommended drinking water, getting fresh air, thinking positive thoughts, and listening to calming music—but never asked: "Does this feel doable for you?" "What barriers might get in the way?" "How confident are you that you can try this?" The patient's statement "It's hard sometimes to see progress when most days feel so hard" suggests she may not have fully bought into the approach, yet the therapist did not explore this resistance.

There is no evidence the therapist adjusted behavior based on patient responses. When the patient mentioned bloating, nausea, and lack of appetite as barriers to eating, the therapist did not revisit these obstacles or problem-solve around them.

**Summary:**

The therapist failed to systematically elicit feedback about understanding, helpfulness, or agreement with recommendations, missing critical opportunities to ensure the patient was engaged and the approach was working.

### 5. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**
