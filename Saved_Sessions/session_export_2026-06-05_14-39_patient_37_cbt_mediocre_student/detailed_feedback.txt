
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made minimal effort to establish a structured, collaborative agenda. In the opening, the psychiatrist used vague language: "I was thinking maybe we could talk a bit about what's been on your mind lately... We can just kind of see where the conversation goes" and later "Maybe we can talk a bit about what's been on your mind or any problems you've been having lately... For a 12-year-old with DMDD and ADHD presenting with severe temper outbursts, property destruction, and school suspensions, the agenda should have explicitly targeted: (1) identifying specific high-risk situations triggering outbursts, (2) establishing baseline frequency/intensity of anger episodes, (3) developing concrete behavioral interventions, and (4) clarifying the role of ADHD medication compliance. **Summary:**
The therapist failed to establish a structured, collaborative agenda with prioritized goals, time allocation, or measurable objectives. Session direction was vague and reactive rather than strategic.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Summary:**

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated partial understanding of Wyatt's presenting concerns but missed critical clinical nuances. The therapist accurately reflected that "anger is kind of taking over" and acknowledged the pattern of rapid escalation and post-outburst remorse. The statement "it sounds like you feel like people might be against you or picking on you" shows basic comprehension of Wyatt's cognitive distortions.

However, significant gaps emerged. The therapist did not demonstrate understanding of the *involuntary nature* of DMDD—Wyatt explicitly stated "It's like this anger just takes over, and then I feel awful after. It's like I want to stop, but I can't." This is a critical clinical feature distinguishing DMDD from oppositional defiant disorder or simple anger management problems. A therapist with genuine understanding would have explored: "So even when you want to stop, your body/brain just takes over? Tell me more about that experience." Instead, the therapist proceeded as if Wyatt had volitional control and could simply "think differently" or "remind himself."

The therapist also missed the comorbid ADHD presentation. Wyatt's difficulty with impulse control ("sometimes I feel like I have to say or do something right then or I'll explode") and his fidgetiness (noted in intake) suggest executive dysfunction and impulse dysregulation beyond cognitive distortions. The therapist did not ask about medication compliance, ADHD symptom severity, or whether ADHD management was optimized—critical for DMDD treatment.

The therapist did not explore the temporal sequence of emotions. Wyatt mentioned "frustration and sadness" alongside anger, but the therapist did not investigate whether these are precursors, concurrent, or consequences. Understanding this sequence is essential for early intervention.

The therapist's empathy was present but surface-level. Statements like "Yeah, I get that it's tough" lack the depth needed to communicate genuine understanding of a 12-year-old experiencing involuntary rage episodes that damage his relationships and self-esteem.

**Summary:**
