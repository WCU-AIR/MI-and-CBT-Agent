### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard. The session is characterized by explicit judgment, disapproval, and conditional regard based on compliance. The opening directive ("I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do") signals that acceptance is contingent on the patient's willingness to comply. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient's emotional experience with phrases like "you can't keep making excuses about your feelings," "you can't be overwhelmed," and "you can't keep saying it's too hard." When the patient expresses fear and ambivalence, the psychiatrist responds with dismissal rather than normalization. Shame-inducing language ("Why didn't you quit when you had the chance?" and "If you don't, you're only hurting yourself") communicates blame rather than support. There is no affirmation of the patient's worth, no acknowledgment of the difficulty of behavior change, and no warmth. The tone is consistently authoritarian and punitive.

Instead, the psychiatrist mischaracterizes the patient's statements as "excuses" rather than legitimate barriers. There are no complex reflections that capture emotional nuance. When the patient explicitly requests empathic understanding ("Maybe there's another way I can start slowly without feeling like I'm failing from the beginning?" and "I want to feel ready and supported, not forced"), the psychiatrist's response is to repeat the same directive with increased pressure. This demonstrates complete failure to recognize or respond to the patient's emotional needs.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains focus on smoking cessation as the target behavior but does so in a way that is entirely clinician-driven rather than collaboratively developed. The psychiatrist never explores what direction the patient might want to move, what the patient's own goals are, or whether smoking cessation is the patient's priority at this moment. The patient mentions "other health issues" and feeling overwhelmed by multiple concerns, but the psychiatrist never explores whether addressing smoking first is appropriate. The direction lacks strategic progression—effective MI direction would involve exploring the patient's own reasons for change, understanding barriers, collaboratively identifying realistic steps, and building commitment gradually. Instead, the psychiatrist skips directly to prescription. The repeated demand to "skip that first cigarette tomorrow" is presented as the only option, with no exploration of alternatives, readiness, or the patient's own ideas. The direction becomes increasingly rigid and pressuring rather than strategically refined.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** This session demonstrates zero collaboration—it is entirely expert-driven and hierarchical. The opening statement establishes this immediately: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to quit." ### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** This session actively undermines autonomy at every turn. The psychiatrist repeatedly removes choice language and replaces it with obligation: "You have to quit," "You need to stop," "You must push through," "You have to be strong." The psychiatrist also uses pressure and threat to coerce compliance: "If you don't, you're only hurting yourself" and "If you don't quit now, your health will get worse, and you'll regret it." These are coercive tactics that attempt to manipulate the patient into compliance through fear and guilt rather than supporting autonomous decision-making. When the patient expresses ambivalence ("I'm not sure I'm ready to commit to quitting right now"), this is a legitimate expression of autonomy. The psychiatrist's response is to pressure harder: "No, no, you can't keep saying it's too hard. You have to quit now." The psychiatrist also dismisses the patient's stated needs and preferences. When the patient says "I want to feel ready and supported, not forced," the psychiatrist continues forcing. There is no acknowledgment that the patient has the right to choose not to quit, to quit slowly, or to address other concerns first.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** This session contains no evocation—it is entirely advice-giving and prescription. The psychiatrist never asks open-ended questions designed to elicit the patient's own reasons for change. Questions like "What concerns you most about your smoking?" or "What would be different in your life if you quit?" would evoke the patient's own motivation. Instead, the psychiatrist tells the patient why they should quit: "You're risking serious health problems," "Your health depends on this." The psychiatrist also never explores the patient's previous quit attempts to understand what the patient learned or what resources they might draw upon. When the patient mentions "I've tried quitting before, and it didn't really work out," the psychiatrist responds with judgment: "You need to be serious this time." The patient does offer spontaneous change talk ("I do want to take this seriously," "I want to be healthier and stop smoking"), yet these moments are ignored and unreinforced. The psychiatrist never explores the patient's values, strengths, or resources. The entire approach is "tell and sell"—the psychiatrist tells the patient what to do and tries to sell them on it through fear and pressure.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths

**There are no identifiable strengths in this session.** The psychiatrist demonstrates no motivational interviewing competence. Every domain is scored 1/5, indicating fundamental deficiencies and inconsistency with MI spirit. The session does not embody any of the core MI principles: partnership, acceptance, compassion, or evocation.

---

### Critical Areas for Improvement

This session represents a **fundamental violation of MI principles** across all domains. The psychiatrist employs an authoritarian, confrontational, prescriptive approach that is antithetical to MI and research-supported practice. Specific deficiencies include:

1. **Absence of Acceptance and Empathy**: The psychiatrist invalidates the patient's emotional experience, judges the patient's barriers as "excuses," and communicates disapproval rather than support. The patient's fear, overwhelm, and ambivalence are pathologized rather than normalized.

2. **Coercive Rather Than Collaborative**: The psychiatrist unilaterally prescribes solutions without exploring the patient's perspective, preferences, or readiness. Power is wielded rather than shared.

3. **Advice-Giving Without Evocation**: The psychiatrist tells the patient why they should quit and what they must do, rather than drawing out the patient's own motivations, values, and resources. The patient's spontaneous change talk is ignored.

4. **Confrontational Response to Resistance**: When the patient expresses ambivalence or barriers, the psychiatrist responds with confrontation and dismissal rather than empathy and exploration. This increases defensiveness and resistance.

5. **Pressure and Coercion**: The psychiatrist uses threats, shame, and obligation language to try to force compliance. This undermines autonomy and typically backfires.

---

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change

**This session is highly likely to decrease rather than increase the patient's motivation for change.** The patient enters with ambivalence—a normal, treatable stage of change (contemplation). However, the psychiatrist's confrontational, coercive approach:

- **Increases defensiveness**: The confrontational stance triggers the patient to defend their position and explain barriers rather than explore motivation.
- **Decreases self-efficacy**: The repeated message that the patient "can't" handle their emotions undermines confidence in their ability to change.
- **Damages the therapeutic relationship**: The invalidation, judgment, and coercion communicate that the psychiatrist does not respect or understand the patient. This erodes trust and alliance.
- **Amplifies ambivalence**: Rather than resolving ambivalence toward change, the psychiatrist's approach creates ambivalence toward the psychiatrist and treatment itself.

By the end of the session, the patient is likely to feel more resistant, more defensive, and less motivated to quit smoking. The patient may also avoid future appointments or seek care elsewhere.

---

### Recommendations for Immediate Action

1. **Adopt the MI Spirit**: Shift from an expert, directive stance to a collaborative, evocative stance. This requires a fundamental change in worldview—from "I am the expert who will fix the patient" to "The patient is the expert on their own life, and my role is to support their own motivation for change."

2. **Replace Confrontation with Empathic Exploration**: When the patient expresses ambivalence, barriers, or sustain talk, respond with curiosity and empathy rather than confrontation. Use reflective listening to demonstrate understanding.

3. **Develop Open-Ended Questioning Skills**: Replace closed and rhetorical questions with genuine open-ended questions designed to evoke the patient's own perspective, motivations, and ideas.

4. **Recognize and Amplify Change Talk**: Listen carefully for moments when the patient expresses motivation for change. When you hear change talk, reflect it back and explore it further.

5. **Support Autonomy and Collaboration**: Communicate that the patient has the right to choose and that your role is to support the patient's own decision-making. Use collaborative language: "What do you think?" "What feels manageable to you?" "Let's figure this out together."

6. **Seek Formal MI Training and Supervision**: This level of deficiency cannot be corrected through self-reflection alone. The psychiatrist should enroll in formal MI training (2-3 days) followed by ongoing supervision with an MI-trained clinician. Record sessions and review them with a supervisor, focusing on stance, technique use, and patient response.

---

### Final Assessment

**The psychiatrist should not continue seeing patients for behavior change counseling until formal MI training and supervision have been completed.** The current approach is likely to harm patients and reduce treatment effectiveness. This session demonstrates a fundamental lack of MI competence and employs techniques that research shows are ineffective and often counterproductive for behavior change.
