# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal unconditional positive regard. While opening is warm, the clinician quickly adopts a directive, expert-driven stance. No explicit affirmation of Aisha's inherent worth, resilience, or self-awareness. Generic acknowledgments ("It's good that you're thinking...") lack genuine affirmation of her as a person. Vulnerability about gym anxiety is not validated or normalized; instead, solutions are immediately offered. Prescriptive language ("maybe you should," "you might want to") positions clinician as expert rather than supportive partner.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflective listening is present but superficial, capturing facts without emotional depth. Reflections are simple restatements (e.g., "you want to start working out again because you feel uncomfortable") rather than emotionally attuned explorations. Multiple missed opportunities for complex reflections: when Aisha says "I'm sick of feeling this way," the clinician does not reflect the exhaustion or desperation; when she expresses gym anxiety, the fear and shame are not explored. Clinician listens for problems to solve rather than people to understand. Prioritizes problem-solving over deepening understanding.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Session maintains adequate focus on target behavior (exercise initiation and consistency). Psychiatrist identifies barriers (motivation dips, home setup, gym anxiety, busy schedule) but lacks strategic exploration of which barrier to prioritize. Multiple solutions offered simultaneously (class, buddy, reminders, goal-setting) without helping Aisha determine which is most impactful or feasible. Premature problem-solving before fully understanding Aisha's perspective. Missed opportunity to explore why importance rating is 8 rather than 10. Direction is present but lacks strategic depth and client-centered prioritization.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration is severely deficient. Psychiatrist consistently adopts expert-driven, hierarchical stance contradicting MI principles of partnership. Prescriptive language used at least 12 times ("Maybe you should try," "Do you think it would probably help if," "You might want to"). Clinician-generated solutions offered without asking Aisha what she thinks might work. When Aisha offers input ("A workout buddy sounds nice"), ideas are acknowledged but not built upon or integrated. Pseudo-collaboration ("What do you think?") asked after psychiatrist has already prescribed steps. No shared decision-making; plan reflects clinician's suggestions, not co-creation with client.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is critically absent throughout session. Prescriptive framing ("Maybe you should just commit," "you should write down your reasons") presents psychiatrist's suggestions as right course of action, not options. Repeated use of "probably" subtly pressures Aisha toward agreement—soft coercion. No explicit affirmation of Aisha's right to choose or control over change process. Compliance-focused language ("Keep at it," "follow through") frames success as following clinician's prescription rather than Aisha's ownership. Missed opportunity to explore ambivalence about relapse; instead, clinician reassures and continues with plan. No acknowledgment that Aisha can decide how to approach change.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Evocation severely underdeveloped. Psychiatrist relies on telling rather than asking, missing opportunities to draw out Aisha's intrinsic motivation, values, and resources. Clinician-generated reasons for change are restatements, not explorations of deeper motivations. Minimal open-ended questions; mostly closed or leading questions. No exploration of values, what matters most, or what kind of person Aisha wants to be. Substantial spontaneous change talk from Aisha ("I really want to feel better," "I'm actually feeling hopeful") is acknowledged briefly but not amplified or deepened. No exploration of past successes or what Aisha learned from previous exercise attempts. Clinician-driven problem-solving replaces asking Aisha to generate solutions.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist operates as expert throughout; client in passive, compliant role. Expert positioning established early ("I'm here to talk," "I usually like to start") and never transitions to collaborative stance. Every barrier met with clinician-generated solution without asking "What do you think?" or "What have you considered?" Client contributions not integrated into session direction. Unequal influence: psychiatrist's ideas shape session; Aisha's do not. No acknowledgment of client expertise ("You know yourself best," "You're the expert on what will work for you"). Hierarchical language ("I'm here to support you," "I'm sure you'll see positive changes") positions clinician as guide and client as recipient. Final summary is clinician-driven, not co-created.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk minimally cultivated despite substantial spontaneous offerings from Aisha ("I really want to start working out," "I'm sick of feeling this way," "I want to feel like myself again," "I'm actually feeling hopeful"). Psychiatrist does not strategically evoke, amplify, or reinforce change talk. No deepening reflections when Aisha expresses motivation. No open-ended questions exploring desire ("What does feeling comfortable mean to you?"), ability ("What shows you can stick with something?"), reasons ("How would your life improve?"), or need ("What would happen if you didn't change?"). Minimal reflection of change talk in summary—restatement rather than deepening. No strategic sequencing through DARN-CAT framework. Commitment talk ("I'm willing to give those steps a shot") not strengthened through follow-up exploration.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk is minimal, which is appropriate given Aisha's high motivation. When sustain talk emerges, handling is inconsistent. Barriers ("It just feels hard," "Something always gets in the way") are redirected rather than explored. Significant sustain talk about gym anxiety ("What if people stare?") not validated or explored; instead, clinician immediately suggests beginner classes as solution. Concern about relapse ("I'm worried I might just give up again") met with reassurance ("Don't be too hard on yourself—that's normal"), which is gentle softening but does not deeply explore concern or help Aisha develop own strategies. Psychiatrist avoids confrontation and does not reinforce sustain talk, but does not use sophisticated softening techniques like double-sided reflections or reframing. Opportunities to explore roots of sustain talk missed.

### **Summary of Key Findings**

**Strengths:**
- Clear, warm opening with open-ended question about client's goals
- Session maintains focus on target behavior (exercise) throughout
- Non-confrontational, supportive tone; no arguing or criticism
- Clear summary of plan at end; concrete steps are understandable
- Client's intrinsic motivation preserved despite directive approach; Aisha ends feeling "hopeful"

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Expert-driven stance dominates** — Most pervasive issue affecting nearly every criterion; psychiatrist operates as problem-solver and solution-provider rather than collaborative partner
- **Minimal evocation** — Clinician tells rather than asks; spontaneous change talk not strategically cultivated or deepened
- **Prescriptive language throughout** — Repeated "should," "probably," "might want to" pressures client toward clinician-determined solutions
- **Superficial empathy** — Reflections are factual restatements, not emotionally attuned explorations; clinician listens for problems to solve, not people to understand
- **No genuine collaboration** — Solutions presented as psychiatrist's ideas; client input acknowledged but not integrated
- **Missed opportunities for deepening** — Follow-up questions that would deepen understanding of motivations, barriers, values, and past experiences are absent

---

### **Impact on Client Engagement and Change**

Despite deficiencies, Aisha maintains motivation and ends feeling hopeful because: (1) her intrinsic motivation is strong, (2) psychiatrist's suggestions are reasonable and aligned with her needs, and (3) clinician is warm and non-confrontational. However, this session **does not embody MI spirit** and likely **underestimates Aisha's capacity for self-directed change**. More collaborative, evocative approach would strengthen intrinsic motivation, increase sense of ownership, and improve long-term adherence.

**Likelihood of sustained motivation:** **Moderate to Low.** Aisha may experience plan as something psychiatrist wants her to do rather than something *she* wants to do. If barriers arise or motivation dips, she may lack internal resources or sense of ownership to persist. Risk of relapse increased due to lack of deep exploration of values, strengths, and past successes.

---

### **Priority Recommendations for Improvement**

1. **Shift from expert-driven to collaborative stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)** — Replace prescriptive language with genuine curiosity; position self as partner exploring options *with* client, not expert dispensing solutions

2. **Develop complex, emotionally attuned reflections** — Move beyond factual restatements to reflections capturing emotional nuance, meaning, and deeper understanding

3. **Strategically evoke change talk** — Ask open-ended questions drawing out client's own motivations, values, reasons, and confidence using DARN-CAT framework

4. **Explore and validate barriers before solving** — First understand and validate concerns; allow client to generate solutions before offering clinician's suggestions

5. **Amplify and reinforce spontaneous change talk** — Pause and deepen client's change talk through reflective listening and follow-up questions rather than moving quickly to problem-solving

6. **Support autonomy explicitly and throughout** — Consistently affirm client's right to choose and ownership of change process; use autonomy-supporting language

7. **Use collaborative language and shared decision-making** — Replace "I think you should" with "What do you think?"; invite client to generate solutions first

8. **Deepen exploration of sustain talk and barriers** — Explore concerns deeply; help client develop own strategies rather than reassuring and moving on

9. **Establish true partnership by acknowledging client expertise** — Explicitly communicate that client is expert on self and life; position self as facilitator of client's wisdom

10. **Reduce prescriptive language; increase curiosity** — Audit language for "should," "probably," "might want to"; replace with genuine questions inviting exploration

---

### **Training Recommendations**

- Intensive MI training focusing on spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)
- Supervised practice with feedback on collaborative stance, evocation, and reflective listening
- Role-play practice: asking rather than telling; exploring rather than solving
- Audio/video review of sessions to identify prescriptive language and missed evocation opportunities
- Mentorship from experienced MI practitioner modeling collaborative, client-centered practice
- Target ratio: 70% client talking, 30% clinician talking
