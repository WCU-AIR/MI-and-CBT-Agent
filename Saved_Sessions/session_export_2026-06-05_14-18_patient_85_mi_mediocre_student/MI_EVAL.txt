### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates critical failure to support client autonomy. Clinician repeatedly undermines Malik's right to choose and sense of agency through prescriptive language: "it would probably help if," "you might want to," "you should also think about," "Maybe you should write it down." Autonomy-supporting language would be: "What do you think might help?" "What feels doable to you?" "What are you willing to try?" Instead, clinician tells Malik what to do. When Malik expresses clear ambivalence ("I'm not promising anything. It's just a thought"), clinician does not respect this boundary; instead continues to push small steps and confidence scaling, implicitly communicating "you should be more committed to change." Malik's stated priorities (keep things chill with friends, avoid fights with mom) are not honored; clinician assumes substance use reduction is goal and works toward that end, overriding Malik's own stated priorities. Clinician asks "How confident are you?" and "How important is change?" without first exploring Malik's ambivalence or reasons for low confidence—compliance-checking questions, not autonomy-supporting exploration. Session structure assumes change is goal and Malik should move toward it; no genuine exploration of "Do you want to change?" or "What would change mean to you?" Clinician's agenda presented as natural direction, not as one option among many. Malik's final comment ("Thanks for not just telling me what to do") is actually ironic given clinician has been prescriptive throughout.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates fundamental failure of evocation—core MI principle of drawing out client's own motivations, values, and reasons for change. Clinician adopts "install motivation" approach, telling Malik why he should change rather than exploring why Malik might want to change. Clinician never asks open-ended questions designed to evoke Malik's own reasons for change: "What do you think might be good about changing this?" "What matters most to you about your relationship with your mom?" "What would be different if things were better at home?" Instead, clinician asks closed or leading questions assuming clinician's agenda. When clinician wants Malik to consider benefits of change, says "Do you think there might be some benefits?" (leading question assuming benefits exist) rather than "What do you see as pros and cons of how things are right now?" Minimal exploration of ambivalence despite Malik's emerging change talk ("I guess I don't want things to get worse with my mom either")—clinician does not pause to deepen this significant statement; instead immediately pivots to action planning. Scaling questions ("How important is it for you to make some changes on a scale from 1 to 10?") measure readiness but do not evoke motivation; true evocation would explore reasons behind low scores. Clinician never explores what matters to Malik—his values, hopes, vision for his life. Strong evocation would involve: "What's important to you in your friendships?" "What do you want your relationship with your mom to look like?" "What are you good at?" Instead, session remains focused on problem and clinician's solutions. Multiple missed opportunities to evoke change talk: when Malik says "It's not like it messes up my life or anything," clinician could explore this; when Malik says "I guess sometimes it does make my mom mad," this is change talk not explored. Entire session structure is: (1) identify problem, (2) tell client why change is good, (3) tell client what to do—opposite of evocation.

### Partnership

**Comments:** Session demonstrates complete absence of genuine partnership. Clinician assumes expert role and maintains it throughout with no meaningful power-sharing or collaborative stance. Clinician positions himself as authority on what Malik should do: diagnoses problem (substance use in social situations), prescribes solutions (talk to mom when calm, write things down, stay calm during conversations), monitors compliance (asks confidence scales). This is classic expert model, not partnership. Clinician's agenda—reduce substance use, improve family communication—presented as session's direction without genuine negotiation with Malik. When Malik states priority is "to keep things chill with my friends," clinician does not honor this as equally valid; instead works toward clinician's agenda (substance use reduction) while framing it as helping Malik avoid fights with mom. Malik's expertise about his own life—what his friends think, what his mom responds to, what he's tried before, what feels possible—not genuinely solicited or incorporated. Instead, clinician offers generic advice (talk when calm, write things down, stay calm) without asking "What have you tried before?" or "What do you think your mom would respond to?" Language reflects power imbalance: "I'm here to help" (expert helping client), "it would probably help if" (expert knows what helps), "you might want to" (expert suggesting to client), "you should also think about" (expert directing client's thinking), "Maybe you should write it down" (expert prescribing). True partnership language would be: "What do you think might help?" "What have you tried?" "What feels doable to you?" "Let's figure this out together." No shared decision-making present; when clinician suggests talking to mom when calm, writing things down, staying calm during conversations, these presented as plan—not as options to discuss. Malik positioned as passive recipient of clinician's expertise throughout session. Malik's final comment ("Thanks for not just telling me what to do") highlights irony: clinician has been telling him what to do throughout entire session. Malik's low confidence (4-5/10) and repeated statements of reluctance ("I'm not promising anything," "I'm not all about changing everything overnight") suggest he does not feel like genuine partner in this process.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Clinician demonstrates minimal skill in evoking, recognizing, or reinforcing change talk. While some change talk emerges naturally from Malik, clinician fails to strategically cultivate or amplify it. Multiple instances of change talk not recognized or developed: (1) "I guess sometimes it does make my mom mad"—change talk (acknowledgment of impact) not explored; (2) "I guess I don't want things to get worse with my mom either"—significant change talk (desire for change in relationship) not paused to deepen; clinician immediately pivots to action planning; (3) "I can try writing some stuff down or thinking about what to say"—commitment talk (willingness to try) not reinforced; clinician immediately adds more suggestions; (4) "I'm not like I want to make things worse at home"—change talk (desire to improve home situation) not explored. When Malik produces change talk, clinician's typical response is to move forward with advice-giving rather than pause and reinforce statement. Scaling questions ("How important is it for you to make some changes on a scale from 1 to 10?" and "How confident do you feel about trying these steps on a scale from 1 to 10?") measure readiness but do not evoke motivation; true cultivation would explore reasons: "What would need to happen for this to feel more important?" or "What makes you think you could do this, even at a 4 or 5?" Clinician never asks questions specifically designed to evoke change talk: "What concerns you most about how things are right now?" "What would be different if you made a change?" "What's the best thing that could happen if you talked with your mom?" "What are you good at that could help you with this?" Minimal reinforcement of commitment talk; when Malik says "I can try writing some stuff down," clinician does not reinforce with genuine affirmation; instead adds more suggestions, which may dilute Malik's commitment by overwhelming him. Sustain talk produced by Malik ("Not all the time, just once in a while," "They don't think it's a problem, so I don't either," "My mom's the one making me come here") not strategically addressed; clinician moves past it to problem-solving. Overall pattern: clinician moves quickly from problem identification to action planning without pausing to cultivate change talk that naturally emerges.

## Overall Summary

### Strengths
- **Basic Structure:** Clinician maintains general focus on substance use and family conflict throughout session, providing some organizational coherence.
- **Attempt at Summarization:** Clinician provides summary at end of session, demonstrating effort to consolidate discussion.
- **Minimal Confrontation:** Clinician does not directly confront or argue with Malik, avoiding overt power struggles.
- **Acknowledgment of Difficulty:** Clinician occasionally acknowledges that change is difficult ("I get that it's tough to change, especially when your friends are involved").
- **Scaling Questions:** Clinician uses scaling questions to assess importance and confidence, which can be useful for measuring readiness (though not skillfully applied here).

### Critical Areas for Improvement

**Fundamental Failure to Embody MI Spirit:**

This session fundamentally fails to embody spirit of Motivational Interviewing. Clinician adopts expert-driven, directive stance antithetical to MI principles. Clinician tells Malik what to do rather than evoking Malik's own motivations. Clinician's agenda (reduce substance use, improve family communication) is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. Malik is positioned as passive recipient of expertise rather than equal partner in understanding and addressing his situation.

**Specific Gaps in MI Competence:**

1. **Collaboration (1/5):** Session dominated by prescriptive language ("you should," "you might want to," "it would probably help if"). Clinician unilaterally identifies problems and solutions without genuine partnership or shared decision-making.

2. **Autonomy Support (1/5):** Clinician systematically undermines Malik's right to choose through directive language, minimization of stated resistance, and assumption that change is inevitable. Malik's stated priorities (keeping things chill with friends) overridden by clinician's agenda.

3. **Partnership (1/5):** Clinician assumes expert role and maintains it throughout with no meaningful power-sharing. Malik's expertise about his own life not solicited or incorporated. Clinician talks; Malik responds.

4. **Evocation (1/5):** Clinician tells Malik why change is good rather than exploring why Malik might want to change. Minimal exploration of Malik's own motivations, values, or reasons for change. Approach is "install motivation" rather than "evoke motivation."

5. **Cultivating Change Talk (2/5):** While Malik produces change talk ("I guess I don't want things to get worse with my mom"), clinician does not recognize or develop it. Instead, clinician moves quickly to action planning, missing opportunities to strengthen Malik's intrinsic motivation.

6. **Softening Sustain Talk (2/5):** Malik produces significant sustain talk (peer loyalty, minimization, external locus of control), but clinician does not strategically address it. Clinician ignores or moves past sustain talk rather than exploring or reframing it.

7. **Empathy (2/5):** Reflective listening present but superficial. Clinician misses opportunities to capture Malik's ambivalence, fear of peer rejection, and internal conflict. Empathy remains cognitive rather than affective.

8. **Acceptance (2/5):** Clinician fails to establish unconditional positive regard or affirm Malik's autonomy. Session is problem-focused and expert-driven with minimal warmth or genuine curiosity about Malik's world.

In fact, clinician's directive, expert-driven approach may actually reinforce Malik's resistance and external locus of control. Malik repeatedly states low importance (2-3/10) and low confidence (4-5/10) for change, and clinician does not meaningfully address these barriers.

He explicitly states that his priority is "to keep things chill with my friends" and that his friends "don't think it's a problem." In this context, directive, expert-driven approach is particularly ineffective because:

1. **Reinforces external locus of control:** Malik came because his mother made him come. Clinician's prescriptive approach reinforces message that change is something imposed on him rather than something he chooses.

3. **Creates power struggle:** Malik is already in conflict with his mother. Clinician's expert-driven stance mirrors his mother's approach (telling him what to do). This is likely to increase resistance rather than decrease it.

4. **Misses opportunity to build alliance:** Malik is guarded and defensive. He needs to feel understood and respected before he will consider change. Clinician's quick pivot to problem-solving communicates "I don't really understand you; I just want to fix you."

**Malik's Likely Response:**
