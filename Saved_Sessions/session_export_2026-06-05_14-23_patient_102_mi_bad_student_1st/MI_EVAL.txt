# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard throughout the session. Multiple instances of explicit judgment and disapproval are evident, including direct character attacks ("Stop being so weak and just do what's necessary"). The clinician repeatedly labels the client's behavior as "making excuses" (stated three times), communicates contempt rather than respect for autonomy, and frames the client's ambivalence as moral failure. There is no warmth, no genuine support, and no communication that the client is valued as a person regardless of her current behavior. The psychiatrist dismisses the client's stated readiness level entirely, violating the MI principle of meeting clients at their stage of change.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no empathic understanding or reflective listening. When the client expresses fear of failure ("I'm worried I might fail if I set my goals too high too fast"), the response is dismissal rather than exploration. When the client explicitly states that pressure makes her want to give up, the psychiatrist continues with identical pressure and confrontation, demonstrating no effort to understand her internal experience. There are zero complex reflections in the session. The clinician never reflects the client's stated strengths (non-drinking friend group, insight into alcohol-contraceptive connection, genuine family history concern). Instead, the focus is exclusively on what the client is "not doing," framed as personal failure.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** While the psychiatrist maintains clear focus on the target behavior (alcohol reduction and contraceptive reliability), the manner in which direction is maintained is fundamentally incompatible with MI. Direction is imposed rather than collaborative. The clinician repeatedly insists on a single predetermined outcome (complete abstinence today) without flexibility, exploration, or negotiation. The psychiatrist's insistence on immediate, complete abstinence as the only acceptable goal contradicts harm reduction principles. The client has identified a more realistic intermediate goal (cutting back, spending time with non-drinking friends), but this is rejected. Repetition of the same directive without modification or response to client feedback suggests the psychiatrist is not listening to or adjusting based on stated needs and readiness.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The session demonstrates the complete opposite of collaboration. The opening statement explicitly frames the interaction as one-directional instruction: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do." Whenever the client offers her perspective, suggests an alternative approach, or expresses her readiness level, the psychiatrist responds with dismissal: "No, that's not good enough," "You're just making excuses," "No, you're not getting it." There is no joint problem-solving, no seeking of client input, and no co-construction of solutions. The psychiatrist never asks what the client thinks might work or what barriers she anticipates. The power dynamic is entirely unequal, with the psychiatrist positioned as sole authority and the client as someone who must obey or be judged as weak and irresponsible.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist actively undermines autonomy at every turn, using pressure, coercion, and implicit threats to attempt to control the client's behavior. Change is repeatedly framed as non-negotiable and mandatory: "You have to cut out drinking completely right now," "You have to take control and quit drinking immediately," "This isn't about feeling ready or not. You have to take control now." These statements explicitly deny the client's right to choose her own pace and approach. When the client expresses her current readiness level (confidence 4/10 for complete abstinence, but willingness to cut back), the psychiatrist responds by invalidating her autonomy: "No, you're not getting it. This isn't about feelings or readiness." Fear and threat are used as motivators ("If you don't stop, you're risking serious consequences"), and ultimatums are issued ("Are you going to commit or not?"), framing the client's autonomy as a problem to be overcome rather than a resource to be respected.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist does virtually none of the core MI work of evoking the client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources. The client has articulated powerful change talk ("I really don't want to go down that path," "If I drink less, I'll be more reliable with birth control," "I have a group of friends that doesn't drink"), but rather than evoking more of this language, the psychiatrist dismisses it as insufficient. There are no open-ended questions designed to help the client explore her own perspective. The clinician never asks: "What concerns you most about your drinking?" "What would be different if you drank less?" "Tell me more about what happens when you're with your non-drinking friends?" or "What would help you feel more confident?" Instead, closed questions and imposing statements dominate. The psychiatrist also fails to evoke the client's own resources and strengths, ignoring her identified protective factors and realistic self-assessment.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist actively assumes the expert role and dismisses the client's contributions entirely. The opening statement explicitly positions the clinician as sole expert: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do." Throughout the session, the client's self-knowledge and lived experience are repeatedly invalidated. When the client says she's not ready for complete abstinence, the psychiatrist responds: "No, you're not getting it. This isn't about feelings or readiness." When the client identifies her protective factor (non-drinking friends), the psychiatrist negates it: "Hanging out with friends who don't drink is fine, but you have to avoid any situations where alcohol is involved." When the client expresses concern about failure if goals are set too high, the response is: "You're just making excuses." There is no genuine curiosity about the client's perspective, no invitation for the client to contribute to the treatment plan, and no acknowledgment that the client might have valuable insights about what would work for her. The power dynamic is entirely one-directional.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist actively suppresses and dismisses the client's change talk while reinforcing sustain talk through pressure and confrontation. Multiple instances of change talk are offered but never amplified: "I really don't want to go down that path" (desire talk—never explored), "If I drink less, I'll be more reliable with birth control" (reasons and ability talk—dismissed as "not good enough"), "I have a group of friends that doesn't drink" (ability talk and protective factor—negated), and "I'm willing to try cutting back more seriously" (commitment talk—labeled as "making excuses"). Rather than reinforcing these statements of change motivation, the psychiatrist explicitly contradicts them and frames them as inadequate. There is no strategic questioning designed to evoke more change talk, no reflection that amplifies change language, and no reinforcement of the client's stated motivations. This approach has the effect of suppressing the client's own change talk and potentially strengthening her sustain talk.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses confrontation and pressure—the opposite of softening sustain talk. The client's legitimate barriers and concerns about her own capacity for change ("It's just hard," "Stopping completely feels really tough," "I'm worried I might fail," "That feels like a massive leap") are met with confrontation: "You're just making excuses," "Why haven't you just quit already?" "You're not getting it." This confrontational approach is well-documented to strengthen sustain talk and resistance. As the psychiatrist increases pressure, the client becomes more entrenched in her position: "I'm not ready to promise quitting cold turkey right now," "I just feel like I need a plan that fits where I'm at." A skillful approach would use double-sided reflections to validate the client's ambivalence without reinforcing either side. Instead, the psychiatrist uses confrontation, which actively strengthens the client's resistance rather than softening it.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.0/5**

**Critical Assessment:**

This session represents a fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles and demonstrates a confrontational, authoritarian approach that is likely to be counterproductive. The psychiatrist violates core MI principles across all nine domains.

**Key Deficiencies:**
- Explicit judgment and shaming language ("You're weak," "You're making excuses")
- Authoritarian, expert-driven stance with no collaborative elements
- Dismissal of client autonomy and readiness for change
- Suppression of the client's own change talk and identified protective factors
- Repeated confrontation and pressure despite clear evidence of ineffectiveness
- Absence of genuine empathy or reflective listening
- No evocation of the client's own motivations or resources

**Impact on Client Engagement:**

The client's responses demonstrate increasing defensiveness and entrenchment as pressure escalates. This is consistent with behavior change psychology research showing that confrontation and pressure increase resistance rather than evoke motivation. The client's final statement—"I'm not ready to promise quitting cold turkey right now, but I want to work on cutting back. Could we start there?"—represents a reasonable, harm-reducing position that the psychiatrist has rejected.

**Critical Recommendation:**

This is not Motivational Interviewing. This is confrontational, directive counseling inconsistent with MI spirit and likely to be harmful. The psychiatrist requires fundamental retraining in MI principles and a complete shift in therapeutic stance from expert-driven to collaborative. Formal MI training is essential before this clinician continues working with clients around behavior change.
