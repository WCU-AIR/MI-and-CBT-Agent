**Authoritarian Stance:** Opening statement—"I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to improve"—establishes judgmental, hierarchical dynamic that contradicts MI principles. ### Clinical Impact Assessment

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation for Change: VERY LOW**

This session is likely to be **counterproductive**. The psychiatrist's confrontational, authoritarian approach will trigger reactance—the patient's natural resistance to perceived threats to autonomy. Rather than increasing motivation to change, this approach decreases it. Patient's escalating pushback throughout session ("I'd prefer a bit more of a conversation," "I'm not ready to commit right this second," "Can we approach this as a conversation instead?") demonstrates reactance in real time.

Research on MI consistently shows that confrontation and pressure **decrease** intrinsic motivation and **increase** resistance. Patients are less likely to follow through on changes they feel pressured into, and more likely to follow through on changes they have chosen themselves. This psychiatrist's approach maximizes likelihood of non-adherence and treatment failure.

**Therapeutic Alliance Damage:** Patient is likely to disengage from this psychiatrist and may avoid future appointments or seek care elsewhere. Repeated rejection of patient's requests for collaborative dialogue communicates that psychiatrist does not respect patient's preferences or autonomy. This damages therapeutic alliance and reduces likelihood of any positive health outcomes.

### Recommendations for Immediate Action

1. **Adopt Collaborative Partnership as Foundation:** Replace "I will tell you what to do" with "Let's work together to understand what's important to you."

2. **Develop Genuine Reflective Listening Skills:** Provide reflective listening response for every patient statement before asking another question. Complete at least 10 hours of MI-specific training focused on reflective listening.

3. **Recognize and Amplify Change Talk:** When patient expresses willingness, ability, reasons, or commitment to change, explicitly recognize and reinforce it. Identify at least three instances of change talk per session.

4. **Eliminate Controlling Language:** Replace directive statements ("You have to," "You must," "You need to") with collaborative language ("What do you think about...?" "How might you...?" "What would work for you?").

5. **Respond to Patient Requests for Collaboration:** When patient explicitly requests different approach, honor that request. Adapt style to match patient preference.

6. **Use Open-Ended Questions:** Replace closed yes/no questions with open-ended questions that invite patient to share thinking, concerns, and ideas. Aim for 70% open-ended, 30% or fewer closed questions.

7. **Explore Barriers Collaboratively:** When patient expresses concern about sustainability or feasibility, explore collaboratively rather than dismissing as "excuses."

8. **Validate Difficulty of Change:** Acknowledge that behavior change is genuinely difficult. Normalize struggle rather than pressuring or criticizing.

9. **Address Patient's Actual Concerns:** Listen carefully to what patient brings up and address it. In this case, medication concerns (citalopram) should have been addressed before moving to health habits.

10. **Complete Formal MI Training and Ongoing Supervision:** Enroll in comprehensive Motivational Interviewing training program (minimum 2-3 days) followed by ongoing clinical supervision with MI-certified trainer. This is not optional for competent MI practice.
