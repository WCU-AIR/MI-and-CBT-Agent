
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish a Structured Assessment Protocol for Acute Mania**
- **Action:** Before offering any interventions, conduct a systematic assessment of manic symptoms using a structured format. Document: (a) sleep pattern and fatigue level, (b) mood and irritability, (c) grandiose or racing thoughts, (d) pressured speech and distractibility, (e) risky behaviors (financial, sexual, substance use), (f) insight and judgment, (g) safety concerns (self-harm, harm to others, neglect of dependents).
- **Example:** "Nancy, I want to understand what's been happening. You mentioned you haven't slept much in the past 10 days. Tell me about that. How many hours of sleep are you getting? Do you feel tired? What are your thoughts like when you're awake? Are they racing? Are you working on anything specific?"
- **Practice:** Use a manic symptom checklist (e.g., DSM-5 criteria for manic episode) to ensure all domains are assessed. Practice asking open-ended questions that invite detailed responses rather than yes/no questions.

### 2. **Eliminate Excessive Self-Disclosure and Maintain Professional Boundaries**
- **Action:** Stop using personal anecdotes to build rapport. While some self-disclosure can be therapeutic, the frequency and content in this session (5+ personal stories) blur boundaries and normalize pathology. Instead, build rapport through empathy, validation, and genuine interest in the patient's experience.
- **Example:** Instead of "I stayed up all night working on a project I was sure would change everything," say: "I'm noticing that you haven't slept much, but you're feeling energized rather than tired. That's actually something I want to understand better, because it's unusual. Can you tell me more about that?"
- **Practice:** Record yourself in role-plays and count instances of self-disclosure. Aim for zero personal anecdotes in acute psychiatric assessments. Instead, practice reflective listening: "So you're feeling energized and focused, even though you haven't slept. Help me understand what that's like."

### 3. **Use Guided Discovery and Socratic Questioning to Explore Grandiose Beliefs**
- **Action:** Replace direct advice with Socratic questions that help the patient examine their beliefs. For grandiose beliefs about the stock blog, ask questions that invite reality-testing without confrontation.
- **Example:** "You mentioned you have a unique ability to predict the stock market. Tell me about that. How did you discover this ability? What evidence do you have? Have you tested it? What would happen if the blog didn't take off as expected? How would you feel then?"
- **Practice:** Write out 5-10 Socratic questions for each of Nancy's core beliefs (e.g., "I can predict the stock market," "I don't need sleep," "Everyone's trying to hold me back"). Practice asking these questions in a curious, non-judgmental tone. Record yourself and listen for any hint of sarcasm or confrontation—eliminate it.

- **Action:** At the beginning of the session, explicitly establish what will be discussed and what the goals are. Involve the patient in this process.
- **Example:** "Nancy, I want to make sure we use our time well today. I'm concerned about a few things: your sleep, your safety, and making sure your family is okay. I also want to understand your goals—what would be most helpful for you right now? Let's figure out together what we should focus on."
- **Practice:** In role-plays, practice opening with: "I'd like to set an agenda for our time together. Here are some things I'm concerned about [list 3-4 items]. What's most important to you? What would you like to focus on?" Practice negotiating priorities if the patient's agenda differs from yours.

### 5. **Assess and Address Safety Concerns Directly**
- **Action:** Conduct a thorough safety assessment covering: (a) childcare and supervision of dependents, (b) financial risk-taking and potential harm, (c) substance use, (d) driving safety, (e) suicidality/homicidality. Document findings and develop a safety plan.
