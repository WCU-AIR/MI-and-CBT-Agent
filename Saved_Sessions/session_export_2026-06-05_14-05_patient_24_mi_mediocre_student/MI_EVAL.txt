### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal unconditional positive regard and fails to establish genuine acceptance of client's ambivalence. While opening is warm, the session quickly shifts to a directive, problem-focused stance that communicates subtle judgment rather than acceptance. Prescriptive language ("should," "would probably help") throughout undermines collaborative stance. Client's current coping mechanism (wine for relaxation) is redirected without first validating its legitimate function. Strengths (self-awareness, willingness to engage) are not explicitly affirmed, nor is the difficulty of change normalized.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Empathy is superficial and inconsistently applied. Psychiatrist demonstrates basic factual understanding (accurately restating drinking pattern) but fails to achieve complex, emotionally attuned reflective listening. Multiple missed opportunities to reflect emotional nuance: when client states "It's kind of my way to unwind, so it's tricky," psychiatrist pivots to prescriptive advice rather than exploring the tension. When client expresses ambivalence ("I'm kind of torn"), no double-sided reflection is offered. Scaling ratings (importance 6-7, confidence 5) are accepted without exploratory follow-up to understand underlying concerns. Depression and its link to drinking are never explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains adequate but inconsistent focus on target behavior (alcohol reduction). Session stays centered on drinking throughout, preventing aimless wandering. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. Psychiatrist asks about consequences early but does not deeply explore client's personal reasons for change. Focus on concrete strategies (keeping less wine, replacing with tea, tracking) comes prematurely, before full exploration of "why change matters" from client's perspective. Discrepancy between client's goal (two drinks per night) and clinical guideline (seven drinks per week) is mentioned once but never explored, representing missed opportunity for strategic direction.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Collaboration is significantly compromised. Psychiatrist adopts expert-driven stance, making decisions and recommendations rather than jointly exploring options. Pervasive prescriptive language dominates: "Maybe you should try to find other ways to unwind," "It would probably help if you planned these changes," "You might want to keep track." These are directives, not collaborative explorations. Client's contributions are primarily reactive to psychiatrist's suggestions rather than generative. No genuine power-sharing or co-creation of change plan. When client offers ideas ("Maybe having less wine in the house"), psychiatrist adds more expert recommendations rather than building on client's wisdom.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Autonomy support is minimal and inconsistently applied. While no explicit pressure or threats are present, session is permeated with subtle directive language that undermines client's sense of choice and self-determination. Repeated use of "should" and "would probably help" communicates expectation and prescription rather than genuine support for autonomy. Psychiatrist does not explicitly affirm client's freedom to change at own pace or right to choose different path. When client expresses hesitation ("I'm still a bit unsure"), psychiatrist reassures rather than validating right to move slowly. Client's stated boundary about not being "ready" for seven drinks per week is not honored or explored. No explicit permission for struggle or failure.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out client's own motivations and reasons for change—is largely absent. Psychiatrist relies on telling and prescribing rather than strategic questioning to elicit intrinsic motivation. While some evocative questions are asked early (about consequences), client's responses indicating minimal work/home consequences are not followed up. Critical missed opportunities: when client spontaneously offers change talk ("I do think I'd probably feel better if I drank less," "Sometimes I feel worse after drinking"), psychiatrist does not amplify or explore these statements. Scaling questions are asked but not followed up with exploratory questions ("What would it take to move from 6 to 8?" or "What's keeping it from being 10?"). Client's mention of depression and drinking to manage low mood is never explored as opportunity to evoke intrinsic motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Partnership—understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client—is fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role and maintains this stance throughout, with minimal invitation for client's expertise about own life, values, and change process. Language consistently positions psychiatrist as expert with solutions and client as recipient of advice. Psychiatrist does not ask about client's past successes, strengths, support systems, or own ideas about what might help. When client offers ideas, these are not built upon as evidence of client's wisdom. Power dynamic is unequal throughout: psychiatrist asks questions, client answers; psychiatrist makes suggestions, client agrees. No moment where client's perspective substantially shapes session direction in way that would not have occurred otherwise.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk is present but not strategically cultivated or reinforced. Multiple instances of client's spontaneous change talk are NOT amplified: "Sometimes I just don't like how much I drink" (desire language); "I do think I'd probably feel better if I drank less" (desire/benefit language); "Sometimes I feel worse after drinking" (consequence language linking to depression); "I think I could cut back a bit" (ability language); client's own strategies ("Maybe having less wine in the house," "I could try just the bath without the wine") are not explored or reinforced; "I'm willing to give it a try this week" (commitment language) is not strengthened. Psychiatrist provides prescriptive advice that shifts focus away from client's own motivations. Scaling questions about importance (6-7) and confidence (5) are asked but not followed up with evocative questions that would elicit elaboration on ratings. Critical missed opportunity to strengthen intrinsic motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk is minimal and handled adequately but not skillfully. Client expresses ambivalence and hesitation ("not ready for seven drinks per week," "It's kind of my way to unwind, so it's tricky," "I'm kind of torn," "I'm still a bit unsure," "I'm not promising I won't mess up"), but psychiatrist does not use sophisticated MI techniques to reframe or redirect. Psychiatrist does not argue with or reinforce sustain talk, which is appropriate. However, psychiatrist does not validate legitimate function wine serves before offering alternatives, does not use double-sided reflections to acknowledge both sides of conflict, and does not explore what doubt means. When client expresses doubt about maintaining changes, psychiatrist reassures rather than exploring. Psychiatrist does not use strategic questioning to help client resolve own ambivalence. Adequate handling of sustain talk prevents harm but misses opportunities for skillful softening.

## **OVERALL ASSESSMENT**

### **Overall Session Score: 2.3/5**

**Summary:** This session demonstrates **below-average MI practice** with significant deficiencies in core MI competencies. While the psychiatrist is warm, respectful, and non-confrontational, the session is fundamentally **expert-driven rather than client-centered**. The psychiatrist assumes the role of expert with solutions, uses prescriptive language throughout, and fails to strategically evoke the client's intrinsic motivation for change.

**Most Critical Findings:**
- Client spontaneously offers multiple instances of change talk (desire, ability, reasons, benefits) that are not amplified or explored
- Ambivalence is not deeply explored or validated through double-sided reflections
- Depression and its link to drinking are never addressed
- Function of drinking is not explored before offering alternatives
- Expert-driven stance undermines client autonomy and partnership
- Prescriptive language dominates collaborative language

**Likelihood of Sustained Change:** Moderate to low. While client may reduce drinking short-term due to concrete strategies and external accountability, intrinsic motivation is not significantly strengthened. Without resolution of ambivalence and deeper exploration of own reasons for change, relapse is likely when external supports or motivation wanes.

**Most Critical Improvements Needed:**
1. Shift from expert-driven to collaborative stance
2. Strategically evoke and amplify change talk
3. Use complex reflections to validate ambivalence
4. Explore function of drinking before offering alternatives
5. Address depression-drinking link
