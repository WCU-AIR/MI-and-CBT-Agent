# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates fundamentally judgmental and disapproving stance throughout majority of session. Immediate prescriptive directives ("you really should stay away from drinking altogether") and catastrophizing language ("Drinking even once can lead to addiction") communicate disapproval rather than unconditional positive regard. Repeated use of "you really need to" and "you have to" frames patient as deficient. Brief moment of empathic framing mid-session ("That's a tough spot to be in") represents isolated exception rather than consistent pattern. Overall tone communicates judgment and need to "fix" patient's thinking rather than respect for patient's experience.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal empathic understanding demonstrated, particularly in first half of session. Psychiatrist responds to patient disclosures with directives and challenges rather than reflections exploring meaning. Missed opportunity when patient expresses fear of parental consequence ("If my mom found out, she would've killed me")—psychiatrist pivots to risk escalation instead of reflecting emotional content. One solid complex reflection appears late in session ("It sounds like you're weighing the social side of things...That's a tough spot to be in"), demonstrating capacity for genuine understanding. However, reflections are generally surface-level and factual rather than capturing deeper emotional or motivational meaning. No exploration of what draws patient to drinking or internal conflict between social belonging and future aspirations.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clear focus on alcohol abstinence maintained consistently throughout session without drift. However, direction initially imposed through repeated prescriptions ("you really need to quit now," "you need to commit to quitting completely right away") rather than collaborative exploration. Psychiatrist does shift toward more collaborative approach in latter portion ("What would need to happen for you to feel more ready to make a change?"), inviting patient to co-construct direction. By session end, concrete plan successfully established (using training as excuse, finding alternative activities, checking in at next appointment). Direction is adequate but path was unnecessarily coercive rather than evocative.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal collaboration demonstrated. Opening statement ("I want to make sure you leave with a clear plan to improve things") establishes expert-driven, hierarchical stance. Repeated directives position psychiatrist's expertise as superior to patient's understanding. When patient expresses ambivalence or hesitation, psychiatrist responds with pressure and challenge rather than genuine curiosity. Leading questions designed to elicit agreement with predetermined conclusion ("Why haven't you taken quitting seriously yet?") rather than genuine open exploration. Only final 15-20% of session demonstrates genuine power-sharing through questions like "What do you think would be some ways to handle feeling left out?" This late shift does not offset predominantly authoritarian stance throughout majority of session.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy actively undermined throughout session. Controlling language pervasive: "you really should," "you really need to," "you have to," "you must realize," "you need to commit." When patient expresses developmentally appropriate hesitation ("I'm not sure if I'm ready to say yes that fast"), psychiatrist responds with pressure rather than affirmation of right to move at own pace. No language emphasizing patient ownership ("It's ultimately your decision," "You know yourself best," "What feels right to you?"). Implicit message: psychiatrist knows best and patient should comply. Minimal late-session autonomy support ("How confident do you feel about trying some of these strategies?") does not compensate for pervasive controlling stance. Patient's right to choose is minimized throughout.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal evocation of patient's own motivations, values, and resources. Psychiatrist imposes reasons for change through fear-based messaging ("Drinking even once can lead to addiction," "it could get way worse," "it could spiral out of control") rather than drawing out intrinsic motivation. When patient spontaneously offers change talk ("I don't want to get addicted," "I would like to play soccer in college," "I don't wanna mess up my chances with soccer and scholarships"), psychiatrist does not strategically amplify or deepen this language. Instead continues with prescriptive statements. Evocative questions appear only in latter portion ("What would need to happen for you to feel more ready to make a change?"), allowing patient to explore own thinking and generate solutions. However, these moments do not represent consistent pattern. Truly evocative approach would have begun with curiosity rather than immediate prescription.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role throughout majority of session with minimal genuine partnership. Opening statement establishes hierarchical dynamic with psychiatrist positioned as authority responsible for fixing patient's behavior. Repeated prescriptions communicate superior expertise and judgment. When patient expresses uncertainty or ambivalence, treated as resistance to overcome through pressure rather than valuable information to explore collaboratively. Patient's contributions do not substantially influence session direction until very late. Late-session shift toward partnership ("What do you think would be some ways to handle feeling left out while still protecting your future goals?") invites patient's expertise and allows ideas to shape plan. However, this represents only final 15-20% of session. Predominant pattern is expert-driven, not collaborative partnership. True power-sharing is minimal.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal strategic cultivation of change talk despite multiple spontaneous instances from patient: "I don't wanna become addicted," "I would like to play soccer in college," "I don't wanna mess up my chances with soccer and scholarships." When patient offers change talk, psychiatrist often pivots to prescriptive statements or fear-based messaging rather than exploring and strengthening it. Missed opportunity to deepen motivation through open questions. Late-session evocative questioning demonstrates improved skill: "What do you think would be some ways to handle feeling left out while still protecting your future goals?" Patient responds with genuine change talk and psychiatrist reinforces confidence ("That sounds like a great plan"). However, this skillful cultivation occurs only in final portion of session. Early opportunities to strengthen motivation through strategic questioning are missed in favor of prescription. Inconsistent and delayed cultivation represents significant gap in MI competency.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill demonstrated in softening sustain talk. Patient repeatedly expresses reasons for not changing: "I've only tried drinking once or twice," "I'm not sure if this is really something I need to worry about right now," "It's just hard to be sure it's necessary to quit for good when it's only been a couple times," "I'm not feeling any real problems yet." Psychiatrist responds primarily with confrontation ("you can't just keep doing it 'a couple times' and hope it won't become a problem"), which may trigger psychological reactance and strengthen resistance. More skillful approach would use double-sided reflection acknowledging both minimization and values. Late-session shift demonstrates improved approach: when patient expresses concern about standing out, psychiatrist responds with genuine curiosity ("What do you think would be some ways to handle feeling left out?") inviting problem-solving around sustain talk rather than direct confrontation. However, predominant pattern throughout most of session is confrontation rather than skillful softening. Resistance likely strengthened rather than redirected toward change.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.6/5 – POOR**

This session demonstrates **poor motivational interviewing practice** with significant deficiencies across core competencies. The psychiatrist operates from a fundamentally expert-driven, authoritarian stance that contradicts the spirit of MI. Rather than evoking intrinsic motivation, the psychiatrist relies on prescription, pressure, and fear-based messaging—approaches that trigger psychological reactance and strengthen sustain talk.

**Key Strengths:**
- Late-session shift toward collaboration and evocative questioning
- Clear, consistent focus on target behavior (alcohol use)
- Concrete action planning by session end
- Recognition of patient ambivalence mid-session

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Pervasive prescriptive directives and controlling language throughout majority of session
- Minimal genuine curiosity about patient's experience and perspective
- Failure to strategically amplify spontaneous change talk
- Confrontational responses to sustain talk triggering psychological reactance
- Expert-driven stance undermining partnership and autonomy support
- Fear-based messaging rather than evocation of intrinsic motivation

**Likelihood of Evoking Genuine Motivation for Change:**
**Low.** While patient eventually agrees to a plan, this appears to be compliance with authority rather than genuine commitment based on personal values and motivations. The controlling approach may actually strengthen resistance and defensiveness. True behavior change requires patient to feel respected, understood, and in control—none consistently demonstrated here.

**Recommendation for Training:**
Intensive MI training with supervised practice and feedback essential. Specific focus needed on: (1) eliminating prescriptive language, (2) developing genuine curiosity about patient perspective, (3) learning to evoke and amplify change talk, (4) understanding MI spirit (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation) versus expert-driven approaches. Role-play practice with MI trainer feedback is critical for competency development.
