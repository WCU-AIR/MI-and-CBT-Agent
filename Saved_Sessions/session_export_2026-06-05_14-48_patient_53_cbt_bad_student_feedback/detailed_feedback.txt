
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

3. Would this lead to measurable symptom improvement?
4. Can the patient apply learnings independently?

2. What was missing (specific techniques that should have been used)

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

### 3. Understanding

### 1. **URGENT: Conduct Comprehensive Suicide Risk Assessment**
- **Action**: In the next session, conduct a formal suicide risk assessment. Ask directly about suicidal ideation, intent, plan, and means. The patient has already expressed passive suicidal ideation ("I should have died instead of him"). Determine if this has progressed to active ideation or planning.
- **Example**: "Andrew, I want to check in about something you mentioned—that you think you should have died instead of your son. Tell me more about that thought. How often do you have it? Do you ever think about harming yourself? Do you have a plan? Do you have access to means?"
- **Practice**: Review suicide risk assessment protocols. Practice asking these questions directly and without judgment. Develop a safety plan template that includes warning signs, coping strategies, people to contact, and crisis resources.

### 2. **Develop a Coherent Case Conceptualization and Treatment Plan**
- **Action**: Before the next session, develop a written case conceptualization that identifies: (1) precipitating factors (son's suicide, grief), (2) maintaining factors (rumination, self-blame, social withdrawal, alcohol use, behavioral inactivity), (3) cognitive patterns (personalization, catastrophizing, all-or-nothing thinking), (4) behavioral patterns (avoidance, withdrawal), and (5) biological factors (early-morning insomnia, fatigue). Use this to develop a coherent treatment strategy with specific, measurable goals.
- **Example**: "Andrew's depression is maintained by a cycle of rumination about his son's death, self-blame and guilt, social withdrawal to avoid pain, behavioral inactivity, and alcohol use to manage insomnia. His core thoughts are 'I should have died instead of him' and 'I could have prevented this.' Treatment will focus on: (1) breaking the rumination cycle through behavioral activation and thought-challenging, (2) addressing guilt and self-blame through cognitive restructuring, (3) gradually increasing social contact and pleasurable activities, (4) addressing alcohol use, and (5) managing early-morning insomnia through sleep hygiene and cognitive work."
- **Practice**: Study case conceptualization frameworks. Practice writing conceptualizations for different presentations. Discuss your conceptualization with a supervisor before implementing it.

### 3. **Establish a Collaborative Agenda and Treatment Goals**
- **Action**: At the beginning of the next session, explicitly establish a collaborative agenda. Ask the patient what he wants to achieve through therapy, what symptoms are most bothersome, and what he would like to focus on. Prioritize issues together and establish specific, measurable goals.
- **Example**: "Andrew, I want to make sure we use our time together effectively. I'm hearing several things that are troubling you—the guilt about your son, the early-morning waking and lying in bed feeling worthless, the wine you're using to sleep, and the feeling that life has lost meaning. What feels most important to address first? What would recovery look like for you? What would be different if you were feeling better?"
- **Practice**: Practice collaborative goal-setting with colleagues. Learn to balance the therapist's clinical judgment with the patient's preferences and priorities.

### 4. **Apply Cognitive Restructuring to Core Thoughts**
- **Action**: Identify the patient's core distorted thoughts ("I should have died instead of him," "I could have prevented my son's suicide," "I'm worthless") and systematically apply cognitive restructuring. Use guided discovery to help the patient examine evidence for and against these thoughts and develop more balanced alternatives.
- **Example**: "Andrew, I want to explore the thought 'I should have died instead of my son.' What evidence do you have that you could have prevented his suicide? What factors were beyond your control? What would you tell a friend who was blaming themselves in this situation? Is it realistic to expect that you could have known or prevented this? What's a more balanced way to think about this?"
- **Practice**: Study cognitive restructuring techniques. Practice using thought records. Practice guided discovery questions. Role-play with colleagues.

### 5. **Implement Behavioral Activation with Clear Structure and Rationale**
- **Action**: Develop a structured behavioral activation plan. Explain the rationale (how inactivity maintains depression, how increasing activity can improve mood). Start with small, achievable activities and gradually progress. Frame activities as behavioral experiments to test hypotheses about the relationship between activity and mood.
- **Example**: "Andrew, depression makes us want to withdraw and stay inactive. But research shows that inactivity actually makes depression worse. By gradually increasing your activity level, we can break this cycle. Let's start with something small. What's one activity you used to enjoy that you could try this week? Let's say you commit to doing it for 20 minutes. Before you do it, predict how your mood will change. After you do it, rate your mood and see if your prediction was accurate. This is an experiment to test whether activity affects your mood."
- **Practice**: Study behavioral activation protocols. Practice developing activity schedules. Practice explaining the rationale to patients. Role-play with colleagues.

### 6. **Address Social Withdrawal Through Graded Exposure**
- **Action**: Identify social withdrawal as a key maintaining factor. Develop a graded exposure hierarchy starting with small social contacts and progressing to more challenging ones. Frame social contact as a behavioral experiment.
- **Example**: "Andrew, I notice you've been withdrawing from people. While that feels safer right now, research shows that isolation actually makes depression worse. Let's develop a plan to gradually increase social contact. Week 1: Send one text or email to someone. Week 2: Have a brief phone call with someone. Week 3: Meet someone in person for coffee. Each time, notice how you feel before and after. This is an experiment to test whether social contact affects your mood."
- **Practice**: Study exposure therapy principles. Practice developing hierarchies. Practice explaining the rationale for gradual exposure.

### 7. **Teach Rumination Management Techniques**
- **Action**: Identify rumination as a problem behavior. Teach the patient to recognize when rumination is occurring and develop strategies to interrupt it (behavioral activation, worry postponement, mindfulness, thought-stopping).
- **Example**: "Andrew, I notice you spend a lot of time thinking about 'what ifs'—what if you had been there more, what if you had noticed the signs sooner. This is called rumination, and it's a common symptom of depression. The problem is that rumination keeps depression going. When you notice yourself ruminating, here's what we can do: Set a timer for 10 minutes and allow yourself to ruminate during those 10 minutes. When the timer goes off, do something else—go for a walk, call someone, work on a project. This helps interrupt the rumination cycle."
- **Practice**: Study rumination management techniques. Practice teaching these to patients. Role-play with colleagues.
