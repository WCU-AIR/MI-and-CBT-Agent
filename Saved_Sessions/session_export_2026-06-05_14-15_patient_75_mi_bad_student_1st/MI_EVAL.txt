# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates a near-complete absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. Repeated use of judgmental language ("excuses," "playing with fire," "not taking this seriously") communicates disapproval of the patient's experience. No affirmation of strengths (concern for children, honesty, willingness to attend) is provided. The tone is authoritarian and punitive rather than warm and supportive. Patient's defensive responses ("being pushed like this makes me dig in more") indicate the impact of this judgmental stance.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal to no empathetic engagement is demonstrated. There are zero reflective listening statements throughout the session. The psychiatrist uses rapid-fire interrogation ("Why didn't you try earlier?" "Have you noticed any health problems?") rather than attempting to understand the patient's internal frame of reference. When the patient provides substantive responses about smoking as relaxation and stress management, these are not acknowledged or validated. The patient's explicit requests for understanding ("I need some understanding that quitting isn't easy") are ignored entirely.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains extremely clear and consistent focus on smoking cessation as the singular target behavior. Every statement and question points toward immediate quitting with no topic drift. However, this direction is pursued through coercive pressure and commands rather than collaborative exploration. Direction without the other MI spirit elements becomes control rather than therapeutic guidance. High score reflects technical consistency in maintaining focus, but this must be contextualized within the broader failure of the session's relational foundation.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The session demonstrates an entirely authoritarian, expert-driven stance with zero collaboration. The psychiatrist establishes hierarchical power dynamics from the opening ("We need to get straight to the point") and issues unilateral directives ("Just stop smoking today," "Just throw away your cigarettes"). When the patient attempts to participate as an equal partner ("I think it's got to come from me wanting it"), the psychiatrist explicitly rejects this contribution ("No, you don't get it"). There is no shared decision-making, no exploration of patient preferences, and no genuine invitation for the patient to shape the session's direction.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist systematically undermines the patient's autonomy through controlling language ("You must quit now," "You have to quit immediately," "No more delays, no more excuses") and explicit denial of the patient's right to assess readiness ("You can't just say you're not ready"). When the patient states current readiness level, the psychiatrist responds with dismissal rather than respect for self-determination. No affirmation of the patient's freedom to choose is provided. The patient explicitly requests autonomy support ("If quitting is going to stick, it has to come from me wanting it, not because I'm being forced"), which is directly denied.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no evocation is demonstrated. The psychiatrist does not ask about the patient's own reasons for change, values regarding children's health, ideas about what might help, or past successes. Instead, external reasons are imposed ("Smoking leads to lung cancer and heart disease"). The patient provides spontaneous change talk about concern for children ("I'm worried about my children," "I don't want them to start"), but the psychiatrist does not amplify, explore, or reinforce this. No inquiry into patient resources, strengths, or past accomplishments occurs. Curiosity about the patient's internal world is entirely absent.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates complete absence of partnership and power-sharing. The clinician assumes exclusive expert authority and maintains rigid control over session direction, content, and conclusions. The patient's contributions are dismissed ("Look, you have to stop making excuses," "No, you don't get it," "That's exactly the problem"). The patient's explicit request for partnership ("I'm here to talk about it and understand it better") is ignored. The psychiatrist lectures and commands rather than engaging in genuine dialogue. The patient's perspective is communicated as irrelevant to the session's outcome.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The patient spontaneously provides multiple instances of change talk rooted in values regarding children's wellbeing ("I'm worried about my children," "I don't want them to start," "I do want my kids to be healthy and not start smoking because of me"). The psychiatrist completely fails to evoke, explore, amplify, or reinforce any of this. No reflections deepen the patient's own motivation. No questions invite elaboration on what being a good role model means. The psychiatrist's response to change talk is silence and redirection to external pressure. This represents a critical missed opportunity and fundamental failure of MI's most powerful technique.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist confronts the patient's sustain talk aggressively rather than softening it skillfully. When the patient expresses that smoking helps relaxation and is part of daily routine, the psychiatrist responds with confrontation ("Look, you have to stop making excuses," "You're playing with fire"). This triggers psychological reactance; the patient's sustain talk intensifies ("being pushed like this makes me dig in more," "makes me want to hang onto my cigarettes more"). A double-sided reflection validating both ambivalence sides would be more effective. The score reflects at least an attempt to address sustain talk, though the execution is severely counterproductive.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.3/5**

**Session Assessment:**

This session represents a **severe failure of Motivational Interviewing practice** and a fundamental violation of evidence-based behavior change counseling principles. The psychiatrist demonstrates an authoritarian, confrontational approach directly contradicted by decades of research on effective counseling. Across nine MI competency domains, the psychiatrist scores 1/5 on eight dimensions and 5/5 on Direction—but only because direction is pursued through coercive control rather than collaborative guidance.

**Critical Findings:**

- **Relational Foundation Absent:** The session embodies the opposite of MI spirit—hierarchy instead of partnership, judgment instead of acceptance, coldness instead of compassion, imposition instead of evocation.

- **Counterproductive Impact:** The patient's explicit statements indicate the psychiatrist's approach has moved her further from change: "being pushed like this doesn't make me feel like quitting—it actually makes me dig in more" and "feeling pressured like this just makes me want to hang onto my cigarettes more." This is predictable psychological reactance to autonomy-undermining counseling.

- **Missed Opportunities:** The patient expressed genuine concern about her children multiple times—her most powerful motivation for change. The psychiatrist completely failed to recognize, explore, or amplify this change talk.

- **Violation of Patient Rights:** The psychiatrist explicitly denied the patient's right to assess her own readiness ("You can't just say you're not ready") and her right to self-determination ("You must quit immediately").

**Recommendation:**

This psychiatrist requires **urgent, comprehensive retraining in Motivational Interviewing principles and practice**. Formal MI training (minimum 20 hours), ongoing supervision with an MI-trained clinician, and recorded session review are essential before continuing behavior change counseling. The current approach is not only ineffective—it is actively harmful and likely to increase treatment disengagement and resistance to future change efforts.
