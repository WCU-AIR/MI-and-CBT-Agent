### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Solid reflective listening with accurate, complex reflections capturing patient's dual perspective and ambivalence ("You value how drinking helps you relax and connect socially, and the idea of cutting back feels like it might come with its own challenges or losses"). Good acknowledgment of realistic starting point. However, empathy could be deeper and more emotionally attuned. Misses underlying anxiety when patient mentions routine ("once I start, it's kind of routine") and doesn't explore emotional undercurrent of reluctance to involve family (potential shame or fear of judgment).

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintains appropriate focus on alcohol consumption and readiness for change with logical progression: exploring perspective → acknowledging external concerns → exploring ambivalence → identifying benefits → developing concrete plan → establishing accountability. Opening question establishes clear direction while respecting autonomy. Could be more directive in exploring key barrier about routine drinking. Could more explicitly connect patient's stated goals (reducing tension at work/family) to drinking behavior throughout session.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4

Solicits input on planning ("What kind of plan or support do you think would fit best with your life?"). Positions patient as expert on own life and preferences. Subtle moments where clinician edges toward expert positioning (e.g., "It's great that you're choosing something small and concrete") rather than purely exploring patient's own reasoning.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently affirms patient's right to choose and emphasizes ownership of change process ("You're thoughtful about the kind of support that feels comfortable and sustainable for you"). Avoids prescriptive advice, asking what patient thinks would work. However, subtle moments where autonomy support could strengthen—implicit endorsements of particular strategies ("Starting small and testing the waters feels like a practical and realistic approach") could be perceived as subtle pressure. Could have explored what would increase confidence level of 6 rather than moving forward with planning.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates adequate but inconsistent evocation of patient's own motivations and reasons for change. Effectively asks "What do you think makes you curious or open to this conversation?" and evokes benefits of change. However, fails to deeply amplify or reinforce change talk. When patient articulates "It'd be nice to not have that tension hanging over me," clinician reflects but doesn't ask follow-up questions to deepen exploration. Significant gap: accepts confidence level of 6 without exploring what contributes to it or what would increase it. Misses opportunity to explore past success with changes more deeply.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong partnership with genuine power-sharing and respect for patient's expertise. Uses inclusive language and positions patient's contributions as shaping session. Explicitly affirms patient's knowledge of self ("You recognize that without those structures, it's easy to drift back into old habits"). Subtle moments where clinician assumes slightly more expert role—framing of "recognizing the value" subtly positions clinician as having identified what patient should value. Could have more explicitly invited patient to co-create accountability structure rather than suggesting check-ins.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates adequate but inconsistent cultivation of change talk. Patient articulates several important change statements ("Maybe I've overdone it a few times," "It'd be nice to not have that tension hanging over me," "Starting small and testing the waters might make sense"). However, clinician does not strategically amplify or reinforce these statements. Fails to explore what contributes to confidence level of 6. Misses opportunity to deepen exploration of past success with changes. Minimal exploration of patient's values and how they connect to change. Change talk is present but lacks strategic depth and frequency characteristic of strong MI practice.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates good skill in handling patient's sustain talk without confrontation. Skillfully avoids arguing against minimization. Uses effective double-sided reflections acknowledging both benefits of drinking and costs ("You value how drinking helps you relax and connect socially, and the idea of cutting back feels like it might come with its own challenges or losses"). Reframes concerns collaboratively rather than dismissing them. However, when patient identifies barrier ("once I start, it's kind of routine"), clinician doesn't explore deeply or help develop strategies to address it. Could have asked more strategic follow-up questions about interrupting the routine.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 3.8/5**

**Strengths:**
- Consistent acceptance and warmth with genuine unconditional positive regard
- Strong collaborative stance with appropriate power-sharing
- Skillful handling of ambivalence using double-sided reflections without confrontation
- Logical session structure with appropriate direction
- Avoidance of confrontation around minimization
- Development of concrete, manageable action steps

**Areas for Development:**
- Insufficient strategic amplification and reinforcement of change talk
- Missed opportunities to explore and strengthen patient's self-efficacy
- Limited exploration of values and connection to behavior change
- Superficial exploration of key barriers (routine drinking)
- Inconsistent depth in emotional attunement of reflections
- Subtle expert positioning that undermines pure partnership
- Premature movement to planning before full exploration of ambivalence

**Impact on Patient Engagement:**
This session demonstrates solid MI competence that maintains patient engagement and openness to exploring change. The warm, nonjudgmental stance creates psychological safety. However, the session falls short of maximizing intrinsic motivation for change. Patient leaves with concrete plan and adequate engagement but has not deeply articulated compelling reasons for change or fully recognized personal resources and past successes. With more skillful evocation and strategic amplification of change talk, this session could move from adequate to good MI practice and significantly increase likelihood of sustained behavior change.

**Recommendations for Development:**
Prioritize developing greater skill in strategic evocation and amplification of change talk. Specifically: (1) identify every instance of change talk and ask follow-up questions to deepen exploration; (2) explore and strengthen self-efficacy by asking what contributes to confidence; (3) explicitly connect behavior change to patient values; (4) deeply explore barriers and collaboratively develop strategies; (5) increase emotional attunement in reflections; (6) resist premature movement to planning until ambivalence is fully explored; (7) use scaling questions more strategically with follow-up exploration.
