
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **Implement Systematic Case Conceptualization and CBT Formulation**
- **Action**: Before the next session, develop an explicit written case formulation that identifies: (1) precipitating factors (breakup), (2) maintaining factors (rumination, avoidance, behavioral inactivity, hopeless cognitions, sleep disruption), (3) predisposing factors (family history, prior depression), (4) protective factors (friends, therapist, insight). Use this formulation to guide all interventions.
- **Example**: "Helena's depression is maintained by a cycle in which: (1) intrusive thoughts about her ex trigger sadness and hopelessness; (2) she attempts to escape these thoughts through nighttime distraction (journaling, calling friends), which provides short-term relief but strengthens the rumination cycle; (3) poor sleep from staying awake until 5-6 AM leads to daytime fatigue; (4) fatigue and low mood reduce motivation for activity; (5) inactivity worsens mood, creating a vicious cycle. Treatment will target: (1) reducing nighttime avoidance and building tolerance for difficult thoughts, (2) increasing daytime activity to improve mood and sleep, (3) challenging hopeless cognitions with evidence, (4) developing skills to manage racing thoughts."
- **Practice**: Develop formulations for 3-5 cases and discuss with a supervisor. Practice articulating the formulation to patients and explaining how interventions address specific maintaining factors.

### 2. **Master Cognitive Restructuring and Thought Records**
- **Action**: Learn to systematically identify automatic thoughts, examine evidence for and against them, and develop alternative thoughts. Practice using structured thought records with patients.
- **Example**: When Helena mentions "I can't shake this heaviness," ask: "What specific thoughts go through your mind when you feel that heaviness? For example, are you thinking 'I'll never feel better' or 'There's something wrong with me' or 'I'm broken'?" Once identified, explore: "What evidence do you have that this thought is true? What evidence contradicts it? What would a friend say? What would you say to a friend in this situation? What's a more balanced way to think about this?"
- **Practice**: Complete 10 thought records with a supervisor, focusing on identifying core depressive thoughts (hopelessness, helplessness, worthlessness) and developing evidence-based alternatives. Practice the Socratic method of questioning rather than telling patients what to think.

### 3. **Implement Behavioral Activation Systematically**
- **Action**: Assess baseline activity level, identify valued activities, create a hierarchy of activities by difficulty, and schedule them systematically. Track mood before and after activities to establish the behavior-mood connection.
- **Example**: "Helena, let's create an activity schedule. First, what activities did you enjoy before you felt depressed? What activities feel meaningful to you now, even if you don't feel like doing them? Let's rank them by difficulty from easiest to hardest. This week, I want you to schedule three activities from the easier end of the list. Before each activity, rate your mood 0-10. After, rate it again. We're testing whether activity affects mood."
- **Practice**: Conduct behavioral activation with 3-5 depressed patients. Practice creating hierarchies, scheduling activities, and reviewing mood data. Learn to help patients overcome the barrier of "I don't feel like it" by explaining that behavior can change mood, not just the reverse.

### 4. **Eliminate Excessive Therapist Self-Disclosure**
- **Action**: Stop sharing personal anecdotes about your own experiences. Instead, focus entirely on understanding the patient's experience. If you feel the urge to share a personal story, pause and ask yourself: "Does this serve the patient's treatment, or does it shift focus to me? Is this normalizing in a helpful way, or is it minimizing the severity of their experience?"
- **Example**: Instead of "When I was stressed, I found that keeping busy helped," say: "Many people find that activity helps with mood. Let's explore what activities might help you specifically. What did you enjoy before you felt depressed?"
- **Practice**: Record yourself in role-plays or actual sessions and count instances of self-disclosure. Aim for zero personal anecdotes per session. Practice redirecting the focus back to the patient when you feel the urge to share.

### 5. **Establish Collaborative Agenda-Setting at the Beginning of Every Session**
- **Action**: At the start of each session, explicitly ask: "What would be most helpful to focus on today? We have about 50 minutes. What are your priorities?" Establish 2-3 specific agenda items, allocate time, and reference the agenda throughout the session to maintain structure.
- **Example**: "Helena, I want to make sure we use our time well today. What's most important for you to work on? [Listen] Okay, so you want to focus on managing the racing thoughts at night and figuring out how to get better sleep. We also need to check in on safety. Let's spend about 20 minutes understanding the racing thoughts pattern, 15 minutes on sleep strategies, and 10 minutes on safety. Does that work for you?"
- **Practice**: Establish a collaborative agenda in every session for the next month. Have a supervisor review recordings and provide feedback on how well you maintained the agenda throughout the session.

### 6. **Implement Guided Discovery Through Socratic Questioning**
- **Action**: Replace direct advice with skillful questioning that helps patients discover their own insights. Ask open-ended questions, explore the patient's thoughts and beliefs, and help them reach conclusions independently.
- **Example**: Instead of "Try breathing exercises," ask: "You mentioned that silence makes the racing thoughts louder. What do you think happens when you try to push the thoughts away or distract yourself? What if we tried a different approach—just noticing the thoughts without fighting them? What do you think might happen?"
