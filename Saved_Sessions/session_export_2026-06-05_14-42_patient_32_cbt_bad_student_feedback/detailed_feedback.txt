
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **Understand Delusional Disorder and Psychotic Symptoms**
- **Action:** Obtain training in the phenomenology and treatment of psychotic disorders. Delusions are not equivalent to anxiety or worry; they are fixed false beliefs resistant to evidence. They require different clinical approaches than cognitive distortions in anxiety disorders.
- **Example:** Recognize that when a patient says "I believe there are listening devices in my apartment," this is not a thought that can be replaced through positive thinking. It is a conviction that requires reality-testing, medication, and specialized psychotherapy.
- **Practice:** Read evidence-based resources on delusional disorder (e.g., DSM-5 criteria, cognitive models of psychosis). Observe or co-facilitate sessions with an experienced clinician treating psychotic disorders.

### 2. **Establish Clear Agenda and Session Structure**
- **Action:** At the beginning of each session, collaboratively set an agenda with the patient. Explicitly state: "We have 50 minutes today. I'd like to focus on X. What's most important to you?"
- **Example:** "Itsuki, I noticed you're still struggling with the thoughts about listening devices and feeling watched. Today, I'd like to understand more about when these thoughts are strongest and what you've tried so far. Does that sound okay?"
- **Practice:** Use a written agenda template. Practice setting agendas in role-plays with supervisors or peers. Record sessions and review agenda-setting.

### 3. **Implement Systematic Feedback Elicitation**
- **Action:** Ask for feedback throughout the session, not just at the end. Check understanding after each intervention. Solicit reactions to suggestions.
- **Example:** After suggesting an intervention, ask: "Does that make sense to you?" "How does that land?" "What questions do you have?" "What concerns do you have about trying this?"
- **Practice:** Develop a habit of pausing after each major point and asking: "Am I on the right track?" "Is this helpful?" Record sessions and count feedback-elicitation instances.

### 4. **Use Guided Discovery Instead of Advice-Giving**
- **Action:** Replace directive suggestions with Socratic questions. Help the patient reach his own insights rather than telling him what to think.
- **Example:** Instead of "Try to think positive," ask: "When you have these thoughts about the devices, what evidence makes you believe they're real? What evidence might suggest they're not? What would need to happen for you to feel more confident about what's actually happening?"
- **Practice:** Develop a list of Socratic questions for common situations. Practice asking questions in role-plays. Record sessions and review the ratio of questions to statements (aim for 70% questions, 30% statements).

### 5. **Focus on Core Cognitions and Behaviors**
- **Action:** Identify the central beliefs and behaviors maintaining the patient's distress. For this patient, that means the delusional beliefs and associated avoidance. Explore these systematically.
- **Example:** "Tell me more about the listening devices. What do they look like? How do you know they're there? When did you first notice them? What do you think would happen if you went near them?"
- **Practice:** For each patient, write down the 3 core cognitions and 3 core behaviors maintaining symptoms. Ensure that at least 50% of session time is spent on these core issues.

### 6. **Develop a Coherent Treatment Strategy**
- **Action:** Before each session, develop a clear strategy: What is the conceptualization? What are the goals? What interventions will we use? In what order? Why?
- **Example:** "Itsuki's delusions appear to be maintained by isolation, sleep deprivation, and lack of reality-testing. Our strategy will be: (1) psychoeducation about how stress affects perception, (2) gentle reality-testing through guided discovery, (3) behavioral experiments to test beliefs, (4) psychiatric evaluation for medication, (5) gradual behavioral activation."
- **Practice:** Write a treatment plan for each patient. Review it with a supervisor. Adjust based on outcome.

### 7. **Apply CBT Techniques Appropriately and Systematically**
- **Action:** Use evidence-based CBT techniques appropriate for the diagnosis. For delusional disorder, this includes reality-testing, behavioral experiments, and psychoeducation—not positive thinking or simple distraction.
- **Example:** "Let's design an experiment to test your belief about the devices. What would we need to observe to know whether they're really there? What would we observe if they weren't there? Let's try this and see what happens."
- **Practice:** Develop competence in specific techniques (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, exposure, activity scheduling). Practice each technique in role-plays with feedback from supervisors.

### 8. **Assign Formal, Specific Homework with Rationale and Review**
- **Action:** Assign explicit homework with written instructions, clear rationale, and commitment check. Review homework at the start of the next session.
- **Example:** "This week, I'd like you to go outside for 5 minutes each day, even if you feel anxious. Here's why: we want to test whether your fear about people watching you is as strong as you think. I'd like you to notice what actually happens. Are you willing to try this? What might make it difficult? Let's problem-solve any barriers."
- **Practice:** Use a homework sheet template. Always review homework at the start of the next session. If homework wasn't completed, explore barriers without judgment.

### 9. **Reduce Self-Disclosure and Maintain Focus on Patient**
- **Action:** Minimize personal self-disclosure. When you do share, ensure it serves a clear therapeutic purpose and is brief. Keep focus on the patient's experience.
- **Example:** Instead of "When I felt stressed, I reminded myself 'one day at a time,'" ask: "What strategies have you tried in the past when you felt overwhelmed? What helped?"
- **Practice:** Record sessions and count instances of therapist self-disclosure. Aim for zero to minimal instances. When you notice the urge to share a personal experience, pause and ask a question instead.

### 10. **Recognize When Psychotic Symptoms Require Psychiatric Intervention**
- **Action:** Understand that delusional disorder typically requires antipsychotic medication. Recognize early signs that psychiatric referral is needed and make the referral promptly with clear explanation.
- **Example:** "Itsuki, based on what you've described—persistent beliefs about listening devices and people trying to control your thoughts—I think it's important to have a psychiatrist evaluate you. These symptoms often respond well to medication, which can help reduce the intensity of these thoughts and improve your sleep. This is a standard part of treatment. Would you be willing to see a psychiatrist?"
- **Practice:** Develop clear criteria for psychiatric referral. Discuss cases with supervisors to calibrate judgment about when referral is appropriate.

### 11. **Develop Competence in Psychosis-Specific Interventions**
- **Action:** Obtain specialized training in cognitive therapy for psychosis (CTP). This includes reality-testing, belief modification, and coping strategies specific to psychotic symptoms.
- **Example:** Learn techniques such as: examining evidence for and against delusions, identifying triggers for psychotic symptoms, developing coping strategies for intrusive thoughts, and collaboratively designing behavioral experiments.
- **Practice:** Attend workshops or training programs in cognitive therapy for psychosis. Seek supervision from an expert in this area. Read evidence-based literature (e.g., Kingdon & Turkington, Chadwick et al.).

### 12. **Improve Collaboration and Shared Decision-Making**
- **Action:** Position the patient as an active partner in treatment. Solicit input on problems, goals, and interventions. Make decisions collaboratively.
- **Example:** "What do you think is the most important thing to work on first? What feels most doable for you? What would help you feel more confident about what's actually happening?"
- **Practice:** In each session, explicitly ask for the patient's input at least 3 times. Use phrases like "What do you think?" "What feels right to you?" "How would you like to approach this?"
