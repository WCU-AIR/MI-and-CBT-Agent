### Autonomy Support

No explicit statements affirming autonomy such as "This is ultimately your decision" or "You get to decide what's right for you." The framing suggests the clinician expects eventual change rather than truly supporting Ahmed's autonomy to make his own choice.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Significant weakness. The clinician demonstrates minimal strategic effort to draw out Ahmed's intrinsic motivation, values, reasons for change, or resources. No exploration of what health means to Ahmed, what concerns him about throat irritation, or what mini-cigars provide him. When Ahmed minimizes harm, the clinician does not evoke deeper exploration. Critical questions about values, fears, and ambivalence are entirely absent from the session.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The clinician uses collaborative language and invites Ahmed's input, but doesn't fully embody the principle that expertise about change resides within the client. Occasionally assumes an expert interpretive role ("there might be some curiosity or concern underneath") rather than discovering Ahmed's experience collaboratively. Never explicitly affirms Ahmed's expertise about his own body and life, or invites him to co-interpret clinical findings.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Critical deficiency. Ahmed expresses only sustain talk (reasons against change) throughout the entire session—"I don't smoke cigarettes," "They're just little cigars," "I'm not ready to quit"—and the clinician does nothing to evoke, amplify, or reinforce any statements favoring change. No strategic questioning to draw out Ahmed's own concerns, reasons for change, or ability to change. This is a fundamental MI failure that undermines the entire approach.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill demonstrated. The clinician largely accepts and reflects Ahmed's minimization and resistance without strategically introducing complexity or double-sided reflections. When Ahmed says "I'm not ready to quit," the clinician responds by reframing it positively, which inadvertently reinforces sustain talk rather than softening it. No use of reflections that acknowledge both Ahmed's perspective and the clinical concern simultaneously.

## Summary of Clinical Findings

### Strengths
- Establishes genuinely collaborative opening by inviting Ahmed's agenda
- Maintains warm, nonjudgmental stance without pressure or confrontation
- Demonstrates accurate basic reflective listening
- Avoids lecturing or arguing about health risks
- Collaboratively develops follow-up plan with patient input
- Creates safe environment where patient feels heard and respected

### Critical Areas for Improvement
- **Evocation (HIGHEST PRIORITY):** No strategic effort to draw out Ahmed's intrinsic motivation, values, or concerns about health
- **Change Talk Cultivation:** Ahmed expresses only sustain talk; clinician accepts without strategic response
- **Softening Sustain Talk:** Clinician accepts minimization and resistance without introducing complexity or double-sided reflections
- **Strategic Direction:** Allows Ahmed to define problem as "routine check-up" rather than gently guiding toward clinical concern
- **Depth of Empathy:** Surface-level reflections lack exploration of underlying values, fears, and meaning
- **Autonomy Support:** Minimal explicit affirmation of Ahmed's right to choose or agency in change process

### Impact on Client Engagement
This session is unlikely to increase Ahmed's motivation for change. By accepting sustain talk without strategic response, the clinician inadvertently reinforces Ahmed's minimization and resistance. Ahmed leaves with his original position intact—mini-cigars are not a problem, change is unnecessary—and no new understanding of his own ambivalence. Ahmed's stage of change remains precontemplation. While the clinician has prioritized maintaining alliance, this has come at the expense of evoking motivation—a common early-stage MI error.

### Key Recommendations
1. **Strategically evoke change talk** through open questions designed to elicit Ahmed's own concerns and reasons for change
2. **Use double-sided reflections** to soften sustain talk while acknowledging Ahmed's perspective
3. **Deepen empathy** by exploring emotional experience, values, and what health means to Ahmed
4. **Explicitly affirm autonomy** through language emphasizing Ahmed's control and decision-making power
5. **Maintain strategic direction** while remaining collaborative by gently guiding toward clinical concern
6. **Explore ambivalence** rather than accepting Ahmed's position as final
7. **Amplify change talk** through reflections, follow-up questions, and summaries whenever it emerges
8. **Ask about barriers and facilitators** to explore what would help Ahmed consider change
9. **Avoid presumptive reflections**—discover Ahmed's experience rather than interpreting it
10. **Develop competence in recognizing change talk** and responding strategically to amplify it

---

**Overall Assessment:** This clinician demonstrates foundational MI competencies but falls significantly short of MI excellence. The session prioritizes maintaining alliance over evoking motivation. With focused practice on core MI skills—particularly evocation and change talk cultivation—this clinician can significantly improve effectiveness in helping clients move toward change.
