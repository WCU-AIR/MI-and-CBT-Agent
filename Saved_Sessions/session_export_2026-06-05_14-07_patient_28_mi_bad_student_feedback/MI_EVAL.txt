## Overall Session Summary

### Key Findings

**This session represents a fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles across nearly every domain.** The psychiatrist operates from a directive, authoritarian, expert-driven model that is antithetical to MI spirit. The approach systematically:

- **Judges and invalidates** the client's emotional experience
- **Suppresses emerging change talk** rather than cultivating it
- **Coerces rather than collaborates**
- **Imposes solutions** rather than evoking the client's own motivation
- **Undermines autonomy** through repeated use of coercive language

### Critical Deficiencies

1. **Complete absence of acceptance and empathy** — Client's fear, desire to feel normal, and need for autonomy are framed as obstacles rather than understandable human experiences.

2. **Systematic undermining of autonomy** — Coercive language ("you must," "you have to," "this isn't optional") removes choice and communicates conditional regard.

3. **Active suppression of change talk** — Rather than cultivating Daniela's emerging motivation, the psychiatrist crushes it through confrontation and pressure.

4. **Authoritarian rather than collaborative stance** — Solutions are imposed; client's requests for partnership are rejected; client's expertise about her own readiness is dismissed.

### Impact on Client Engagement

**This session is likely to decrease Daniela's intrinsic motivation for change and increase her resistance.** Research demonstrates that confrontational, directive approaches with adolescents typically result in:
- Increased sustain talk and arguments against change
- Decreased perceived autonomy and self-efficacy
- Reduced therapeutic alliance
- Higher dropout rates
- Worse health outcomes

Daniela is already signaling disengagement: "I'm not ready," "Forcing me isn't going to make it easier." These are clear signals that the current approach is counterproductive.

---

### Recommendations for Improvement

1. **Shift from expert-directive to collaborative-evocative stance** — Reframe role from "expert who tells client what to do" to "collaborative partner who helps client discover her own reasons for change."

2. **Develop genuine acceptance and empathy** — Recognize adolescent developmental needs (autonomy, peer relationships, identity) as legitimate, not obstacles.

3. **Eliminate coercive language** — Replace "you must," "you have to," "this isn't optional" with open-ended questions that invite client's perspective.

4. **Recognize and amplify change talk** — When client produces change talk, pause and reflect it back with genuine interest to strengthen her motivation.

5. **Respect autonomy and readiness** — Stop pressuring client to commit on psychiatrist's timeline; instead explore readiness and collaborate on manageable timeline.

6. **Use double-sided reflections** — Acknowledge both sides of ambivalence without pushing toward change to reduce defensiveness.

7. **Connect disclosure to medication adherence through client's values** — Explore how peer support might help her manage medication better, not as separate compliance demand.

8. **Seek formal MI training** — Attend MINT-certified workshop and engage in monthly MI supervision to develop genuine competency.

9. **Shift conceptualization of resistance** — Recognize client's "resistance" as feedback that current approach isn't working; use it as information to adjust strategy.

10. **Develop adolescent-specific MI skills** — Tailor approach to adolescent developmental sensitivity to autonomy threats and peer relationships.

**Overall Session Score: 1.2/5**

This session demonstrates **fundamental misalignment with Motivational Interviewing principles** and represents a directive, authoritarian approach that is **likely to harm Daniela's engagement and decrease her likelihood of sustained behavior change.** The psychiatrist requires **fundamental retraining in MI principles** with particular focus on shifting from expert-directive to collaborative-evocative stance, developing genuine acceptance and empathy, and respecting client autonomy. **Formal MI training and supervision are strongly recommended.**
