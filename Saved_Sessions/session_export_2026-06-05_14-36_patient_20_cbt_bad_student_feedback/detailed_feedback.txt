
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

No agenda was established at any point in this session. The therapist opened with casual social conversation ("Hey there! Good to see you. How's your day been so far?") and never transitioned to collaborative goal-setting. There is no evidence that the therapist and patient discussed what they would work on during the session, what problems were most pressing, or how time would be allocated. The session drifted organically from topic to topic (tiredness → work worries → parenting guilt → social anxiety → walking outside) without any explicit prioritization or structure. 

A proper CBT agenda would have included: (1) reviewing the patient's current presenting problems (depressed mood, low energy, hypersomnia, hyperphagia, suicidal ideation), (2) collaboratively identifying 2-3 target problems for the session, (3) establishing time allocation, and (4) checking whether the patient agreed with the priorities. Instead, the therapist passively followed the patient's associations without steering toward specific, measurable treatment goals.

This is particularly critical given Pamela's bipolar II diagnosis with moderate depression and mild-to-moderate suicidal ideation. Without a structured agenda, suicide risk assessment was never systematically addressed, and no treatment strategy was established to address her core problems.

**Summary:**

No agenda was set. The session lacked structure, prioritization, or collaborative goal-setting—fundamental CBT requirements that establish the foundation for all subsequent work.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made minimal attempt to elicit or respond to patient feedback throughout the session. There is one instance where the therapist asks, "Have you had any moments recently where you felt even a little bit better?" but this is a content question, not feedback elicitation. ### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated surface-level understanding of explicit statements but repeatedly missed the deeper clinical picture and failed to grasp the patient's internal experience. 

**What was understood:** The therapist accurately reflected that Pamela feels tired, watches TV, eats more, worries about work performance, feels guilty about parenting, and experiences social anxiety. These are explicit, surface-level observations.

**What was missed—critically:**

1. **Depressive symptomatology:** The patient explicitly stated hypersomnia ("I do sleep a lot—sometimes too much"), anergia ("the tiredness doesn't go away"), anhedonia (activities don't provide relief), and guilt ("I'm failing as a mom"). The therapist's response—"rest is important, and it's okay to take it easy sometimes"—fundamentally misunderstands that excessive sleep is a *symptom*, not a solution. Validating oversleeping as acceptable self-care is contraindicated in depression treatment.

2. **Suicidal ideation:** The case profile explicitly notes "mild to moderate suicidal ideation without plan." The patient never mentions this in the session, but a competent therapist conducting an intake or early session would systematically assess suicide risk. The therapist never asked about suicidal thoughts, intent, or plan—a critical omission for a patient with this diagnosis.

3. **Cognitive distortions:** The patient articulated core depressive cognitions: "Maybe they're true. Maybe I'm just not good enough" and "It feels like I'm disappointing everyone." These are central automatic thoughts amenable to cognitive work. The therapist acknowledged them superficially ("I hear you") but never explored them, challenged them, or helped the patient examine evidence—the foundation of cognitive therapy.

4. **Behavioral activation barriers:** The patient stated, "It feels so hard, and I worry that it won't change how I feel anyway. What if I just end up feeling worse?" This is anticipatory anxiety and hopelessness—core depressive cognitions blocking behavioral change. The therapist's response ("Maybe try something simple like that") bypassed this barrier entirely.

5. **The patient's internal model:** Pamela is expressing learned helplessness ("no matter what I tell myself, those thoughts don't go away") and hopelessness ("It'll get better eventually" is met with doubt). The therapist's repeated reassurances ("It'll get better, I promise") do not address the patient's actual belief system or the mechanisms maintaining her depression.

**Summary:**

The therapist grasped explicit content but missed critical clinical features: depressive cognitions, suicidal risk, behavioral activation barriers, and the patient's hopelessness. Understanding remained superficial and did not reflect genuine empathic grasp of Pamela's internal experience.

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist displayed warmth and non-hostility but demonstrated significant interpersonal problems that undermined therapeutic effectiveness. The approach was well-intentioned but ultimately counterproductive.

**Problematic elements:**

1. **Self-disclosure dominance:** The therapist repeatedly inserted personal anecdotes ("I remember one time I felt totally wiped out," "I once totally flubbed a presentation," "I remember when I was around my niece," "I once went for a walk even when I felt awful"). While some therapist self-disclosure can normalize experiences, this pattern is excessive and shifts focus from the patient to the therapist. Each anecdote consumed session time and implicitly suggested the therapist's experience was comparable to Pamela's clinical depression with suicidal ideation—a false equivalence that minimizes her suffering.

2. **Invalidation through normalization:** Statements like "everyone goes through tough patches like this" and "it's normal to feel that way sometimes" minimize Pamela's bipolar II disorder and moderate depression. Her experience is not a "tough patch"—it is a clinical disorder requiring specific treatment. This normalization can increase hopelessness ("If everyone feels this way, why can't I just snap out of it?").

3. **Cheerleading without substance:** "You're doing great just by thinking about it! Keep your head up" and "You've got this!" are generic encouragements disconnected from actual therapeutic work. They feel hollow to a patient experiencing moderate depression and suicidal ideation.

4. **Lack of genuine curiosity:** The therapist asks questions but rarely follows up with depth. When Pamela says, "It feels like I'm failing as a mom, and that makes everything worse," the therapist responds with a personal anecdote rather than exploring this guilt, its origins, or its accuracy—a missed opportunity for empathic understanding.

5. **Professional boundary confusion:** The therapist's repeated personal sharing blurs the boundary between peer support and professional treatment. A patient in moderate depression may feel obligated to care for the therapist's emotional experience or may question whether the therapist truly understands clinical depression versus normal stress.

**What was adequate:** The therapist was not hostile, demeaning, or dismissive. Tone was warm and the therapist did not shame the patient. However, warmth alone does not constitute effective interpersonal effectiveness in CBT.

**Summary:**

Interpersonal approach was warm but problematic. Excessive self-disclosure, normalization of clinical symptoms, and generic cheerleading undermined authenticity and created distance from genuine empathic understanding. The therapist appeared more focused on reassurance than on understanding Pamela's internal experience.

### 5. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration was minimal and largely absent. The therapist functioned as an advice-giver rather than a collaborative partner, and the patient was positioned as a passive recipient of suggestions rather than an active agent in treatment.

**Evidence of lack of collaboration:**

1. **Unilateral recommendations:** The therapist repeatedly offered suggestions without soliciting the patient's input: "Maybe you could try something simple like that?" "Maybe try to think positive thoughts," "Have you been drinking enough water lately?" "Maybe try to get to bed a bit earlier?" "Maybe you could try to do something small that you enjoy," "Have you tried going outside for a short walk?" "Maybe you could try just stepping outside for a minute or two."

   These are framed as questions but function as directives. A collaborative approach would involve: "What have you already tried?" "What feels most manageable to you right now?" "What barriers do you anticipate?" "What would you be willing to experiment with?" "How could we test whether this would help?"

2. **No shared decision-making:** The therapist never discussed treatment options, asked about the patient's preferences, or invited the patient to help design interventions. The patient is told what to do, not invited to co-create solutions.

3. **Patient passivity:** Notice the patient's language: "I'll try," "I'll do my best," "Thanks for saying that." The patient is compliant but not engaged as a partner. There is no evidence the patient feels ownership of the treatment plan or believes she is working *with* the therapist.

4. **No collaborative empiricism:** CBT's hallmark is collaborative empiricism—therapist and patient working together to test beliefs and gather evidence. This session contains zero instances of this. The therapist never said, "Let's test whether that thought is true," "What evidence do we have?" "How could we find out?" or "What would change your mind?"

5. **Therapist as expert, patient as passive:** The structure is expert-to-novice rather than team-based. The therapist knows what's best; the patient should follow recommendations.

**Summary:**

Collaboration was essentially absent. The therapist functioned as an advice-giver; the patient as a passive recipient. No shared decision-making, no collaborative empiricism, no invitation for the patient to actively shape treatment. This violates a core CBT principle.

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time

## Recommendations

### 1. **URGENT: Implement Suicide Risk Assessment and Safety Planning**
- **Action:** In the next session, conduct a systematic suicide risk assessment using a validated tool (e.g., Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale). Assess frequency, intensity, and specificity of suicidal ideation; presence of plan and intent; access to means; protective factors; and prior attempts.
- **Example:** "I want to check in about something important. You mentioned in your intake that you've had thoughts of death. Can you tell me more about those thoughts? How often do you have them? Do you have any thoughts about harming yourself? Do you have a plan? What's kept you safe so far?"
- **Practice:** Review suicide assessment protocols. Practice the assessment with a supervisor or peer. Develop a safety planning template that includes crisis resources, coping strategies, and emergency contacts.

### 2. **Establish a Collaborative Agenda and Treatment Conceptualization**
- **Action:** At the start of the next session, explicitly set a collaborative agenda. Say: "I'd like to understand what's most important to work on. You mentioned feeling depressed, struggling with work stress, and having difficulty with energy. Which of these feels most pressing right now? What would be most helpful to focus on today?" Prioritize 2-3 targets. Then develop a case conceptualization: "It sounds like your depression involves a cycle where low mood leads you to withdraw and avoid activities, which reduces opportunities for positive experiences, which deepens your mood. We'll work to break this cycle by gradually increasing activities, examining thoughts that get in the way, and monitoring your mood. Does that make sense?"
- **Example:** "Based on what you've told me, here's how I understand what's happening: [brief conceptualization]. Our treatment plan will involve: (1) assessing and managing suicide risk, (2) gradually increasing activities to improve mood, (3) examining and challenging thoughts that maintain depression, and (4) monitoring your mood to catch early warning signs. We'll also coordinate with your psychiatrist about medication. Does this approach feel right to you? What would you add?"
- **Practice:** Develop case formulations for several patients. Practice articulating them clearly and collaboratively. Ensure the formulation is based on CBT theory (thoughts-behaviors-emotions-physiology interactions).

### 3. **Implement Systematic Behavioral Activation with Specificity and Monitoring**
- **Action:** Replace vague suggestions ("go outside") with structured behavioral activation. Identify valued activities, schedule them with specific times/durations, and monitor mood before/after. Use an activity schedule or behavioral log.
- **Example:** "Let's identify activities that are important to you or that you used to enjoy. [Identify 3-5 activities.] Now, let's pick one small activity you could do this week. You mentioned your kids make you smile. Could you spend 15 minutes with them doing something specific—maybe playing a game or reading together—on Tuesday at 6 PM? Before you do it, rate your mood 0-10. After, rate it again. We'll see if the activity affects your mood. Does that feel doable?"
- **Practice:** Learn behavioral activation protocols. Practice creating specific, scheduled activities with clear monitoring instructions. Role-play with a supervisor.

### 4. **Teach Systematic Cognitive Restructuring Rather Than Generic "Positive Thinking"**
- **Action:** When the patient identifies a depressive thought (e.g., "I'm not good enough"), use structured cognitive work: (1) identify the thought, (2) examine evidence for and against it, (3) develop a realistic alternative, (4) test it through behavioral experiments.
- **Example:** "You mentioned thinking 'Maybe I'm just not good enough.' Let's examine that thought. What evidence do you have that you're not good enough? [Listen.] What evidence do you have that you ARE good enough? [Listen.] What would someone who cares about you say? What would you tell a friend in this situation? Based on all this, what's a more realistic thought? Let's test it: this week, notice moments when you do something competently, and we'll see if that changes your view."
- **Practice:** Learn cognitive restructuring protocols. Practice identifying automatic thoughts, examining evidence, and developing alternatives. Use thought records. Role-play with a supervisor.

### 5. **Address Hypersomnia as a Symptom and Behavioral Target, Not as Self-Care**
- **Action:** Recognize that excessive sleep maintains depression. Develop a sleep intervention: establish a consistent wake time, gradually reduce time in bed, schedule morning activities, and monitor mood/energy.
- **Example:** "I notice you're sleeping a lot—sometimes too much. While rest is important, sleeping too much can actually make depression worse because it reduces activity and opportunities for positive experiences. Let's work on gradually adjusting your sleep. What time are you currently waking up? [Listen.] What if we aimed to wake up 15 minutes earlier this week and do something specific—maybe have coffee and read the news? We'll see if that affects your mood and energy. Does that feel manageable?"
- **Practice:** Learn sleep intervention protocols for depression. Understand the difference between healthy sleep and depressive hypersomnia. Practice explaining this to patients.

### 6. **Eliminate Excessive Therapist Self-Disclosure; Maintain Professional Boundaries**
- **Action:** Limit personal anecdotes to rare, brief moments that directly normalize a patient's experience. Focus on understanding the patient's experience rather than sharing your own. If you share, immediately redirect to the patient.
- **Example:** Instead of "I remember one time I felt totally wiped out and just wanted to hide under my blanket all day," say: "Many people with depression describe feeling that way. Tell me more about what that's like for you. When you feel that way, what happens?"
- **Practice:** Record yourself in sessions and count instances of self-disclosure. Aim for zero to one brief, relevant anecdote per session. Practice redirecting to the patient after any self-disclosure.

### 7. **Implement Systematic Feedback Elicitation Throughout Sessions**
- **Action:** After each intervention or suggestion, ask for feedback: "Does this make sense?" "How are you feeling about this?" "Does this feel doable?" "What are your thoughts?" At the end of the session, ask: "What was most helpful today?" "What would you like to focus on next time?" "Do you have any concerns about what we discussed?"
- **Example:** After suggesting behavioral activation, say: "I'm suggesting we start with small activities because isolation and avoidance maintain depression. ### 8. **Use Socratic Questioning (Guided Discovery) Rather Than Advice-Giving**

When you tell yourself those things, what happens? What do you think would need to change for you to believe something different? How could we find out?"

- **Example:** "You mentioned worrying about what people think when you're outside. What specifically do you worry they'll think? [Listen.] What's the worst thing that could happen? [Listen.] Has that happened before? [Listen.] Let's test this worry. This week, could you go outside for 5 minutes and notice what people actually do or say? We'll see if your worry comes true.
