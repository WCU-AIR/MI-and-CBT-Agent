
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS)
This document outlines a structured framework for assessing therapist competence in Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT). 
It is used for training, supervision, and evaluation. Each item is scored on a 0–6 scale:

0 = Poor
2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Implement Explicit Safety Assessment and Planning (URGENT)**
- **Action:** In the next session, conduct a comprehensive suicide risk assessment including current ideation, intent, plan, access to means, and protective factors. Develop a written safety plan with the patient that includes: (a) warning signs of increased risk, (b) internal coping strategies, (c) people and social settings that provide distraction, (d) people to ask for help, (e) professional contacts and crisis lines, (f) ways to make the environment safer.
- **Example:** "I want to make sure we have a clear plan in place if you start having thoughts of harming yourself. Let's identify what warning signs might tell you that things are getting worse, and what you can do to keep yourself safe. I'll also give you my crisis number and the crisis line number so you know exactly who to call."
- **Practice:** Review safety planning protocols in CBT supervision; practice conducting risk assessments with role-plays; ensure all patients with recent suicidal behavior have documented safety plans.

### 2. **Develop a Comprehensive Case Conceptualization Linking Symptoms to Maintaining Factors**
- **Action:** Before the next session, write out a detailed case conceptualization that identifies: (a) the patient's presenting problems (depressed mood, sleep deprivation, rumination, low motivation), (b) precipitating factors (breakup), (c) maintaining factors (rumination cycles, sleep deprivation, avoidance, loss of reinforcement, possible mixed features), (d) predisposing factors (family history, prior episodes). Use this to guide treatment planning.
- **Example:** "Helena's depression appears to be maintained by a cycle of rumination about the breakup, which leads to sleep deprivation, which worsens mood and cognitive function, which increases rumination. Additionally, her avoidance of work and social engagement reduces positive reinforcement, further maintaining low mood. The sleep deprivation may also be exacerbating mixed features (racing thoughts, agitation), which could benefit from mood stabilization and behavioral sleep intervention."
- **Practice:** Develop case conceptualizations for all patients; discuss with supervisor; use conceptualization to guide session planning and intervention selection.

### 3. **Implement Behavioral Activation as a Primary Intervention**
- **Action:** In the next session, assess current activity level and schedule valued activities. Use the Behavioral Activation for Depression Scale (BADS) or similar tool to measure baseline. Collaboratively identify 3-5 activities that are meaningful to the patient and schedule them for specific times during the week. Start with small, achievable activities and gradually increase.
- **Example:** "I notice you mentioned feeling unmotivated and disconnected. One of the most effective things we can do for depression is to gradually increase activities that matter to you, even when you don't feel like it. Let's identify some activities—maybe time with friends, exercise, hobbies, or work on something meaningful—and schedule them into your week. We'll track how you feel before and after each activity."
- **Practice:** Learn the behavioral activation protocol; practice scheduling activities with patients; use activity logs to track and reinforce progress.

### 4. **Address Sleep Deprivation with Behavioral Sleep Intervention**
- **Action:** Conduct a detailed sleep assessment: sleep onset time, wake time, total sleep duration, sleep quality, daytime fatigue, relationship between sleep and mood. Introduce sleep hygiene and behavioral sleep interventions: consistent sleep schedule, stimulus control (bed only for sleep), sleep restriction if appropriate, relaxation techniques, limiting nocturnal journaling/phone use.
- **Example:** "Your sleep is really important for your mood and your ability to think clearly. Right now you're getting 2-3 hours, which is making everything harder. Let's work on a sleep plan. First, we'll set a consistent bedtime and wake time. Second, we'll keep your bed for sleep only—no journaling in bed. Third, we'll find a way to manage the racing thoughts that keep you awake—maybe through relaxation or a worry time earlier in the evening."
- **Practice:** Learn sleep hygiene and behavioral sleep intervention protocols; assess sleep in every session; track sleep as a key outcome measure.

### 5. **Introduce Rumination Management Techniques**
- **Action:** Identify rumination as a maintaining factor. Teach the patient to recognize rumination (repetitive thinking about the past or hypotheticals that doesn't lead to problem-solving) and distinguish it from productive thinking. Introduce techniques: (a) time-limited worry/journaling (set a specific time to process thoughts, then move on), (b) behavioral activation to interrupt rumination, (c) mindfulness or acceptance techniques to observe thoughts without engaging, (d) problem-solving for solvable issues.
- **Example:** "I notice you're spending a lot of time thinking about the breakup and replaying what happened. This kind of repetitive thinking, called rumination, actually keeps depression going. Let's try something different: we'll set aside 20 minutes in the evening for journaling about your feelings, and then we'll move on to other activities. Outside that time, when the thoughts come up, we'll notice them but not get pulled into them."
- **Practice:** Learn rumination management protocols; teach patients to distinguish rumination from problem-solving; practice mindfulness and acceptance techniques.

### 6. **Provide Written, Structured Homework with Clear Instructions and Templates**
- **Action:** For all homework assignments, provide written materials including: (a) a clear description of the task, (b) step-by-step instructions, (c) a template or worksheet if applicable, (d) examples of completed work, (e) troubleshooting strategies for common barriers, (f) clear success criteria.
- **Example:** Provide a written thought record with columns for: Situation, Automatic Thought, Evidence For, Evidence Against, Balanced Thought, Mood Before/After. Include an example completed by the therapist. Write: "This week, please complete one thought record when you notice sadness or anxiety. Use the attached template. Here's an example of how to fill it out. If you have trouble remembering to do it, set a phone reminder for 5 PM each day."
- **Practice:** Develop a library of homework templates and written instructions; review homework completion rates; adjust instructions based on patient feedback.

### 7. **Systematically Review Prior Homework and Between-Session Progress**
- **Action:** At the beginning of each session, explicitly review homework from the previous session: (a) Did the patient complete it? (b) What did they learn? (c) What barriers did they encounter? (d) How did it affect their mood or symptoms? Use this information to adjust the treatment plan.
- **Example:** "Before we start today, let's talk about the thought records you were going to do. Did you complete any? What did you notice? Were there any times when it was hard to remember or hard to do? Let's problem-solve any barriers so it's easier this week."
- **Practice:** Make homework review a standard part of session opening; track homework completion rates; problem-solve barriers collaboratively.

### 8. **Deepen Cognitive Restructuring with More Thorough Evidence Examination**
- **Action:** When working with automatic thoughts, spend more time examining evidence. Ask follow-up questions: "What else is evidence for this thought? What evidence contradicts it? How much do you believe this thought now (0-100%)? After considering the evidence, how much do you believe it?" Help the patient develop nuanced, realistic balanced thoughts rather than overly positive ones.
- **Example:** When the patient said "he did leave me, so I must have done something wrong," ask: "Does someone leaving always mean it's because of something the other person did? Can you think of times when people end relationships for reasons that have nothing to do with the other person? What do you know about your ex's situation, his feelings, his own issues?"
- **Practice:** Practice deeper evidence examination in supervision; use thought records to slow down and deepen the process; avoid rushing to balanced thoughts.

### 9. **Assess and Address Other Depressive Symptoms Systematically**
- **Action:** Use a structured depression assessment tool (PHQ-9, BDI-II) at baseline and regularly throughout treatment to track: depressed mood, anhedonia, sleep, appetite/weight, energy, concentration, guilt, psychomotor changes, suicidal ideation. Address each symptom with targeted interventions.
