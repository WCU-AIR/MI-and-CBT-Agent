### Empathy

Fails to recognize that low importance rating (4-5/10) and repeated statements about "just getting through this" signal external motivation and disengagement. Psychiatrist conflates presenting problem (community service compliance) with assumed problem (drinking), creating confusion and misalignment from start. Eventually acknowledges court mandate as main reason for visit, but then immediately shifts focus to stress management and coping strategies—topics patient has not identified as priorities and has explicitly minimized in importance. ### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session is fundamentally non-collaborative. Psychiatrist adopts expert-driven, hierarchical stance throughout, making decisions about what patient should do rather than inviting him into problem-solving. Makes assumptions without verification. Uses repeated prescriptive language: "Maybe you should think," "You should still consider," "You might want to start small." Dismisses patient's input when patient rates stress management importance as 4-5/10—psychiatrist responds by prescribing exercise anyway, overriding patient's own assessment. Unilateral summarization presented as fact rather than collaboratively constructed. Never asks "What do you think might help?" or solicits patient's ideas. Patient's wisdom and resources are never accessed.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is virtually absent. Psychiatrist repeatedly pressures patient toward predetermined solutions while minimizing his right to choose. Overrides patient's stated preferences (when patient says talking "doesn't really sound like my thing," psychiatrist responds with "But maybe you should still consider"). Uses prescriptive language ("should," "maybe you should") that frames suggestions as obligations rather than choices. Dismisses low importance ratings as invalid feedback about priorities. Never explicitly affirms patient's right to choose or ownership of change process. No language reinforcing that "this is entirely up to you." Session framed around compliance with external demands rather than patient's own values or self-determined goals.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out patient's own motivations, values, and reasons for change—is almost entirely absent. Psychiatrist operates in "telling" mode rather than "asking" mode. Never explores intrinsic motivation ("What matters to you about staying out of trouble?"). No values exploration or attempt to understand what patient cares about or envisions for himself. Uses prescriptive problem-solving (exercise, hobbies, walking) rather than asking what patient thinks might help. Fails to amplify change talk: when patient says "I just don't want to mess it up or anything," psychiatrist does not explore this commitment language. When patient expresses doubt ("I don't know if it'd really help"), psychiatrist does not explore confidence or ability. Session reads as series of clinician directives with minimal curiosity about patient's perspective, resources, or motivations.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership is fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist maintains expert-authority stance throughout, positioning himself as the knower and patient as recipient of guidance. Violates core MI principle that expertise and wisdom about change reside within the client. Adopts expert positioning through diagnostic questions, assumptions, and prescriptions. Sets unilateral agenda (stress, exercise, coping strategies) without collaborative agreement. Controls conversation flow, asks most questions, makes final determinations about plan. When patient offers information (stress at home, keeping to himself), psychiatrist acknowledges but does not build on it collaboratively. No sense that patient's ideas are shaping session. True power-sharing and collaborative partnership are absent.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Change talk is neither evoked nor cultivated. Psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to strengthen patient's own language about change. When patient says "I just don't want to mess it up or anything" (desire-based change talk), psychiatrist does not explore: "What would it look like to stay on track?" When patient states "I'm still focused on getting the hours done" (commitment language), psychiatrist does not amplify. When patient says "Yeah, okay. I guess I could try a short walk" (weak commitment), psychiatrist accepts vague agreement without strengthening. Psychiatrist accepts patient's sustain talk without challenge ("I don't think it'd make much difference," "It's not super important right now"). Prescribes solutions rather than evoking patient's own reasons for change. Asks closed questions eliciting yes/no responses rather than open questions drawing out change talk. Fails to use strategic questioning to strengthen commitment language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Sustain talk is present throughout session and never skillfully addressed. Patient expresses: minimization ("I don't think that's the big thing here"), lack of prior effort ("I haven't really tried to cut down"), hopelessness ("I don't really have a plan to avoid stuff; it's not that simple"), resistance ("talking about it doesn't really sound like my thing"), doubt ("I don't think it'd make much difference for me"), skepticism ("I don't really see how doing stuff like that would change much"), and low importance ("It's not super important right now"). Psychiatrist ignores sustain talk rather than exploring it. Reinforces it by prescribing solutions patient has already expressed doubt about, implicitly validating skepticism. Fails to use double-sided reflection to acknowledge both sides and invite exploration. Fails to reframe doubt as valid concern about efficacy. Never softens sustain talk through strategic responses: double-sided reflections, reframing, exploring ambivalence, or shifting focus.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.1/5**

**Assessment:** This session represents a significant departure from Motivational Interviewing principles and practice. The psychiatrist operates almost entirely in an expert-driven, directive mode rather than an evocative, collaborative mode. While the clinician maintains a respectful, non-hostile tone and does eventually acknowledge the court mandate as the main concern, these basic elements do not constitute MI practice.

**Core Deficiencies:**
- Assumption-making without exploration (assumes drinking is primary issue despite patient's explicit statement)
- Prescriptive rather than evocative approach (tells patient what to do rather than asking what patient thinks)
- Dismissal of patient autonomy and preferences (overrides low importance ratings and stated preferences)
- Failure to explore ambivalence (does not address external vs. internal motivation)
- No evocation of change talk (misses opportunities to draw out and amplify patient's own motivations)
- Non-collaborative power dynamic (maintains expert-authority stance throughout)

**Impact on Patient Engagement:** This session is likely to decrease intrinsic motivation and increase resistance. Patient's responses become increasingly disengaged ("Yeah, okay," "I guess," "That sounds fine"), indicating withdrawal rather than engagement. Psychiatrist has created situation where patient can easily disengage, and foundation for genuine motivation has not been established.

**Recommendation:** Psychiatrist requires formal MI training, including didactic instruction, skills practice, and supervised feedback. Without significant changes to approach and technique, clinician will continue to operate in expert-driven mode that undermines patient autonomy, evocation, and intrinsic motivation for change.
