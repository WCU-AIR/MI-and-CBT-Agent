# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance throughout. Validates Julian's uncertainty and normalizes ambivalence appropriately. However, acceptance lacks depth—minimal explicit affirmation of Julian's intrinsic worth independent of task completion, limited normalization of the court-mandated experience, and insufficient warmth to create genuine human connection. Could strengthen by acknowledging difficulty of Julian's position and affirming his courage in showing up.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates solid reflective listening with several complex reflections capturing Julian's perspective (e.g., "You're weighing the desire for clarity against the fear of being seen as not managing well"). Accurately reflects ambivalence and internal conflict. However, empathy lacks depth in exploring underlying emotions such as shame, fear, and resentment. Missed opportunities to explore emotional weight of court-mandated experience and deeper fears beneath surface-level hesitations.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Maintains general focus on community service logistics throughout session. However, direction is narrow and primarily task-focused rather than strategically oriented toward meaningful change. Lacks exploration of deeper questions: Why does this matter to Julian? What does completing this represent? What are his broader life goals? Direction becomes prescriptive without exploring Julian's autonomy and choice within constraints of mandate.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates partial collaboration, particularly when Julian suggests writing down talking points. However, collaboration is inconsistent and often one-directional. Psychiatrist frequently offers solutions or guides toward predetermined answers rather than co-constructing them with Julian. Power dynamic remains tilted toward clinician as expert. True collaboration would involve more genuine co-creation of plans and fuller exploration of Julian's own thinking before offering suggestions.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Uses autonomy-supportive language ("whenever you feel ready," "what do you think") and respects Julian's right to choose timing and approach. However, autonomy support is limited by subtle prescription embedded in questions, lack of explicit acknowledgment of external pressure from court mandate, and minimal exploration of Julian's intrinsic values or preferences. Does not address fundamental autonomy issue: Julian is externally motivated by legal consequences, not intrinsic motivation.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Significant weakness. Psychiatrist focuses almost entirely on "how to complete hours" rather than "why this matters" or "what Julian wants for himself." Minimal effort to draw out Julian's own motivations, values, reasons for change, or internal resources. One moment of evocation occurs when asking about strengths, and Julian offers genuine change talk about resilience. However, clinician does not amplify or deepen this insight. Session is fundamentally advice-giving and problem-solving focused rather than evocation-focused. Never explores Julian's ambivalence about court mandate itself.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Major deficiency. While psychiatrist maintains respectful tone and avoids overt authoritarianism, clinician consistently positions themselves as expert who knows what Julian should do. Frequently interprets Julian's experience before inviting input. Guides toward predetermined solutions through questions rather than exploring Julian's own ideas. Functions as problem-solver rather than equal partner. No explicit acknowledgment of Julian's expertise about himself. Session reads as: Psychiatrist asks → Julian responds → Psychiatrist validates and guides → Julian agrees. This is helper-helped dynamic, not true partnership.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk is present but minimally cultivated. Julian offers resilience talk ("I've had to deal with stuff like this before... usually it's not as bad as I think") and ability talk, but psychiatrist validates without deepening. Does not strategically evoke, amplify, or develop change talk through follow-up questions. No systematic effort to draw out Julian's own reasons, ability, or commitment to change. Lacks strategic questioning designed to naturally elicit and strengthen change talk. Does not address sustain talk deeply or explore whether hesitations represent genuine barriers or manageable discomfort.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates moderate skill in addressing Julian's hesitations through reframing ("seeing case worker as support, not judge"), normalizing discomfort, and drawing on past success. However, approach is more validating than strategically redirecting. Does not fully explore whether hesitations are genuine barriers or manageable discomfort. Limited use of double-sided reflections that would acknowledge both barrier and reason for moving forward. Does not engage with fundamental sustain talk: Julian's external motivation and potential lack of personal investment in completing hours.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Session Score: 3.0/5 – Basic Supportive Counseling with Significant MI Gaps**

### Strengths
- Consistent validation and empathic tone throughout
- Solid reflective listening with complex reflections
- Practical problem-solving focus that reduces immediate anxiety
- Autonomy-supportive language respecting Julian's agency
- Effective use of confidence scaling (0-10 ratings)
- Affirmation of Julian's identified strengths

### Critical Deficiencies
1. **Task-focused rather than change-focused:** Session emphasizes logistics (where to serve, how to track hours) rather than evoking intrinsic motivation or exploring Julian's perspective on his situation
2. **Weak partnership:** Clinician maintains expert authority; limited genuine power-sharing or positioning of Julian as equal partner with expertise about own life
3. **Minimal change talk cultivation:** While Julian offers change talk, psychiatrist does not strategically deepen or amplify it
4. **Unaddressed external motivation:** No exploration of whether Julian has personal investment in moving forward or what intrinsic motivation might exist
5. **Advice-giving disguised as collaboration:** Clinician guides Julian toward predetermined solutions through questions rather than true co-construction

### Impact on Client Engagement
**Likelihood of sustained engagement: Moderate to Low.** While Julian leaves with concrete plan and reduced immediate anxiety, session does not address fundamental ambivalence or build intrinsic motivation. Without exploration of personal values and genuine partnership, Julian is unlikely to develop investment in process. If obstacles arise, he may abandon plan due to lack of intrinsic motivation.

### Key Recommendations for Improvement
1. Shift from task-focused to change-focused interviewing—explore Julian's deeper perspective, values, and intrinsic motivation
2. Systematically evoke and amplify change talk through follow-up questions rather than simple validation
3. Establish true partnership by sharing power genuinely and inviting Julian's ideas first
4. Explore and address external motivation directly; help identify intrinsic reasons for change
5. Use double-sided reflections to soften sustain talk while gently emphasizing change side
6. Deepen empathy by exploring underlying emotions (shame, fear, resentment) beneath surface-level hesitations
7. Explicitly affirm Julian's worth and acknowledge difficulty of court-mandated situation
8. Ask open-ended questions before offering interpretations or framing problems

---

**Overall Assessment:** This session represents a **missed opportunity** to help Julian move from external motivation (court mandate) toward intrinsic motivation (personal investment in change). With focused practice on recommendations above, this psychiatrist can develop stronger MI skills and significantly increase likelihood of evoking genuine client motivation for change.
