
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

No agenda was established at any point during either session. The therapist did not collaboratively identify target problems, prioritize concerns, or establish session goals with Ethan. In a first session with a 9-year-old presenting with separation anxiety disorder and provisional tic disorder, establishing a clear agenda is foundational—particularly given the complexity of his presentation (academic decline, tics, ritualistic behaviors, sleep disturbance, and anxiety about parental harm).

The therapist opened with a generic social greeting ("How's your day been so far?") but never transitioned into structured agenda-setting. When Ethan spontaneously mentioned school difficulties and fidgetiness, the therapist responded with personal anecdotes rather than exploring what Ethan wanted to work on. No time allocation was discussed, no priorities were negotiated, and no explicit session structure was communicated. In the second session, the therapist again opened with "How's everything going with you?" without establishing what would be addressed that day.

For a child with anxiety and tics, agenda-setting is clinically critical because it: (1) reduces anxiety by providing predictability and structure, (2) helps the child feel heard and respected, (3) allows the therapist to focus on the most impairing symptoms, and (4) models collaborative problem-solving. The complete absence of this foundational skill undermines the entire therapeutic process.

**Summary:**

No agenda was set in either session. The therapist failed to collaboratively identify, prioritize, or structure work around Ethan's presenting problems, representing a fundamental breach of CBT protocol.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish a Structured Agenda and Session Framework**
- **Action:** At the beginning of each session, collaboratively set a specific agenda with Ethan and his parents. Identify 2-3 target problems, prioritize them, and allocate time accordingly. Use a visual agenda (written on whiteboard or paper) so Ethan can see the structure.
- **Example:** "Today, I want to make sure we talk about three things: (1) what's been happening with your worries about your parents, (2) how your tics have been, and (3) what we can practice this week. Does that sound good? Is there anything else you want to talk about?"
- **Practice:** Practice agenda-setting with a supervisor or peer before the next session. Role-play with a colleague playing a 9-year-old to develop comfort with this skill.

### 2. **Conduct Systematic Functional Assessment**
- **Action:** Use structured questions to understand the triggers, frequency, intensity, and consequences of Ethan's anxiety and tics. Create a clear timeline of symptom onset. Identify what maintains the symptoms (avoidance, accommodation, reassurance-seeking).
- **Example:** "When you worry about your parents, what exactly are you worried might happen? How often does this worry come up during the day? What do you do when you feel worried? Does it help? What happens if you try NOT to do that?"
- **Practice:** Develop a functional assessment template for anxiety and tics. Practice asking open-ended questions that elicit specific, behavioral information rather than vague responses.

### 3. **Develop and Communicate a Clear Case Conceptualization**
- **Action:** Explicitly explain to Ethan and his parents how his anxiety and tics are related and what maintains them. Use simple language and visual aids (e.g., a diagram showing how worry → avoidance → more worry).
- **Example:** "Ethan, your brain is trying to protect you by worrying about your parents. When you worry, you feel scared, so you stay close to them or go to their room at night. That feels better right away, but it teaches your brain that there IS something to be scared of. So the worry gets stronger. We're going to help your brain learn that you and your parents are safe even when you're apart."
- **Practice:** Write out a one-paragraph case formulation for Ethan before the next session. Practice explaining it in simple, concrete language.

### 4. **Eliminate Excessive Self-Disclosure and Establish Professional Boundaries**
- **Action:** Stop sharing personal anecdotes, experiences, and stories. Redirect focus to Ethan's experience. If tempted to share a personal story, ask yourself: "Does this serve Ethan's treatment goals, or does it serve my need to relate?" If the latter, don't share it.
- **Example:** Instead of "I remember when I was a kid, I used to worry about my dog," say: "That sounds really scary. Tell me more about what you're worried might happen."

- **Practice:** Develop a list of Socratic questions for anxiety and tics. Practice asking questions in supervision. Role-play with a colleague to develop comfort with this skill.

### 6. **Develop a Graded Exposure Hierarchy for Separation Anxiety**
- **Action:** Work with Ethan and his parents to create a step-by-step plan for gradually increasing time away from parents. Start with small, manageable steps and progress to more challenging situations. Use behavioral experiments to test anxious predictions.

- **Practice:** Learn the principles of exposure therapy. Develop a sample exposure hierarchy for a child with separation anxiety. Practice explaining the rationale for exposure to a parent who may be resistant.
