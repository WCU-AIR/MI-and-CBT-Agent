### Collaboration

**Comments:** Strong collaborative stance throughout most of session. Opening question immediately establishes partnership and invites client direction. Psychiatrist frequently positions Irina as expert on her own life through questions like "What do you think that voice is trying to tell you?" and "Can you tell me about a time when you successfully made a change?" However, subtle moments exist where clinician moves toward expert stance—particularly when offering strategies with phrasing "Many people find..." which positions clinician as knowledgeable authority rather than curious partner. ### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently respects and affirms Irina's right to choose throughout session. Opening establishes this clearly: "What's most important to you?" Validates both sides of ambivalence without pressuring. Affirms her discovery of own goal rather than prescribing it. Explicitly supports her autonomy regarding pace and feasibility. Closing statement is exemplary: "It's entirely your choice how you move forward, and I'm here to support you however you decide." However, one subtle area where autonomy support could strengthen: framing "Would you be interested in hearing some information about ways people often manage cutting back on alcohol?" presupposes direction rather than asking more neutrally about exploring ideas. Overall, powerfully reinforces autonomy and removes sense of coercion.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist skillfully evokes Irina's own motivations, values, and resources. Early questions invite her to access her own wisdom rather than receiving interpretation. Particularly strong evocative moment when asking about past successes with smoking—Irina spontaneously applies these strategies to current situation, demonstrating excellent evocation of capability and wisdom. Asks about confidence and what would help her stick to plan, inviting her to articulate own motivation and strategies. However, opportunities exist where evocation could be deeper. When offering multiple strategies, this represents telling rather than asking. More evocative approach would ask: "What ideas do you have about how you might manage this?" before offering suggestions. Could have evoked more about values and reasons: "What matters most to you about making this change?" or "How would your life be different if you were drinking one drink instead of two?" These would deepen her own articulation of motivation.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist skillfully evokes and reinforces Irina's change talk throughout session. Affirms her own ideas about change without overstating them. When she expresses confidence at 6, clinician responds by amplifying: "A 6 shows a good amount of confidence and openness to trying...you have a strong motivation to change." Reinforces her connection between past smoking success and future drinking change. Affirms her self-generated plan: "That sounds like a thoughtful and manageable plan." However, opportunities exist to cultivate change talk more strategically. Could ask more directly about reasons: "What are the most important reasons you want to cut back?" or "How would things be better if you were drinking one drink instead of two?" When she expresses confidence at 6, could ask: "What would move you from a 6 to a 7?" to evoke more change talk about strengthening commitment. Could deepen change talk about values: "Tell me more about what it means to you to be present for your son" or "How important is it to feel less guilty?" These would strengthen her own articulation of motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Significant gap in MI technique. Sustain talk emerges multiple times and clinician's handling is inconsistent and sometimes ineffective. When Irina says "Two drinks doesn't seem like a big deal...it feels manageable," psychiatrist reflects this back without strategically redirecting, essentially reinforcing sustain talk. When she expresses concern about deprivation ("I don't want everything I enjoy taken away"), clinician validates without strategically redirecting toward her values or reasons for change. When she mentions difficulty finding time for alternatives, psychiatrist accepts without exploring or evoking her own problem-solving. Most significantly, core sustain talk about alcohol being her "reward" and "the only time I get for myself" is never directly addressed or explored. These powerful statements about alcohol's function represent significant barriers to change that require strategic softening through double-sided reflections, reframing, or evocative questioning. Clinician's approach tends to validate and move forward rather than strategically soften or redirect sustain talk—a critical gap in MI execution.

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall Competency Score: 4.1/5 – Good MI Practice**

### Strengths
- ✓ Exceptional empathic accuracy with sophisticated, double-sided reflections
- ✓ Strong collaborative stance positioning client as expert on her own life
- ✓ Excellent autonomy support with explicit reinforcement of client's right to choose
- ✓ Skillful evocation of resources and past successes (smoking reduction)
- ✓ Consistent reinforcement of change talk and amplification of commitment
- ✓ Warm, respectful tone maintaining unconditional positive regard throughout
- ✓ Client-centered opening and closing with genuine partnership

### Areas for Improvement
- ⚠ **CRITICAL:** Sustain talk management—significant gap in MI technique. Core sustain talk about alcohol's function never explored or addressed
- ⚠ Subtle directional bias presupposing reduction goal rather than remaining fully open to client-generated solutions
- ⚠ Premature strategy offering—presents established wisdom rather than first evoking client's own ideas
- ⚠ Insufficient deepening of change talk through targeted questions about reasons, values, and commitment
- ⚠ Limited exploration of alcohol's function and why it feels essential to client
- ⚠ Acceptance could be deepened with more explicit affirmation of inherent worth independent of choices

### Clinical Impact
The psychiatrist creates a safe, respectful environment where Irina feels heard and valued. Her confidence increases from 6 to 7, and she generates her own plan for change. However, failure to strategically address sustain talk represents significant missed opportunity. Irina's underlying ambivalence—tension between needing stress relief and wanting to be present for her son—is not fully resolved. Without exploring alcohol's function and identifying alternative coping strategies, her commitment to change may be fragile when encountering stress or fatigue. Session is good but not excellent—builds rapport and generates initial commitment without fully leveraging MI's power to evoke intrinsic motivation and resolve ambivalence.

### Recommendation
Focus supervision on: (1) Strategic sustain talk management using double-sided reflections and reframing; (2) Deeper exploration of behavior's function before offering strategies; (3) More targeted evocation of change talk about reasons and values; (4) Maintaining neutral language to avoid subtle directional bias; (5) Using confidence scaling as evocative tool to strengthen commitment.
