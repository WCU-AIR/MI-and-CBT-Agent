# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal unconditional positive regard. While opening is warm, clinician quickly pivots to problem-solving and advice-giving rather than validating Jordan's emotional experience of hopelessness and despair. No affirmation of Jordan's struggle as understandable or normalized. When Jordan expresses vulnerability about fear of failure ("I don't want to feel worse if I can't keep it up"), psychiatrist moves directly to problem-solving rather than acknowledging this fear or affirming his worth independent of task success. Approach is transactional and solution-focused rather than genuinely person-centered.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist uses basic reflective listening but fails to capture emotional nuance or deeper experience. Reflections are factual rather than empathic (e.g., "So, you mentioned feeling like nothing changes and everything is a mess" misses the emotional weight of exhaustion and futility). Powerful metaphor—"pushing a boulder uphill that's just gonna roll back down"—is not reflected or explored. When Jordan states "I just want to get out of this stuck feeling," psychiatrist does not explore what "unstuck" means or deepen understanding. No clarifying questions to understand Jordan's internal experience, values, or what matters most. Reflections miss opportunities to demonstrate genuine understanding of learned helplessness and cyclical defeat.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains some topical focus on stress management and healthy habits relevant to Jordan's concerns. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. When Jordan says "I don't even know what's worth working on anymore," this invitation to explore values is missed. Instead, psychiatrist asks leading questions that guide toward clinician's predetermined agenda. Direction is narrow and prescriptive (stress management → small goals → external support) rather than emerging from Jordan's own motivations. Psychiatrist never explores what Jordan actually wants his life to look like, what matters to him, or what meaningful change would mean. Focus maintained but clinician-driven rather than values-based.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration is fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist operates almost entirely in expert-driven, hierarchical mode. Clinician unilaterally decides what the problem is, what the solution should be, and how to implement it without asking Jordan's perspective. Rhetorical questions masquerade as collaboration ("Do you think it's mostly the stress...?") but are actually leading questions designed to guide toward clinician's conclusion. When Jordan expresses skepticism about relaxation techniques, psychiatrist minimizes rather than explores this valid concern. Unilateral decision-making evident throughout ("setting a reminder or having a buddy to check in with could help"). Final summary is entirely clinician-constructed; Jordan presented with fait accompli rather than invited to co-create plan. No genuine power-sharing or joint exploration.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is virtually absent. Psychiatrist consistently prescribes solutions using directive language ("should," "might want to," "you need to") that frames recommendations as obligations rather than choices. Pressure disguised as collaboration—when psychiatrist asks "Do you think you could give something like that a shot, or maybe there's something else you think might help?" this is false choice pressuring toward clinician's suggestion. Legitimate concerns minimized rather than respected as valid autonomy ("I'm skeptical it'd make much difference" met with persuasion rather than exploration). Scaling questions function as persuasion tactics rather than genuine exploration. Psychiatrist never asks "What do you want to do?" or "What feels right to you?" or "What would you be willing to try?" Instead, clinician tells Jordan what to do and asks if he'll comply. This directly undermines Jordan's core issue—feeling powerless and unable to control circumstances.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation is essentially absent; session characterized entirely by advice-giving and prescription. Multiple instances of change talk offered by Jordan but not amplified: "I just wish something would go right for once" (desire), "I guess I'd like to not feel stuck anymore" (desire/need), "I'm willing to try" (commitment), "Maybe it'll keep me from just giving up completely" (commitment/hope), "Maybe there's a chance" (hope). Psychiatrist fails to explore any of these sparks. Instead of asking "What would 'something going right' look like?" or "What does 'not giving up' mean to you?", clinician pivots to advice. No exploration of strengths, resources, past successes, or values. No curiosity about Jordan's perspective ("What do you think would help?" never asked). Psychiatrist installs solutions rather than evoking Jordan's own wisdom and intrinsic motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership is absent. Psychiatrist assumes expert role almost entirely, positioning Jordan as passive recipient of advice. Expert-driven stance evident in repeated prescriptions ("Maybe you should try," "You might want to," "You should reach out"). Client relegated to accepting or rejecting clinician's suggestions rather than co-creating solutions. No genuine power-sharing or statements conveying that expertise and wisdom about change reside within client. True partnership statements absent ("You know yourself better than anyone. What do you think might help?" never appears). Clinician controls narrative entirely—decides problem, solution, and implementation. Closing summary is clinician-constructed; no invitation for Jordan to summarize or co-create understanding. Fundamental violation of MI principle that client is primary source of wisdom about own change.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** While Jordan offers multiple instances of change talk, psychiatrist fails to recognize, amplify, or build upon them. Change talk present but not cultivated: desire ("I just wish something would go right"), need ("I'd like to not feel stuck"), commitment ("I'm willing to try"), hope ("Maybe there's a chance"). Psychiatrist's response to each is to move to problem-solving or advice rather than explore and deepen. When Jordan says "I just wish something would go right," psychiatrist does not ask what that would look like or what matters about it. When Jordan says "I'm willing to try," psychiatrist does not explore what this willingness is based on. Significant sustain talk present ("It doesn't matter what I do," "I'm skeptical," "What's the point?") but not skillfully addressed. Psychiatrist attempts to overcome sustain talk with gentle persuasion ("But maybe...") rather than skillfully exploring and softening it. Missed opportunities for strategic evocation throughout.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill demonstrated in handling sustain talk. Significant sustain talk present: "I can't get a break," "Every time I try, something goes wrong," "Nothing changes," "I'm skeptical it'd make much difference," "What's the point if I'm just gonna keep getting knocked down?" When Jordan expresses skepticism, psychiatrist responds with validation followed immediately by contradiction and persuasion ("I get that... But maybe trying some stress management could..."). This can entrench sustain talk rather than soften it. Psychiatrist does not explore why Jordan is skeptical or what would need to be true for him to believe change is possible. No double-sided reflections acknowledging both sustain talk and change talk (e.g., "Part of you feels hopeless, and part of you is willing to try"). Persuasion and advice-giving used as response to sustain talk rather than skillful reflection and exploration. Approach risks reinforcing Jordan's hopelessness rather than softening it.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.4/5 – Below Beginner Level**

**Key Strengths:**
- Warm, welcoming opening tone establishes basic rapport
- Uses basic reflective listening to capture factual content
- Maintains topical focus on relevant concerns (stress, habits, coping)
- Offers concrete, actionable suggestions
- Acknowledges client's willingness to try

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Absence of genuine partnership and collaboration** — Fundamental MI principle violated; client positioned as passive recipient rather than co-creator
- **Failure to evoke and amplify change talk** — Multiple missed opportunities to strengthen intrinsic motivation
- **Inadequate acceptance and validation** — No genuine affirmation of emotional experience or person-centered approach
- **Superficial empathy** — Reflections capture facts but miss emotional nuance and deeper meaning
- **Undermining autonomy** — Prescriptive approach directly contradicts need to support client's sense of agency
- **Persuasion instead of skillful sustain talk softening** — Risks entrencing resistance rather than resolving ambivalence

**Impact on Client Engagement:**
This session is likely to **decrease** Jordan's intrinsic motivation for change rather than increase it. By positioning psychiatrist as expert and prescribing solutions, session reinforces Jordan's core issue—powerlessness. Failure to amplify change talk and explore values misses critical opportunities to strengthen motivation. Significant risk of dropout or non-compliance, further entrenching hopelessness.

**Recommendation:**
Psychiatrist requires formal MI training (2-day workshop with ongoing supervision) and fundamental reframing of clinical approach from expert-driven to collaborative and evocative. Without this shift, well-intentioned advice risks harming rather than helping clients struggling with powerlessness and hopelessness.
