
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk

## Recommendations

### 1. **Shift from Expert-Driven to Collaborative Stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Fundamentally reframe your role from expert who prescribes solutions to partner who explores and evokes the client's own thinking. This requires asking questions rather than giving advice.

**Example:** Instead of:
- "Maybe you should try to find a way to manage stress better first, like some relaxation techniques or planning your time differently."

Say:
- "You mentioned trying the gym and eating better but falling off. What do you think gets in the way? What have you noticed about what works or doesn't work for you?"

Or instead of:
- "You might want to try some simple stress relief techniques, like deep breathing or short walks."

Say:
- "What's your instinct about what might help you feel a bit less stuck? What have you considered trying?"

**Practice:** In your next session, commit to asking at least 5 open-ended questions before offering any suggestions. Listen to the client's answers and build on them rather than pivoting to your agenda. Record yourself and count the ratio of questions to statements—aim for at least 2:1 (two questions for every one statement).

---

### 2. **Recognize and Amplify Change Talk (SECOND PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Train yourself to identify change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment) and respond by exploring and reinforcing it rather than moving to advice-giving.

**Example:** When Jordan says, "I just wish something would go right for once," instead of moving to problem-solving:

Say:
- "Tell me more about that. What would 'something going right' look like for you?"
- "What matters most to you about that?"
- "If things did start going right, what would be different?"

When Jordan says, "Maybe it'll keep me from just giving up completely," instead of accepting and moving on:

Say:
- "That's important—not giving up. What does that mean to you?"
- "What would it look like to not give up, even in small ways?"
- "What gives you that willingness, even a little bit?"

**Practice:** In your next session, write down every instance of change talk you hear. After the session, review it and ask yourself: "Did I amplify this? Did I explore it? Or did I move to advice-giving?" Aim to amplify at least 3 instances of change talk per session.

---

### 3. **Develop Genuine Empathic Reflections That Capture Emotional Nuance**

**Action:** Move beyond factual reflections to reflections that capture the emotional weight and meaning of what the client is experiencing. Use metaphors, emotional language, and deeper understanding.

**Example:** Instead of:
- "So, you mentioned feeling like nothing changes and everything is a mess."

Say:
- "It sounds like you're carrying an enormous weight, and no matter what you do, it feels like you're just getting pushed back down. That must feel incredibly defeating."

Or instead of:
- "It sounds like stress from money and work is really making things tough for you."

Say:
- "It sounds like you're drowning in stress—money, work, everything piling up at once. And every time you try to get your head above water, something pulls you back under."

**Practice:** After each client statement, pause and ask yourself: "What is the emotional experience here? What metaphor captures this? What would it feel like to be in this person's shoes?" Then reflect that back. Record yourself and listen for whether your reflections capture emotion and meaning or just facts.

---

### 4. **Validate and Explore Sustain Talk Rather Than Overcome It with Persuasion**

**Action:** When the client expresses skepticism, hopelessness, or reasons for not changing, resist the urge to persuade. Instead, validate the concern and explore it with curiosity.

**Example:** When Jordan says, "I'm skeptical it'd make much difference," instead of:
- "But maybe trying some stress management could at least give you a bit of relief, you know?"

Say:
- "You're skeptical. That makes sense—you've tried things before and been disappointed. Tell me about that. What happened when you tried before?"

Or use a double-sided reflection:
- "Part of you feels like nothing's going to help, and part of you is willing to try. Both of those make sense. I'm curious—what's the part of you that's willing to try based on?"

**Practice:** In your next session, when you hear sustain talk, pause before responding. Ask yourself: "What is the valid concern here? What has this person learned from their experience?" Reflect that back before offering any suggestions. Aim for at least 2 instances of skillfully handling sustain talk per session.

---

### 5. **Explore and Affirm Client Autonomy and Agency**

**Action:** Consistently communicate that the client has the right to choose and that you respect their autonomy. Avoid prescriptive language ("should," "need to") and instead use collaborative language ("What do you think?" "What feels right to you?").

**Example:** Instead of:
- "You might want to reach out to a friend or family member who can check in with you about those small steps."

Say:
- "Some people find it helpful to have someone check in with them. Would that be something you'd want to explore? If so, who in your life might be a good fit?"

Or instead of:
- "You might want to pick one small goal for this week, like a short walk or a few minutes of deep breathing."

Say:
- "What's one small thing that feels manageable to you this week? Something that you'd actually be willing to try?"

**Practice:** Review your language. Replace "should," "need to," and "might want to" with "What do you think?" "What feels right to you?" "What would you be willing to try?" Aim to use collaborative language in at least 80% of your suggestions.

---

### 6. **Explore Values and Meaning to Build Intrinsic Motivation**

**Action:** Ask questions that help the client connect change to what matters most to them. This builds intrinsic motivation, which is more durable than compliance-based motivation.

**Example:** Ask:
- "What matters most to you in life?"
- "If you weren't feeling stuck, what would that make possible for you?"
- "What would it mean to you to feel less overwhelmed?"
- "What do you want your life to look like?"
- "What's important about getting unstuck?"

**Practice:** In your next session, ask at least one values-based question. Listen to the answer and explore it further. Build the treatment plan around the client's values rather than your clinical agenda.

---

### 7. **Use Scaling Questions Strategically to Explore Rather Than Persuade**

**Action:** When you ask scaling questions (importance, confidence), use them as a springboard for exploration rather than as a tool to elicit commitment or persuade.

**Example:** Instead of:
- "How important do you feel it is to make some changes right now, on a scale from 1 to 10?" [Client: "4"] "Okay, so you're at about a 4 on importance. That's not too high, but at least you're thinking about it."

Say:
- "How important do you feel it is to make some changes right now, on a scale from 1 to 10?" [Client: "4"] "You're at a 4. Tell me about that. What would need to happen for it to move to a 5 or 6? What would make it feel more important?"

**Practice:** After each scaling question, ask a follow-up that explores the client's thinking rather than moving to advice. Aim for at least 2 follow-up questions per scaling question.

---

### 8. **Invite the Client to Summarize and Co-Create the Plan**

**Action:** Rather than summarizing the session and prescribing a plan, invite the client to summarize what feels important and co-create next steps.

**Example:** Instead of:
- "Okay, so to sum up, you're feeling pretty overwhelmed with stress from work and money, and you've tried some healthy habits before but had trouble sticking with them. You're open to trying some small steps like short walks or breathing exercises and getting support from someone to check in with you."

Say:
- "How would you sum up what we've talked about today? What feels important to you about our conversation?"
- "What's one small thing you'd be willing to try this week? What would make that feel doable for you?"
- "Who in your life might be a good support for you? What kind of support would actually be helpful?"

**Practice:** In your next session, invite the client to summarize at least once. Listen to their summary and build the plan from there rather than imposing your own.

---

### 9. **Develop Skill in Recognizing and Responding to Ambivalence**

**Action:** Recognize that ambivalence (wanting change and fearing it simultaneously) is normal and healthy. Use double-sided reflections and strategic responses to help the client move through ambivalence rather than trying to eliminate it.

**Example:** When Jordan expresses both change talk ("I'd like to not feel stuck") and sustain talk ("But nothing changes"), use a double-sided reflection:
- "On one hand, you really want to feel less stuck and you're willing to try. On the other hand, you've tried before and been disappointed, so you're protecting yourself by staying skeptical. Both of those make complete sense."

Then explore: "What's the part of you that wants to try based on? What gives you that hope, even a little bit?"

**Practice:** In your next session, identify at least one instance of ambivalence. Use a double-sided reflection and then explore the change side. Record yourself and listen for whether you're helping the client move through ambivalence or getting stuck in it.

**Action:** Record your sessions (with client consent) and review them with a supervisor or peer trained in MI. Use the MITI (Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity) scale to assess your own performance.

**Practice:** Commit to reviewing at least one session per month with a supervisor. Focus on the 9 criteria in this evaluation and track your progress over time.
