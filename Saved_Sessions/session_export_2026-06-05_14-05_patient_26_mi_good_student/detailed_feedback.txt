
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Explicitly and Repeatedly Affirm Autonomy and Client Ownership**
- **Action:** Integrate explicit autonomy-supportive statements throughout the session, particularly when the patient expresses ambivalence or commits to action. Use language that emphasizes the patient's right to choose and his ownership of the change process.
- **Example:** When the patient says *"I wouldn't say I'm ready to cut it out completely,"* respond with: *"It sounds like you want to find your own approach to this rather than having someone else's rules about what you should do. That makes sense—you're the expert on what will work for your life and your relationships."* Or when committing to action: *"This is your decision to make, and I'm here to support whatever direction you choose."*
- **Practice:** Before each session, identify 3-4 moments where you can explicitly affirm autonomy. Practice phrases like: *"You get to decide what's right for you,"* *"This is your choice to make,"* *"You're the expert on your own life,"* *"What feels right to you?"* Record yourself and listen for how often you emphasize client ownership vs. collaborative problem-solving.

---

### 2. **Deepen Empathic Reflections to Capture Emotional Nuance and Underlying Motivations**
- **Action:** Move beyond behavioral description to reflect the emotional experience and underlying needs. When the patient describes a behavior, ask yourself: "What is the emotional experience here? What need is being met? What does this mean to the patient?" Then reflect both the behavior and the emotional/motivational layer.
- **Example:** Instead of: *"It sounds like the social atmosphere and the desire to unwind after a busy day really pull you toward drinking more than you might intend,"* try: *"It sounds like after a long day of work and family demands, you're looking for a way to feel like yourself again—to let go of all that stress and just relax. And in social situations, alcohol becomes that escape, but then you realize you've gone further than you wanted, and that can feel frustrating because you're not in control."* This captures both the function and the emotional experience.
- **Practice:** In supervision or role-play, practice making reflections that include: (1) the behavior, (2) the underlying need or emotion, (3) the consequence or meaning. Ask for feedback on whether the reflection deepens understanding or remains surface-level.

---

### 3. **More Strategically Explore Whether Proposed Alternatives Actually Address Alcohol's Function**
- **Action:** When the patient proposes alternatives to drinking (walking, quiet time), explicitly explore whether these alternatives provide the same function. This prevents surface-level problem-solving and ensures strategies actually address the underlying need.
- **Example:** When the patient mentions walking as an alternative, ask: *"Walking sounds like a good way to decompress—I'm curious, does it give you that same sense of letting go and unwinding that alcohol does, or is it a different kind of relief? And what about the social piece—does walking help you feel connected to others the way drinking does?"* This explores whether the alternative genuinely competes with alcohol or addresses a different need.
- **Practice:** In role-play, practice asking follow-up questions about proposed strategies: *"How would that feel compared to...?"* *"Would that address the same need?"* *"What would be different about that?"* Develop comfort with exploring whether alternatives are truly viable.

---

### 4. **Establish Clear, Explicit Follow-Up Accountability and Success Metrics**
- **Action:** At the end of the session, explicitly establish when you'll check in, what the patient will report back on, and what success would look like. This provides structure and accountability without being prescriptive.
- **Example:** *"So let's plan to check in in two weeks. When we talk, I'd love to hear how the conversation with your wife went and what you've tried at social events. What would you want to report back that would show you're making progress with this?"* Or: *"What would be a sign that you're moving in the right direction?"*
- **Practice:** At the end of each session, explicitly establish: (1) when you'll check in, (2) what the patient will try, (3) what success looks like. Write this down and review it at the next session.

---

### 5. **More Comprehensively Evoke Reasons, Ability, and Commitment for Change**
- **Action:** Systematically evoke different types of change talk (reasons, ability, need, commitment) rather than relying on what emerges naturally. Use strategic questioning to draw out the patient's own articulation of why change matters, what he's capable of, and what he's willing to commit to.
- **Example:** After the patient identifies his family as a motivation, ask: *"What else matters to you about managing your drinking? What do you want your life to look like?"* When he identifies strategies, ask: *"What would it mean to you if you were able to follow through on this? How would that change things?"* When he commits to talking with his wife, ask: *"What would you want to say to her about why this matters to you?"*
- **Practice:** Develop a mental checklist of change talk types: reasons, ability, need, commitment, taking steps. In each session, ensure you've evoked at least 2-3 of these. Practice asking: *"What are your reasons for wanting to...?"* *"What makes you think you could...?"* *"How important is this to you?"* *"What are you willing to try?"*

---

### 6. **Reduce Subtle Leading Questions and Allow More Patient Self-Discovery**
- **Action:** When you notice yourself suggesting a particular direction or answer, pause and ask a more open question instead. Trust the patient to make connections and generate solutions.
- **Example:** Instead of: *"What do you think has helped you stay on track with quitting smoking that might also support you in managing your drinking?"* (which subtly suggests that smoking cessation strategies will transfer), try: *"As you think about managing your drinking, what comes to mind? What do you think might help?"* This allows the patient to make the connection themselves if it's relevant.
- **Practice:** Record yourself and listen for leading questions. Identify moments where you're subtly suggesting an answer. Practice rephrasing these as more open questions. Ask a colleague to listen and give feedback on whether your questions are truly open or subtly leading.

### 7. **More Explicitly Explore and Affirm the Patient's Values and Identity**
- **Action:** Ask deeper questions about what matters to the patient, what kind of person/parent/partner he wants to be, and what his life would look like if he made changes. This strengthens intrinsic motivation by connecting change to identity and values.
- **Example:** *"What kind of father do you want to be? What do you want your relationship with your son to look like?"* or *"When you imagine yourself managing your drinking the way you want to, what does that look like? How would you feel about yourself?"* or *"What matters most to you in your life right now?"*
- **Practice:** In each session, ask at least one question about values or identity. Practice phrases like: *"What matters most to you?"* *"What kind of person do you want to be?"* *"What would that mean for your life?"* Develop comfort with deeper, more personal questioning.

---

### 8. **Use More Explicit Reframing to Address Potential Sustain Talk**
- **Action:** When the patient expresses potential sustain talk (minimization, desire to maintain status quo, concerns about change), explicitly reframe this as compatible with his deeper values and goals rather than as an obstacle.
- **Example:** When the patient says *"I still want to have a good time occasionally,"* respond with: *"So you're looking for a way to enjoy yourself socially while also being more in control—that's not about giving up fun, it's about finding a balance that works for you and your family. That makes a lot of sense."* This reframes the sustain talk as part of the change goal.
- **Practice:** In role-play, practice reframing statements like: *"I don't want to give up drinking entirely,"* *"It's hard to control in social situations,"* *"My friends won't understand."* Develop skill in finding the kernel of truth in sustain talk and reframing it as compatible with change.

### 9. **Strengthen the Closing by Inviting Patient Feedback and Summarizing Collaboratively**
- **Action:** Rather than telling the patient what to remember or what comes next, invite his reflection on the session and collaboratively summarize what he's decided. This reinforces partnership and ensures the patient feels ownership of the plan.
- **Example:** Instead of: *"Remember it's all about what feels right for you and taking steps that fit your life. I'm here to support you whenever you want to check in,"* try: *"As you think about moving forward, what feels most important to keep in mind? Is there anything else you'd like to focus on today, or does this feel like a good place to start? What would be most helpful from me as you work on this?"*
- **Practice:** Practice closing statements that invite patient reflection rather than clinician prescription. Record yourself and listen for how often you're telling vs. asking. Aim for at least 50% of closing statements to be questions that invite the patient's reflection.
