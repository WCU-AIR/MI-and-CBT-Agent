
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made no attempt to establish a collaborative agenda at any point during this session. A proper CBT agenda would involve: (1) explicitly identifying the session's target problems, (2) prioritizing which symptoms to address given the 50-minute timeframe, (3) establishing measurable goals for the session, and (4) gaining patient agreement on how time would be spent. None of these occurred.

The session drifted without direction, driven entirely by the therapist's impulse to share personal stories rather than collaborative problem-solving.

**Summary:**

No agenda was established. The therapist failed to identify, prioritize, or collaboratively structure the session around Dylan's presenting problems, representing a critical foundational failure in CBT delivery.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated surface-level understanding of Dylan's explicit statements but consistently missed the clinical significance of his disclosures and failed to demonstrate deeper empathic comprehension of his internal experience.

What the therapist understood: Dylan acknowledged having intrusive thoughts, sleep problems, anxiety around cars, and irritability. The therapist reflected these back in generic terms ("I know what you mean about the brain playing the worst thing on repeat").

What the therapist missed—critically:

1. **Avoidance as a core ASD symptom**: Dylan explicitly stated, "I've been avoiding going outside too much, especially if I have to be around cars or loud noises" and "it's hard to think about anything except just wanting to get out of there." This is textbook avoidance behavior maintaining ASD. The therapist's response was to suggest humming and positive thinking—interventions that reinforce avoidance rather than address it. A therapist with genuine understanding would recognize that avoidance perpetuates trauma symptoms and requires graduated exposure, not distraction.

2. **Hyperarousal and physiological dysregulation**: Dylan stated, "When those images and feelings come up, it doesn't feel like something I can just ignore or push away" and "when my heart is racing or I start thinking about the crash again." This describes the autonomic nervous system activation characteristic of ASD. The therapist's response—"just tell yourself it's in the past and you're safe"—demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of trauma physiology. You cannot cognitively override a racing heart; this requires somatic interventions (grounding, breathing, progressive muscle relaxation) or exposure-based techniques.

3. **Anger and guilt**: Dylan disclosed, "I get mad at myself for feeling scared or weak" and "Why did that SUV run the light?" The therapist acknowledged anger as "normal" but did not explore the guilt, shame, or anger toward the driver—all of which are treatable targets in trauma therapy. No cognitive work was done to address these emotions.

4. **Developmental context**: Dylan is 15 years old, in the midst of identity formation and peer integration. His avoidance of driving represents a developmental setback. The therapist made no acknowledgment of this developmental disruption or its implications for his self-concept and peer relationships.

5. **Reluctance to disclose**: Dylan stated, "I don't usually like talking about this stuff, so it's kind of hard to open up." This is a critical clinical observation—Dylan is avoidant of trauma discussion, which is both a symptom and a barrier to treatment. The therapist did not address this directly, normalize it, or develop a collaborative plan to gradually increase his willingness to process the trauma.

The therapist's repeated personal anecdotes (traffic jam, missed bus, exam stress) suggest a fundamental misunderstanding of the difference between normal stress and trauma. These comparisons, while well-intentioned, minimize Dylan's experience and suggest the therapist does not grasp the severity of ASD or its neurobiological basis.

**Summary:**

The therapist grasped Dylan's explicit statements but missed critical clinical features of ASD, failed to recognize avoidance as a maintaining factor, misunderstood trauma physiology, and did not demonstrate comprehension of the developmental or emotional complexity of Dylan's presentation.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish and Maintain a Structured CBT Agenda (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action**: At the start of each session, explicitly set a collaborative agenda. ### 2. **Eliminate Personal Anecdotes and Maintain Professional Boundaries**
- **Action**: Do not share personal stories about your own stress, trauma, or coping strategies. If you feel the urge to normalize Dylan's experience through personal disclosure, use psychoeducation instead. Say: "Many people who experience trauma like yours have similar responses. Your racing heart and jumpiness are your brain trying to protect you. This is normal, and we can help your brain learn that you're safe now."
- **Practice**: Record yourself in role-plays and count how many times you share personal information. Aim for zero personal anecdotes per session. If you feel the urge to share, pause and ask yourself: "Is this about me or about the patient? Does this serve the patient's treatment?"
