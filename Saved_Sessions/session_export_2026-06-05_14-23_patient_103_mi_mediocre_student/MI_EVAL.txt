### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1

Presupposing questions establish clinician expertise over patient's perspective. Dismissal of patient's self-knowledge communicates "Your judgment is insufficient; I know better." Unilateral decision-making about advice without consulting patient about usefulness. Power imbalance established through repeated "It would probably help if..." framing. ### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal change talk elicited throughout session. Few instances offered by patient are weak and tentative ("Maybe I could try thinking about it"; "I can try to do that"). No amplification when change talk emerges—clinician moves to next question instead. No strategic questioning designed to evoke change talk (e.g., "What concerns you most?" "What would be benefits of cutting back?"). No explicit reinforcement or affirmation of patient's change language. Advice-giving substitutes for evoking, preventing patient from developing intrinsic motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill demonstrated in handling patient's arguments for status quo. When patient expresses sustain talk ("I don't think it's that bad"; "I don't really see why I should cut back"), clinician either contradicts directly, asks leading questions, or eventually accepts position without exploration. Direct contradiction ("But sometimes having a plan can prevent problems") can intensify sustain talk. No double-sided reflections acknowledging both sustain talk and change talk simultaneously. No reframing that might open new perspectives. By session end, clinician accepts sustain talk without skillful redirection.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.4/5**

### Strengths
- Maintains consistent focus on drinking behavior throughout session
- Establishes warm, non-threatening opening tone
- Avoids harsh confrontation or moral judgment that could escalate defensiveness
- Offers pragmatic, low-barrier harm reduction advice by session end

### Critical Deficiencies
This session demonstrates **fundamental failures across all core MI domains**. The psychiatrist operates from an expert-driven, clinician-centered stance that directly contradicts MI's foundational principles of partnership, evocation, and autonomy support.

**Most Critical Issues:**
1. **Absence of Evocation:** The heart of MI is absent. No exploration of patient's values, motivations, ambivalence, or resources. Clinician repeatedly tells patient what would help rather than drawing out intrinsic motivation.

2. **Systematic Autonomy Undermining:** Repeated directive framing ("It would probably help if..." × 6), dismissal of patient's self-knowledge, and advice presented as obligation rather than choice systematically undermine patient autonomy.

3. **Failed Collaboration:** Unilateral agenda-setting, leading questions presupposing problems, and contradiction of patient's self-assessment establish clinician as authority rather than partner.

4. **Minimal Change Talk Cultivation:** Patient offers tentative change language that is never amplified or deepened. No strategic questioning to evoke change talk.

5. **Expert-Driven Partnership:** Clinician positions self as authority dispensing wisdom rather than as collaborative partner. No genuine power-sharing or co-construction of solutions.

### Likely Outcome
**Minimal behavior change expected.** Patient leaves with vague commitment to "be a little more aware" but no genuine motivation for change. Has not explored own reasons for change, values, or ambivalence. Clinician's expert-driven approach likely reinforced patient's defensiveness and resistance by providing no opportunity to develop own change talk or experience genuine partnership.

### Critical Recommendation
**This clinician requires intensive training in MI fundamentals**, particularly in:
- Evocation and strategic questioning
- Partnership and power-sharing
- Autonomy support and genuine choice-offering
- Cultivating and amplifying change talk

Current approach is fundamentally inconsistent with MI framework and unlikely to effectively engage patient in change process.
