
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

No agenda was established at any point in this session. The psychiatrist opened with a casual social greeting ("Hey there! Good to see you today. How's your day been so far?") but never transitioned into collaborative goal-setting or prioritization of session topics. There is no evidence that the therapist and patient discussed what they would work on together, what problems were most pressing, or how time would be allocated. Given that this patient presented to the psychiatric emergency room with suicidal ideation (even if attention-seeking in nature) and has a diagnosis of Histrionic Personality Disorder with documented maladaptive patterns, establishing a clear agenda was clinically essential.

The session drifted aimlessly through the patient's complaints about her husband, her back pain, and her need for attention without any structured direction. A proper agenda would have included: (1) assessment of current suicidal risk and safety planning, (2) clarification of the relationship between her attention-seeking behaviors and her presenting complaints, (3) identification of specific behavioral targets for change, and (4) time allocation for each topic. The absence of agenda-setting is particularly problematic in emergency psychiatric care, where structure and clarity are protective factors.

**Summary:**

The therapist completely failed to establish a collaborative agenda, leaving the session without direction, clear goals, or prioritization of the patient's presenting problems.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish Clear CBT Structure and Agenda-Setting**
- **Action**: Begin every session by collaboratively setting an agenda with specific, prioritized topics and time allocation. Explicitly state: "Today we have [X] minutes. I'd like to focus on [specific problems]. What's most important to you today?"
- **Example**: "Karmen, I know you're dealing with your back pain, your relationship with your husband, and some thoughts about harming yourself. Those are all important. What feels most urgent to address today? Let's pick one or two things we can really focus on in our time together."
- **Practice**: Role-play agenda-setting with a supervisor. Practice with 3-5 patients before considering this skill developed. Record sessions and review for agenda clarity.

### 2. **Eliminate Excessive Self-Disclosure and Maintain Professional Boundaries**
- **Action**: Redirect any impulse to share personal experiences back to the patient. Use phrases like "That's something many people experience" or "That's a common struggle" instead of "I felt that way too."
- **Example**: Instead of "When I was feeling invisible, I started drinking more water," say: "Many people find that basic self-care—like hydration and sleep—can help them feel more grounded. Have you noticed any connection between how you take care of yourself physically and how you feel emotionally?"
- **Practice**: Review recordings of this session and identify every instance of self-disclosure. For each one, write an alternative response that redirects focus to the patient. Practice these alternatives until they feel natural.

### 3. **Conduct Formal Risk Assessment and Safety Planning**
- **Action**: Given the patient's presentation with suicidal ideation, conduct a structured suicide risk assessment including: intent vs. plan, access to means, protective factors, and previous attempts. Develop a written safety plan with the patient.
- **Example**: "Karmen, you mentioned thoughts of overdosing on Advil. I want to understand this better. Do you have a plan for how you would do this? Do you have access to large quantities of Advil? When you have these thoughts, what stops you from acting on them? Let's create a plan for what you'll do if these thoughts become stronger."
- **Practice**: Use a structured risk assessment tool (e.g., Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale) with every patient presenting with suicidal ideation. Practice until you can administer it smoothly and document findings clearly.

### 4. **Use Guided Discovery Instead of Advice-Giving**
- **Action**: Replace advice with Socratic questions that help the patient reach insights independently. Ask "What do you think would happen if...?" instead of "You should..."
- **Example**: Instead of "Just try to think positive," ask: "When you use dramatic statements or threats to get your husband's attention, what happens next? Does he come closer, or does he pull away? What do you think he's thinking when you do that? What would it look like if you told him directly what you need without the drama?"
- **Practice**: For every piece of advice you're tempted to give, write down 3 Socratic questions instead. Practice asking questions in supervision until guided discovery feels natural.

### 5. **Identify and Target Core Cognitions Related to Histrionic Personality Disorder**
- **Action**: Explicitly explore the patient's core beliefs about visibility, worth, and relationships. Use cognitive restructuring to examine evidence for and against these beliefs.
- **Example**: "You've mentioned several times that you feel invisible unless you do something dramatic. Let's examine that belief. Can you think of times when someone noticed you or cared about you without you having to be dramatic? What was that like? What does it mean about you if people can see you without the drama?"
- **Practice**: Develop a case conceptualization for this patient that identifies her core beliefs and the behaviors that maintain them. Use this conceptualization to guide all interventions.

### 6. **Conduct Functional Analysis of Attention-Seeking Behaviors**
- **Action**: Help the patient understand the function of her attention-seeking behaviors (what need do they serve?) and the consequences (what happens as a result?). Use this to develop alternative strategies.
- **Example**: "When you mention overdosing or do something dramatic, what are you hoping will happen? What need are you trying to meet? [Listen.] Now, what actually happens? Does your husband come closer, or does he withdraw? Does it bring you the kind of attention and connection you really want? What would happen if you tried a different approach?"
- **Practice**: Conduct a functional analysis with this patient and document it. Use it to guide behavioral interventions.

### 7. **Apply Specific CBT Techniques: Thought Records and Cognitive Restructuring**
- **Action**: Teach the patient to use thought records to identify automatic thoughts, examine evidence, and develop more balanced thoughts. Focus on thoughts like "I'm invisible," "I'm too much," "People don't really see me."
- **Example**: Create a thought record together: Situation: "Husband ignores me when I talk about my back pain." Automatic thought: "He doesn't care about me. I'm invisible." Evidence for: "He said 'you'll be fine.'" Evidence against: "He came to the ER when I mentioned overdosing. He's been married to me for years." Balanced thought: "My husband may not always respond the way I want, but that doesn't mean he doesn't care. I can ask directly for what I need."
- **Practice**: Teach thought records to this patient and assign them as homework. Review and refine in subsequent sessions.

### 8. **Assign Structured, Specific Homework with Clear Rationale**
- **Action**: Assign homework that directly targets the identified problems. Explain the rationale, ensure understanding, and plan to review at the next session.
- **Example**: "This week, I'd like you to try something. When you feel the urge to be dramatic or do something to get your husband's attention, I want you to pause and write down: (1) What am I feeling right now? (2) What do I need? (3) What's one way I could ask for that directly without drama? Then try the direct approach at least once. Write down what happens. We'll review this together next time."
- **Practice**: Assign homework to every patient. Always explain the rationale and check understanding before the patient leaves.

### 9. **Develop a Coherent Treatment Conceptualization and Strategy**
- **Action**: Create a written case conceptualization that explains: (1) the patient's core beliefs and assumptions, (2) the behaviors that maintain her distress, (3) the function these behaviors serve, (4) the treatment goals, and (5) the specific interventions that will address each goal.
- **Example**: Conceptualization: "Karmen believes she is only visible/valuable when she is dramatic or in crisis. This belief drives attention-seeking behaviors (self-dramatization, suicidal threats, flirtation) that temporarily get her attention but ultimately damage relationships and reinforce her sense of being 'too much.' Treatment will focus on: (1) identifying and challenging this core belief, (2) developing alternative ways of communicating needs, (3) building genuine self-worth independent of others' attention, (4) improving relationship skills."
- **Practice**: Develop a written conceptualization for this patient before the next session. Use it to guide all interventions.

### 10. **Implement Behavioral Experiments to Test Maladaptive Beliefs**
- **Action**: Design experiments where the patient tests her beliefs about what happens when she doesn't use drama to get attention. Predict outcomes, conduct the experiment, and review results.
- **Example**: "You believe that if you don't do something dramatic, your husband won't notice you. Let's test that. Let's see what actually happens and compare."
- **Practice**: Design 2-3 behavioral experiments for this patient and assign them as homework.

### 11. **Elicit and Respond to Feedback Throughout the Session**
- **Action**: Regularly check for understanding and patient satisfaction. Ask: "Does this make sense?" "How are you feeling about what we're discussing?" "Is this helpful?" "What are your thoughts?" Adjust based on feedback.
- **Example**: After explaining a concept, ask: "I want to make sure I'm explaining this clearly. What's your understanding of what I just said?" If the patient seems confused or disengaged, pause and ask: "I'm noticing you seem uncertain. What's going on?"
- **Practice**: Set a goal to elicit feedback at least 5 times per session. Record sessions and count instances of feedback-seeking.

### 12. **Receive Supervision and Training in CBT**
- **Action**: This session reveals fundamental gaps in CBT competence. Seek regular supervision from a CBT-trained supervisor. Consider formal CBT training (e.g., through the Academy of Cognitive Therapy or similar organizations).
- **Example**: Bring recordings of sessions to supervision. Have your supervisor review your agenda-setting, use of guided discovery, application of techniques, and homework assignment.
