No affirmation of patient's strengths or reflective capacity.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Empathy largely absent or superficial. Few reflective statements made; those present are minimal, inaccurate, and used as setups for advice rather than genuine understanding. Critical emotional content missed repeatedly—patient's fear of deprivation ("I like smoking too much to just give it up"), withdrawal concerns ("stressed out and cranky"), and identity/ritual significance ("work breaks without a cigarette just don't sound the same") are not explored. Complex reflections capturing ambivalence entirely absent. Session lacks depth of understanding characteristic of empathic MI practice.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clear focus on smoking cessation maintained throughout; conversation doesn't drift aimlessly. However, direction becomes problematic when it shifts from maintaining focus to imposing predetermined agenda. Linear, clinician-driven trajectory leaves little room for patient's priorities or evolving readiness. Lacks strategic flexibility—when patient expresses ambivalence, clinician pushes ahead rather than exploring it. Eventually adjusts when patient resists quit date, accepting "cutting down gradually," but this is reactive rather than proactive strategic direction.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration nearly absent. Psychiatrist consistently adopts expert-driven, hierarchical stance positioning clinician as authority. Pervasive prescriptive language throughout: "maybe you should think about cutting down," "have you tried nicotine patches," "maybe you should try to push that number up," "you might want to set a quit date," "maybe you should pick one or two small changes." Patient's expertise about his own life never solicited or valued. At no point does clinician ask "What ideas do you have?" or "What do you think might work?" Only moment approaching collaboration is reactive acceptance when patient suggests "cutting back gradually"—not true co-construction.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Autonomy support significantly compromised throughout. Pervasive directive language ("you should," "you might want to," "it would probably help if") pressures rather than invites choice. Phrase "it would probably help if" appears repeatedly, functioning as soft directive disguised as collaboration. Clinician never explicitly affirms patient's right to choose differently, maintain smoking, or move at own pace. When patient says "I'm not really planning on it anytime soon," response pivots to persuasion rather than validation. Token autonomy statement appears only at end ("it's your choice and you know what works best for you"), insufficient to offset controlling tone throughout entire session.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation almost entirely absent—fundamental violation of MI spirit. Clinician consistently tells rather than asks, provides reasons for change rather than eliciting patient's own, prescribes solutions rather than facilitating self-discovery. Critical missed opportunities: when patient rates importance 4-5, clinician tells him reasons to increase it rather than asking "What keeps it from being lower?" When patient mentions stress, clinician prescribes solutions rather than asking "What does smoking do for you when stressed?" When patient expresses ambivalence, clinician tells him what should concern him rather than evoking his own concerns. Workplace policy—powerful external motivator—never explored. Only genuine change talk ("Saving money does sound good") generated spontaneously by patient; clinician doesn't amplify or deepen it. Session demonstrates "tell" approach rather than "ask" approach.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role throughout, positioning himself as authority on what patient should do. Closed questions used to gather information rather than open questions inviting patient's perspective. Unsolicited advice provided repeatedly: nicotine patches, relaxation techniques, exercise, short walks, deep breathing, avoiding smoking at work breaks. Solutions framed as clinician's ideas rather than co-constructed with patient. Power entirely with clinician; patient positioned as passive recipient of guidance. Patient's contributions minimal and reactive—responds to clinician's questions and suggestions but never substantially shapes session direction. True partnership absent: clinician never asks "What do you think might help?" or "How would you want to approach this?" or "Your ideas matter—what are you thinking?"

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk minimal and largely unelicited or unrefined. Patient generates some spontaneously: "Sure. I can quit" (ability talk), "Saving money does sound good, and if I feel better, that's definitely a plus" (reasons/desire talk), "I could try taking a short walk during breaks or just breathe a little deeper" (ability/commitment talk), "If it helps, maybe I'll think more about quitting down the line" (commitment talk). However, clinician fails to amplify, deepen, or strategically reinforce. When patient says "Sure. I can quit," no exploration or "Tell me more about that." When patient mentions "Saving money," valuable change talk not deepened. Clinician never asks open questions designed to elicit change talk: "What would be benefits of cutting down?" "What concerns you about smoking?" "What would it be like if you quit?" Clinician provides reasons for change rather than evoking patient's own—critical distinction, as intrinsic motivation more powerful than externally imposed reasons.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk handled minimally; clinician doesn't actively reinforce it but fails to skillfully redirect or explore with curiosity. Typical response to sustain talk is confrontational—providing counter-arguments rather than collaborative techniques. When patient says "I like smoking, so quitting isn't exactly easy for me," clinician responds with prescriptive advice ("maybe you should think about cutting down," "have you tried patches?") rather than double-sided reflection. When patient expresses fear ("I just don't want to feel all stressed out and cranky if I'm trying to quit before I'm ready"), clinician briefly validates but immediately pivots to solutions rather than exploring concern. Clinician never uses reframing (e.g., "You value relaxation and stress relief—how else could you get those things?") or explores function of sustain talk with curiosity. Eventually shows flexibility by accepting "cutting down gradually" instead of pushing quit date, but this comes late and isn't part of strategic approach to sustain talk.

## Summary Assessment

### Strengths
- Clear focus maintained on smoking cessation throughout session
- Basic warmth established in opening greeting
- Patient remains engaged despite directive approach
- Eventual flexibility accepting patient's preference for "cutting down gradually"
- Late acknowledgment of patient autonomy ("it's your choice and you know what works best for you")

### Critical Areas for Improvement
- **Pervasive directive language** undermines autonomy and collaboration
- **Expert-driven stance** violates MI spirit of partnership
- **Minimal empathy and reflection** fail to demonstrate genuine understanding
- **Failure to evoke** patient's own motivations, values, and ideas
- **Inadequate exploration of ambivalence** leads to missed opportunities for deepening motivation
- **Minimal change talk cultivation** fails to amplify spontaneous motivation
- **Confrontational sustain talk handling** risks amplifying resistance
- **Absence of true collaboration** positions patient as passive recipient rather than expert on own life

### Clinical Impact
Session unlikely to increase intrinsic motivation for change. Directive, expert-driven approach may actually decrease motivation and trigger reactance. Patient's final statements ("If it helps, maybe I'll think more about quitting down the line," "I'm not promising I'll quit or anything") suggest conditional, external motivation rather than intrinsic commitment. No evidence underlying motivation to quit has increased.

### Recommendation
Psychiatrist requires focused Motivational Interviewing training and deliberate practice to shift from directive, expert-driven stance to collaborative, evocative approach. Core competencies to develop: asking rather than telling, genuine empathy through reflective listening, soliciting patient's ideas before offering suggestions, exploring and validating ambivalence, strategically cultivating change talk, supporting autonomy explicitly, using collaborative language, skillfully handling sustain talk with curiosity, and establishing true partnership.
