Tells patient why to quit ("smoking causes serious diseases," "Smoking damages your lungs and heart," "it's just a matter of time before it catches up with you," "Smoking leads to cancer and heart disease")—all external reasons imposed by psychiatrist, not reasons patient has generated. Research shows intrinsic motivation (reasons client generates) far more powerful than extrinsic motivation (reasons imposed by others). Psychiatrist relies entirely on extrinsic motivation and never attempts to evoke patient's own reasons for change. Missed evocative opportunities throughout: when patient says smoking "isn't like a big deal," psychiatrist could ask "What would make it feel like a bigger deal to you?" but instead dismisses it; when patient says "it's not that simple," psychiatrist could ask "What makes it complicated?" but instead attacks statement; when patient expresses doubt ("I don't think it'll work"), psychiatrist could ask "What would need to be different for you to feel more confident?" but instead tells patient to stop doubting; when patient says "Doesn't matter if I try or not," psychiatrist could explore past successes but instead demands continuation. ## Overall Clinical Summary

### **Overall Session Score: 1/5**

---

### Strengths

- **Consistent focus on target behavior:** Psychiatrist maintains focus on smoking cessation throughout session (though this focus is rigid, imposed, and counterproductive rather than collaborative and strategic).

---

### Critical Areas for Improvement

**This session represents a fundamental failure of Motivational Interviewing practice.** Nearly every core MI principle is violated:

1. **Authoritarian stance replaces partnership:** Psychiatrist operates as expert who tells rather than partner who explores. Phrases like "I'll be telling you exactly what steps to take" and "Follow my plan exactly" establish hierarchical, controlling dynamic that undermines engagement.

2. **Pressure and confrontation increase resistance:** Rather than evoking patient's own motivation, psychiatrist uses commands, threats, and ultimatums. This triggers psychological reactance—patient becomes more defensive and entrenched in resistance as session progresses.

3. **Sustain talk is strengthened, not softened:** Every time patient expresses doubt or reasons for status quo, psychiatrist responds with confrontation. This is opposite of MI technique and predictably strengthens resistance.

4. **No genuine empathy or understanding:** Psychiatrist never attempts to understand patient's perspective, barriers to change, or concerns. Reflective listening absent. Patient's statements dismissed rather than explored.

5. **Autonomy is explicitly denied:** Phrases like "It's not a choice anymore" and "You have to commit fully" remove patient's sense of agency and control. This decreases intrinsic motivation and increases likelihood of non-adherence.

6. **Change talk never evoked:** Psychiatrist imposes reasons for change (health consequences) rather than drawing out patient's own reasons. Intrinsic motivation never explored or developed.

7. **Compliance mistaken for commitment:** By end, patient says "Fine," "Okay," and "Maybe," but these are surface-level compliance responses, not genuine commitment. Final statement—"Whatever"—indicates disengagement and likely non-adherence.

---

### Predicted Clinical Outcomes

**This session is likely to harm rather than help patient's motivation for change:**

- Patient will likely not follow prescribed plan (throw away cigarettes, avoid smoking places, stay busy).
- If patient attempts to quit, likely to relapse quickly because motivation is external (pressure from psychiatrist) rather than internal.
- Patient's trust in mental health system likely to decrease, making future engagement more difficult.
- Therapeutic relationship damaged. Patient leaves session feeling judged, pressured, and unheard.
- Patient's ambivalence about change not explored or resolved; it is attacked, leaving patient more entrenched in resistance.

---

### Recommendations for Improvement

1. **Abandon expert-directive stance and adopt collaborative partnership:** Begin sessions with genuine curiosity about patient's perspective, not predetermined plan. Invite patient to co-create agenda.

2. **Develop genuine empathic listening and reflective responses:** When patient speaks, pause and reflect back both content and emotion before offering own perspective.

3. **Recognize and respect patient autonomy—stop using controlling language:** Replace "you have to," "you need to," "you must" with "you could," "you might consider," "what do you think about?"

4. **Evoke rather than impose motivation—ask about patient's own reasons for change:** Instead of telling patient why they should change, ask what they think, what matters to them, what they've noticed, what concerns them.

5. **Respond to sustain talk with curiosity, not confrontation:** Use double-sided reflections and strategic redirects rather than arguing against patient's position.

6. **Recognize patient's stage of change and adjust approach accordingly:** Patient is in precontemplation. At this stage, goal is engagement and exploration, not action planning.

7. **Build rapport and trust before addressing behavior change:** Spend time understanding patient's world, concerns, strengths, and values before jumping to behavior change.

8. **Stop confronting resistance:** Recognize that confrontation strengthens resistance. When urge to argue arises, pause and reflect patient's perspective with curiosity instead.

9. **Develop skill in evoking and reinforcing change talk:** Learn to identify change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment) and respond in ways that strengthen it.

10. **Receive supervision and feedback on MI competence:** Record sessions, review with MI-trained supervisor, and receive specific feedback on MI techniques. Commit to ongoing development.

---

### Critical Clinical Note

**This psychiatrist requires immediate, intensive training in Motivational Interviewing before continuing to see clients independently.** Current approach is not only ineffective; it is potentially harmful. It violates MI spirit of partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation. It damages therapeutic relationship and decreases likelihood of positive outcomes.

Psychiatrist should:
- Enroll in comprehensive MI training program (minimum 2-3 days intensive training)
- Receive weekly supervision from MI-trained supervisor
- Record and review sessions with feedback
- Practice MI skills in role-plays until competent
- Demonstrate MI competence (MITI score of 4+ on global ratings) before resuming independent practice

Without this training, psychiatrist will continue to harm clients by using confrontation, pressure, and control rather than evoking their own motivation for change.
