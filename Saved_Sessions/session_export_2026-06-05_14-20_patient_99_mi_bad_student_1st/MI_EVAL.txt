### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Complete absence of unconditional positive regard. Psychiatrist demonstrates explicit judgment and disapproval throughout, including direct accusation of laziness ("Are you sure you're not just being lazy about your exercises?") despite clear evidence of patient effort. No affirmation of patient's actual compliance with treatment (attending therapy, taking medications, attempting exercises). Closing threat ("if you don't, your condition will only get worse, and you'll regret it") uses fear and shame as motivators. Fundamental disrespect for patient's experience violates core MI principle of unconditional positive regard.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** While psychiatrist maintains clear focus on physical activity and pain management (appropriate target behavior), manner of maintaining direction is fundamentally incompatible with MI. Direction is achieved through authoritarian control rather than collaborative exploration. Psychiatrist states direction unilaterally ("I'm giving you a plan: physical therapy every day, anti-inflammatory meds as prescribed, and a clean diet with plenty of water. This is non-negotiable") without exploring patient's own goals, values, or preferences. MI-consistent approach would involve asking "What matters most to you about your recovery?" or "What would success look like for you?" to maintain direction while allowing patient to co-create path forward. Session has strategic focus but lacks collaborative element that makes direction effective in MI.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Systematic undermining of autonomy through coercion, threat, and removal of choice. Psychiatrist uses multiple coercive tactics: threat-based language ("if you don't, your condition will only get worse, and you'll regret it"), removal of choice ("This is non-negotiable"), shame and blame ("Are you sure you're not just being lazy?"), and demand for compliance ("I expect you to follow through"). These approaches communicate that patient has no agency in recovery—psychiatrist will dictate what happens. Patient is positioned as passive recipient of orders. When patient expresses doubt ("I'm not sure it'll be easy"), psychiatrist does not validate autonomy to make informed decisions about own care; instead demands compliance without discussion. Research shows autonomy-supportive approaches increase adherence and motivation, while coercive approaches decrease both. This session actively reduces patient's sense of ownership over recovery.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Complete absence of evocation. Psychiatrist imposes motivation through external pressure and threat rather than drawing out patient's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources. Patient demonstrates intrinsic motivation throughout ("I'm doing all kinds of things in rehab like they tell me," "I want to get better," "I'm willing to try," "I'll do my best to stick with it"), but psychiatrist never amplifies these statements or explores what drives them. Skilled MI clinician would ask: "You said you want to get better—what does that mean to you?" or "You're showing up to therapy even though it's hard. What keeps you going?" Instead, psychiatrist dismisses expressions of motivation and replaces them with external demands. Never asks patient about own reasons for change, values, hopes for future, or strengths. Tells patient what to do rather than evoking what patient wants to do. This is opposite of evocation and will result in compliance-based behavior rather than intrinsically motivated change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Zero partnership demonstrated. Psychiatrist assumes complete expert authority and actively suppresses patient input. Opening statement sets tone: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to get better," explicitly positioning psychiatrist as sole source of knowledge and patient as passive recipient. Throughout session, psychiatrist asks only yes/no questions requiring no elaboration, interrupts patient's attempts to explain experience, dismisses patient's concerns as "excuses," accuses patient of laziness, delivers unilateral plan with no input from patient, and uses language of demand and expectation ("I expect you to follow through"). True partnership would involve language like "You know your body better than anyone. What do you think is realistic for you?" or "Let's figure this out together." Instead, patient is treated as object to be fixed rather than partner in own care. Patient's contributions are not sought, valued, or incorporated. By session end, patient's response is resigned ("Okay. I'll do my best to stick with it. Thanks")—not from motivation but from being worn down by authoritarian pressure.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Complete failure to cultivate change talk. Multiple instances of genuine patient motivation are ignored or dismissed rather than amplified and reinforced. Patient's change talk statements include: "I'm going to physical therapy too," "I've been going to my sessions pretty regularly," "I try to do the exercises at home too," "I want to get better," "I'm willing to try," and "I'll do my best to stick with it." These are genuine expressions of desire, ability, and commitment to change. Skilled MI clinician would reinforce each: "You're showing up consistently to therapy—that takes commitment. What does that tell you about yourself?" or "You want to get better. Tell me more about what that would mean for you." Instead, psychiatrist responds to change talk with dismissal and demand for more. When patient says "I want to get better," psychiatrist responds "You have to be disciplined" and "You have to take this seriously"—implying patient's stated desire is insufficient and external pressure is needed. Psychiatrist never asks open questions that would elicit more change talk, never reflects back patient's efforts with appreciation, never explores patient's confidence or capability. Result is patient's intrinsic motivation is never developed or strengthened—only suppressed by external pressure.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk is not skillfully addressed. Psychiatrist uses confrontation and threat rather than reframing, exploration, or double-sided reflection. Patient's sustain talk includes: "It feels like it's kind of slow going," "The pain is still there," "Sometimes it's tough because the pain just wears me down," "Some days it just hurts too much," "It's hard to stay on top of it when my body feels like it's fighting me," "Progress feels kind of slow," "It's frustrating, honestly," and "I just don't know if pushing harder will make it better or just hurt more." Rather than skillfully reframing these concerns or exploring them with curiosity, psychiatrist responds with direct contradiction and demand: "You have to push through the pain—that's just how it works" and "You have to push yourself, no excuses." More skillful approach would involve double-sided reflection ("So on one hand you're committed to therapy, and on the other hand pain is making it hard to push forward"), reframing ("The pain you're experiencing can be a sign that your body is healing"), or exploring ambivalence ("You're worried pushing harder might make it worse. That's a legitimate concern. What would help you feel more confident?"). Psychiatrist does avoid direct argument, preventing session from becoming overtly adversarial, but this is bare minimum and does not constitute skillful softening of sustain talk. Sustain talk is suppressed rather than softened.

The psychiatrist operates from an authoritarian, expert-driven model that is directly contraindicated in MI and actively harmful to patient motivation, engagement, and therapeutic alliance.

Instead, it relies on coercion, threat, shame, and dismissal—approaches that research consistently shows *decrease* adherence and motivation, particularly in clients with chronic pain and ambivalence about change.

**Embodies MI Spirit:** This session does **not** embody MI spirit. It violates core principles of partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation.

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation for Change:** **Extremely low.** Patient enters with intrinsic motivation ("I want to get better," "I'm willing to try"), but psychiatrist systematically suppresses this through coercion and threat. By session end, patient's response is resigned compliance, not genuine motivation. Research shows this approach will likely result in short-term compliance driven by fear and shame, poor long-term adherence, damaged therapeutic alliance, and increased discouragement. **This approach is contraindicated for chronic pain rehabilitation and will likely harm patient's recovery trajectory.**

**Critical Recommendation:** Immediate retraining in MI principles and supervised practice is essential before this clinician continues behavior change counseling.
