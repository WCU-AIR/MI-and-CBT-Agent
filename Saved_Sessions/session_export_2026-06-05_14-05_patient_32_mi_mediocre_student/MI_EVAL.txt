# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal unconditional positive regard. While opening is warm, the overall stance becomes judgmental through repeated directive language ("You might want to," "It would probably help if," "Maybe you should"). When patient explicitly states "I don't really wanna quit. I mean, I like smoking," the response dismisses this preference rather than affirming autonomy. The closing "Good job today!" rewards compliance with the psychiatrist's agenda rather than acknowledging the patient's authentic struggle or inherent worth. No explicit affirmation of patient strengths or right to make own choices about smoking.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Basic reflective listening is present but lacks depth and emotional attunement. One adequate reflection occurs early ("So, it sounds like stress is the main thing making it tough to quit"), but critical emotional statements are missed. When patient says "I like smoking" and "I don't wanna be told what to do," these reveal attachment to smoking and autonomy needs—opportunities for complex reflection are bypassed. Patient's softening of resistance ("Maybe just something to get me through the workday without lighting up. That sounds a bit more doable") is treated as a practical problem rather than explored with empathic curiosity. No complex reflections capture both sides of ambivalence; patient never feels genuinely "gotten."

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains clear, consistent focus on smoking cessation and nicotine replacement throughout the session without wandering. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively established. The goal of smoking reduction/cessation is the psychiatrist's unilateral agenda, not co-created with the patient. When patient expresses "I don't really wanna quit," the psychiatrist does not pause to explore the patient's own goals or values. Instead, the predetermined endpoint (cessation) is assumed and all conversation steered toward it. Session progresses logically toward a plan, but this plan is the psychiatrist's construction, not the patient's.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration is nearly absent. Psychiatrist consistently positions themselves as the expert with answers while patient is positioned as passive recipient of advice. Expert-driven problem-solving dominates: "Maybe you should just pick up some patches," "It would probably help if you keep track," "Maybe you should set some small goals." Closed questions are disguised as open ("Is it the habit or the stress relief?") and funnel toward predetermined answers. Patient's own ideas are never genuinely solicited ("What do you think might help?" is never asked). Final plan (patches, gradual reduction, trigger monitoring) is entirely psychiatrist's construction. Repeated "probably help" and "should" language creates clear hierarchy where psychiatrist knows best and patient should comply.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is critically deficient. While not overtly coercive, the session systematically undermines patient's sense of choice through pervasive directive framing. Prescriptive language appears 12+ times: "Maybe you should start by getting some patches," "It would probably help if you kept track," "Maybe you should just pick up some patches," "It would probably help if you also think about what triggers," "Maybe you should set some small goals." This communicates that psychiatrist has determined the correct path and patient should follow it. Patient's stated preferences ("I don't really wanna quit," "I'm not gonna stress about quitting entirely—that's just not me right now") are not affirmed; instead, they are reframed as temporary obstacles. No explicit statements affirm choice ("This is entirely your decision," "You're in control here," "Whatever you decide, I support you"). Patient moves from resistance to passive compliance ("Yeah, I can do that"), not autonomous motivation.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out patient's own motivations, values, and reasons for change—is almost entirely absent. Multiple openings for evocation are missed: when patient says "Maybe just something to get me through the workday without lighting up. That sounds a bit more doable," psychiatrist does not explore ("What makes that feel more doable?") but instead treats it as confirmation of predetermined plan. Patient's values are never explored; questions like "What's important to you in your life?" or "How does smoking fit with the person you want to be?" are absent. No exploration of patient's own reasons for reducing smoking. When patient offers change talk, it is not amplified but rather treated as practical problem to solve. Psychiatrist tells rather than asks what might help. Result: patient leaves with psychiatrist's plan and psychiatrist's motivations, not patient's own articulated reasons for change.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership—the collaborative stance that expertise and wisdom about change reside within the client—is fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role and maintains it throughout with minimal genuine power-sharing. Expert positioning evident in statements like "Maybe you should start by getting some patches," "It would probably help if you had some guidance on using them properly," "Maybe you should set some small goals." Patient is positioned as someone who needs to be told what to do, not as someone whose knowledge and experience are valued. When patient directly states "I don't wanna be told what to do," psychiatrist acknowledges this but immediately violates it by telling him what to do. No co-construction of solutions; final plan is entirely psychiatrist's. Unilateral goal-setting throughout. Language of hierarchy ("probably help," "should," "Good job today!") reinforces that psychiatrist is authority and patient is subordinate. No acknowledgment that patient is expert on his own life.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Change talk is present in patient's speech but almost never strategically evoked or reinforced. Patient offers multiple openings: "Maybe like during the day nicotine replacement…" (ability/desire); "Maybe just something to get me through the workday without lighting up. That sounds a bit more doable" (ability + desire); "Yeah, maybe cutting down a bit sounds better than quitting dead cold" (ability/desire); "I'll try the patches or gum during work, keep an eye on how it feels" (commitment). When patient says "Maybe just something to get me through the workday without lighting up," psychiatrist should amplify with curiosity but instead shifts focus away and introduces scaling question that invites sustain talk (patient answers "4," which is low). No strategic questions designed to elicit change talk ("What would be good about cutting down?" "How might your life be different?" "What makes you think you could do this?"). Sustain talk is not addressed with curiosity. Compliance with psychiatrist's plan is mistaken for genuine commitment. Patient leaves without having articulated own reasons for change or own commitment to process.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk is acknowledged without confrontation, which is appropriate and prevents defensive escalation. Patient's sustain talk includes: "I don't really wanna quit. I mean, I like smoking"; "I'm not really convinced it'd fix everything"; "I don't wanna be told what to do"; "I'm not gonna stress about quitting entirely—that's just not me right now"; "I'm not ready to quit completely." Psychiatrist avoids arguing or lecturing (e.g., responds "That's understandable" rather than confronting). However, sustain talk is not skillfully softened or redirected. Missed opportunities for double-sided reflection that would normalize ambivalence ("So part of you really enjoys smoking AND at the same time you're here thinking about nicotine replacement"). When patient expresses doubt ("I'm not really convinced it'd fix everything"), underlying concerns are not explored. Implicit reinforcement occurs: psychiatrist acknowledges patient's need for autonomy ("That makes sense—you want something simple") then immediately violates it by telling him what to do. Scaling question ("How important on a scale of 1-10?") invites sustain talk; low score (4) is not followed with curiosity to explore ambivalence.

## Summary of Key Findings

### Strengths Identified:
- ✓ Avoids confrontation and argument; does not lecture or shame patient
- ✓ Maintains basic professional courtesy and warm opening
- ✓ Stays focused on target behavior (smoking) without wandering
- ✓ Develops concrete, actionable plan by session end
- ✓ Demonstrates basic awareness of patient's ambivalence

### Critical Areas for Improvement:
- ✗ **Fundamental MI violation:** Directive and expert-driven rather than collaborative and evocative
- ✗ **Autonomy undermined:** Prescriptive language and dismissal of stated preferences systematically undermine patient's sense of choice
- ✗ **Evocation absent:** Relies on telling rather than asking; patient's own motivations and values never explored
- ✗ **Partnership absent:** Psychiatrist positions self as expert with answers; patient as passive recipient
- ✗ **Change talk not cultivated:** Multiple openings for evoking change talk are missed; compliance mistaken for commitment
- ✗ **Empathy lacks depth:** No complex reflection capturing emotional nuance or ambivalence

---

## Clinical Implications

**Likelihood of Sustained Change:** Low. Patient will likely show short-term compliance (trying patches for days/weeks) but abandon plan when it doesn't immediately solve stress management. Without articulated intrinsic motivation, behavior change is unlikely to sustain.

**Impact on Therapeutic Relationship:** Potential erosion of trust. Patient's explicit statement "I don't wanna be told what to do" is violated repeatedly, teaching patient that autonomy needs are not genuinely respected in healthcare encounters.

**Missed Opportunity:** Patient is in contemplation—ambivalent but not entirely resistant. This is an ideal opening for genuine MI work to evoke intrinsic motivation. Instead, prescriptions close down the collaborative space where real motivation could emerge.

---

## Recommendations for Improvement

**Priority 1 – Shift from Telling to Asking:**
Replace prescriptive language with genuine open questions. Instead of "Maybe you should start by getting some patches," ask: "You mentioned nicotine replacement during the day. What appeals to you about that approach?"

**Priority 2 – Evoke Patient's Own Reasons for Change:**
When patient offers openings (e.g., "Maybe just something to get me through the workday without lighting up"), pause and explore with curiosity: "Tell me more about that. What makes that feel more doable to you?"

**Priority 3 – Explicitly Affirm Autonomy:**
Include statements like: "This is entirely up to you. You know yourself best. Whatever you decide, I'm here to support you."

**Priority 4 – Use Double-Sided Reflection:**
Normalize ambivalence: "So part of you really enjoys smoking and isn't ready to quit completely. And at the same time, you're here, you're thinking about nicotine replacement. Both of those things are true."

**Priority 5 – Collaborate on Goal-Setting:**
Ask patient what he thinks might help and build plan together rather than imposing psychiatrist's construction.
