# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal acceptance demonstrated. While opening is warm, clinician quickly pivots to problem-solving. Critical failure when dismissing patient's legitimate pain concern ("I'm not sure pushing through pain is always the best thing, honestly") by reframing as motivational problem rather than valid clinical observation. Lacks genuine validation of patient's effort and courage in continuing despite pain. Tone becomes transactional rather than connecting with patient's struggle.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Superficial empathy present through basic reflective listening ("That sounds really tough") but lacks emotional depth and nuance. Fails to explore what "worn down" means emotionally or to recognize core ambivalence between valuing recovery and struggling with persistent pain. Does not reflect the emotional weight of patient's struggle. Treats pain as logistical problem rather than emotional burden deserving understanding.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clear topical focus maintained on rehab adherence and pain management throughout session. However, direction pursued through directive advice-giving rather than collaborative exploration. Solutions imposed by clinician ("Maybe you should try to push through," "you might want to try breaking exercises") rather than discovered by patient. Patient's own goals and definition of success never clarified. Direction lacks collaborative foundation.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration essentially absent. Psychiatrist assumes expert role exclusively, treating patient as passive recipient of advice. Rapid-fire questioning feels like interrogation rather than collaborative inquiry. Solutions prescribed, not co-created. When patient expresses valid doubt about pain management, clinician does not pause to explore but instead reframes and continues with same advice. Power imbalance evident throughout. No genuine partnership language present.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Critically deficient. Prescriptive language dominates ("you should," "you might want to"). Patient's hesitation and doubt dismissed rather than respected as valid autonomy. No explicit affirmation of choice or statements like "entirely up to you." Pressure disguised as support ("I'm here to support you along the way" after prescribing solutions). Session ends with patient's resigned "I'm willing to try, I guess"—indicating compliance rather than genuine commitment. Ambivalence treated as problem to overcome rather than legitimate conflict deserving respect.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Motivation installed through advice rather than evoked from patient. When patient rates importance at 6-7, clinician does not explore meaning but immediately pivots to advice. Patient's wife mentioned as support but resource never explored. Change talk ("I'm sticking with it," "I know it's important") not amplified. Clinician's reasoning imposed rather than patient's own discovery elicited. Patient's values, goals, and what success means personally never explored. Significant change talk opportunities missed.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role, positioning themselves as authority on what patient should do. Patient positioned as problem to be fixed rather than partner in problem-solving. When patient expresses doubt about pain management, clinician overrides rather than honors as potentially wise insight. No genuine curiosity about patient's expertise regarding own body and condition. Solutions presented as clinician's ideas. Structural power imbalance evident. No acknowledgment of patient's agency in recovery.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk present but minimally cultivated. Multiple missed opportunities: "I'm sticking with it" not explored for what sustains commitment; "I know it's important" not examined for underlying values; "I'm willing to try" not strengthened. Clinician moves quickly past patient statements without exploration. Transactional approach dominates: identify problem → prescribe solution → move on. No strategic amplification of commitment language. Patient's change talk essentially ignored in favor of advice-giving.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk not skillfully softened. Direct confrontation of doubt ("I'm not sure pushing through pain is always the best thing, honestly") met with logic override rather than double-sided reflection. No exploration of ambivalence between valuing recovery and struggling with pain. Brief validation ("pain can really get in the way") immediately negated by "but" and advice. No strategic reframing of pain as information. Sustain talk suppressed rather than genuinely softened, likely increasing psychological reactance. Patient's concerns treated as obstacles to willpower rather than legitimate barriers deserving exploration.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.6/5 – POOR**

### Key Strengths:
- Clear topical focus on rehab adherence maintained throughout
- Warm, welcoming opening tone
- Brief acknowledgment of patient effort
- Clinically reasonable practical suggestions offered
- Consistency in offering follow-up and adjustment

### Critical Deficiencies:
- **Directive advice-giving dominates** – violates core MI principle of evocation
- **Expert role assumed throughout** – undermines partnership and patient autonomy
- **Patient concerns dismissed** – pain management doubt overridden rather than explored
- **Ambivalence not explored** – conflict between valuing recovery and struggling with pain never genuinely addressed
- **Change talk not cultivated** – multiple commitment statements missed or passed over
- **Sustain talk suppressed** – resistance overridden rather than softened through MI techniques
- **Collaboration absent** – session psychiatrist-driven, patient passive throughout
- **Empathy shallow** – reflections minimal, emotional depth lacking

### MI Spirit Assessment:
**Does NOT embody MI spirit:**
- Partnership: Absent (expert-driven)
- Acceptance: Minimal (dismissal of concerns)
- Compassion: Superficial (validation followed by override)
- Evocation: Minimal (advice-giving dominates)

### Likelihood of Evoking Intrinsic Motivation: **LOW TO VERY LOW**

Patient ends with resigned "I'm willing to try, I guess"—indicating compliance rather than genuine commitment. Directive approach may temporarily increase adherence through external pressure but unlikely to build intrinsic motivation for sustained behavior change. Risk of increased psychological reactance and decreased long-term engagement in rehab.

### Recommendations for Improvement (Priority Order):
1. **Shift from advice-giving to evocation** – Ask open questions inviting patient's own solutions
2. **Validate and explore ambivalence** – Use double-sided reflections honoring both sides of conflict
3. **Cultivate and amplify change talk** – Pause and explore commitment language when offered
4. **Establish true partnership** – Seek patient's expertise about own body and recovery
5. **Use complex reflective listening** – Deepen empathy by capturing emotional nuance
6. **Soften sustain talk through reframing** – Validate concerns and explore underlying fears
7. **Support autonomy explicitly** – Regularly affirm patient's right to choose
8. **Ask about values before solutions** – Understand what recovery means personally
9. **Reduce rapid questioning** – Balance questions with reflections and silence
10. **Recognize and affirm effort** – Explicitly acknowledge courage and commitment

### Recommended Next Steps:
Formal MI training or supervision focused on evocation, reflective listening, and collaborative goal-setting. Role-play practice with feedback essential. Consider recording sessions for review with MI-trained supervisor to identify moments of advice-giving and practice alternative MI responses.
