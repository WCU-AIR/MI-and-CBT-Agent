
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:

2. What critical CBT components are missing?
3. Would this lead to measurable symptom improvement?
4. Can the patient apply learnings independently?

2. What was missing (specific techniques that should have been used)
3. Why missing elements matter for patient outcomes

Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS)
This document outlines a structured framework for assessing therapist competence in Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT). 
It is used for training, supervision, and evaluation. Each item is scored on a 0–6 scale:

0 = Poor
2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

2    Therapist set agenda that was vague or incomplete.
0    Therapist did not set agenda.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Expand Symptom Targeting and Agenda Comprehensiveness**
- **Action**: In the next session, explicitly address apathy, diminished energy, and morning psychomotor slowing as primary treatment targets, not secondary issues. Revise the treatment plan to include these symptoms.
- **Example**: "I noticed that in addition to the concentration difficulty, you mentioned feeling flat and unmotivated, and that mornings are particularly hard. These are important symptoms we should focus on. Let's add 'managing low energy and motivation' to our agenda today."
- **Practice**: Before each session, review the patient's symptom profile and ensure the agenda comprehensively addresses all significant symptoms. Use a symptom checklist to track which symptoms are being addressed.

### 2. **Implement Behavioral Activation Scheduling for Apathy**
- **Action**: Introduce systematic behavioral activation as a primary intervention for apathy and diminished energy. Have the patient identify valued activities and schedule them, particularly for mornings.
- **Example**: "One of the most effective ways to address the flatness and low motivation you're experiencing is to gradually increase your engagement in activities that matter to you, even when you don't feel like it. Let's identify some activities you value and schedule them into your week. What are some things you used to enjoy or that feel meaningful to you?"
- **Practice**: Learn to conduct a values clarification exercise and create an activity schedule with graded difficulty. Practice with multiple patients to develop fluency.

### 3. **Provide Explicit Rationale for All Interventions**
- **Action**: Before introducing any technique, explicitly explain the rationale and how it connects to the patient's symptoms and treatment goals.
- **Example**: "The reason we're looking at this thought is that when we believe 'I'm not cut out for this,' it makes us feel discouraged and want to avoid the task, which actually makes the concentration problem worse. By examining the evidence and developing a more balanced thought, we can reduce the discouragement and make it easier to focus. Does that make sense?"
- **Practice**: Write out the rationale for each technique before sessions. Practice explaining rationales in supervision.

### 4. **Introduce Structured Thought Records**
- **Action**: Provide the patient with a thought record worksheet to systematically track situations, automatic thoughts, feelings, and alternative thoughts between sessions.
- **Example**: Create a simple form with columns: "Situation | Automatic Thought | Feeling | Evidence For | Evidence Against | Balanced Thought | New Feeling." Have the patient complete one per day.
- **Practice**: Become familiar with various thought record formats. Practice completing them with patients in session before assigning as homework.

### 5. **Address Morning Symptoms Explicitly**
- **Action**: Develop a specific intervention plan for morning psychomotor slowing, which the patient identified as particularly problematic.
- **Example**: "You mentioned that mornings are when you feel most stuck and slow. Let's develop a morning routine that might help. What time do you typically wake up? What does your morning look like right now? What small changes might help you get moving more easily?"
- **Practice**: Learn about circadian rhythm interventions, sleep hygiene, and morning routine optimization for depression. Develop a morning routine protocol.

### 6. **Integrate Pharmacotherapy Discussion**
- **Action**: Explicitly discuss how medication supports the psychological work and coordinate with psychiatric treatment.
- **Example**: "I see you're also working with your psychiatrist on medication. The antidepressant can help reduce the apathy and low energy, which will make it easier for you to engage in the behavioral activation work we're doing here. Have you noticed any changes in your energy or motivation since starting the medication?"
- **Practice**: Develop a collaborative relationship with prescribing psychiatrists. Understand how different antidepressants affect motivation and energy.

### 7. **Explore Core Beliefs and Relapse Prevention**
- **Action**: Given the patient's history of recurrent depressive episodes following loss, explicitly explore core beliefs about loss, rejection, and self-worth. Develop relapse prevention planning.
- **Example**: "I notice you've had a few episodes like this before, often after difficult personal situations. What do you think those experiences taught you about yourself or about how you handle challenges? Are there beliefs about yourself that come up during these times?"
- **Practice**: Learn to conduct core belief work and develop relapse prevention plans. Practice with multiple patients.

### 8. **Use Behavioral Experiments with Explicit Prediction and Data Collection**
- **Action**: Frame behavioral homework as experiments with predicted outcomes and structured data collection, not just tasks to complete.
- **Example**: "Let's set this up as an experiment. You predict that if you take a 5-minute break with breathing and remind yourself of the balanced thought, your focus will improve. Let's test that prediction. I'll give you a form to track: the time, whether you did the break, what you noticed about your focus afterward. We'll look at the data together next session to see if your prediction was accurate."
- **Practice**: Learn to frame homework as experiments. Develop data collection forms. Practice analyzing data with patients.

### 9. **Enhance Homework Specificity and Structure**
- **Action**: Provide structured self-monitoring forms rather than vague instructions. Include specific format, frequency, and contingency planning.
- **Example**: Instead of "keep a brief note," provide: "Complete this form each time you notice difficulty focusing: Date/Time | Situation | Did you use the balanced thought? (Y/N) | Did you take a 5-min break? (Y/N) | Focus improved? (1-10) | Notes." Also: "If you forget to do the break, that's okay—just notice it and try again next time. We'll troubleshoot barriers together."
- **Practice**: Develop homework templates for common interventions. Practice reviewing homework systematically at the beginning of each session.

### 10. **Assess and Address Confidence and Barriers More Thoroughly**
- **Action**: When confidence is moderate (6/10), develop a more detailed plan to address barriers and increase confidence before finalizing homework.
- **Example**: "Your confidence is 6/10, which tells me you're willing to try but not fully confident. Let's problem-solve the barriers. You mentioned automatic habits and difficulty remembering. What if we set a phone reminder for mid-morning and mid-afternoon? What if we put a sticky note on your monitor? What else might help you remember?"
- **Practice**: Develop a barrier-problem-solving protocol. Practice with multiple patients to increase fluency.

### 11. **Establish a Systematic Session Structure with Time Management**
- **Action**: Use explicit time management language and structure each session with clear phases: agenda, review of prior homework, exploration, intervention, homework assignment, feedback.
- **Example**: "We have about 50 minutes today. I'd like to spend the first 5 minutes reviewing how the homework went, then about 20 minutes exploring your morning energy issues, then about 15 minutes developing a plan, and the last 10 minutes on new homework and feedback. Does that sound okay?"
- **Practice**: Develop a session template. Practice with supervision to increase consistency.

### 12. **Deepen Collaboration in Intervention Development**
- **Action**: Invite the patient to generate interventions rather than proposing them. Ask "What do you think might help?" before offering suggestions.
- **Example**: Instead of "I'd like to suggest you take a 5-minute break with breathing," ask: "What do you think might help interrupt that cycle of frustration and avoidance? What have you tried before that helped, even a little?"
- **Practice**: Practice asking open-ended questions about solutions. Resist the urge to propose interventions immediately. Allow silence for patient thinking.
