Alcohol Use Disorder (in early remission)
Medication-Induced Anxiety Disorder (steroids)

--- CASE STUDY ---

Robert Jennings, a 50-year-old married white man, was referred for psychiatric evaluation by a liver transplant liaison service. He had advanced cirrhosis secondary to alcohol use disorder and was recently hospitalized for acute alcohol-associated hepatitis. Following discharge, he began a mandatory addiction treatment program required for transplant eligibility. Alprazolam 0.5 mg as needed was prescribed for anxiety, initially helpful, but soon he became irritable, lethargic, and forgetful.

Mr. Jennings had been unaware of the severity of his liver disease until hospitalization and reported an initial post-discharge period of enhanced energy and well-being, followed by anxiety, restlessness, poor sleep, irritability, distractibility, and worry about health, finances, and family. He denied depressive symptoms, suicidality, panic attacks, or trauma-related symptoms. He had a 10-year history of daily alcohol use but denied consumption since hospitalization.

On examination, he appeared thin, jaundiced, and tired. He was fidgety but cooperative, with anxious and irritable affect. Speech was terse, thought processes logical and coherent, cognition mildly impaired (MoCA 26/30), and attention slightly reduced (Trails B required repeated instructions). There were no psychotic symptoms or overt delirium.

Discussion: Mr. Jennings’s anxiety and irritability appear temporally related to steroid therapy (prednisolone), which can induce initial euphoria followed by negative mood symptoms. Other potential contributors include early alcohol withdrawal and minimal hepatic encephalopathy (subtle cognitive and behavioral changes associated with advanced liver disease). Major depressive disorder and trauma-related disorders were considered but ruled out based on symptom profile. Benzodiazepines can worsen cognitive function and risk relapse; they were discontinued. Focused management includes monitoring for hepatic encephalopathy, optimizing ammonia-reducing treatments, and careful psychiatric follow-up in the context of transplant readiness.
