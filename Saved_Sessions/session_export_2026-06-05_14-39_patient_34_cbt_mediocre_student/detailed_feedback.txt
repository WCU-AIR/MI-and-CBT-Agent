
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda

**Explanation:**

Does that sound okay?" This is minimally collaborative and lacks specificity. While the therapist did ask permission, there was no discussion of priorities, time allocation, or concrete session goals. Critically, the therapist never established a treatment plan or session-by-session strategy aligned with Ken's diagnosis of depersonalization/derealization disorder. In the second session, the therapist repeated the identical opening without reference to the previous session's homework or progress, suggesting no continuity of treatment planning. A proper CBT agenda would have included: (1) review of homework from last session, (2) identification of target symptoms (depersonalization/derealization), (3) prioritization of which symptoms to address first, and (4) time allocation for specific interventions.

The therapist also failed to establish priorities collaboratively. Ken presented multiple overlapping problems (depersonalization, derealization, social withdrawal, academic decline, emotional numbness, rumination), but the therapist never helped Ken identify which was most distressing or most functionally impairing. This represents a fundamental gap in CBT structure.

**Summary:**

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated basic comprehension of Ken's explicit statements but missed critical nuances of his presentation and failed to grasp the core nature of his disorder. When Ken said, "I feel sort of detached all the time. Like I'm not really... here, you know?" the therapist accurately reflected this as "feeling detached and low energy." However, the therapist then immediately reframed Ken's depersonalization/derealization symptoms as primarily a cognitive problem ("What kind of thoughts go through your mind?"), which represents a significant misunderstanding of the disorder.

Depersonalization/derealization disorder is characterized by persistent, distressing feelings of detachment from one's body, self, or environment—not primarily by negative automatic thoughts. While cognitions may accompany these symptoms, the core pathology is the dissociative experience itself. The therapist's repeated attempts to identify and challenge "pointless" thoughts missed the fundamental issue: Ken's sense of unreality and emotional numbness is not primarily driven by thought content but by dissociative processes.

When Ken explicitly stated, "It's like I'm watching myself from far away, and it's hard to get involved," the therapist did not explore this depersonalization experience in depth. Instead, the therapist pivoted to thought-challenging, suggesting Ken remind himself that "showing up is still important." This demonstrates a failure to understand that Ken's problem is not motivational or thought-based—it's a dissociative disconnection from his body and self.

Additionally, when Ken said, "It's hard to believe it would make much of a difference though" and "It's like even if I want to connect, there's this wall or gap between me and everyone else," the therapist did not recognize these as expressions of hopelessness and persistent dissociation. Instead, the therapist continued with the same cognitive intervention approach, suggesting Ken practice self-talk. The therapist missed Ken's implicit message: "Your interventions don't address what I'm actually experiencing."

The therapist also failed to understand the severity and persistence of Ken's symptoms. Ken has depersonalization/derealization disorder—a chronic condition requiring specialized treatment—not situational depression from a breakup. The therapist's approach was generic and did not reflect understanding of this specific diagnosis.

**Summary:**
The therapist grasped Ken's surface-level complaints but fundamentally misunderstood the nature of depersonalization/derealization disorder, treating it as a cognitive/motivational problem rather than a dissociative condition requiring specialized intervention.

### 5. Collaboration
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**
