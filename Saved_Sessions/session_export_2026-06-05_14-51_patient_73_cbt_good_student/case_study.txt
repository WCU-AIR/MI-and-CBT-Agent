--- DISORDER ---
Panic Disorder

--- CASE STUDY ---

Maria Greco, a 23-year-old single woman, was referred for psychiatric evaluation by her cardiologist after multiple episodes of sudden-onset palpitations, shortness of breath, sweating, trembling, and fear of dying. Over the prior 2 months, she presented to the emergency room four times with these symptoms, which peaked within minutes and were followed by exhaustion and persistent worry about her heart health despite normal medical evaluations.

Her panic episodes occurred at home, at work, while driving, and even during sleep. She developed anticipatory anxiety, avoiding driving, exercise, and caffeine, and took multiple days off work. These behavioral restrictions contributed to sleep disruption, mood decline, and social withdrawal. She did not accept reassurance from physicians or friends, believing negative medical workups were inconclusive.

Maria has a childhood history of anxiety diagnosed as school phobia, which appears to have remitted. Her mother had recurrent major depressive disorder and died by suicide four years earlier. She lives with her father and two younger siblings, works as a telephone operator, and has no romantic relationships.

On examination, Maria was anxious but cooperative and coherent. She denied depression, psychosis, or suicidal ideation. Cognition was intact, insight was limited, and judgment was fair.

Discussion: Maria meets criteria for panic disorder, with recurrent unexpected panic attacks accompanied by physical symptoms (palpitations, sweating, trembling, chest pain, shortness of breath) and persistent worry about future attacks. Symptoms have lasted more than one month and significantly impair social and occupational functioning. Medical and substance causes have been excluded. Although depressive symptoms are present, they do not meet criteria for a major depressive episode, and other psychiatric conditions, including bipolar disorder and posttraumatic stress disorder, should be assessed longitudinally. Panic episodes are classified as unexpected. Avoidance behaviors (driving, exercise, caffeine) represent complications of the disorder rather than agoraphobia.
