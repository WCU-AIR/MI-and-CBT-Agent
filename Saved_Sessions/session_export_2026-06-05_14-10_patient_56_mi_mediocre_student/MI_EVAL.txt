# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal acceptance and fails to communicate unconditional positive regard. While the opening greeting is warm, the clinician quickly shifts to a directive, problem-focused stance. When Andrea explicitly states she doesn't see the need for change, the psychiatrist dismisses her perspective and prescribes change anyway ("But maybe it would help if you tried to get more sleep or eat better, right?"). When Andrea expresses legitimate concerns about being busy, the psychiatrist acknowledges briefly but then minimizes and pushes forward with the agenda. Surface-level politeness masks a fundamental lack of genuine acceptance of Andrea's perspective, autonomy, and current choices.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Empathy is superficial and inconsistently applied. The psychiatrist makes minimal effort to understand Andrea's actual perspective or the meaning behind her statements. When Andrea provides clear signals about her ambivalence and lack of motivation, the clinician offers surface-level acknowledgments ("I see") followed immediately by contradiction ("But maybe it would help..."). True empathy would involve deeper reflection of emotional and motivational content. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore Andrea's actual concerns—when she mentions being "used to how things are," this suggests fear of disruption or lack of perceived benefit, neither of which are explored. Empathy is transactional rather than transformative.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains clear focus on health habits (exercise, diet, sleep) throughout the session, demonstrating adequate directional consistency. However, the direction is problematic in MI terms because it is clinician-imposed rather than collaboratively established. The psychiatrist decides at the outset that health habits are the focus without exploring what Andrea actually wants to discuss. Most significantly, given that Andrea was identified as "high-risk for alcohol involvement" through school screening, the complete absence of any exploration of alcohol use represents a critical directional failure. Within the narrow domain of general health habits, direction becomes increasingly prescriptive rather than exploratory.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** This session demonstrates a profound absence of genuine collaboration. The psychiatrist operates almost entirely from an expert-driven, hierarchical stance. Unilateral agenda-setting occurs without asking Andrea what she wants to discuss. When Andrea expresses low motivation ("maybe like a 2"), the psychiatrist overrides it rather than exploring it. All proposed solutions come from the psychiatrist; Andrea is never asked "What do you think might work?" The clinician's closing summary is entirely clinician-generated, not co-constructed. Andrea's input is solicited minimally and incorporated rarely. This is fundamentally non-partnership, authoritarian practice.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** This is the most severely deficient area. The psychiatrist systematically undermines Andrea's autonomy through repeated prescriptions, dismissal of her stated priorities, and minimization of legitimate barriers. When Andrea rates importance as "2/5," the psychiatrist responds "maybe you should still try"—direct pressure that contradicts her stated priorities. When Andrea identifies real constraints, these are acknowledged superficially but then minimized ("Alright, I get that you're busy... Maybe you could just try one small thing"). The language is consistently directive ("you should," "maybe you could") rather than autonomy-supporting ("It's completely up to you," "You're the expert on your own life"). True autonomy support is entirely absent.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out the client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources—is almost entirely absent. The psychiatrist relies on telling rather than asking. Most questions are closed or leading ("Do you exercise enough?" "Does that make it hard to feel rested?") rather than open-ended. Andrea's values are never explored; when she mentions being in the band and IB program, these are never explored as resources or sources of meaning. The psychiatrist tells Andrea what would help rather than asking her to generate ideas. Andrea's ambivalence is never explored to evoke change talk. There is virtually no change talk in this session because the psychiatrist never asks questions designed to evoke it.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership requires that the clinician conveys understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client. This psychiatrist actively assumes the expert role and maintains hierarchical control throughout. The clinician decides the topic unilaterally, prescribes solutions, and asserts expertise about outcomes. When Andrea provides information about her current habits and low motivation, these contributions do not substantially influence session direction. The psychiatrist hears them but does not incorporate them into a collaborative plan. All proposed solutions are clinician-generated. The closing summary is entirely clinician-constructed. There is no acknowledgment that Andrea is the expert on her own life, constraints, priorities, or readiness for change.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Change talk—client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change—is virtually absent, and the psychiatrist makes no meaningful effort to evoke or cultivate it. The psychiatrist never asks strategic questions designed to elicit change talk such as "What would be the benefits?" or "How confident are you that you could...?" When Andrea produces one small piece of change talk at the end ("I'll think about maybe trying to get to bed a bit earlier"), the psychiatrist's response is minimal and non-reinforcing. Andrea's sustain talk is met with prescription rather than skillful softening. The session relies entirely on clinician prescription rather than client-generated motivation.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Sustain talk is abundant in this session, and the psychiatrist handles it in ways directly contrary to MI principles. When Andrea says "I don't really see the point in changing stuff like that," the psychiatrist responds with "But maybe it would help..."—the word "but" directly negates her statement and attempts to override her perspective. When Andrea identifies legitimate barriers ("extra stuff to do when I already have a lot on my plate"), these are minimized rather than validated. No double-sided reflections are used to acknowledge both sides of her ambivalence. No reframing occurs to soften resistance. By the end of the session, Andrea's sustain talk has likely been strengthened, not softened, as evidenced by her final statement "But no promises."

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths
- Warm opening greeting establishes a polite, welcoming tone
- Clear topic focus maintained throughout the session
- Organized closing summary, though clinician-generated
- Persistence in engagement despite client resistance

### Critical Areas for Improvement
- **Fundamental MI spirit violations:** Systematic violations of core MI principles; expert-driven, hierarchical stance rather than collaborative partnership
- **Autonomy undermined:** Andrea's stated priorities and resistance repeatedly overridden with prescriptions
- **Evocation absent:** Reliance on telling rather than asking; motivation installed rather than evoked
- **Sustain talk mishandled:** Client resistance met with direct contradiction and prescription, which strengthens rather than softens resistance
- **Critical content missing:** Complete absence of exploration of presenting concern (alcohol risk); focus on generic health habits suggests inadequate preparation
- **Change talk not cultivated:** Despite multiple opportunities, no strategic evocation or reinforcement of Andrea's own reasons for change

### Impact on Client Engagement
This session is likely to **decrease** Andrea's intrinsic motivation and **increase** her resistance. Her final statement ("But no promises") reflects minimal change talk with explicit hedging, suggesting low commitment. The psychiatrist's approach of prescribing change despite Andrea's stated low motivation and legitimate barriers will likely be experienced as controlling and dismissive, damaging the therapeutic alliance and reducing likelihood of behavior change.

---

## Key Recommendations

1. **Establish genuine collaboration from the outset** – Ask what the client wants to discuss rather than imposing an agenda
2. **Address the actual presenting concern** – Clarify and explore the alcohol risk screening collaboratively
3. **Respond to low motivation with curiosity, not prescription** – Explore what would make change feel important
4. **Validate legitimate barriers** – Acknowledge time, energy, and competing priorities as real constraints
5. **Ask rather than tell** – Replace prescriptive statements with open-ended evocative questions
6. **Cultivate and reinforce change talk** – Strategically evoke and amplify client's own reasons for change
7. **Skillfully handle sustain talk** – Use double-sided reflections to validate ambivalence rather than contradict it
8. **Support autonomy explicitly** – Use language emphasizing client choice and ownership; eliminate "should"
9. **Conduct collaborative goal-setting** – Invite client to summarize and co-create next steps
10. **Receive formal MI training** – Engage in workshop, certification program, or supervised practice to develop competence

---

**Overall Assessment:** This session represents a significant departure from motivational interviewing practice. Immediate action is needed: formal MI training, clinical supervision on recorded sessions, and fundamental shift from expert-driven to collaborative, client-centered approach. Without these changes, likelihood of evoking meaningful behavior change is minimal and therapeutic alliance is at risk.
