
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and contains multiple instances of explicit judgment and disapproval. The psychiatrist frames Isabella's struggles as "laziness" ("Forgetting meds is just laziness; you need to be more disciplined"), directly pathologizes her emotional experience as "weakness" ("Feeling tired or unmotivated is just a weakness you need to overcome"), and dismisses her legitimate concerns with "stop feeling sorry for yourself." These statements communicate clear disapproval of Isabella as a person, not just her behaviors.

The psychiatrist's opening statement—"I want to talk about your health and what you need to do to improve it"—immediately establishes a judgmental, prescriptive stance rather than collaborative exploration. When Isabella expresses that changes feel "overwhelming," the psychiatrist responds with pressure and criticism rather than validation. There is no affirmation of Isabella's efforts (she is taking meds sometimes, which represents partial compliance), no normalization of struggle, and no genuine warmth. The repeated rhetorical question "Why haven't you done that already?" carries implicit blame and judgment.

Most critically, the psychiatrist never acknowledges Isabella's ambivalence, fear of failure, or the genuine difficulty she experiences. When Isabella states "I'm scared it won't help or that I'll just mess up again," the appropriate response would be acceptance and understanding—instead, she receives pressure to "push through" and accusations of not taking things seriously. This directly violates the MI principle of accepting the client's experience as valid.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates consistently judgmental, disapproving language that communicates conditional regard based on behavioral compliance. Acceptance is entirely absent; the session is characterized by blame, criticism, and dismissal of Isabella's legitimate struggles.

---

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective from their internal frame of reference. This session contains virtually no reflective listening and demonstrates a fundamental failure to understand or validate Isabella's experience.

Isabella explicitly communicates her internal struggle: "It's hard to stay disciplined when I'm feeling this way," "It's not that simple for me," "Sometimes it feels like I don't have the energy or motivation," and "I'm scared it won't help or that I'll just mess up again." These are clear windows into her emotional and psychological state. The psychiatrist's response to each is dismissal: "that's just weakness," "you have to push through that," "stop feeling sorry for yourself."

There are zero reflective statements in this entire session. A single empathic reflection might sound like: "It sounds like you want to do better, but you're worried that if you try a strict schedule, you might fail again and feel even worse about yourself—is that right?" Instead, the psychiatrist asks only closed questions ("Are you forgetting or just not motivated?") and makes declarative statements about what Isabella "must" do.

The psychiatrist never explores Isabella's ambivalence, her fears, her previous experiences with failure, or what might make change feel more manageable. When Isabella says "it's overwhelming," the psychiatrist interprets this as resistance to be overcome rather than information about her current capacity. This represents a fundamental failure of empathic understanding.

**Summary:**

The session contains no reflective listening, no validation of Isabella's emotional experience, and no genuine attempt to understand her perspective. Empathy is entirely absent; the psychiatrist operates from an expert-driven, problem-focused stance that dismisses rather than understands.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

While the psychiatrist maintains a clear focus on medication adherence and exercise (which are appropriate targets), the approach to maintaining direction is fundamentally misaligned with MI principles. Direction in MI means maintaining strategic focus while remaining client-centered and collaborative—not imposing direction through pressure and authority.

The psychiatrist does consistently redirect toward the target behaviors (meds, exercise, lifestyle change), which prevents aimless wandering. However, this is accomplished through directive statements ("You really must," "You have to," "It's not optional; it's a must") rather than collaborative exploration. The psychiatrist never asks Isabella what she thinks might help, what barriers she faces, or how she might approach change in a way that feels manageable to her.

The repeated "Why haven't you done that already?" questions are particularly problematic. Rather than exploring Isabella's perspective on barriers and solutions, these questions imply blame and demand justification. They maintain focus through confrontation rather than collaboration. A more MI-consistent approach would maintain direction while exploring: "What do you think would need to happen for medication reminders to feel doable?" or "What would make exercise feel less overwhelming?"

The psychiatrist also fails to clarify Isabella's own goals and values. We never learn what Isabella wants for herself, what matters to her about health, or what she envisions as success. Direction without this foundation becomes imposition rather than strategic guidance.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains focus on target behaviors but does so through directive pressure rather than collaborative exploration. Direction is present but executed in ways inconsistent with MI spirit and client-centered practice.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have valuable knowledge. This session demonstrates an entirely hierarchical, expert-driven approach with no genuine collaboration.

The psychiatrist establishes authority immediately: "I want to talk about your health and what you need to do to improve it." The language is prescriptive throughout ("You really need," "You must," "You have to," "You should"). Isabella's input is never solicited regarding solutions. When she tentatively suggests "maybe I could" try reminders, the psychiatrist doesn't build on this—instead, she continues with demands about exercise.

True collaboration would sound like: "You mentioned maybe trying reminders—what do you think that might look like for you?" or "You know yourself better than anyone. What do you think might help you get started with even small changes?" Instead, the psychiatrist tells Isabella what to do and demands explanations for why she hasn't done it.

The psychiatrist never asks Isabella about her preferences, her ideas about what might work, her previous attempts at change, or what she thinks would be realistic. When Isabella expresses concern about "forcing" herself on a strict schedule because she's "worried I'll just fail and feel worse about it," the psychiatrist doesn't explore this—she simply insists Isabella must do it anyway. This completely dismisses Isabella's self-knowledge about what might backfire.

The power dynamic is entirely one-directional: psychiatrist as expert with answers, Isabella as non-compliant patient who needs to be corrected. There is no co-construction of solutions, no shared problem-solving, and no genuine partnership.

**Summary:**

The session is entirely hierarchical and expert-driven. The psychiatrist makes all decisions, provides all solutions, and demands compliance. No genuine collaboration or power-sharing occurs; Isabella's contributions are ignored or overridden.

### 5. Autonomy Support
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Autonomy support means actively fostering the client's perception of choice and emphasizing their ownership of the change process. This session does the opposite—it minimizes choice and emphasizes external control and obligation.

The psychiatrist uses language that eliminates choice: "You really must," "You need to," "It's not optional; it's a must," "You have to push yourself." These statements communicate that Isabella has no choice in the matter. When Isabella expresses hesitation or concern, the psychiatrist interprets this as resistance to be overcome rather than as valid input about her own capacity and readiness.

Most problematically, when Isabella says "Maybe I could try a little bit, but I'm not sure I can handle a big, strict routine right now," the psychiatrist responds by insisting on the strict routine anyway. This directly contradicts autonomy support. An autonomy-supportive response would be: "You're thinking about trying something, which is great. What would feel manageable to you right now—even if it's smaller than 30 minutes of exercise?"

The psychiatrist also uses fear and threat to motivate: "If you don't, your health will only get worse. Why are you still hesitating? Are you not taking this seriously?" This is controlling motivation, not autonomy support. It pressures Isabella to comply out of fear rather than supporting her intrinsic motivation to change.

There is no acknowledgment of Isabella's right to choose, no affirmation that she is the expert on her own life, and no genuine respect for her autonomy. The message is clear: "Do what I say or face consequences."

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist consistently minimizes choice, uses controlling language, and pressures compliance through fear and obligation. Autonomy is actively undermined; Isabella receives no support for her right to self-determination or ownership of her change process.

### 6. Evocation
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Evocation means drawing out the client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources rather than installing motivation through advice and persuasion. This session demonstrates the opposite—pure installation of motivation through directive statements and pressure.

Isabella actually provides several opportunities for evocation. She says "I guess I want to do better," "I do want to take this seriously," and "Maybe I could try."
