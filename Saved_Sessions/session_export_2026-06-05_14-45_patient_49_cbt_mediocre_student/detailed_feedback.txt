
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made a minimal attempt at agenda-setting in the opening ("I thought we could talk a bit about whatever's been on your mind recently"). This is vague and non-collaborative. The therapist offered to "just pick something to focus on" if the patient preferred—an abdication of shared decision-making that undermines collaborative CBT. **Summary:**

Agenda-setting was absent in both sessions. The therapist used vague, non-directive language and failed to establish explicit, measurable session goals or prioritize among treatment targets. This is a critical foundational failure in CBT structure.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrates basic comprehension of the patient's explicit presenting problem: early morning awakenings at 3:00 AM, daytime fatigue, difficulty concentrating, and worry about work performance. The therapist accurately reflects these concerns ("that sounds tough with your sleep being disrupted like that").

However, the therapist's understanding is superficial and misses critical nuances:

1. **Missed the maintenance cycle:** The patient explicitly states, "It's hard to just tell myself my body is resting when my mind feels so active worrying about the day ahead. It's like that anxious part of me kicks in and makes it worse." This is a clear description of the cognitive-behavioral maintenance cycle: early awakening → worry about the day → anxiety → inability to return to sleep. The therapist does not identify or name this cycle, nor does the therapist explore how worry *perpetuates* the insomnia. Instead, the therapist offers generic cognitive reframing without addressing the functional relationship between anxiety and sleep maintenance.

2. **Missed the anticipatory anxiety component:** The case formulation notes "anticipatory anxiety about the day ahead" as a key maintaining factor. The patient confirms this in Session 1 ("I worry that I won't finish my dissertation or that I'm not at my best when teaching"). The therapist never explores *when* these worries occur, *how long* they persist, or *what specific content* triggers them. This is essential for targeted cognitive work.

The therapist never asks about this behavior or its consequences. Getting up to work may reinforce the association between wakefulness and productivity, undermining sleep consolidation. This is a critical behavioral target that goes unaddressed.

**Summary:**

The therapist grasps the surface-level complaint but misses critical maintaining factors, behavioral patterns, and the patient's own insights about stress and belief change. Understanding is below the level needed for effective case conceptualization.

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
### **Score: 4/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrates adequate warmth and professionalism. The opening ("Hi there! Good to see you today") is friendly and welcoming. The therapist uses supportive language ("that sounds tough," "that makes sense," "that's good to hear you're willing to try"). The tone is non-judgmental and encouraging.

However, there are subtle interpersonal limitations:

1. **Lack of genuine curiosity:** The therapist's questions are often leading or prescriptive rather than genuinely curious. For example, "Maybe something like, 'I won't be able to sleep again,' or 'I'm going to be tired all day'? Those kinds of thoughts can definitely make it harder to fall back asleep." 1. **Reduced efficacy:** Without stimulus control and sleep restriction—the most effective behavioral interventions—the patient is unlikely to achieve sustained improvement. Generic cognitive reframing and sleep hygiene alone have modest effects.

2. **Reduced engagement:** The prescriptive, non-collaborative approach reduces the patient's sense of agency and ownership. Patients are more likely to engage with and adhere to treatments they have helped design.

3. **Missed opportunities:** Critical maintaining factors (anticipatory anxiety, behavioral patterns, substance use) are not addressed. Without addressing these, the insomnia is likely to persist.

4. **Lack of progress monitoring:** Without explicit goals, systematic homework review, and treatment adjustment, the therapist cannot determine whether the patient is improving or whether the approach needs to change. This risks prolonging ineffective treatment.

6. **Inadequate preparation for relapse:** No discussion of relapse prevention or maintenance strategies is provided. If the patient does improve, he may not know how to maintain gains or prevent relapse.

## Recommendations

### 1. Develop and Communicate an Explicit Case Conceptualization
- **Action:** Before the next session, write out a clear case formulation identifying: (1) the presenting problem (early morning awakenings, daytime fatigue, impaired concentration), (2) maintaining factors (anticipatory anxiety about the day, secondary anxiety about sleep, possible behavioral patterns, possible substance use), (3) the treatment approach (CBT-I with specific components), and (4) expected timeline and goals. Share this with the patient in the next session.
- **Example:** "Based on what you've told me, here's how I understand your insomnia: You wake up at 3:00 AM, and then your mind starts racing with worry about the day ahead—about your dissertation and teaching. That worry makes it hard to fall back asleep. Over time, you've started to worry about sleep itself, which makes the problem worse. Our treatment will focus on: (1) reducing the worry about the day through cognitive work and worry scheduling, (2) breaking the sleep-wake association through behavioral changes, and (3) consolidating your sleep. We expect to see improvement within 4-6 weeks. Does this make sense? What questions do you have?"
- **Practice:** Use the CBT-I manual or protocol (e.g., Perlis, Aloia, & Kuhn's "Behavioral Treatments for Sleep Disorders") as a guide. Write out your formulation and review it with a supervisor.

### 2. Implement Stimulus Control and Sleep Restriction
- **Action:** Introduce stimulus control in the next session: use bed only for sleep and sex, get out of bed if awake >20 minutes, maintain a consistent sleep schedule. Follow with sleep restriction: limit time in bed to match actual sleep time, then gradually increase. These are the most effective behavioral interventions for insomnia.
- **Example:** "One of the most effective things we can do is to strengthen the association between bed and sleep. Right now, you're lying awake in bed worrying, which weakens that association. Here's what I'd like you to try: If you're awake for more than 20 minutes, get out of bed and do something quiet and boring in another room until you feel sleepy. This helps your brain learn that bed is for sleep. Also, let's look at how much time you're actually sleeping. If you're in bed 8 hours but only sleeping 6, we'll gradually reduce your time in bed to 6 hours, then increase it as your sleep improves. This might feel counterintuitive, but it's very effective. What questions do you have?"
- **Practice:** Review the stimulus control and sleep restriction protocols. Practice explaining them to a colleague or supervisor. Be prepared to address the patient's concerns (e.g., "Won't I be more tired if I reduce my time in bed?").

### 3. Shift to Collaborative Empiricism
- **Action:** In the next session, explicitly invite the patient to collaborate. Ask open questions, listen to the patient's ideas, and work together to design interventions. Frame interventions as experiments to test hypotheses, not as therapist prescriptions.
- **Example:** Instead of "You could try thinking about it differently," say: "You mentioned that when you wake up, you start worrying about the day. Let's think about this together. What do you think would help you manage that worry? What have you tried before? What might we test to see what works? Here are some ideas I have, but I'd like to hear your thoughts first."
- **Practice:** In your next session, count how many times you ask open questions vs. closed questions. Aim for at least 70% open questions. Practice phrases like "What do you think?" "Tell me more about that." "How might we approach this together?" "What ideas do you have?"

### 4. Conduct Systematic Assessment of Maintaining Factors
- **Action:** In the next session, systematically assess: (1) anticipatory anxiety (what specifically worries the patient about the day? when does the worry occur? how long does it last?), (2) secondary anxiety about sleep (does the patient worry about not sleeping? what does he fear will happen?), (3) behavioral patterns (what does he do when he wakes? does he get up to work? does he lie awake worrying?), (4) substance use (alcohol timing and amount, caffeine timing and amount), (5) medication timing (when does he use his asthma inhaler?), (6) sleep environment and schedule.
- **Example:** "I want to understand your sleep better. When you wake up at 3:00 AM, what's the first thing that goes through your mind? Then what? How long do you lie awake? What do you do? Do you ever get up? If so, what do you do? Do you worry about not getting back to sleep? What do you fear will happen if you don't sleep well? Let's also talk about your daily routine. You mentioned playing tennis in the morning—that's great. What about the rest of the day? When do you work on your dissertation? When do you teach? When do you feel most stressed? And let's talk about alcohol and caffeine. You mentioned having wine in the evening—how much and when? How does that affect your sleep?"
- **Practice:** Develop a structured assessment form for insomnia that includes all these domains. Use it in your next session.

### 5. Use Thought Records for Systematic Cognitive Work
- **Action:** Instead of suggesting generic reframes, use structured thought records. Identify the trigger (waking at 3:00 AM), the automatic thought (e.g., "I won't be able to sleep again"), evidence for and against the thought, and an alternative thought that is both realistic and helpful.
- **Example:** "Let's use a thought record to work with this thought. The trigger is waking at 3:00 AM. The automatic thought is 'I won't be able to sleep again.' Let's examine this. What evidence do you have that you won't be able to sleep again? [Patient: 'I've been waking up for four months.'] Okay. What evidence do you have that you *can* fall back asleep? [Patient: 'Well, sometimes I do fall back asleep after an hour or so.'] Right. So sometimes you do fall back asleep. What's different about those times? [Explore.] So an alternative thought might be: 'I've woken up before and sometimes fallen back asleep. I'm not sure what will happen tonight, but I can try the strategies we discussed.' Does that feel more realistic and helpful?"
- **Practice:** Learn to use thought records. Practice with a colleague or supervisor. Teach the patient to use them as homework.

### 6. Design Behavioral Experiments to Test Beliefs
- **Action:** Frame interventions as experiments to test the patient's beliefs. For example, if the patient believes "If I don't sleep well, I'll be terrible at teaching," design an experiment to test this.
- **Example:** "You mentioned that you worry about not being at your best when teaching if you're tired. Let's test that belief. This week, notice times when you're tired but still teach or work effectively. What do you notice? Does your prediction come true, or do you manage better than you expect? Let's also try the relaxation activities and see whether they help you fall back asleep. We're testing whether these strategies work for you."
- **Practice:** Identify the patient's key beliefs about sleep and fatigue. Design experiments to test them. Review the results in the next session.

### 7. Implement Worry Time Scheduling
- **Action:** If anticipatory anxiety is a significant maintaining factor, introduce worry time scheduling. Schedule a specific time (e.g., 3:00 PM) for 15 minutes to worry about the day ahead. Outside this time, postpone worry.
- **Example:** "You mentioned that when you wake up, you start worrying about your dissertation and teaching. One strategy is to schedule a specific time to worry. Let's say 3:00 PM for 15 minutes. During that time, you can worry as much as you want. Outside that time, if you notice worry, you can say to yourself, 'I'll think about this at 3:00 PM.' This helps contain the worry and prevents it from occurring at bedtime. Would you like to try this?"
- **Practice:** Learn about worry time scheduling and other anxiety management techniques. Practice explaining them to a colleague.

### 8. Explicitly Assign Homework with Clear Rationale and Specificity
- **Action:** In the next session, explicitly assign homework (not "you could try"). Provide clear rationale, specific instructions, discuss barriers, and check commitment.
- **Example:** "I'd like to assign you some homework this week. First, I'd like you to keep a sleep diary. Here's the form. Each morning, record: what time you went to bed, what time you fell asleep, how many times you woke up, how long you were awake, what time you got out of bed, and how tired you felt during the day (0-10). This will help us understand your sleep patterns and see whether the changes we're making are working. Second, I'd like you to try stimulus control: if you're awake for more than 20 minutes, get out of bed and do something quiet in another room. This helps your brain learn that bed is for sleep. Third, I'd like you to try one relaxation activity before bed—either reading or listening to calm music. Pick whichever sounds more appealing. What questions do you have? What might get in the way? How confident are you that you can do these things? On a scale of 0-10, how likely are you to complete the sleep diary? What support do you need?"
- **Practice:** Write out your homework assignments in advance. Include rationale, specific instructions, and anticipated barriers. Practice assigning homework with a colleague.

### 9. Review Homework and Adjust Treatment in Session 2
- **Action:** In Session 2, begin by reviewing homework. Ask what worked, what didn't, what barriers were encountered. Use this information to adjust treatment. Do not repeat the same suggestions without modification.
- **Example:** "Let's review your sleep diary. I see that you slept about 6 hours on average and woke up twice per night. That's helpful information. You also tried the relaxation activity—how did that go? Did it help you fall back asleep? What about the stimulus control—did you get out of bed when you were awake for more than 20 minutes? What happened? What barriers did you encounter? Based on what we're learning, here's what I'd like to adjust..."
- **Practice:** In your next session, spend the first 10-15 minutes reviewing homework. Ask specific questions about what worked and what didn't. Use this information to inform treatment adjustments.

### 10. Elicit and Respond to Feedback Throughout Sessions
- **Action:** Regularly ask for feedback. Use open questions, not yes/no questions. Adjust your approach based on feedback.
- **Example:** "How is this approach feeling for you? Does it make sense? What concerns do you have? What would be most helpful to focus on? Is there anything I'm missing or not understanding about your situation? How are you feeling about the progress so far?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, aim to elicit feedback at least 3-4 times. Use open questions. Listen carefully and adjust your approach based on what you hear.

### 11. Establish Explicit Session Goals and Treatment Timeline
- **Action:** At the beginning of treatment, establish explicit goals and timeline. Review these regularly.
- **Example:** "Our goal is to help you sleep through the night without waking, or if you do wake, to return to sleep within 20 minutes. We expect to see improvement within 4-6 weeks. We'll measure progress using your sleep diary. If we don't see improvement after 6 weeks, we'll reassess and adjust our approach. Does this sound reasonable? What are your goals?"
- **Practice:** Develop a treatment plan document that includes goals, timeline, and specific interventions. Share this with the patient and review it regularly.

### 12. Deepen Understanding of the Patient's Internal Experience
- **Action:** Move beyond surface-level reflection to deeper exploration of the patient's thoughts, feelings, and beliefs. Ask follow-up questions that demonstrate genuine curiosity and understanding.
- **Example:** Instead of "That sounds tough," say: "When you wake up at 3:00 AM and realize you're not going to get back to sleep, what goes through your mind? What do you feel in your body? What do you fear will happen? Tell me more about that fear. What would it mean if you were tired during teaching? What's the worst that could happen?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, practice asking follow-up questions. When the patient says something important, pause and ask, "Tell me more about that." Aim to understand not just what the patient is experiencing, but why it matters and what it means to him.
