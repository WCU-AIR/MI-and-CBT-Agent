
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

This document outlines a structured framework for assessing therapist competence in Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT). 
It is used for training, supervision, and evaluation. Each item is scored on a 0–6 scale:

0 = Poor
2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Expand Behavioral Conceptualization and Assessment of Maintaining Factors**
- **Action**: In the next session, conduct a detailed functional analysis of the patient's reassurance-seeking, symptom documentation, and avoidance behaviors. Ask: "How often do you seek reassurance from doctors or online? What happens when you get reassurance—does it help, and for how long? How much time per day do you spend documenting or researching your symptoms? What activities have you avoided because of your symptoms?"
- **Example**: "I notice you mentioned researching symptoms online and worrying about bringing concerns to your doctor. Let's explore this more carefully. When you research symptoms, what typically happens? Does it calm you down, or does it increase your worry? And how long does any relief last?"
- **Practice**: Review the literature on behavioral maintaining factors in SSD (e.g., Kleinstäuber et al., 2014; Henningsen et al., 2018) to deepen understanding of how reassurance-seeking and symptom monitoring maintain the disorder.

### 2. **Integrate Behavioral Activation for Depression**
- **Action**: Assess the patient's activity level and identify valued activities that have been reduced due to depression or health anxiety. Develop a graded behavioral activation plan that includes both mood-related activities and activities that challenge health anxiety (e.g., gentle exercise, social engagement).
- **Example**: "You mentioned feeling down and trapped in this cycle. One thing that often helps with mood is gradually increasing activities that are meaningful to you or that give you a sense of accomplishment. What are some activities you used to enjoy or that you value, even if they feel hard right now?"
- **Practice**: Use the Behavioral Activation for Depression Scale (BADS) to measure activity level and track changes over time. Assign graded behavioral activation homework (e.g., "This week, take a 10-minute walk on three days" or "Spend 15 minutes on a hobby you enjoy").

### 3. **Develop a Plan to Reduce Reassurance-Seeking and Symptom Monitoring**
- **Action**: Explicitly address the patient's reassurance-seeking and symptom documentation behaviors as maintaining factors. Develop a graded plan to reduce these behaviors, with the rationale that reducing reassurance-seeking and symptom monitoring will help break the anxiety cycle.
- **Example**: "We've talked about how researching symptoms online tends to increase your worry. One important part of treatment is gradually reducing this behavior. This might feel scary at first, but research shows that when people stop seeking reassurance and stop monitoring symptoms so closely, their anxiety actually decreases over time. Would you be willing to try gradually reducing your online research and symptom documentation?"
- **Practice**: Assign homework to reduce online research by a specific amount (e.g., "This week, limit symptom research to 10 minutes per day instead of your usual amount"). Use a behavioral experiment to test the prediction: "Notice what happens to your anxiety when you resist the urge to research for a few hours."

### 4. **Introduce Acceptance-Based Strategies for Uncertainty and Somatic Sensations**
- **Action**: Given that the patient explicitly identified uncertainty as "the hardest part," introduce acceptance-based strategies (e.g., mindfulness, acceptance and commitment therapy principles) to help the patient tolerate uncertainty and somatic sensations without needing to resolve them through reassurance-seeking or catastrophic thinking.
- **Example**: "You mentioned that the uncertainty is the hardest part. One thing we can work on is learning to live with uncertainty—not trying to eliminate it, but learning that you can have uncertainty and still be okay. This might involve noticing the worry without fighting it or trying to figure everything out."
- **Practice**: Teach brief mindfulness or acceptance exercises (e.g., "Notice the sensation in your leg without trying to change it or figure out what it means"). Assign homework: "Spend 5 minutes daily noticing one physical sensation without judgment or analysis."

### 5. **Implement Graded Exposure to Avoided Activities**
- **Action**: Identify activities that the patient has avoided due to health anxiety or physical symptoms (e.g., exercise, social activities, work tasks). Develop a graded exposure hierarchy and assign homework to gradually increase engagement in these activities.
- **Example**: "You mentioned that the aches and spasms make it hard to focus or relax. Let's think about activities you've reduced or avoided because of your symptoms. We can gradually increase these activities in a planned way, which often helps people realize that the symptoms don't actually get worse when they're active."
- **Practice**: Create a hierarchy of avoided activities (e.g., 1 = light stretching, 5 = 30-minute walk, 10 = exercise class). Assign graded exposure homework starting with lower-anxiety activities.

### 6. **Refine Cognitive Restructuring with More Socratic Questioning**
- **Action**: In future sessions, use more Socratic questioning to help the patient generate her own alternative thoughts and conclusions rather than the therapist proposing them. Ask the patient to draw conclusions from evidence before offering suggestions.
- **Example**: Instead of "What might be an alternative or more balanced thought?" ask: "Looking at all this evidence—the normal tests, the partial medication response, the persistent symptoms—what do you make of it? What conclusion would you draw? How would you explain what's happening to a friend?"
- **Practice**: Review Socratic questioning techniques (e.g., Padesky & Mooney, 2012) and practice asking open-ended questions that invite the patient's own reasoning rather than leading questions that guide toward a predetermined answer.

### 7. **Establish Explicit Measurement and Progress Monitoring**
- **Action**: Implement regular measurement of key outcomes: anxiety about serious illness (0-10 scale), health anxiety (using a validated measure like the Short Health Anxiety Inventory), depressed mood, functional impairment, and behavioral targets (frequency of reassurance-seeking, symptom documentation, avoidance).
- **Example**: "Let's track your anxiety about serious illness each week on a 0-10 scale. We'll also track how much time you spend researching symptoms and how often you seek reassurance from doctors. This will help us see whether the changes we're making are actually helping."
- **Practice**: Administer validated measures at baseline and regularly (e.g., weekly or every other session). Use measurement data to guide treatment adjustments.

### 8. **Address the Relationship Between Somatic Symptoms and Depression**
- **Action**: Explore the temporal relationship between depressive symptoms and somatic symptoms. Assess whether the somatic symptoms are primarily manifestations of depression or whether they are maintaining the depression through functional impairment and health anxiety.
- **Example**: "I'm noticing that you have both physical symptoms and depressed mood. Sometimes these are connected—when people feel down, they notice their body more and worry more about symptoms. Other times, the worry about symptoms makes people feel more depressed. Let's explore how these are connected for you."
- **Practice**: Use a mood and symptom tracking log to identify patterns. Consider whether antidepressant medication adjustment might be warranted (in consultation with prescribing physician).

### 9. **Develop a Relapse Prevention Plan**
- **Action**: As treatment progresses, explicitly discuss what the patient has learned and how she will maintain gains. Develop a plan for managing setbacks (e.g., "If you notice yourself slipping back into reassurance-seeking or catastrophic thinking, here's what you'll do...").
- **Example**: "As we make progress, let's talk about what you're learning and how you'll keep using these skills after we finish therapy. What will you do if you notice the worry starting to take over again?"
- **Practice**: Create a written relapse prevention plan that includes: (1) early warning signs of relapse, (2) coping strategies to use, (3) when to seek additional support.

### 10. **Collaborate with Primary Care and Prescribing Physician**
- **Action**: Establish communication with the patient's primary care physician and psychiatrist (if applicable) to ensure coordinated care. Discuss whether medication adjustment might be warranted and how psychotherapy complements pharmacotherapy.
- **Example**: "I'd like to check in with your primary care doctor and the doctor who prescribed your medications to make sure we're all working together on this. Sometimes adjusting medications can help, and sometimes psychotherapy helps more when combined with the right medication."
- **Practice**: Send a brief treatment summary to the referring physician outlining the CBT approach and key behavioral targets. Request feedback on medication effectiveness and any medical concerns.
