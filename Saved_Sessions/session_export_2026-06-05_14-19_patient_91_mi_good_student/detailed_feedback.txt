
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Strategically Evoke and Amplify Change Talk Throughout the Session**
- **Action:** When Marcus produces change talk (statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment to change), respond with genuine interest and follow-up questions that deepen and strengthen it rather than moving to reflection and acceptance alone.
- **Example:** When Marcus says "my asthma is out of control and that's the thing that's kinda scaring me," respond with: "That fear is important. Tell me more about what scares you most. How is that affecting your daily life?" Or when he says "I want to be able to breathe easy like before," ask: "What would that make possible for you? What could you do if you weren't worried about your breathing?"
- **Practice:** Record yourself in practice sessions and count instances of change talk. For each instance, ask yourself: "Did I deepen this, or did I move past it?" Commit to asking at least one follow-up question for every change talk statement. Practice with a colleague, having them produce change talk and you responding with deepening questions.

### 2. **Use Strategic Summaries to Selectively Highlight and Reinforce Change Talk**
- **Action:** Periodically summarize the session by selectively emphasizing change talk while minimizing sustain talk. This helps Marcus hear his own motivation reflected back and strengthens his commitment.
- **Example:** "Let me make sure I'm understanding. You've told me that your asthma is getting worse and that scares you. You want to breathe easily again like you used to. You've also shown me that when you had a routine and took your medication regularly, you felt significantly better and had more energy. You're worried about losing your dog, and that's important to you, and you're also looking for ways to manage your asthma without that sacrifice. You're at about a 4 or 5 in readiness, which means part of you is ready to move forward. Is that right? What else would you add?"
- **Practice:** After each client statement, pause and write down what change talk you heard. At natural transition points in the session, practice delivering a summary that emphasizes change talk. Have a supervisor review these summaries for balance and strategic emphasis.

### 3. **Maintain Evocative Stance Longer Before Offering Information**
- **Action:** Resist the urge to provide information or solutions until you have thoroughly explored the client's own ideas, past attempts, knowledge, and resources. Use the "ask-tell-ask" framework: ask what they already know, tell information if needed, then ask how they'll use it.
- **Example:** Before offering information about air purifiers and cleaning, ask: "What do you already know about what triggers your asthma? What have you tried before that helped? What ideas do you have about managing it without giving up your dog?" Only after exploring these should you offer information, and then ask: "How does this fit with what you were thinking? What of this might work for you?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, commit to asking at least 5 exploratory questions before offering any information or advice. Use the "ask-tell-ask" framework explicitly. Have a supervisor listen for when you shift from evocation to education and provide feedback on timing.

### 4. **Ask Targeted Questions to Evoke Specific Types of Change Talk**
- **Action:** Deliberately ask questions designed to elicit desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change. These are the five categories of change talk that most strongly predict behavior change.
- **Example:** 
  - **Desire:** "What would be most important to you about getting your asthma under control?"
  - **Ability:** "What strengths do you have that could help you manage this? What have you successfully managed in the past?"
  - **Reasons:** "What are the main reasons you'd want to improve your asthma?"
  - **Need:** "How urgent does this feel to you? What would happen if things stayed as they are?"
  - **Commitment:** "On a scale of 0-10, how committed are you to taking steps to manage your asthma? What would move you from a 5 to a 6?"
- **Practice:** Create a list of 3-4 questions for each category of change talk. Practice asking these questions in role-plays. Record yourself and count how many times you ask each type. Aim for at least one question from each category per session.

### 5. **Explore and Build on Past Success More Thoroughly**
- **Action:** When clients describe times they managed their problem well, don't just acknowledge it—deeply explore what made it work, how they felt, and how to recreate those conditions. This is powerful evidence of their capability.
- **Example:** When Marcus says "What helped was having a routine, I guess," respond with: "Tell me more about that routine. What did it look like? How did you feel when you were sticking to it? What made it work then? What's different now that makes it harder? What would it take to rebuild something like that?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, when a client describes past success, spend at least 3-4 minutes exploring it in depth. Ask about the specifics, the feelings, the context, and the barriers to recreating it. Have a supervisor listen for whether you adequately explored past success or moved past it too quickly.

### 6. **Collaboratively Develop Specific, Concrete, Measurable Change Goals**
- **Action:** Don't accept vague commitments like "talk to my doctor about adjusting medication." Instead, collaboratively clarify what this means, when it will happen, what barriers might exist, and how it connects to broader goals.
- **Example:** When Marcus says he'll talk to his doctor about adjusting medication, ask: "That sounds like a good first step. Tell me more about what you'd want to discuss with your doctor. What specifically would you ask about? When do you think you'd have that conversation? What might get in the way? How would you know if the conversation went well?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, when a client proposes a change step, spend time clarifying it collaboratively. Write down the specific, concrete, measurable goal. Have a supervisor review whether your goals are vague or specific.

### 7. **Use Double-Sided Reflections to Acknowledge Ambivalence Without Dismissing Either Side**
- **Action:** When clients express ambivalence, reflect both sides of the conflict in a way that validates both and subtly emphasizes the change side. This is more skillful than simple acceptance.
- **Example:** When Marcus expresses worry about medication complexity, reflect: "So you want your asthma to be better and you're worried that managing it might add to your stress rather than help. You're looking for a way to manage it that actually reduces your burden rather than adding to it. That makes sense."
- **Practice:** In your next session, identify moments of ambivalence and practice double-sided reflections. Have a supervisor listen for whether you're subtly emphasizing the change side while validating both sides.

### 8. **Explicitly Affirm Client Autonomy and Right to Choose**
- **Action:** Strengthen autonomy support by explicitly stating that the client can choose not to change, can take time to decide, and that you will support whatever choice they make. This paradoxically increases motivation.
- **Example:** "I want to be clear—this is entirely your choice. You can decide to make changes, or you can decide not to right now. You can take time to think about it. Whatever you decide, I'm here to support you. What feels right to you?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, explicitly affirm client autonomy at least once. Notice whether this increases or decreases their openness to change.

### 9. **Minimize Sustain Talk Through Strategic Redirection Rather Than Confrontation**
- **Action:** When clients express sustain talk (reasons not to change), don't argue or confront. Instead, use reframing, exploring the concern more deeply, or redirecting toward possibility.
- **Example:** When Marcus says "I'm not ready to make big changes yet," respond with: "You're saying big changes feel overwhelming right now. What would a small change look like—something that wouldn't feel like too much? What's one small thing you could try that might help?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, identify sustain talk and practice responding with reframing or redirection rather than confrontation. Have a supervisor listen for whether you're skillfully softening sustain talk or inadvertently reinforcing it through argument.

### 10. **Maintain Collaborative Stance During Information-Giving**
- **Action:** When you do offer information, frame it as one perspective among many, invite the client's reaction, and collaboratively determine how to use it. Avoid expert positioning.
- **Example:** "Here's what I know about asthma management from my experience and the research. Many people find that [information]. But you know your life better than I do. What of this makes sense to you? What wouldn't work? What ideas do you have?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, when offering information, explicitly invite the client's reaction and build on their ideas rather than presenting information as the solution. Have a supervisor listen for whether you maintain collaboration during information-giving.
