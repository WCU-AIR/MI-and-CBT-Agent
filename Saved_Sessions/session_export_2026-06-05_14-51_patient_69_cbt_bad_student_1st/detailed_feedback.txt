
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish a Collaborative Agenda (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** At the start of every session, spend 2-3 minutes collaboratively identifying session goals. Ask: "What would be most helpful to focus on today?" "Of all the things you're dealing with, what feels most urgent right now?" "What do you hope we can accomplish in our time together?" Establish 1-2 priorities and allocate time accordingly.
- **Example:** "Peter, I know you're dealing with knee pain, mood, and concerns about medication use. These are all connected, and we'll work on all of them. But for today, what feels most important to tackle? What would be most helpful?"
- **Practice:** Role-play agenda-setting with a supervisor. Practice with 3-5 patients, recording sessions and reviewing how well you established collaborative priorities.

### 2. **Learn and Apply Guided Discovery (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Replace advice-giving with skillful questioning. When a patient expresses a concern, ask questions to help them explore it rather than telling them what to do. Use the Socratic method: ask about evidence, alternatives, implications, and conclusions.
- **Example:** Instead of "Try thinking positive," ask: "You said it's hard to feel like things will get better. What makes you feel that way? What evidence do you have for that? Is there any evidence against it? What would need to happen for you to feel more hopeful?"
- **Practice:** Review recordings of your sessions with a supervisor. Identify every instance where you gave advice and rewrite it as a guided discovery question. Practice with role-plays until guided discovery becomes your default approach.

### 3. **Develop a Clear Conceptualization and Treatment Strategy**
- **Action:** Before each session, develop a written conceptualization of the patient's problem (how symptoms are connected, what maintains them) and a corresponding treatment strategy (what to target, what techniques to use, in what order). Share this with the patient and refine collaboratively.
- **Example:** "Peter, here's how I'm understanding your situation: Chronic pain led to opioid use, which helped initially but created dependence. When you try to cut back, withdrawal and cravings feel unbearable, so you use more. This cycle keeps you stuck and contributes to low mood. Depression makes pain feel worse and makes it harder to do things that could help. We need to break this cycle. Here's my plan: First, we'll work on behavioral activation—doing things that matter to you even when it's hard. Second, we'll build coping skills for managing pain and cravings without medication. Third, we'll work on the thoughts that keep you stuck. Does this make sense? What would you add or change?"
- **Practice:** Write out conceptualizations for 5-10 patients. Review with a supervisor. Refine until your conceptualizations are clear, specific, and directly linked to treatment interventions.

### 4. **Eliminate Personal Anecdotes; Focus Entirely on the Patient**
- **Action:** Stop sharing personal stories about your own experiences. This shifts focus away from the patient and suggests your struggles are comparable to theirs. Instead, use empathic reflection: "That sounds really difficult" or "I can see why that would be frustrating." Keep the focus on understanding their experience.
- **Example:** Instead of "I remember when I had a cold that wouldn't quit," say: "It sounds like the pain just won't let up, and that's wearing on you. Tell me more about how that affects your day-to-day life."
- **Practice:** Record your sessions. Count how many times you share personal anecdotes. Set a goal of zero. Review with a supervisor and get feedback on your empathic reflection skills.
