# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal unconditional positive regard demonstrated. While opening is warm, clinician quickly shifts to directive problem-solving stance. Fails to affirm Erik's inherent worth or normalize struggle with self-comparison. Progress acknowledged superficially without genuine celebration of concrete wins (reduced back pain, better-fitting pants). Tone remains neutral and task-focused rather than warm and supportive. Lacks explicit validation of Erik's feelings as understandable human experiences.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflective listening is minimal and superficial. Clinician provides basic factual summaries rather than emotionally attuned reflections. Reduces Erik's complex struggle (self-comparison, age-related discouragement, ambivalence about progress) to a checklist of "issues." Does not capture emotional nuance or existential doubt. No complex reflections that explore tension between desire to change and fear of failure. Fails to reflect meaning behind statements or deepen insight into Erik's internal experience.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clear focus maintained on gym attendance and lifestyle change, which is appropriate. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. Session lacks strategic progression toward clarifying Erik's own goals and values. No exploration of deeper motivations through questions like "What would it mean to you to feel stronger?" or "What matters most to you about your health?" Misses opportunities to explore ambivalence strategically. Concrete plan emerges from clinician suggestion rather than Erik's own discovery.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Severely compromised throughout session. Psychiatrist adopts expert stance and makes unilateral decisions about what Erik should do. Series of prescriptions disguised as questions: "Do you think maybe you should try to schedule...or maybe find a workout buddy?" When Erik provides feedback (workout buddy won't work—he's private), clinician does not genuinely incorporate this into collaborative process. Continues with more suggestions. Language is softer ("maybe") but direction remains unilateral. Power remains with clinician, not shared.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal and inconsistent. While not explicitly pressuring, session saturated with subtle coercion and advice-giving that undermines Erik's sense of choice and self-determination. Persistent use of "You might want to try," "You should try to remind yourself," "You might want to try scheduling." Never explicitly affirms Erik's autonomy or right to choose. Implicit message: clinician has "right answer" and Erik should comply. When Erik expresses preference (no workout buddy), clinician continues offering alternatives, subtly communicating autonomy not truly respected.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Almost entirely absent. Psychiatrist tells rather than asks; provides reasons for change rather than drawing out Erik's own motivations. Repeated opportunities missed to elicit and amplify intrinsic motivation. Erik spontaneously offers change talk ("My pants feel better," "My back doesn't hurt as much," "Those little wins keep me going"), but clinician minimally acknowledges and does not amplify. Pivots to external solutions (apps, scheduling), implicitly communicating intrinsic motivation insufficient. No open-ended questions designed to elicit change talk. When Erik expresses ambivalence, clinician offers solutions rather than exploring what's underneath.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Severely compromised. Psychiatrist adopts expert role throughout, treating Erik as recipient of advice rather than co-creator of change process. Opens with unilateral agenda; makes diagnostic statements; offers solutions; consolidates plan as if it emerged from joint decision-making when largely clinician-driven. True partnership would involve asking what Erik wants to focus on, what he thinks is getting in the way, what he thinks might help. Collaborative language ("What do you think?") is superficial—questions asked after clinician has already suggested answer, functioning as requests for agreement rather than genuine invitations for input.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually absent. Erik spontaneously offers multiple instances of change talk: "My pants feel better," "My back doesn't hurt as much," "I'm glad we talked last time—it got me the push I needed," "Those little wins keep me going." Clinician's response is minimal acknowledgment followed by pivoting to advice. No amplification or deepening of change talk. No strategic questions asked to elicit additional change talk. When Erik expresses ambivalence or sustain talk ("it's almost not worth the effort"), clinician does not explore or reframe—offers solutions instead, bypassing opportunity to evoke Erik's own resolution of ambivalence. Fundamental failure in MI technique.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill demonstrated. While not reinforcing sustain talk, clinician fails to skillfully redirect it toward change talk or explore with curiosity. Typical response: acknowledge sustain talk and offer solution. Does not use double-sided reflections acknowledging both difficulty and desire to change. Does not explore sustain talk with curiosity to understand what's underneath. Does not reframe sustain talk in ways that open new perspectives. Approach is to minimize sustain talk through advice-giving rather than help Erik resolve own ambivalence. Missing opportunities to use strategic reframing or help client integrate conflicting perspectives.

## Overall Summary

### **Overall MI Competency Score: 1.8/5**

**Session Strengths:**
- Clear focus on target behavior (gym attendance and lifestyle change)
- Warm, welcoming opening that establishes basic rapport
- Concrete, actionable plan provided at session end (Tuesdays/Thursdays, app tracking)
- Minimal acknowledgment of Erik's progress (gym attendance, improved fit, reduced pain)

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Expert-driven stance dominates:** Clinician assumes role of problem-solver; solutions prescribed rather than evoked. Fundamental violation of MI principles.
- **Minimal evocation of intrinsic motivation:** Tells rather than asks. Spontaneous change talk minimally acknowledged and not amplified. Pivots to external solutions, implicitly communicating intrinsic motivation insufficient.
- **Superficial empathic understanding:** Reflective listening is factual rather than emotionally attuned. Does not capture nuance of ambivalence, self-doubt, or fear of failure.
- **Sustain talk not skillfully addressed:** When Erik expresses discouragement or doubt, clinician offers solutions rather than exploring ambivalence with curiosity or using double-sided reflections.
- **Autonomy subtly undermined:** Saturated with prescriptions ("You might want to try," "You should try") communicating clinician's agenda rather than supporting self-determination.
- **Collaboration superficial:** Questions asked after solutions suggested, functioning as requests for agreement. When Erik offers expertise ("I'm private about this"), not genuinely incorporated.

---

### **Likelihood of Evoking Sustained Client Motivation for Change: LOW**

While Erik leaves with concrete plan and some affirmation, he has not been invited to deeply explore own reasons for change, values, or self-efficacy. Plan is clinician-driven rather than client-owned. This approach may lead to compliance (following plan because clinician suggested it) rather than commitment (following plan because it aligns with own values). When obstacles arise, Erik may lack internal motivation to persist.

---

### **Recommended Priority Actions for Clinician Development:**

1. **Shift from expert to evocative stance** (HIGHEST PRIORITY) — Eliminate prescriptions; ask open-ended questions inviting Erik to discover own answers
2. **Amplify and deepen change talk** (CRITICAL) — Pause when Erik expresses change talk; ask follow-up questions that deepen connection to own motivation
3. **Use double-sided reflections** — Address ambivalence by reflecting both sustain talk and change talk simultaneously
4. **Develop genuine collaborative problem-solving** — Invite Erik's expertise; build plan based on his input, not clinician suggestions
5. **Strengthen empathic reflections** — Move beyond factual summaries to emotionally attuned reflections capturing nuance and meaning

---

**Recommendation for Training:** Intensive MI training with supervised practice, session recording review, role-play practice of evocative questions, and peer consultation with experienced MI practitioners.
