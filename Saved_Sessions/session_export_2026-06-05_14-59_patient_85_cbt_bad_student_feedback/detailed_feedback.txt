
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated surface-level empathy and reflected some explicit statements, but fundamentally misunderstood Felicia's clinical presentation and internal experience. The therapist repeatedly normalized Felicia's symptoms as universal human experiences rather than recognizing them as manifestations of active psychosis and cognitive impairment.

**Critical misunderstandings:**

2. **Inappropriate self-disclosure:** The therapist repeatedly shared personal anecdotes ("I remember when I was stressed out about a big test," "When I was feeling down, I used to just take a walk," "I was really nervous about a party") to normalize Felicia's experience. This is problematic because:
   - It equates the therapist's stress management with treatment for schizophrenia
   - It minimizes the severity of Felicia's condition
   - It suggests that willpower and positive thinking can resolve psychosis

The therapist suggested she "remember a happy place" and "think about nice things"—abstract cognitive tasks that directly contradict her stated cognitive limitations. The therapist did not adjust strategy to match her concrete thinking style.

4. **Failure to recognize medication-symptom relationship:** Felicia mentioned that clozapine has helped her hallucinations (per case study), yet the therapist never explored medication adherence, side effects, or the role of pharmacotherapy in her current functioning. For schizophrenia, this is a critical oversight.

5. **Missed safety concerns:** Felicia mentioned hearing voices and paranoia about others' intentions. The therapist did not assess for command hallucinations, suicidality, or homicidality—standard safety protocols for psychotic patients.

The therapist did demonstrate basic listening (reflected some content) and expressed warmth, but this does not constitute genuine understanding of Felicia's clinical reality.

**Summary:**

### 5. Collaboration

## Recommendations

### 1. **URGENT: Learn CBT-for-Psychosis (CBTp) Methodology**
- **Action:** Obtain specialized training in CBT-for-psychosis. This is the evidence-based approach for schizophrenia and is distinct from CBT for anxiety or depression.
- **Example:** Instead of "Try to think positive," use reality testing: "You believe people are talking about you. Let's look at the evidence. What specifically did you observe? What else could explain what you heard? What would it mean if someone was talking about you? How would you know for sure?"
- **Practice:** Complete a CBTp training program (e.g., through the International Association for Cognitive Psychotherapy or a specialized training institute). Seek supervision from a CBTp-trained clinician.

### 2. **Establish Collaborative Agenda and Case Conceptualization**
- **Action:** At the start of each session, explicitly set a collaborative agenda. Ask Felicia: "What would be most helpful to talk about today?" "What's bothering you most right now?" Prioritize together and allocate time.
- **Example:** "I hear that you're struggling with hearing voices, worrying about what people think, and having trouble sleeping. Those are all important. Today, let's focus on the voices—understanding them better and finding ways to manage them. Does that sound okay?"
- **Practice:** Before each session, write down 3-4 potential topics and be prepared to discuss priorities with the patient. Use a written agenda visible to both therapist and patient.

### 3. **Conduct Systematic Assessment of Psychotic Symptoms**
- **Action:** Assess hallucinations and delusions systematically. For hallucinations: What do you hear? How often? When does it happen? What does it sound like? How distressing is it? For paranoia: What specifically do you believe people are thinking/doing? What evidence supports this? What evidence contradicts it? How certain are you?
- **Example:** "You mentioned hearing mumbling and whispers. Tell me more about that. What exactly do you hear? Does it sound like real people or something else? When does it happen most? What makes it better or worse?"
- **Practice:** Use a structured assessment tool (e.g., the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale or Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale) to systematically evaluate symptoms at baseline and track changes.

### 4. **Develop Reality-Testing Strategies for Paranoia**
- **Action:** Use guided discovery to help Felicia examine paranoid beliefs. Ask: "What evidence do you have that people are talking about you? What evidence suggests they might not be? What would it take to know for sure? Can we test this together?"

### 6. **Adapt Interventions to Concrete Thinking**
- **Action:** Recognize Felicia's concrete thinking and adjust all interventions accordingly. Use concrete, present-focused language. Avoid abstract visualization or future-oriented thinking. Use concrete examples and real-world scenarios.
- **Example:** Instead of "Imagine a happy place," say "Remember the park you went to? What did you see there? What did it feel like? Can you go there again?"
