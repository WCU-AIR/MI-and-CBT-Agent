# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal acceptance throughout the session. While opening with basic warmth ("It's nice to meet you today"), the clinician quickly pivots to problem-solving and advice-giving, undermining genuine acceptance of the patient's autonomy. Most critically, when the patient explicitly states frustration with repetitive medical advice, the psychiatrist responds by continuing to offer more advice—the exact pattern the patient complained about. There is no explicit affirmation of the patient's worth, struggle, or right to feel overwhelmed. True acceptance would involve acknowledging the patient's frustration as valid and respecting their pace rather than reinforcing the problematic pattern they identified.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist makes surface-level reflective attempts ("It sounds like stress and lack of support are making quitting quite difficult") but fails to demonstrate accurate, deep understanding of the patient's core experience. The critical missed opportunity occurs when the patient states frustration with repetitive medical advice—the psychiatrist does not reflect back or explore this emotional core. When the patient says "I've got enough to handle already without adding a bunch of new stuff," the psychiatrist minimizes this concern rather than genuinely acknowledging the underlying overwhelm and competing demands. Reflections remain simple and factual rather than complex and emotionally attuned, demonstrating lack of understanding of the patient's internal world and values.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains general focus on smoking cessation throughout the session, demonstrating some directional consistency. An early attempt to clarify the target behavior ("Do you think smoking is the main problem you want to work on right now?") shows some effort toward collaborative direction-setting. However, once direction is established, the psychiatrist abandons collaborative exploration and imposes a series of interventions: relaxation techniques, support groups, breathing exercises, tracking cigarettes, and avoiding smoking situations. The psychiatrist does eventually narrow focus to tracking only, showing some strategic thinking about pacing, but this emerges only after the patient has repeatedly resisted and expressed overwhelm. Direction is present but poorly executed; it feels imposed rather than co-constructed.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** This session demonstrates a striking absence of genuine collaboration. The psychiatrist adopts an expert stance from the beginning and maintains it throughout. Most tellingly, when the patient explicitly states frustration with repetitive advice, the psychiatrist responds by offering more advice—the opposite of collaboration. The psychiatrist repeatedly uses directive phrases like "It would probably help if you..." and "Maybe you should..." that position the clinician as the expert with solutions and the patient as the recipient of advice. Questions are primarily fact-gathering rather than collaborative problem-solving. There is virtually no power-sharing or co-construction of the change process. By the end, the psychiatrist summarizes what the patient agreed to, but this is confirmation of the psychiatrist's suggestion, not genuine collaboration.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** This session demonstrates minimal autonomy support and, in several moments, active undermining of patient autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly uses prescriptive language: "It would probably help if you tried some relaxation techniques," "It would probably help if you gave it a shot," "Maybe you should just try cutting back," "It would probably help if you kept track." This language pattern is subtly coercive and positions the psychiatrist's suggestion as the obvious right choice. The psychiatrist does not explicitly affirm the patient's right to choose, freedom to decline suggestions, or ownership of the change process. When the patient says "I'm not making any promises," the psychiatrist does not affirm this boundary but continues suggesting actions. True autonomy support would involve genuine questions like "What do you think might help?" and "You get to decide what works for you."

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out the patient's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources—is almost entirely absent from this session. The psychiatrist does not ask questions designed to elicit the patient's reasons for change, values, past successes, internal motivation, or own ideas about what might help. Questions are primarily factual and problem-focused ("Have you tried quitting before?" "Do you have support?") rather than evocative. The patient offers subtle change talk ("common sense somewhere in there…I know smoking's bad") and the psychiatrist does not amplify or explore this. When the patient mentions stress as a barrier, the psychiatrist does not evoke her ideas about managing stress or past coping strategies but simply suggests interventions. The session is characterized by the psychiatrist telling and suggesting rather than asking and evoking—the opposite of MI's core principle that motivation resides within the client.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership—the understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client—is fundamentally absent from this session. The psychiatrist actively assumes the expert role throughout and does not foster genuine power-sharing. From the opening moments, the psychiatrist positions herself as the expert with knowledge about what the patient should do. The most striking evidence of this is the psychiatrist's response to the patient's explicit statement about frustration with repetitive advice: rather than recognizing the patient's expertise about what she needs, the psychiatrist continues to offer advice—the very thing the patient said doesn't work. This demonstrates a fundamental failure to recognize the client's expertise about her own experience. A partnership-oriented response would invite the patient to shape the session based on her needs. Instead, the psychiatrist shapes the session unilaterally, offering suggestions and asking the patient to comply.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist makes virtually no effort to cultivate, evoke, or amplify the patient's change talk. When the patient offers change talk—"common sense somewhere in there…I know smoking's bad," "Maybe a little breathing thing won't hurt," "Maybe trying to smoke a little less every day isn't the worst idea," "Yeah, I can try just keeping track"—the psychiatrist fails to amplify or deepen any of these statements. When the patient says "common sense somewhere in there," the psychiatrist does not respond with curiosity but moves on to suggest interventions. When the patient agrees to tracking, the psychiatrist does not explore her motivation but simply summarizes and closes. A skilled MI clinician would ask evocative questions to cultivate more change talk: "What would be good about smoking less?" "What strengths do you have that could help?" Instead, the psychiatrist's directive approach actually suppresses change talk by positioning the psychiatrist's ideas as the solution.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk is present throughout this session, and the psychiatrist's handling of it is inconsistent and often counterproductive. The patient's sustain talk includes: "every time I go to any doctor, they always say the same thing," "I've got enough to handle already," "I'm not really into trying all that extra stuff," "I'm not all that confident," "I've tried cutting back before…then I just fall back into the same routine," and "I'm not sure I'm ready to change all that stuff yet." The psychiatrist's primary response to sustain talk is to offer more advice and suggestions, which often reinforces rather than softens the resistance. When the patient says "I've got enough to handle already," the psychiatrist suggests "just a simple breathing exercise," minimizing her concern rather than genuinely softening the sustain talk. The psychiatrist makes one attempt at softening ("I get that cutting back hasn't worked well before, but maybe this time...") but does not deeply explore why cutting back failed or what would be different. There is limited use of double-sided reflections or exploration of the roots of resistance.

---

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall Score: 1.4/5**

**Summary:** This session demonstrates **fundamental misalignment with Motivational Interviewing principles and spirit**. The psychiatrist operates almost entirely from an expert stance, offering directive advice and suggestions rather than evoking the patient's own motivation. Most critically, the psychiatrist replicates the exact pattern the patient explicitly complained about—repetitive medical advice—which will reinforce frustration and resistance rather than resolve it.

**Key Findings:**
- The psychiatrist fails to respond to explicit client feedback about the therapeutic process
- Collaboration, evocation, partnership, and autonomy support are virtually absent
- Change talk is not amplified; sustain talk is often reinforced through continued advice-giving
- The patient's agreement to track smoking appears to be compliance rather than genuine commitment rooted in intrinsic motivation

**Clinical Impact:** The likelihood that this session will evoke the patient's intrinsic motivation for change is very low. Without a fundamental shift toward evocation, collaboration, and autonomy support, the patient is unlikely to follow through on agreed-upon actions or to ultimately achieve sustained behavior change.

**Recommendation:** The psychiatrist must fundamentally restructure their approach to align with MI principles, moving from an expert stance to collaborative partnership, from directive suggestions to evocative questions, and from prescriptive pressure to genuine autonomy support. Focused practice and supervision on core MI competencies is essential.
