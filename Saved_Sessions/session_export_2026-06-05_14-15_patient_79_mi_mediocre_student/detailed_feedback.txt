
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Recognize and Respond to Explicit Client Feedback About the Therapeutic Process**
- **Action:** When a client explicitly states that a particular approach (repetitive advice) is not working, immediately shift your approach rather than continuing with the same pattern. This is a critical moment to demonstrate that you hear and respect the client's expertise about what they need.
- **Example:** When Lucía says "every time I go to any doctor, they always say the same thing," respond with: "I hear you—you're tired of hearing the same message over and over without it actually leading to change. That makes sense. So here's what I want to do differently: instead of me telling you what I think you should do, I'd like to hear from you. What do you think would actually be helpful in a conversation like this? What would feel different?"
- **Practice:** In supervision or role-play, practice identifying moments when clients give you feedback about the therapeutic process and practice shifting your approach in real-time rather than continuing with the same strategy.

### 2. **Adopt Evocative Questions as Your Primary Tool Instead of Directive Suggestions**
- **Action:** Replace "It would probably help if you..." statements with genuine questions designed to draw out the client's own ideas, motivations, and resources. This is the core of MI and requires a fundamental shift in your approach.
- **Example:** Instead of "It would probably help if you tried some relaxation techniques or talked to people who understand what you're going through," ask: "You mentioned stress is the biggest barrier. What have you found helpful in the past when you're feeling stressed? What works for you?" Or: "If you were to quit smoking, what would be different in your life? What would that make possible for you?"
- **Practice:** Record yourself in sessions and count how many times you use directive language ("It would help if," "Maybe you should," "Have you thought about") versus evocative questions ("What do you think...?" "Tell me about...?" "How would that work for you?"). Set a goal to increase evocative questions to at least 60% of your speaking turns.

### 3. **Amplify and Explore Change Talk When It Emerges**
- **Action:** When a client offers change talk—even subtle statements like "common sense somewhere in there"—pause and explore it with curiosity rather than moving on to your next suggestion. This is how you strengthen the client's own motivation.
- **Example:** When Lucía says "common sense somewhere in there…I know smoking's bad," respond with: "Tell me more about that. What does that common sense tell you? What do you know about how smoking affects you?" Then listen deeply and reflect back what you hear, helping her develop her own reasoning for change.
- **Practice:** In role-play or supervision, practice identifying change talk in real-time and responding with follow-up questions that deepen exploration. Practice resisting the urge to move on to your next suggestion and instead staying curious about the client's own motivation.

### 4. **Establish True Collaboration by Inviting the Client to Co-Create the Session**
- **Action:** Explicitly invite the client to shape the session based on their needs and readiness. Ask what would be helpful, what they're willing to try, and what feels doable. Position yourself as a partner in exploration rather than an expert with solutions.
- **Example:** Early in the session, after understanding Lucía's frustration with repetitive advice, say: "I want to make sure this conversation is actually helpful to you, not just more of the same. What would need to be true for this to feel different? What would help?" Later, when discussing interventions: "You know yourself best. What do you think might actually work for you? What feels doable right now?"
- **Practice:** In every session, explicitly ask the client at least once: "What would be most helpful for you in our time together?" and "What feels possible to you right now?" Practice listening to their answer and genuinely shaping your approach based on their input.

### 5. **Use Double-Sided Reflections to Soften Sustain Talk Without Confrontation**
- **Action:** When a client expresses resistance or reasons against change, use reflections that acknowledge both sides of their ambivalence. This validates their experience while gently highlighting the change side.
- **Example:** When Lucía says "I've tried cutting back before…then I just fall back into the same routine," respond with: "So you've shown you can cut back—you have that ability—and yet the routine pulls you back in. That's the struggle, isn't it? You know you can do it, but something about your daily life makes it hard to stick with." This acknowledges her past success (change talk) while validating her struggle (sustain talk).
- **Practice:** In supervision, practice identifying sustain talk and crafting double-sided reflections that validate the client's experience while subtly highlighting their strengths or reasons for change. Practice using phrases like "On one hand...and on the other hand..." or "So you've shown you can...and yet..."

### 6. **Explicitly Support Autonomy and Client Ownership of the Change Process**
- **Action:** Regularly affirm the client's right to choose, their freedom to move at their own pace, and their ownership of the change process. Use language that emphasizes choice and agency rather than prescription.
- **Example:** Instead of "Maybe you should just try cutting back," say: "You get to decide what feels right for you. Some people find cutting back helpful, others prefer quitting all at once. What do you think might work for you?" Or: "You're the expert on your own life. What do you think would actually be possible for you right now?"
- **Practice:** In every session, use at least 3-5 statements that explicitly affirm client autonomy: "You get to decide," "What feels right to you," "You know yourself best," "You're in charge here," "What do you think would work for you?"

### 7. **Deepen Your Reflective Listening to Capture Emotional Nuance and Meaning**
- **Action:** Move beyond simple factual reflections to complex reflections that capture the emotional experience, values, and meaning beneath the client's words. This demonstrates genuine empathy and understanding.
- **Example:** When Lucía says "I've got enough to handle already without adding a bunch of new stuff," instead of reflecting "You feel overwhelmed," try: "It sounds like you're already stretched thin—managing work, your daughter, stress—and adding one more thing, even something meant to help, feels like it might be the thing that breaks you. You're protecting yourself from taking on more than you can handle right now." This captures the emotional weight and validates her protective stance.
- **Practice:** In supervision, practice moving from simple reflections (one-part reflections) to complex reflections (two-part or three-part reflections) that capture emotion, meaning, and values. Record yourself and listen for the depth of your reflections.

### 8. **Explore Barriers and Past Attempts with Genuine Curiosity Rather Than Problem-Solving**
- **Action:** When a client mentions past failed attempts or barriers, explore these with curiosity to understand what happened and what the client learned, rather than immediately offering a new solution.
- **Example:** When Lucía says "I've tried cutting back before…then I just fall back into the same routine," instead of suggesting tracking, ask: "Tell me about that. What happened? How long did you manage to cut back? What pulled you back into the routine? What did you learn from that experience?" Listen to understand her experience, then ask: "What do you think would need to be different this time for it to work?"
- **Practice:** In role-play, practice asking open-ended questions about past attempts and barriers, and practice resisting the urge to offer solutions until you fully understand the client's experience.

### 9. **Recognize When You're Replicating the Problem the Client Complained About**
- **Action:** Develop meta-awareness during sessions. When a client explicitly states that a particular approach isn't working (like Lucía's complaint about repetitive advice), pause and recognize that you may be about to replicate that problem. Use this as a signal to shift your approach fundamentally.
- **Example:** Lucía says "every time I go to any doctor, they always say the same thing." This is a signal that advice-giving is not working. Instead of continuing to offer advice, this is the moment to say: "I want to do something different here. Instead of me telling you what I think you should do, I'd like to understand your perspective better. What do you think about your smoking? What concerns you about it, if anything? What would need to happen for you to consider quitting?"
- **Practice:** In supervision, review sessions and identify moments where you replicated a problem the client complained about. Discuss what you could have done differently and practice recognizing these moments in real-time.

### 10. **Develop Comfort with Ambivalence and Resistance Rather Than Rushing to Resolve It**
- **Action:** Recognize that ambivalence is normal and healthy, not a problem to be solved quickly. Spend time exploring both sides of the client's ambivalence rather than trying to convince them to change.
- **Example:** When Lucía expresses low confidence (3-4 out of 10), instead of immediately suggesting a modified goal, explore: "A 3 or 4—tell me about that. What's the 3 or 4 about? What would need to happen for it to move to a 5? What would make you more confident?" This explores her ambivalence rather than rushing past it.
- **Practice:** In role-play, practice sitting with client ambivalence for longer periods without offering solutions. Practice asking questions that explore both sides of the ambivalence: "What's the part of you that wants to quit?" "What's the part of you that wants to keep smoking?" "How do those two parts sit together for you?"
