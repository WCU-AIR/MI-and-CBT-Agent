# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates profound lack of acceptance throughout the session. Judgmental language and moral pressure are pervasive ("Why didn't you stop already if you care about your baby's health?"). Fear-based coercion and catastrophizing are used repeatedly ("If you don't, you're risking lifelong damage to your child"). Isabella's autonomy and cultural traditions are framed as obstacles rather than values to be respected. No genuine affirmation of her concern for her baby or her worth as a person is offered. The repeated reframing of her independence as selfishness violates the core MI principle of absolute worth.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Empathic understanding is virtually absent. When Isabella expresses her need for autonomy, the psychiatrist acknowledges it only to immediately dismiss it ("I get that you want to feel in control, but honestly, this isn't the time to be hesitant"). No complex reflections capture Isabella's emotional experience, cultural identity, or genuine ambivalence about change. When Isabella proposes involving her partner as a collaborative approach, the psychiatrist responds with interrogation ("Why haven't you talked to your partner about quitting yet?") rather than reflection. The clinician never explores what wine means to Isabella's family, her relaxation needs, or anticipated barriers. True empathic listening is absent throughout.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** While the psychiatrist maintains clear focus on alcohol cessation (appropriate), the manner of maintaining direction is fundamentally inconsistent with MI principles. Direction is imposed through prescription ("You really should quit," "You have to take this seriously," "Just cutting out wine completely") rather than evoked through collaboration. One brief moment demonstrates skillful, client-centered direction ("Would you be willing to talk more about what would make it easier for you to reduce or stop drinking?"), but this is immediately undermined when the psychiatrist reverts to directive pressure. The session lacks progressive clarification of Isabella's values hierarchy or her vision of herself as a mother. Direction is maintained through coercion rather than collaboration.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian expert stance throughout, positioning themselves as the sole authority on what Isabella "must" do. The opening statement ("I'll be guiding you through what you must do") establishes a hierarchical, non-collaborative frame. Isabella repeatedly attempts to co-construct solutions (proposing to involve her partner, requesting information to share), but these collaborative overtures are dismissed or redirected with interrogation. The only moment of genuine collaboration occurs briefly when the psychiatrist asks what would help Isabella feel supported, but this is quickly abandoned in favor of directive pressure. The session is characterized by clinician-directed telling rather than collaborative exploration.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Autonomy support is critically deficient. Isabella explicitly states her core value ("I'm independent and I'm gonna do what I want"), which should be an opportunity to affirm autonomy while exploring alignment with her values for her baby. Instead, the psychiatrist responds with pressure ("this isn't the time to be hesitant or half-hearted... You really have to put the alcohol away now—no ifs, ands, or buts"). Controlling language pervades the session ("You really should," "You have to," "You need to," "Just do it"). The final question ("Can you promise me you'll put the alcohol out of reach today?") frames commitment as being to the psychiatrist rather than to Isabella's own values. When Isabella identifies her own pathway to change (involving her partner), the psychiatrist questions rather than reinforces it. No affirmation of her right to choose or emphasis on her ownership of the change process is demonstrated.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Evocation—the core of MI—is almost entirely absent. Isabella provides multiple change talk opportunities that should be explored and amplified: "It's really important to me to have a healthy baby and be a good parent" (met with pressure, not exploration); "If I have to quit drinking, I guess ultimately I have to make that decision" (met with directive pressure); "If I could take a brochure home and show it to my partner, and we put the alcohol away, I think that's probably the best thing I can do today" (met with interrogation). The psychiatrist uses telling rather than asking throughout. One brief moment of evocation occurs ("Would you be willing to talk more about what would make it easier for you?"), and Isabella responds thoughtfully with concrete ideas about support. However, this is immediately followed by reversion to telling ("You need to be strong and just stop drinking now"). Change talk is consistently met with pressure rather than reflection and amplification, which actively suppresses rather than cultivates motivation.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Partnership is fundamentally absent. The opening statement ("I'll be guiding you through what you must do") positions the psychiatrist as authority and Isabella as passive recipient. Prescriptive statements dominate ("You really should," "You have to," "Just cutting out wine completely"), and Isabella's substantive contributions are either ignored or redirected. When Isabella offers her collaborative plan (involving her partner, taking home information), the psychiatrist responds with interrogation rather than partnership. One brief moment demonstrates awareness of partnership principles ("I appreciate you sharing that. It's important that you don't feel judged"), but this is immediately followed by reversion to expert stance and directive pressure. Isabella is never positioned as the expert on her own life, family dynamics, cultural values, or readiness for change. The session is characterized by clinician-directed telling rather than genuine power-sharing.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal skill in cultivating change talk and actively suppresses it through pressure and dismissal. Isabella's desire for change ("It's really important to me to have a healthy baby and be a good parent") is met with accusatory questioning rather than amplification. Her commitment language ("If I have to quit drinking, I guess ultimately I have to make that decision") is met with prescriptive pressure rather than exploration. Her concrete action steps ("If I could take a brochure home and show it to my partner") are questioned rather than reinforced. Her willingness ("I'm willing to think about putting the alcohol away") is met with pressure ("You really have to put the alcohol away now—no ifs, ands, or buts"). Rather than reflecting and amplifying change talk, the psychiatrist uses pressure, which typically suppresses motivation. Isabella's responses become increasingly defensive and conditional ("I can't promise I'll be perfect right away, but I want to start moving in the right direction"), suggesting the pressure is having the opposite of the intended effect.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist's approach to sustain talk is confrontational rather than skillful. When Isabella expresses sustain talk ("I have friends who drank during pregnancy without any problems," "I don't see the evidence"), the response is direct contradiction ("It's not just about tradition or what your friends do"), which typically strengthens sustain talk. The psychiatrist avoids some serious MI errors (does not engage in direct argumentation or debate), but lacks the skillfulness needed to truly soften sustain talk. No double-sided reflections are used to acknowledge both her concern for her baby and her reasons for continuing to drink. No reframing is employed to redirect her focus. When Isabella asserts her independence, the psychiatrist acknowledges her autonomy need but immediately dismisses it, which does not effectively soften her defensive stance. Overall, sustain talk is not effectively addressed, and the approach likely strengthens Isabella's resistance.

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall Score: 1.1/5**

### Strengths:
- ✓ Clear and consistent focus on target behavior (alcohol cessation during pregnancy)
- ✓ Avoids some serious MI errors such as direct argumentation or debate
- ✓ One brief moment of collaborative exploration demonstrates awareness of MI principles
- ✓ Provides accurate medical information about alcohol and pregnancy

### Critical Deficiencies:
- ✗ **Fundamental violation of MI spirit:** Session characterized by directive pressure, confrontation, and control rather than partnership, evocation, and autonomy support
- ✗ **Suppression of change talk:** Isabella's own motivations for change are met with pressure and dismissal rather than reflection and amplification
- ✗ **Lack of genuine empathy:** Minimal effort to understand Isabella's perspective, values, cultural identity, or the meaning of wine in her life
- ✗ **Authoritarian stance:** Opening statement and repeated prescriptive language establish hierarchical, non-collaborative relationship that undermines autonomy and intrinsic motivation
- ✗ **Missed evocation opportunities:** Multiple opportunities to draw out Isabella's own reasons for change, her vision of motherhood, and her resources are missed in favor of telling
- ✗ **Ineffective handling of ambivalence:** Rather than exploring Isabella's genuine ambivalence (cultural traditions vs. baby's health), the psychiatrist attempts to eliminate it through pressure, which strengthens resistance

### Impact on Client Engagement:
This session is likely to **decrease** Isabella's intrinsic motivation for change and **increase** her resistance and defensiveness. Isabella enters in contemplation/preparation with genuine concern for her baby and willingness to consider change. The psychiatrist's repeated pressure and dismissal of her autonomy need will activate psychological reactance. Her genuine change talk remains underdeveloped and unamplifed. Her concrete action plan is questioned rather than reinforced. Isabella may comply superficially without developing genuine intrinsic motivation, resulting in lower likelihood of sustained behavior change.

### Immediate Recommendations:
1. **Adopt genuine collaborative stance** – Reframe entire approach as partnership between two experts
2. **Amplify Isabella's change talk** – Explore and reinforce her own motivations rather than pressuring
3. **Respect and explore autonomy** – Treat independence as core value to be respected, not obstacle to overcome
4. **Use evocation rather than telling** – Replace directives with open questions that draw out Isabella's own reasons
5. **Develop genuine empathic understanding** – Spend time understanding what wine means to Isabella and her family
6. **Reinforce concrete action steps** – Support rather than question Isabella's identified pathway to change
7. **Use double-sided reflections** – Acknowledge both sides of ambivalence rather than arguing against sustain talk
8. **Provide information collaboratively** – Offer medical facts in way that invites input and respects autonomy
9. **Eliminate rhetorical questions and pressure** – Replace with genuine open questions inviting exploration
10. **Seek formal MI training and supervision** – This session demonstrates fundamental misunderstanding of MI principles requiring structured professional development

---

**CLINICAL CONCLUSION:** This session represents **severe deficiencies in motivational interviewing practice** and a fundamental violation of MI principles. The psychiatrist must undergo formal MI training and ongoing supervision with an MI expert. Without fundamental restructuring of approach, the likelihood of sustained behavior change is low and risk of harm to therapeutic relationship is high.
