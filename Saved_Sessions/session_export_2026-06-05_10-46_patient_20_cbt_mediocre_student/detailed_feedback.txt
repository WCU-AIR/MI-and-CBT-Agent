
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 2. **Establish a Coherent, Bipolar-Specific Treatment Strategy**
- **Action:** Develop a clear conceptualization linking the patient's cognitions, behaviors, mood, and diagnosis. Create a written treatment plan with specific goals, interventions, and timeline.
- **Example:** "Based on our assessment, I see that your perfectionism and self-criticism are maintaining your depression. - **Practice:** Study bipolar-specific CBT protocols (e.g., Lam et al.'s Cognitive Therapy for Bipolar Disorder). Develop treatment plans for bipolar patients with a supervisor.

### 3. **Implement Structured Thought Records Instead of Simple Thought Replacement**
- **Action:** Replace vague "think differently" suggestions with structured cognitive work. Introduce a thought record that includes: situation, automatic thought, evidence for/against, alternative thought, and outcome. Use this to collaboratively examine beliefs rather than imposing alternatives.
- **Example:** "Let's create a thought record together. When you had the thought 'I'm not good enough,' what happened right before? What evidence do you have for that thought? What evidence contradicts it? What's a more balanced way to think about it?" (Patient generates alternatives, not therapist.)
- **Practice:** Complete thought records with patients in session. Have patients practice between sessions with written worksheets. Review and refine in subsequent sessions.

### 4. **Use Socratic Questioning to Facilitate Guided Discovery**
- **Action:** Replace directive advice with open-ended questions that help the patient discover insights independently. Practice asking rather than telling.
- **Example:** Instead of "Maybe it would help to try thinking about it differently," ask: "When you have that thought, what evidence do you have for it? What evidence contradicts it? What would a friend say about this situation? What's another way to look at this?"
- **Practice:** Record sessions and review your use of questions vs. advice. For each suggestion you make, ask yourself: "Could I ask a question instead?" Practice with a supervisor.

### 5. **Implement Systematic Behavioral Activation with Activity Scheduling**
- **Action:** Assess current activities and mood. Create a structured activity schedule targeting specific depressive symptoms (hypersomnia, hyperphagia, social withdrawal). Start with small, achievable steps.
- **Example:** "Let's look at your typical day. I notice you're sleeping a lot and watching TV. What activities used to help you feel better? Let's schedule three activities this week—maybe a 15-minute walk on Monday, coffee with a friend on Wednesday, and 30 minutes of a hobby on Friday. We'll track your mood before and after each activity."
- **Practice:** Learn activity scheduling protocols. Practice with patients in session. Use mood-activity logs to track outcomes.

### 6. **Address Core Beliefs and Protective Functions of Symptoms**
- **Action:** Move beyond surface-level automatic thoughts to explore core beliefs. When the patient says "It feels safer to expect the worst," explore this protective function: "What do you think would happen if you didn't expect the worst? How has expecting the worst protected you?"
