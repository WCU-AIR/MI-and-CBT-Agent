### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates basic evocation through open-ended questions about motivation and resources but misses multiple opportunities to strategically deepen change talk. When Aisha offers change statements ("I could try going from six to three"), the psychiatrist reflects but doesn't amplify or strengthen them. When she expresses powerful motivation (motherhood, health concerns), the clinician reflects accurately but doesn't explore further with questions like "Tell me more about what it means to be there for your son?" When ambivalence emerges (5/10 confidence), the psychiatrist validates but doesn't ask "What would help you move from a 5 to a 6?" to evoke change talk. The session relies on clinician reflections rather than consistently drawing out Aisha's own language and reasoning—a notable gap in MI technique.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist evokes some change talk ("I could try going from six to three... It feels doable") but lacks strategic amplification and deepening. When Aisha offers change statements, the psychiatrist reflects but doesn't strengthen them with follow-up questions like "What makes three feel like the right number?" When she expresses motherhood motivation, the clinician pivots to confidence assessment rather than exploring this powerful motivator further. Missed opportunities include: not asking "What would help you move from a 5 to a 7?" when she rates confidence at 5/10; not asking "How could you use that same approach with smoking?" when she mentions past successes; and not asking "What else would change if you cut down?" to evoke additional commitment talk. The psychiatrist does not strategically amplify or strengthen Aisha's commitment language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal explicit handling of sustain talk, representing a notable gap in MI technique. When Aisha minimizes her drinking ("I don't think it's really a problem... It's pretty normal"), the psychiatrist reflects but doesn't gently explore or offer a double-sided reflection. When she compares herself to coworkers ("I don't smoke that much compared to others"), this minimization strategy is not addressed. When she expresses ambivalence ("I'm kind of in the middle"), the psychiatrist validates but doesn't strategically explore the hesitation side. Missed opportunities include: not using double-sided reflections to acknowledge both change and sustain talk simultaneously; not reframing stress as a reason FOR change ("managing stress is key—that's a reason to develop new ways to handle it"); and not exploring her ambivalence about cutting down vs. quitting. Sustain talk is reflected but not strategically redirected.

---

## **Key Recommendations for Skill Development**

1. **Strategically amplify change talk** – When clients offer change statements, use follow-up questions to strengthen and explore them rather than simply reflecting and moving on.

2. **Use double-sided reflections** – Acknowledge both sides of ambivalence simultaneously to validate experience while gently highlighting the change side.

3. **Explore and reframe barriers** – When clients identify barriers (like "smoking helps me manage stress"), reframe to highlight how addressing the barrier is a reason to change.

4. **Gently explore behavioral connections** – Use strategic curiosity to explore whether deflected health behaviors connect to stated concerns without pressure.

5. **Ask about values and identity** – Beyond motivation for change, ask about broader values and identity to deepen intrinsic motivation.

6. **Explore ambivalence more thoroughly** – Spend more time exploring both sides of ambivalence before jumping to solutions.

7. **Co-construct coping strategies** – Ask clients what they've already tried before offering expert suggestions; build on their existing wisdom.

8. **Address confidence strategically** – When clients rate low-to-moderate confidence, ask "What would help you move up the scale?" rather than simply validating ambivalence.
