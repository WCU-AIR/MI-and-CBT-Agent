
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Feedback: Therapist Evaluation

---

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

No agenda was established at any point in this session. The therapist opened with a generic greeting ("Hey there! How's it going today?") and immediately pivoted to open-ended exploration without collaboratively identifying session goals, priorities, or time allocation. A proper agenda-setting process would have involved: (1) acknowledging Rachel's presenting concerns about mood fluctuations, (2) explicitly asking what she most wants to work on today, (3) identifying 2-3 specific, prioritized targets for the 45-50 minute session, and (4) establishing time boundaries around each topic.

Instead, the conversation drifted organically from mood fluctuations to personal coping strategies to TV shows to general reassurance. There was no structured return to identified problems, no time management, and no collaborative prioritization. For a 15-year-old with cyclothymic disorder presenting with significant functional impairment (failing grades, lost friendships, family conflict), the absence of agenda-setting represents a fundamental failure in CBT structure. The session lacked direction and accountability.

**Summary:**

The therapist completely failed to establish a collaborative agenda. The session was unstructured, reactive, and lacked clear goals or priorities.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made minimal, inadequate attempts to elicit feedback. There were no explicit check-ins for understanding (e.g., "Does that make sense?" or "How does that land for you?"). The therapist did not ask Rachel to summarize key points, did not solicit her reactions to suggestions, and did not verify whether interventions felt helpful or relevant.

The only quasi-feedback moment occurred when Rachel spontaneously offered information ("Maybe if I tried setting a reminder or something, it might help me keep on track"), to which the therapist responded with validation but did not probe deeper ("Do you think that could work?"). However, this was patient-initiated, not therapist-elicited.

At session end, the therapist offered generic encouragement ("You've got this!") without asking: "What was most helpful today?" "Do you feel like we addressed what you came in for?" "What would be useful to focus on next time?" or "How confident do you feel about trying these suggestions?" For a wary, anxious adolescent with unpredictable mood changes, systematic feedback elicitation is essential to ensure the patient feels heard and to adjust interventions based on her actual experience.

**Summary:**

Feedback elicitation was essentially absent. The therapist did not systematically check understanding, solicit reactions, or verify patient satisfaction.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated surface-level understanding of Rachel's explicit statements but missed critical nuances and failed to demonstrate genuine comprehension of her internal experience. Rachel clearly articulated several key concerns:

1. **Unpredictability and distress:** "It's hard to keep up with school and my friends when I don't know which mood is going to show up. It's frustrating and makes me anxious a lot."
2. **Anhedonia and withdrawal:** "Even things I used to like don't seem fun anymore. It feels like everything is too much."
3. **Mood-dependent motivation deficits:** "When my mood's low, I mostly just want to stay in my room... it's hard to get myself to start sometimes."
4. **Cyclical pattern disruption:** "When I'm hypomanic, I might start lots of things, but I don't keep up once the mood shifts back down."

The therapist's responses were generic platitudes ("life's got its ups and downs for everyone") rather than reflections demonstrating understanding of Rachel's specific cyclical pattern, the distress caused by unpredictability, or the functional impairment across domains. Most critically, the therapist failed to recognize or address the core clinical feature: Rachel's distress stems not from sadness alone, but from the **unpredictability and loss of control** over her mood states. The therapist also missed Rachel's implicit fear about the future ("What if my moods don't get better?") and her shame about functional decline (grades dropping, lost friendships). Instead of exploring these, the therapist offered reassurance disconnected from Rachel's actual experience.

**Summary:**

The therapist grasped Rachel's explicit statements but failed to demonstrate understanding of her core distress (unpredictability, loss of control, functional impairment) or her internal emotional experience. Empathy was superficial.

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist's interpersonal approach was fundamentally problematic and violated core therapeutic boundaries. While not hostile or demeaning, the therapist displayed multiple serious deficiencies:

This violated professional boundaries and shifted focus from the patient to the therapist. For a 15-year-old with an unstable mood disorder, excessive therapist self-disclosure can be confusing, can undermine the therapeutic relationship, and can make the patient feel responsible for the therapist's emotional experience.

2. **Peer-like rather than professional stance:** The therapist used casual language ("Hey there!", "Oh, totally!", "That's funny how little things can have a big impact") and engaged in reciprocal conversation ("What about you? Cyclothymic disorder is not typical adolescent mood variation; it requires specialized understanding and validation.

### 5. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/6**

- "Maybe you could try something like that—just a little step to get going."
- "Maybe you could find something like that too?"

Most critically, the therapist never explored Rachel's own theories about her mood fluctuations, her coping strengths, or her preferences for intervention.
