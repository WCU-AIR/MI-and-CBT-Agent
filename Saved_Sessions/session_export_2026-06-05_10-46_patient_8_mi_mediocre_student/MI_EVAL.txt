# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal acceptance and repeatedly overrides patient's stated boundaries. When patient establishes clear boundary ("It's not really any of your business"), psychiatrist responds with persuasion rather than respect. No affirmation of patient's worth or autonomy. Tone is clinical and directive rather than warm and supportive. Fundamentally anti-MI approach that actively damages rapport.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** No genuine effort to understand patient's perspective. Makes unfounded assumptions ("so you're feeling tired and stressed") without checking accuracy. Uses pseudo-empathic language ("I understand, but") that immediately contradicts itself. Never explores why patient is reluctant or what concerns underlie resistance. Imposes narrative rather than discovering patient's actual concerns.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Maintains narrow, assumed focus on stress and sleep without collaboratively establishing what patient actually wants to address. Direction is imposed rather than co-created. Shifts arbitrarily between topics (main problem → stress vs. physical → tiredness → sleep) without strategic purpose. Does not adjust when patient signals misalignment through boundary-setting.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Operates entirely from expert-driven, hierarchical stance. Language is consistently directive: "you should try," "you should consider," "you should think about." Uses leading questions designed to confirm psychiatrist's hypothesis rather than genuine exploration. No seeking of patient input on what might help. No co-construction of solutions or acknowledgment that patient has expertise about own life.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Actively undermines autonomy at every turn. Pressures patient to disclose when boundary is established. Repeated use of "should" language communicates psychiatrist knows what is best and expects compliance. When patient expresses uncertainty, psychiatrist sets targets rather than exploring ambivalence. Directly contradicts patient's autonomy with "but" statements that override stated preferences.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Makes no genuine effort to evoke patient's own motivations, values, or ideas. Approach is entirely prescriptive and directive—the opposite of evocation. Uses leading questions designed to confirm psychiatrist's hypothesis rather than open-ended exploration. Never asks what patient thinks, what matters to them, or what ideas they have. Imposes agenda rather than drawing out client motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Maintains expert-driven stance throughout with no genuine power-sharing. Diagnoses, prescribes solutions, and sets targets unilaterally. Never asks patient what she thinks or knows about herself. Language is consistently hierarchical ("I can support you better," "it would probably help"). Repeated overriding of patient's stated boundary communicates psychiatrist's judgment supersedes patient's own assessment. Opposite of true partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Does not cultivate change talk; imposes it. Patient never expresses desire, reasons, or commitment to change. Psychiatrist makes assumptions and prescribes solutions rather than asking questions designed to elicit change talk. When patient expresses uncertainty, psychiatrist sets targets rather than exploring ambivalence. Prescriptive, directive approach actually suppresses intrinsic motivation. Only "Okay" from patient appears to be compliance, not genuine change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Confronts patient's sustain talk (boundary-setting: "It's not really any of your business") with pressure and persuasion rather than validating and exploring it. Does not use double-sided reflections or explore underlying concerns about disclosure. Approach is confrontational and does not soften resistance; likely strengthens it. Patient repeats boundary twice, suggesting psychiatrist's approach is ineffective. Minor positive: maintains calm, non-hostile tone without anger or dismissiveness.

## **Overall Session Score: 1.3/5**

### **Summary:**

This session demonstrates **fundamental misalignment with Motivational Interviewing principles** and represents poor MI practice. The psychiatrist operates from an expert-driven, prescriptive stance that directly contradicts core MI spirit of partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation. Most critically, the psychiatrist repeatedly overrides the patient's stated boundary with pressure and persuasion, communicating that the psychiatrist's judgment supersedes patient autonomy. This is not only ineffective MI; it actively harms rapport-building and strengthens resistance.

**Key Deficits:**
- Repeated boundary violations without genuine respect or exploration
- Unfounded assumptions presented as facts
- Pseudo-empathic language that contradicts itself
- Prescriptive solutions imposed rather than evoked
- No collaborative agenda-setting
- Ineffective handling of resistance through confrontation rather than validation

**Clinical Impact:** Patient has not expressed any desire, reasons, or commitment to change. Prescriptive approach suppresses intrinsic motivation. Any changes would be compliance-based rather than intrinsically motivated, reducing likelihood of sustainability.

**Recommendation:** This psychiatrist requires significant training in MI principles with supervised practice, role-plays, and deliberate skill-building in acceptance, autonomy support, evocation, and partnership. Without substantial change, this approach will continue to damage therapeutic relationships and reduce client engagement and change likelihood.
