### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates strong reflective listening and accurate perspective-taking, particularly when capturing Aizada's core ambivalence: "caught between a strong need to be heard and taken seriously, and the frustration of not knowing how to make that happen." Reflections are emotionally attuned and capture both content and emotion. However, empathy could deepen by exploring the emotional meaning underlying her resistance to psychological explanations—what does it feel like to have her pain potentially attributed to stress? The clinician does not fully explore the emotional exhaustion and demoralization represented by her fatigue and pain.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains focus on Aizada's presenting concern but lacks strategic clarity about the specific change goal being pursued. The session does not clearly establish whether the aim is: (1) helping her advocate for biomedical testing, (2) exploring the stress-symptom connection, (3) building confidence in self-advocacy, or (4) reducing health anxiety. Most significantly, when Aizada reveals the critical insight about symptom improvement during the wedding, the psychiatrist does not strategically amplify this as a potential direction for exploration. The final plan is reasonable but somewhat vague regarding specific, measurable goals.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates strong collaborative stance throughout, exemplified by the opening question inviting Aizada to set the agenda. The clinician consistently seeks Aizada's input and perspective rather than imposing direction. Questions like "What do you think would be most helpful for you?" and "What might be the best way for me to check in?" genuinely invite collaboration. However, one moment of subtle directiveness occurs when suggesting family obligation as motivation ("Recognizing that your health is key to being there for your loved ones might be a compelling reason"). A more collaborative approach would invite Aizada to discover this connection herself.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates consistent respect for Aizada's autonomy and choice, particularly by honoring her stated readiness level and not pressuring for intensive intervention. The language "your plan" emphasizes Aizada's ownership of the change process. The clinician respects her preference for "small, steady support" rather than big changes. However, autonomy support could be strengthened by more explicitly affirming her right to pursue biomedical testing if that remains her choice, and by avoiding subtle prescriptions about what should motivate her. A stronger statement would be: "Whatever you decide about testing or how to move forward, it's your choice, and I'm here to support you."

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates some evocation through open-ended questions but misses multiple strategic opportunities to amplify critical change talk. Most significantly, when Aizada reveals that symptoms improved during the wedding and worsen when her husband is away, the clinician does not ask follow-up questions to deepen this insight: "Tell me more about that pattern—what do you notice?" or "What does that suggest to you about what might be contributing to your symptoms?" The psychiatrist does not strategically evoke Aizada's own reasons for change or her intrinsic motivations. When asking scaling questions about confidence, the clinician does not follow up with evocative questions about what would increase confidence.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates strong partnership, positioning Aizada as the expert on her own experience and co-creator of the session direction. The opening question exemplifies this: "What's on your mind or what would you most like to focus on?" The clinician invites Aizada to make meaning of her own experience rather than providing expert interpretation. However, partnership could be stronger by more explicitly acknowledging Aizada's expertise about her own body and symptoms. When the psychiatrist suggests family obligation as motivation, this subtly positions the clinician as having insight into what should matter to Aizada rather than inviting her to discover this herself.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** This represents a significant weakness. While Aizada spontaneously produces important change talk—particularly about the stress-symptom connection ("When my son got married, I was too busy to notice the pain" and "It does get worse when my husband's away")—the psychiatrist does not strategically cultivate, amplify, or reinforce this change talk. The clinician does not ask follow-up questions to deepen these critical insights. When Aizada identifies her own strength in advocating during the wedding, the psychiatrist reflects this but does not amplify it as evidence of capability. The final confidence rating of 6/10 is not explored with evocative questions about what would move her higher. This represents a critical missed opportunity to strengthen Aizada's motivation for change.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates basic skill in softening sustain talk through validation and pacing adjustments but misses opportunities to use double-sided reflections or strategic reframes. Aizada's primary sustain talk—"It's not in my head," "Dr. Brown should be doing more tests," "It might be cancer"—represents her resistance to psychological explanations. The clinician validates these concerns but does not gently introduce alternative perspectives. A more strategic response to "I can't just accept that it's something in my head" would be: "On one hand, you want to make sure nothing serious is missed, and that makes complete sense. At the same time, I'm hearing that you've noticed your symptoms change with stress and what's happening in your life. How do you make sense of both of those things?" The psychiatrist does not address the sustain talk about being labeled "paranoid" or "difficult."

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Session Score: 3.4/5**

### Strengths:
- ✓ Exemplary opening establishing partnership and client-centered agenda-setting
- ✓ Consistent validation and unconditional positive regard throughout
- ✓ Accurate reflective listening capturing both content and emotional nuance
- ✓ Appropriate affirmation of Aizada's demonstrated capability to advocate
- ✓ Respectful honoring of autonomy and stated readiness level
- ✓ Practical, manageable planning that respects current capacity

### Critical Areas for Improvement:
- ✗ **Minimal strategic cultivation of change talk** – Spontaneous change talk about stress-symptom connection is not amplified or deepened
- ✗ **Lack of strategic direction** – Specific change goal remains unclear; no systematic guidance toward exploration of stress-symptom link
- ✗ **Insufficient evocation** – Limited use of questions that help Aizada discover her own insights or articulate intrinsic motivations
- ✗ **Limited softening of sustain talk** – No double-sided reflections or strategic reframes to gently introduce alternative perspectives
- ✗ **Missed amplification of key insights** – Critical pattern about symptom fluctuation with stress is not strategically deepened

### Recommendations for Next Session:
1. Immediately amplify spontaneous change talk about stress-symptom patterns with follow-up questions
2. Establish clear, measurable change goals and strategic direction
3. Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge both biomedical concerns and emerging evidence of stress-symptom connection
4. Ask evocative questions to help Aizada discover her own reasons for change
5. Deepen empathy by exploring emotional meaning underlying resistance to psychological explanations
6. Explicitly validate biomedical concerns before exploring alternative perspectives
7. Establish specific, measurable goals for symptom tracking with clear indicators of progress
8. Use scaling questions strategically to evoke change talk about what would increase readiness

---

**Clinical Impression:**

This session demonstrates solid foundational MI practice with strong partnership, acceptance, and empathy. The psychiatrist establishes genuine collaboration and maintains consistent validation. However, the session has significant gaps in strategic cultivation of change talk and evocation—core MI competencies. Most critically, when Aizada spontaneously reveals the stress-symptom connection, the psychiatrist does not ask follow-up questions to amplify this pivotal insight. The session embodies the MI spirit of compassion but does not fully leverage MI's power to help Aizada discover her own insights or develop intrinsic motivation for exploring psychological factors. With focused attention to strategic amplification of change talk, clearer direction, and deliberate evocation, this clinician could substantially strengthen their MI practice and increase the likelihood of evoking sustained client motivation for meaningful change.
