### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist uses confrontation rather than skillful techniques to address sustain talk, which typically strengthens rather than softens it. Adrian provides sustain talk ("It's just something to do when I'm bored," "helps me deal with boredom," "breaks up the monotony"), and psychiatrist responds with confrontation ("You're playing with fire," "You're risking permanent damage")—direct confrontation that strengthens sustain talk rather than softens it. Demonstrates "righting reflex" in problematic form. Does not use double-sided reflection or reframing. One brief moment of acknowledgment appears late in session ("I get that you're frustrated") but immediately followed by confrontation ("calling me weak or sugarcoating stuff?"), so softening is minimal and inconsistent. ### Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.2/5**

#### **Strengths:**
- Maintains clear focus on methamphetamine use as primary concern
- Communicates accurate medical information about risks of continued use
- Remains engaged with Adrian throughout session rather than discharging him

#### **Critical Deficiencies:**
This session represents a fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles and demonstrates the "righting reflex"—the clinician's impulse to fix the problem by telling the client what to do. While understandable, this approach is directly counterproductive in addiction treatment.

**Key Problems:**

1. **Therapeutic Relationship Damage:** Judgmental stance, repeated demands, and dismissal of Adrian's perspective create shame and defensiveness rather than safety. Adrian explicitly states "That pressure just makes me want to shut down"—direct indicator approach is backfiring.

2. **Amplification of Sustain Talk:** Research demonstrates confrontation and pressure strengthen resistance to change. By repeatedly telling Adrian he "has to" quit and dismissing alternatives, psychiatrist increases Adrian's commitment to status quo.

3. **Missed Evocation Opportunities:** Adrian provides multiple instances of change talk and vulnerability that could be amplified. Instead, these are ignored or overridden. Psychiatrist never asks about Adrian's own reasons for change, values, fears, or what he cares about.

4. **Autonomy Undermined:** Adrian explicitly states need for autonomy ("I need to do it on my terms"); psychiatrist directly overrides this. Research on self-determination theory demonstrates autonomy support is essential for sustained behavior change.

5. **No Collaborative Problem-Solving:** Adrian proposes cutting back; psychiatrist dismisses without exploration. Even if cutting back is not ultimate goal, exploring Adrian's reasoning builds engagement and provides valuable information.

#### **Impact on Client Engagement and Change:**
This session is likely to decrease Adrian's motivation for change and increase defensiveness. Adrian enters with ambivalence (normal contemplation stage) and willingness to engage ("I want to get better, but I need help figuring out how to do it"). Psychiatrist's response is pressure and judgment, typically resulting in either increased defensiveness or superficial compliance without genuine commitment.

Adrian's final statement—"I want to get better, but I need help figuring out how to do it without feeling like I'm setting myself up to fail"—is clear request for collaborative, supportive problem-solving. Psychiatrist's failure to provide this represents critical clinical error undermining likelihood of successful treatment.

#### **Immediate Recommendation:**
This clinician should not continue conducting addiction treatment sessions without formal Motivational Interviewing training and ongoing supervision. Current approach causes harm by damaging therapeutic relationship and strengthening resistance to change. Formal MI training (minimum 2-3 day workshop plus ongoing consultation) is required before resuming addiction treatment practice.
