
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **Implement Structured Exposure Therapy (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Develop a fear hierarchy with the patient and systematically expose her to feared social situations. This is the gold standard for social anxiety disorder and is essential for meaningful improvement.

**Example:**
- Work with the patient to create a list of feared social situations (e.g., speaking in class, making phone calls, attending social events, dating)
- Rate each situation on a 0-100 anxiety scale
- Start with lower-anxiety situations and gradually progress to more challenging ones
- Plan specific exposures (e.g., "This week, I will raise my hand and ask one question in class")
- Review the exposure in the next session (What happened? What did you learn? Did your feared outcome occur?)
- Repeat exposures until anxiety decreases

**Practice:** Role-play exposure scenarios in session. For example, practice speaking in front of the therapist to simulate classroom anxiety. This builds confidence and skill before real-world exposure.

---

### 2. **Conduct Systematic Homework Review and Adjustment (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** At the beginning of each session, spend 10-15 minutes reviewing homework in detail. Ask specific questions about what was attempted, what worked, what didn't, and why. Adjust assignments based on results.

**Example:**
- "Last week, I asked you to practice breathing exercises a few times a day. Let's talk about how that went. How many times did you practice? What situations were you in? Did the breathing help? What got in the way of practicing more often?"
- If homework was not completed: "I notice you didn't practice the breathing exercises. That's important information. Let's figure out what got in the way. Was it that you forgot? That it didn't feel helpful? That you didn't believe it would work? Let's problem-solve together."
- If homework was partially successful: "Great, you practiced three times and it helped a bit. Let's build on that. What would make it easier to remember to practice more often?"

**Practice:** Create a homework review checklist to ensure consistency across sessions.

---

### 3. **Develop and Share a Clear Treatment Conceptualization (HIGH PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Explicitly explain to the patient how her anxiety disorder works and why the treatment approach will help. This increases engagement and collaboration.

**Example:**
"Mathilda, I want to explain what I think is happening with your anxiety. When you're called on in class, you feel anxious and have the thought 'They'll think I'm an idiot.' Because of this anxiety, you freeze and don't answer—you avoid the situation. This avoidance actually makes the anxiety worse over time because you never learn that your feared outcome doesn't happen. You never learn that people don't actually think you're an idiot, or that even if you say something imperfect, it's not catastrophic.

Our treatment will focus on gradually approaching these situations—speaking in class, making phone calls, going to social events—so you can learn that your fears aren't accurate. We'll also work on the thoughts that fuel the anxiety. Does this make sense? What questions do you have?"

**Practice:** Write out the conceptualization and review it with the patient. Refer back to it when assigning exposures.

---

### 4. **Establish Specific, Measurable Treatment Goals (HIGH PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Work with the patient to set clear, measurable goals for treatment. This provides direction and allows progress tracking.

**Example:**
- "By the end of treatment, I want to be able to speak up in class at least once per week without excessive anxiety"
- "I want to make one phone call per week without significant distress"
- "I want to attend one social event per month and stay for at least one hour"
- "I want to go on a date within the next three months"

**Practice:** Use the SMART framework (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound). Review progress toward goals at each session.

---

### 5. **Teach Cognitive Restructuring Systematically Using Thought Records (HIGH PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Move beyond vague "thought substitution" to systematic cognitive restructuring using a structured thought record. Teach the patient to examine evidence and generate believable alternatives.

**Example:**
Provide the patient with a thought record template:

| Situation | Automatic Thought | Feeling | Evidence For | Evidence Against | Alternative Thought | Outcome |
|-----------|-------------------|---------|--------------|------------------|-------------------|---------|
| Called on in class | "They'll think I'm an idiot" | Anxious, ashamed | I sometimes say things that aren't perfect | I've answered correctly many times; people don't laugh at me; my professor seems to like my work | "I might say something imperfect, and that's okay. Most people won't judge me harshly" | Anxiety decreases slightly |

**Practice:** Complete one thought record together in session, then assign the patient to complete one per week. Review results in the next session.

---

### 6. **Implement Behavioral Activation Targeting Valued Activities (HIGH PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Help the patient identify valued activities and social connections, then schedule them systematically. This addresses social isolation and builds approach behaviors.

**Example:**
- "What activities or social connections are important to you? What would you like to do more of?"
- Patient: "I like volunteering and babysitting. I also miss having close friends."
- Therapist: "Let's schedule specific times to volunteer and reach out to friends. For example, this week, you could volunteer for 2 hours on Saturday and text one friend to see if they want to get coffee."

**Practice:** Create a weekly activity schedule with the patient. Include both valued activities and exposure-based activities (e.g., speaking in class).

### 7. **Address Core Beliefs About Inadequacy and Unworthiness (HIGH PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Explore and challenge the patient's core beliefs about being fundamentally inadequate or unworthy of acceptance. These beliefs underlie the avoidant personality disorder.

**Example:**

### 10. **Use Guided Discovery and Socratic Questioning Consistently (MEDIUM PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Replace direct advice-giving with skillful Socratic questioning that helps the patient reach insights independently.

**Example:**
Instead of: "Maybe they just want you to try, and it's okay if you don't get it perfect."
