Opening statement ("I'll be telling you exactly what steps to take") signals conditional regard contingent on compliance. When patient asserts boundary, psychiatrist responds with "you need to be honest here," implying dishonesty and moral failure. ### Direction

Psychiatrist announces unilaterally: "I'll be telling you exactly what steps to take." Focus exists but is fundamentally misaligned with MI principles; direction achieved through coercion rather than strategic evocation and collaboration.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Systematic autonomy violation at every turn. Opening statement explicitly removes choice: "I'll be telling you exactly what steps to take." When patient asserts boundary, psychiatrist responds with pressure and demands, not respect for autonomy. Key autonomy-violating statements permeate session: "You have to quit immediately," "You need to take this seriously now," "You can't just keep avoiding this," "You must go get them now," "There's no excuse," "this is non-negotiable," "Starting tomorrow, you must use nicotine patches every day and avoid all cigarettes. No exceptions," "I expect you to follow this exactly." Every statement removes choice and imposes control. Not a single moment affirms patient's right to make own decisions or emphasizes ownership of process. When patient says "No" to nicotine replacement, psychiatrist responds: "You can't just say no and expect to quit smoking on willpower alone"—removing choice rather than exploring preferences. Session actively undermines autonomy and increases reactance; patient's repeated boundary-setting and minimal responses are predictable reactions to autonomy violation.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Zero evocation present. Pure imposition replaces drawing out patient's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources. Psychiatrist never asks: "What do you think about your smoking?" "How do you feel about your health?" "What matters most to you?" "What would help you?" "What have you tried before?" Instead, psychiatrist lectures: "Smoking ruins your health and leads to serious diseases," "smoking is dangerous and you need to quit now," "you're risking your lungs, heart, and overall health," "cancer, heart disease, and lung problems." Assumes patient is unmotivated and attempts to install motivation through fear and pressure—opposite of evocation. Research shows externally imposed motivation less effective than intrinsic motivation. By lecturing rather than evoking, psychiatrist actively reduces likelihood patient will develop own reasons for change. When patient provides minimal responses, psychiatrist fills every silence with more directives and lectures rather than using silence, open questions, and reflections to invite deeper exploration.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Complete opposite of partnership demonstrated. Psychiatrist positions self as sole expert; patient as passive recipient of expert knowledge and commands. Expert positioning evident in: "I'll be telling you exactly what steps to take," "I'll just assume you're smoking daily," "I'm going to set a strict plan for you," "I expect you to follow this exactly." Patient positioned as passive recipient: no questions about patient's experience, preferences, or ideas; no acknowledgment of patient's knowledge about own life; no invitation for patient to contribute to plan; patient's "No" overridden without exploration. Power imbalance pervasive: psychiatrist makes all decisions; patient's input not sought or valued; plan framed as "non-negotiable"; compliance demanded, not negotiated. True partnership would position patient as expert on own life and psychiatrist as collaborative resource. Instead, patient treated as object to be fixed rather than person to be partnered with.

**Comments:** Zero change talk evoked or cultivated. Patient's responses represent only resistance and superficial compliance: "It's not really any of your business" (sustain talk/resistance), "Mm-hmm" (minimal engagement), "No" (explicit resistance to proposed intervention), repeated "It's not really any of your business" (continued resistance), "Okay" (compliance without commitment), "Fine" (resigned compliance without commitment). None represent genuine change talk about desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment to change. Psychiatrist makes no effort to evoke change talk; continues lecturing and demanding, which predictably generates more resistance. Missed opportunities throughout: when patient states "It's not really any of your business," psychiatrist could explore trust and openness; when patient says "No" to nicotine replacement, psychiatrist could explore concerns and preferences; psychiatrist never asks about patient's values, goals, or reasons for potentially changing—foundation of change talk. By session end, patient has not expressed single statement indicating desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment to change. Psychiatrist has failed to cultivate any intrinsic motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Sustain talk met with confrontation rather than skillful softening. Patient's primary sustain talk—"It's not really any of your business" (stated three times)—is legitimate boundary-setting reflecting both resistance to change and need for respect and autonomy. Psychiatrist's responses: First instance: "Look, you need to be honest here"—confrontation invalidating patient's statement. Second instance: "Look, you can't just keep avoiding this"—confrontation framing boundary as avoidance. Third instance: no direct response, but psychiatrist continues with directives. Skilled MI clinician would use double-sided reflection: "On one hand, you feel like this is private. On the other hand, you're here, which tells me part of you might be open to talking about it. What's that about?" This acknowledges sustain talk without confronting it and gently explores ambivalence. Instead, psychiatrist responds with confrontation and pressure, predictably strengthening sustain talk. By session end, patient still saying "It's not really any of your business," indicating psychiatrist's approach has failed to soften resistance and has likely strengthened it.

### Summary Assessment

**Strengths:**
- Maintains consistent focus on target behavior (smoking cessation) throughout session, preventing aimless wandering
- Explicit clarity about recommendations (nicotine replacement therapy, immediate cessation), though clarity achieved through coercion rather than collaboration

**Critical Deficiencies:**
This session represents **fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles across all nine domains**. Psychiatrist operates from expert-driven, authoritarian model antithetical to MI:

1. Complete absence of acceptance and unconditional positive regard
2. Zero empathy and perspective-taking; lectures replace reflective listening
3. Unilateral direction and control without exploring patient preferences or readiness
4. Authoritarian rather than collaborative stance
5. Systematic autonomy violation generating predictable reactance
6. Imposition rather than evocation of motivation
7. No power-sharing or partnership; patient input not sought or valued
8. Failure to cultivate any genuine change talk
9. Confrontation of sustain talk strengthening rather than softening resistance

**Impact on Client Engagement and Change:**
Session is **highly counterproductive**. Likely outcomes:
- Increased reactance and resistance due to autonomy violation
- Damaged therapeutic relationship reducing future engagement
- Reduced intrinsic motivation; only superficial compliance generated
- High likelihood of relapse once external pressure removed
- Missed opportunity to build rapport with resistant adolescent

**Recommendation:** Psychiatrist requires significant reorientation and formal MI training. Fundamental shift needed from expert-driven to collaborative stance. Seek supervision with MI-trained clinician and enroll in formal MI training program.
