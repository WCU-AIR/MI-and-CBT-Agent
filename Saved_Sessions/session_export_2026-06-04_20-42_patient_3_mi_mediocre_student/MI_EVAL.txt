# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal acceptance and unconditional positive regard demonstrated. While opening is warm, clinician quickly shifts to directive, prescriptive stance communicating judgment rather than genuine acceptance. Directly suggests quitting despite patient's stated preference for reduction. Fails to normalize patient's struggle or affirm her strengths as a mother balancing multiple responsibilities. Repeated use of "should" language communicates expert judgment rather than collaborative exploration. Powerful change talk regarding her son ("I don't want something to happen to me") acknowledged only superficially without returning to it as source of intrinsic motivation. Conditional acceptance evident—warmth contingent on patient accepting clinician's agenda.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Superficial and inconsistently applied empathy. One adequate reflection early on ("So you're smoking more lately, especially in the evenings") demonstrates basic listening, but followed by closed questions and prescriptive statements revealing limited effort to understand internal experience. When patient describes evening smoking as way to "unwind," clinician does not explore emotional meaning; instead pivots to problem-solving. Fails to capture emotional complexity of patient's ambivalence—both motivation and hesitation present but not reflected with complex reflection. When patient expresses doubt about maintaining change, clinician offers reassurance without demonstrating genuine understanding of barriers or concerns. Misses emotional subtext of initial defensiveness. Surface-level acknowledgment only; minimal effort to understand emotional experience or meaning behind behaviors.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** General focus on smoking behavior maintained throughout session with adequate directional consistency; conversation does not wander aimlessly. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively established. Clinician begins with open question but immediately narrows focus based on clinician's agenda, not patient's priorities. Fails to maintain strategic direction toward patient's stated goals and values—when patient mentions son multiple times as motivator, clinician does not use this as strategic anchor to deepen exploration. Direction lacks progressive clarification; rather than helping patient articulate own reasons for change and barriers, clinician imposes plan. Focus present but clinician-driven rather than client-centered.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Severe deficit in collaboration throughout session. Psychiatrist adopts expert-driven, authoritarian stance with minimal genuine power-sharing or joint problem-solving. Prescriptive language dominates: repeated use of "should" ("you should just try to quit," "you should try setting a small goal," "It would probably help if...") communicates clinician has answers and client should follow, not that they are partners. Unilateral goal-setting evident—initially pushes for quitting despite patient's clear statement of readiness for reduction; only reluctantly accepts patient's preference after resistance. Solutions imposed, not co-created: specific interventions (walks, tracking, replacing cigarettes) suggested without asking patient what she thinks might work or what has worked before. Client's input does not substantially influence session direction—when patient states time constraints, clinician does not adjust plan or explore collaboratively. No shared decision-making; final plan presented as clinician's recommendation, not jointly developed. Unequal power dynamic maintained throughout; clinician maintains expert role, not collaborative partner.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Critical failure to support client autonomy throughout session, directly contradicting core MI principle. Subtle pressure to quit evident despite patient's clear statement of non-readiness ("Do you think that maybe you should just try to quit smoking altogether?" communicates quitting is right choice, not that patient has freedom to decide). Patient's explicitly chosen goal of reduction initially resisted; only accepted after patient pushes back, communicating clinician does not respect her autonomy. Prescriptive rather than exploratory language removes agency from client—tells patient what to do ("try to replace at least one evening cigarette," "keep track of how many you smoke") rather than asking what she thinks might work. No explicit affirmation of patient's right to choose, freedom to succeed or fail, or ownership of change process. Implicit judgment of her choice evident—suggesting quitting is better than cutting back undermines confidence in her own decision-making. Ambivalence treated as something to overcome with plan, not as legitimate information about readiness and autonomy. Virtually absent autonomy support directly violates fundamental MI principle and likely reduces intrinsic motivation.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Largely absent evocation of client's own motivations, values, and resources. Clinician relies on advice-giving and problem-solving—opposite of evocation. Minimal exploration of client's own reasons for change: when patient mentions concern about cancer and being there for son, clinician does not explore these powerful motivations; moves to prescriptive solutions instead. No exploration of past successes or resources; clinician asks "Have you tried anything like that before?" but does not explore patient's actual strengths, past successes with behavior change, or internal resources. Advice-giving replaces asking: rather than asking what might help patient relax instead of smoking, clinician tells her ("Maybe some exercise or hobbies could help you unwind instead?"). No exploration of values—while patient mentions son as motivator, clinician does not deeply explore values around motherhood, health, or vision of desired self. Limited change talk elicitation: patient produces some change talk ("I could maybe try cutting back," "I worry about cancer," "I don't want something to happen to me"), but clinician does not strategically amplify or deepen; no follow-up questions like "What else makes you want to cut back?" or "How important is that to you?" No exploration of confidence or ability—when patient rates confidence at 5/10, clinician does not explore what would increase confidence or what patient believes she is capable of. Minimal reliance on advice-giving and problem-solving rather than drawing out patient's own motivations, values, and resources significantly reduces likelihood of evoking intrinsic motivation for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Essentially absent partnership throughout session. Understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within client fundamentally missing. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role and maintains this stance throughout with no genuine power-sharing. Expert-driven agenda evident from opening: "I thought we could talk about any health or lifestyle changes you might be thinking about. I have a few things in mind we could cover" immediately establishes clinician as expert with predetermined agenda, not partner exploring client's priorities. Clinician provides all solutions without asking patient what she thinks might work or inviting her expertise about her own life. Client's contributions do not substantially influence session direction—when patient expresses time constraints ("I barely have time for anything else right now"), this does not lead to collaborative reassessment; clinician suggests walk anyway, implying client's input not truly shaping direction. No genuine curiosity about client's expertise; questions used to gather information supporting clinician's agenda, not to learn from client's expertise. Implicit message throughout: "I know what's best for you," not "We are partners figuring this out together." No acknowledgment of client's wisdom or affirmation of her self-knowledge. Virtually absent partnership with minimal genuine power-sharing or acknowledgment that patient's wisdom about her own life is central to change process.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** While patient produces some change talk during session, clinician does minimal work to actively evoke, amplify, or reinforce it. Change talk present but largely by accident, not through skillful clinician technique. Patient's change talk includes: "I could maybe try cutting back in the evenings—from six down to three cigarettes or something like that" (Ability/Commitment); "Being a mother, I don't want something to happen to me" (Reason/Need); "I worry about cancer" (Reason); "It matters to me" (Importance); "I'm willing to give it a try and see how it goes" (Commitment). However, clinician fails to strategically amplify: when patient states "Being a mother, I don't want something to happen to me," clinician does not follow up with deepening questions; moves to prescriptive solutions instead. No deepening of reasons for change; patient's concern about cancer and motivation as mother left undeveloped despite being powerful sources of intrinsic motivation. No reinforcement of change talk; when patient says "I could maybe try cutting back," clinician accepts and moves to prescribing rather than reinforcing with genuine affirmation or curiosity. Missed opportunities to evoke ability talk; when patient expresses doubt ("I'm not sure how well I'll stick to it"), clinician does not explore what she believes she can do or what has worked before. No exploration of commitment; when patient agrees to try plan, clinician does not deepen commitment by asking about confidence level or what would strengthen it. Prescriptive language crowds out change talk; patient responding to clinician's agenda rather than articulating her own. While some change talk emerges, clinician does minimal work to strategically evoke, amplify, or reinforce it.

When sustain talk does emerge, clinician's response is inconsistent. Patient's sustain talk includes: "I'm not really looking to talk about much, just the usual stuff" (Resistance to change discussion); "I barely have time for anything else right now" (Barrier/Reason against change); "I'm not sure how well I'll stick to it" (Doubt/Ability concern); "I just hope I can keep up with it with everything else I have going on" (Doubt/Barrier). When patient initially resists ("I'm not really looking to talk about much"), clinician does not explore resistance or use double-sided reflection; simply moves forward with agenda. Positive factor: clinician does not reinforce or amplify patient's barriers or doubts, which is appropriate; however, this not due to skillful technique but rather to clinician's focus on prescriptive solutions. Clinician does not skillfully soften sustain talk through strategic technique; sustain talk not amplified (good) but also not addressed with curiosity, exploration, or double-sided reflections.

## Summary of Clinical Findings

### Strengths
- Basic clinical structure maintained; session does not wander aimlessly
- Some reflective listening demonstrated early in session ("So you're smoking more lately, especially in the evenings")
- Acceptance of client's goal of reduction over quitting after initial resistance
- Clear, organized summary at end of session
- Warm, welcoming opening tone establishing basic rapport

### Critical Deficiencies
- **Directive, prescriptive stance dominates:** Repeated use of "should" language, advice-giving, and problem-solving rather than evocation and collaboration—most pervasive problem undermining MI spirit throughout
- **Minimal genuine partnership:** Clinician assumes expert role throughout; client input does not substantially shape session direction; no genuine power-sharing
- **Failure to evoke intrinsic motivation:** Rather than drawing out patient's own reasons for change, values, and resources, clinician prescribes solutions; significantly reduces likelihood of evoking sustainable, intrinsic motivation
- **Autonomy not supported:** Clinician pressures (subtly) toward quitting, prescribes interventions, does not explicitly affirm patient's right to choose or ownership of change process
- **Limited empathy and acceptance:** While basic warmth present, clinician does not demonstrate genuine understanding of patient's emotional experience, ambivalence, or meaning behind behaviors; conditional acceptance evident
- **Change talk not strategically amplified:** Patient's powerful motivations (concern about cancer, desire to be there for son) acknowledged but not explored or deepened; clinician does not strategically evoke or reinforce change talk
- **Sustain talk not skillfully addressed:** When patient expresses doubt or barriers, clinician does not explore with curiosity or use double-sided reflections; instead moves past them

---

## Impact on Client Engagement and Likelihood of Sustained Change

**Likelihood of Sustained Behavior Change: Low to Moderate**

While patient agrees to plan and expresses willingness to try, several factors suggest change may not be sustained:

- **Extrinsic motivation:** Plan largely driven by clinician's agenda, not patient's intrinsic motivation; research demonstrates extrinsically motivated change less likely to be sustained
- **Unresolved ambivalence:** Patient's ambivalence (wanting to change but uncertain about capacity) not explored or resolved; expresses doubt multiple times without clinician helping work through it
- **Barriers not addressed:** Patient's time constraints and fatigue are real barriers not collaboratively addressed; plan may not be realistic given actual life circumstances
- **Weak commitment:** Patient's commitment tentative ("I'll do my best," "I'll try"); not strong, self-generated commitment typically predicting sustained change
- **Missed opportunity to deepen motivation:** Clinician did not explore patient's powerful motivations (her son, her health, her values as mother); these could have strengthened intrinsic motivation and increased likelihood of sustained change

**Positive factor:** Patient does express some intrinsic motivation despite clinician's directive approach ("Being a mother, I don't want something to happen to me"), suggesting internal motivation that could have been more effectively evoked with skillful MI technique.

## Primary Recommendations for Clinician Development

1. **Eliminate prescriptive language; replace with genuine open questions** (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
2. **Strategically evoke and amplify patient's change talk** (HIGH PRIORITY)
3. **Develop genuine partnership and power-sharing** (HIGH PRIORITY)
4. **Explore and validate ambivalence rather than dismissing it** (HIGH PRIORITY)
5. **Support autonomy explicitly throughout sessions** (MEDIUM-HIGH PRIORITY)
6. **Deepen empathy through complex reflections** (MEDIUM-HIGH PRIORITY)
7. **Explore barriers collaboratively rather than dismissing them** (MEDIUM PRIORITY)
8. **Avoid premature problem-solving; explore first** (MEDIUM PRIORITY)
9. **Use open questions to invite client thinking** (MEDIUM PRIORITY)
10. **Strengthen commitment through collaborative planning** (MEDIUM PRIORITY)

---

## Conclusion

This session demonstrates basic clinical competence in structure and rapport-building but falls significantly short of competent motivational interviewing. The psychiatrist adopts a directive, expert-driven stance that directly contradicts core MI principles of partnership, evocation, and autonomy support. The session represents advice-giving and problem-solving disguised as MI.

**Most critical deficiency:** Reliance on prescriptive language rather than genuine evocation and collaboration.

**Path forward:** Clinician must fundamentally shift from expert-driven, prescriptive approach to genuine MI stance characterized by partnership, evocation, and autonomy support through focused practice on core MI competencies.
