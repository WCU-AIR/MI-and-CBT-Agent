
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **Implement Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) as the Primary Treatment for OCD**
- **Action:** Immediately shift to ERP as the core treatment modality. Develop a clear case conceptualization explaining the obsessive-compulsive cycle (obsession → anxiety → compulsion → temporary relief → obsession returns). Explain to the patient that the goal is to break this cycle by gradually confronting feared situations while resisting compulsions, allowing anxiety to decrease naturally.
- **Example:** "Samuel, I want to explain how OCD works. When you have the thought 'I might get contaminated,' your anxiety goes up. Then you wash your hands, and the anxiety goes down temporarily. But the problem is, washing your hands teaches your brain that contamination is dangerous, so the thought comes back stronger. The way to break this cycle is to gradually face situations where you might get contaminated—without washing your hands—and let your anxiety come down on its own. This teaches your brain that contamination is not as dangerous as you think. Does that make sense?"
- **Practice:** Attend advanced CBT training specifically in ERP for OCD. Practice developing exposure hierarchies with other trainees. Observe an experienced OCD therapist conducting ERP sessions. Conduct ERP under supervision with at least 5 OCD cases before practicing independently.

### 2. **Develop a Structured Case Conceptualization and Treatment Plan**
- **Action:** At the beginning of treatment, explicitly develop a case conceptualization with the patient. Identify: (a) the patient's obsessions (contamination, responsibility, aggression, symmetry), (b) the maintaining compulsions (hand washing, checking, mental rituals), (c) avoidance behaviors (not going outside), (d) cognitive distortions (inflated responsibility, moral perfectionism), and (e) how depression interacts with OCD. Use this conceptualization to guide all interventions.
- **Example:** "Samuel, let me summarize what I understand about your situation. You have fears about contamination and responsibility that trigger anxiety. To manage this anxiety, you wash your hands, check things, and avoid situations. These behaviors provide temporary relief, but they keep the fears alive. You also feel depressed, which makes it harder to face these fears. Our treatment will focus on gradually facing these fears while resisting the urge to wash, check, or avoid. We'll also work on getting you more active and engaged in life, which will help your mood. Does this match your understanding?"
- **Practice:** Review case conceptualization frameworks in CBT manuals (e.g., Persons' case formulation approach). Practice developing conceptualizations with supervision. Write out conceptualizations for each patient and review with a supervisor before implementing treatment.

### 3. **Establish a Structured, Collaborative Agenda at the Beginning of Each Session**
- **Action:** At the start of each session, collaboratively set an agenda. Ask the patient: "What would be most helpful to focus on today?" "Of the problems we've identified, which is most bothersome right now?" "What do you want to accomplish in this session?" Prioritize 1-2 target problems and allocate time explicitly. Write the agenda on a whiteboard or paper so both therapist and patient can see it.
- **Example:** "Samuel, we have about 45 minutes today. I want to make sure we use the time well. Last session we talked about your contamination fears, your worries about responsibility, and your low mood. Which of these would be most helpful to focus on today? And what do you hope we'll accomplish?"
- **Practice:** In supervision, role-play setting agendas with different patients. Practice asking open-ended questions about priorities. Practice writing agendas collaboratively and adjusting based on patient input.

### 4. **Systematically Elicit and Respond to Feedback Throughout the Session**
- **Action:** Regularly check for understanding and satisfaction. After explaining a concept or suggesting an intervention, ask: "Does that make sense?" "What are your thoughts about this?" "Do you think this might help?" "What concerns do you have?" Listen to the patient's response and adjust accordingly. At the end of the session, ask: "What was most helpful today?" "What didn't make sense?" "What would you like to do differently next time?"
- **Example:** After explaining ERP, ask: "I know this might sound scary—facing the things you're afraid of. What are your thoughts about this approach? What concerns do you have? Do you think this could work for you?"
- **Practice:** In supervision, practice asking open-ended feedback questions. Record sessions and review your feedback elicitation. Ask supervisors to provide feedback on how well you check for understanding.

### 5. **Review Homework at the Beginning of Each Session and Collaboratively Adjust the Plan**
- **Action:** At the start of each session, review the previous homework. Ask: "Did you do the homework? How many times? What happened? Did it help? What got in the way? What should we adjust?" Use this information to refine the treatment plan. If homework was not completed, explore barriers collaboratively rather than blaming the patient.
- **Example:** "Samuel, last session we talked about practicing breathing exercises and noticing your thoughts. How did that go? Were you able to practice? What happened when you did? What made it hard to remember?"

- **Action:** Homework must be specific, written, and connected to treatment goals. Provide a clear rationale for why the homework will help. Have the patient demonstrate understanding and commit to the plan. Identify potential barriers and problem-solve solutions. For OCD, homework should involve exposure and response prevention (e.g., "Deliberately touch a doorknob without washing your hands for 30 minutes").
- **Example:** "Samuel, for homework this week, I want you to practice an exposure. Here's a written sheet with the instructions. Can you walk me through what you'll do? What might get in the way? How will you remember?"
- **Practice:** Write out homework assignments in detail before sessions. Have supervisors review your homework assignments for specificity, rationale, and connection to treatment goals. Practice explaining homework rationale clearly and checking for understanding.

### 7. **Use Guided Discovery and Socratic Questioning Rather Than Direct Advice**
- **Action:** Instead of telling the patient what to think or do, ask questions that help the patient discover insights independently. For example, instead of saying "Your fears are not realistic," ask: "What evidence do you have that contamination will make you sick? What evidence do you have against it? ### 9. **Provide Psychoeducation About OCD and the Obsessive-Compulsive Cycle**
- **Action:** Early in treatment, provide clear psychoeducation about OCD. Explain: (a) what obsessions are (intrusive, unwanted thoughts), (b) what compulsions are (repetitive behaviors or mental acts), (c) how the cycle works (obsession → anxiety → compulsion → temporary relief → obsession returns), (d) why compulsions maintain OCD (they prevent disconfirmation of feared outcomes), and (e) how ERP works (breaking the cycle by facing fears without compulsions).
- **Example:** "Samuel, OCD is a condition where your brain gets stuck on certain thoughts—like thoughts about contamination or responsibility. These thoughts trigger anxiety. To manage the anxiety, you do things like wash your hands or check things. These behaviors provide temporary relief, but they actually keep the thoughts alive. The way to break this cycle is to gradually face the situations that trigger the thoughts, without doing the behaviors. This teaches your brain that the feared outcome doesn't actually happen, and the thoughts lose their power."
- **Practice:** Develop a psychoeducation handout about OCD. Practice delivering psychoeducation clearly and checking for understanding. Use diagrams or visual aids to explain the obsessive-compulsive cycle.

### 10. **Develop an Exposure Hierarchy and Implement Graduated ERP**
- **Action:** Collaboratively develop an exposure hierarchy—a list of feared situations ranked by anxiety level (0-100). Start with lower-anxiety exposures and gradually move to higher-anxiety exposures. For each exposure, the patient confronts the feared situation while resisting compulsions. Repeat exposures until anxiety decreases by at least 50%.

We'll start with the lower-anxiety situations and work our way up. For each situation, you'll deliberately expose yourself to it and resist the urge to wash your hands. We'll do this together in session and also as homework."

### 11. **Implement Behavioral Activation for Depression**
