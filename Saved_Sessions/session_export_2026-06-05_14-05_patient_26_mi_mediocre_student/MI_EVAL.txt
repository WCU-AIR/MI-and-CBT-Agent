### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal unconditional positive regard. While opening is warm, clinician quickly shifts to directive, prescriptive stance that undermines genuine acceptance. Repeated use of "you should" and "you might want to" communicates that clinician's judgment supersedes client's wisdom. Fails to affirm client's autonomy or normalize struggle with social drinking. Token autonomy statement appears only at end after multiple directives, feeling like acknowledgment rather than genuine stance.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal empathic attunement despite multiple opportunities. When client expresses social pressure concerns ("feel like I'm being a downer"), psychiatrist does not reflect emotional concern or explore meaning. Client states tracking "feels like a bit of a buzzkill"—psychiatrist acknowledges but does not explore ambivalence. When client rates confidence at 6/10, psychiatrist dismisses rather than explores actual experience. Reflections are factual rather than emotionally attuned. No complex reflections capturing both sides of ambivalence or deepening self-understanding.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains consistent focus on alcohol use throughout session, which is appropriate. Final summary clearly restates agreed-upon goals. Logical progression from identifying issue to exploring barriers to developing plan. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. Psychiatrist never asks what client wants to focus on; instead, clinician's suggestions (diet, exercise, stress) frame clinician's agenda as default. Direction maintained through clinician control rather than collaborative exploration.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no genuine partnership demonstrated. Psychiatrist makes unilateral recommendations without seeking client input: "Maybe you should try avoiding those situations," "You should try keeping track," "You might want to write those down." When client expresses concern about avoiding situations ("sounds a little extreme"), psychiatrist doesn't genuinely engage with perspective but redirects with alternative suggestion. No genuine problem-solving dialogue where client's ideas are solicited, explored, and built upon. Power dynamic entirely asymmetrical—psychiatrist decides what should be tried; client complies.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist systematically undermines autonomy through directive language and implicit pressure. Repeated "should" and "probably should" creates subtle but powerful obligation messaging: "You should try avoiding," "You should try keeping track," "It would probably help if." When client expresses hesitation about tracking ("feels like a bit of a buzzkill"), psychiatrist reinforces recommendation rather than honoring concern. Challenges client's confidence rating ("A 6 is not bad, but you probably want to be more confident"), implying client's self-assessment is insufficient. Final plan presented as decided, not as client's choice.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist relies heavily on telling rather than asking, minimizing genuine evocation. Rarely asks open-ended questions inviting client to explore own reasons for change. When client rates confidence at 6/10, psychiatrist doesn't explore what's behind number or what would increase it; simply states client "probably wants to be more confident" and offers suggestions. Never asks about client's values or how they relate to drinking. One partial evocative moment: "What stops you from sticking to a plan?" but immediately followed by psychiatrist's suggestion, preempting client's full exploration of barriers and solutions.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist operates entirely from expert role, positioning themselves as authority on what client should do rather than conveying that wisdom resides within client. Makes unilateral recommendations without genuine power-sharing. When client expresses hesitation, psychiatrist doesn't incorporate input; instead offers alternative suggestion still from clinician's expertise. Never asks client for his ideas or invites collaborative problem-solving. Language is consistently clinician-centered: "I thought," "You should," "You might want to"—not "We could explore," "What do you think," "Let's figure out together."

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill in evoking and reinforcing client's own language about desire, ability, reasons, and commitment. When client expresses change talk—"I'm proud of that," "I think setting a limit could work," "I'm willing to give it a shot"—psychiatrist does not amplify or deepen; instead moves to next directive. Rarely asks questions designed to evoke change talk ("What would be good about drinking less?" "What makes you think you could do this?"). When client suggests "deciding beforehand how many drinks," this is his idea and change talk—psychiatrist should amplify but instead moves to tracking. Challenges rather than explores confidence rating.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist avoids confrontation around sustain talk but doesn't skillfully soften through reflections or reframing. When client states "I'm not saying I want to quit drinking completely," psychiatrist acknowledges without arguing but doesn't use double-sided reflection. When client says avoiding situations "sounds extreme," psychiatrist offers alternative without exploring concern. When client expresses tracking feels like "buzzkill," psychiatrist acknowledges but doesn't strategically reframe. Adequate performance in avoiding aggressive pushback but lacks sophistication in using reflections to deepen understanding of ambivalence.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Current MI Competency Level: Below Average (1.9/5)**

### Key Strengths:
- Maintains consistent topical focus on alcohol use
- Avoids aggressive confrontation or judgment
- Provides clear, organized final summary
- Affirms client's smoking cessation success
- Conveys basic warmth and supportive tone

### Critical Areas for Improvement:
- **Directive prescriptiveness dominates:** Repeated "should" and "might want to" language undermines autonomy and creates subtle pressure
- **Minimal genuine collaboration:** Unilateral recommendations without soliciting or building on client's ideas
- **Absent evocation:** Relies on telling rather than asking; misses opportunities to draw out client's own motivations and resources
- **Superficial empathy:** Doesn't capture emotional nuance or explore ambivalence in depth
- **Change talk not cultivated:** Client expressions of willingness and ideas are not amplified or deepened
- **Partnership absent:** Operates entirely from expert role rather than positioning client as expert on their own life
- **Missed opportunity:** Doesn't explore how client successfully quit smoking or apply those insights to current challenge

### Impact on Sustained Change:
While client appears compliant and cooperative, this reflects his personality rather than clinician skillfulness. Directive approach creates significant risks: (1) Reduced intrinsic motivation—external pressure less sustainable than internal motivation; (2) Compliance vs. commitment—agreement sounds like following clinician recommendations rather than genuine commitment; (3) Unaddressed ambivalence—barriers remain unresolved; (4) Reduced self-efficacy—missed opportunity to build on past success. Client's 6/10 confidence rating combined with lack of genuine exploration suggests initial follow-through but unlikely sustained change without ongoing external pressure.

### Recommendation:
Fundamental shift required from expert-driven to collaborative, evocative stance. Clinician demonstrates foundational skills (organization, warmth, focus) upon which MI competency can be built. With focused training and deliberate practice on replacing prescriptive language with open-ended questions, soliciting client ideas first, amplifying change talk, and exploring ambivalence, this clinician can develop genuine MI competency. Without significant change in approach, sessions unlikely to produce sustained behavior change.
