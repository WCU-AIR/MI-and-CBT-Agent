
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Feedback: Therapist Evaluation

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made minimal effort to establish a structured, collaborative agenda. In the first session, the opening statement ("So, I thought maybe we could talk a bit about what's been on your mind lately and see if there's anything bothering you that we might work on") is vague and non-specific. There is no explicit discussion of session goals, time allocation, or prioritization of problems. The therapist does not clarify which presenting complaint (headaches, fatigue, weight loss, eating difficulties, or psychological distress) will be the focus, nor does the therapist establish measurable session objectives.

In the second session, the therapist repeats the identical opening ("So, maybe we can talk a bit about what's been on your mind lately and see if there's something bothering you that we could work on together"), demonstrating no learning or progression. Critically, there is no review of the previous session's homework or progress, no assessment of what worked or didn't work from the prior week, and no collaborative discussion about whether the treatment plan should be adjusted. For a patient with anorexia nervosa—a serious, potentially life-threatening condition—the absence of a structured, prioritized agenda is a significant clinical failure. The therapist should have explicitly stated: "Today we're going to focus on [specific problem]. We have [X minutes]. Here's what I'm thinking we should cover..." and invited patient input.

**Summary:**

The therapist failed to establish explicit, collaborative agendas in either session. No prioritization of problems occurred, no time allocation was discussed, and critically, no homework review happened in session two—a fundamental CBT requirement.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrates minimal competence in eliciting and responding to patient feedback. Across both sessions, there are only two instances where the therapist explicitly asks for feedback: "What do you think about that idea?" and "Do you think that's something you could do?" **Summary:**

### 5. Collaboration
### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist attempts collaboration but falls short of true collaborative empiricism. The therapist uses collaborative language ("we," "together," "work on") and invites the patient's input ("What do you think about that idea?" "Do you think trying some small changes like that might be helpful?"). The patient is not passive; she engages thoughtfully and agrees to try interventions.

However, true collaboration in CBT requires shared decision-making about the treatment conceptualization and strategy. The therapist does not explicitly discuss the CBT model with the patient or explain how thoughts, feelings, and behaviors interact in her eating disorder. The therapist does not collaboratively develop a case formulation (e.g., "It sounds like when you feel bloated and nauseous, you have the thought 'eating makes me worse,' which leads you to restrict, which maintains your low weight and symptoms. ## Recommendations

### 1. **Implement Structured Agenda-Setting and Session Planning**
- **Action:** Begin every session with an explicit, collaborative agenda. Write it down or state it clearly: "Today I want to focus on [specific problem]. We have [X minutes]. Here's what I'm thinking we should cover: [1, 2, 3]. What do you think? Is there anything else you want to add?"
- **Example:** "Valerie, today I want to focus on your eating and the thoughts that come up when you try to eat more. I also want to check in on how last week's homework went. We have 50 minutes. Does that sound okay to you? Is there anything else you want to talk about?"
- **Practice:** Role-play agenda-setting with a supervisor. Practice with 3-5 patients, getting feedback on clarity and collaboration. Record yourself and review.

### 2. **Conduct Thorough Homework Review at the Start of Every Session**
- **Action:** Allocate 10-15 minutes at the start of each session to review homework. Ask: "What did you try last week?" "What happened?" "What worked?" "What was difficult?" "How did you feel about it?" "What do you want to adjust?"
- **Example:** "Valerie, let's start by talking about last week. You were going to try smaller meals more often and notice your thoughts about eating. How did that go? Did you try it? What happened?"
- **Practice:** Create a homework review checklist. Use it with every patient. Get supervisor feedback on your review process.

### 3. **Conduct Comprehensive Assessment of Maintaining Factors**
- **Action:** In early sessions, systematically assess: (1) Specific eating patterns and caloric intake, (2) Exercise/activity level and type, (3) Thoughts and beliefs about eating and body, (4) Emotional triggers for restriction, (5) Medical complications and symptoms, (6) Cultural/religious/financial factors affecting eating, (7) Family dynamics and support, (8) Motivation for change and ambivalence.
- **Example:** "Valerie, I want to understand your eating better. Can you walk me through a typical day? What do you eat for breakfast? How much? How do you feel after? Then what? Also, I noticed you mentioned walking 3-4 hours a day. Tell me about that. When do you walk? Why? How does it affect your energy?"
- **Practice:** Develop a comprehensive assessment form for eating disorders. Use it with every new patient. Discuss findings with supervisor.

### 4. **Develop an Explicit, Individualized Case Formulation**
- **Action:** Create a written case formulation that explains: (1) The patient's core beliefs and fears, (2) The maintaining cycle (how thoughts, feelings, and behaviors interact), (3) The role of cultural/contextual factors, (4) The treatment targets and rationale. Share this with the patient.
- **Example:** "Valerie, here's how I understand what's happening: You came to the U.S. and lost weight due to stress, financial limitations, and dietary changes. Now your body is weak and you feel tired and have headaches. You think 'eating will make me feel worse' because you associate eating with bloating and nausea. So you eat less. But eating less makes your malnutrition worse, which makes your fatigue and headaches worse. This creates a cycle. Does that match your experience? What would you add?"
- **Practice:** Write case formulations for 5 patients. Have supervisor review them. Discuss with patients and refine based on feedback.

### 5. **Apply Systematic Cognitive Restructuring Using Thought Records**
- **Action:** Move beyond suggesting "positive thoughts." Use structured thought records: (1) Identify the situation, (2) Identify the automatic thought, (3) Identify the emotion and intensity, (4) Examine evidence for and against the thought, (5) Develop a realistic alternative thought, (6) Re-rate emotion. Do this in session and assign as homework.
- **Example:** 
  - Situation: "I'm about to eat lunch"
  - Automatic Thought: "If I eat more, I'll feel worse with bloating and nausea"
  - Emotion: Anxiety (8/10)
  - Evidence for: "I do feel bloated and nauseous after eating sometimes"
  - Evidence against: "But I also feel tired and weak when I don't eat. My doctor said malnutrition causes these symptoms. Maybe eating more nutritious food will actually help."
  - Alternative Thought: "Eating may feel uncomfortable at first, but it might actually reduce my symptoms over time"
  - Re-rate: Anxiety (5/10)
- **Practice:** Create thought record templates. Use them in session with every patient. Assign as homework. Review in next session.

### 6. **Structure Behavioral Experiments with Clear Hypotheses and Data Collection**
- **Action:** Frame behavioral changes as experiments to test beliefs. Establish: (1) The hypothesis being tested, (2) The prediction, (3) The specific behavior to try, (4) How to measure outcomes, (5) When to review results.
- **Example:** "Valerie, let's test your belief that 'eating more makes me feel worse.' Here's my hypothesis: If you eat slightly more food—say, an extra 200 calories per day in smaller meals—your bloating and nausea might actually decrease over time because your body will be better nourished. What do you predict will happen? Let's try this for one week and track your bloating and nausea on a scale of 1-10. We'll write down what you eat and how you feel after. Then next week we'll look at the data together and see what we learned. Does that sound okay?"
- **Practice:** Design 3-5 behavioral experiments for eating disorder patients. Use data collection sheets. Review results in next session. Discuss learning with supervisor.

### 7. **Systematically Address Exercise and Activity**
- **Action:** Assess the patient's exercise level, type, and function (weight loss vs. other reasons). Develop a plan to reduce excessive exercise and replace it with valued activities. Use activity scheduling.
- **Example:** "Valerie, you mentioned you walk 3-4 hours a day. That's a lot of activity. For someone who's malnourished and tired, that much walking can actually make it harder for your body to recover. I'd like to gradually reduce your walking to 30 minutes a day and replace some of that time with activities you enjoy or find meaningful. What activities do you value? What would you like to do instead of walking?"
- **Practice:** Assess exercise in every eating disorder patient. Develop activity reduction plans. Use activity scheduling worksheets. Review adherence in next session.

### 8. **Coordinate Medical Care and Address Urgency**
- **Action:** Communicate with the patient's medical team. Discuss weight restoration targets, medical monitoring, and the relationship between malnutrition and psychiatric symptoms. Explain to the patient why medical stabilization is essential for CBT to work.
- **Example:** "Valerie, your BMI is 14.7, which means your body is significantly malnourished. This is affecting your concentration, energy, and mood. Before we can do the most effective therapy, we need to get your weight up to at least [target]. This means eating more calories. Your doctor will monitor your heart, blood pressure, and other things to make sure you're safe. I'll be in touch with your doctor to coordinate. Does this make sense?"
- **Practice:** Contact the medical team for every eating disorder patient. Discuss weight restoration targets. Explain the rationale to the patient. Document coordination in the chart.

### 9. **Incorporate Cultural and Contextual Factors into Treatment**
- **Action:** Explicitly explore and integrate cultural, religious, financial, and immigration-related factors into the treatment plan. Adapt interventions to fit the patient's context.
- **Example:** "Valerie, I know you have religious dietary restrictions and financial limitations. Let's work together to find foods that fit your beliefs and budget that will help you eat more. What foods do you enjoy that are affordable and acceptable? How can we make eating more feasible for you?"
- **Practice:** Conduct cultural assessment with every patient. Adapt interventions. Discuss with supervisor. Seek consultation if unfamiliar with patient's culture.

### 10. **Elicit and Respond to Patient Feedback Throughout Sessions**
- **Action:** Regularly ask: "How are you feeling about what we're discussing?" "Does this make sense?" "Is there anything I said that didn't sit right with you?" "How confident do you feel about trying this?" "What concerns do you have?" Adjust approach based on feedback.
- **Example:** "Valerie, I've been suggesting that we focus on eating more and reducing your walking. How do you feel about that? Does it feel doable? What worries you about it? What would make it easier?"
- **Practice:** Add 3-5 feedback questions to every session. Record yourself and review. Get supervisor feedback on your responsiveness.

### 11. **Assign Specific, Detailed, Rationale-Based Homework**
- **Action:** Make homework concrete and specific. Explain the rationale. Check commitment. Anticipate barriers. Provide written instructions.
- **Example:** "Valerie, here's your homework for this week: (1) Eat three meals plus two snacks every day. Each meal should be about 400-500 calories, each snack about 150-200 calories. I'm giving you a list of affordable, culturally-appropriate foods. (2) After each meal, do a 10-minute gentle activity like a short walk or stretching. (3) Use this thought record to track one negative thought about eating each day. We'll review all of this next week. Do you feel confident you can do this? What might get in the way? How will you remember?"
- **Practice:** Write detailed homework assignments for every patient. Provide written instructions. Check commitment. Review in next session. Troubleshoot barriers.

### 12. **Receive Supervision and Training in Evidence-Based CBT for Eating Disorders**
- **Action:** Seek regular supervision from an expert in CBT for eating disorders. Attend training workshops. Review CBT manuals (e.g., Fairburn's Enhanced CBT for eating disorders). Record sessions and review with supervisor.
- **Example:** Attend a 2-day workshop on CBT for eating disorders. Join a consultation group. Record 3 sessions and review with supervisor monthly.
- **Practice:** Commit to ongoing training and supervision. Track competency improvements over time.
