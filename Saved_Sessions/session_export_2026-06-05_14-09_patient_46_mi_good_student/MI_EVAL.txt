### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains general focus on diabetes management and lifestyle change. Early open-ended questions appropriately allow patient to define priorities ("What would you like to focus on or talk about during our session?"). However, direction becomes increasingly clinician-driven in middle phase. After establishing patient's ambivalence and 5/10 importance rating, psychiatrist offers specific suggestions (swapping sugary drinks, adding walks, increasing vegetables) without first exploring what patient has already considered. More client-centered approach would ask "What have you already thought about trying?" before offering suggestions. Final outcome (identifying first step) is positive but emerged more from psychiatrist's suggestions than patient's own exploration.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates some collaborative elements but frequently assumes expert role that undermines true partnership. Opening is collaborative and invites patient to shape agenda. However, collaboration diminishes when psychiatrist transitions to offering specific behavioral suggestions. Framing as collaborative ("Would it be alright if I shared some ideas...?") is relatively narrow—psychiatrist has already decided what suggestions to offer rather than co-constructing solutions. More collaborative approach would ask "What ideas do you already have?" before offering suggestions. When patient identifies cutting back on sugary drinks as first step, psychiatrist does not explore whether this was patient's genuine preference or selection based on first suggestion offered.

### Autonomy Support

Early questions appropriately respect patient's autonomy in defining agenda. Lacks statements like "This is entirely up to you" or "You're the expert on what will work for you." When patient expresses hesitation ("I'm not sure how easy it'll be to keep up"), psychiatrist does not explore whether patient feels pressured or genuinely wants change. ### Evocation

Patient generates valuable change talk ("I don't want things to get worse. Psychiatrist does not deeply explore patient's values, strengths, or past successes with change. When patient identifies cutting back on sugary drinks, psychiatrist asks about supporting strategies (good evocation), but does not ask patient to articulate own reasons for change or explore hoped-for outcomes.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates basic cultivation of change talk but misses critical opportunities to amplify and reinforce patient's own language favoring change. Patient spontaneously generates valuable change talk ("I don't want things to get worse. I've seen folks get sick and struggle with stuff like this. Maybe if I don't do anything, I'll end up in a worse spot"), but psychiatrist acknowledges without amplifying through deeper exploration. Psychiatrist asks evocative question "What do you think might help move that number a bit higher?" and patient responds with own ideas (positive). Questions like "What would be different if you made these changes?" or "What matters most to you about your health?" would elicit more robust change talk. ### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates skillful softening of sustain talk without confrontation or argument. Patient expresses multiple instances of sustain talk ("I feel fine, so I'm not sure why it's such a big deal"; "If I have to take pills for the rest of my life, I'd rather just keep things the way they are"). Psychiatrist uses double-sided reflections that acknowledge sustain talk while gently introducing change perspective: "It sounds like feeling well right now makes the idea of changing your routine feel less urgent...At the same time, you recognize that your doctor's advice points to some potential risks ahead." When patient expresses concern about difficulty of change, psychiatrist validates without reinforcing and reframes ambivalence as strength. Psychiatrist avoids arguing and uses empathy and reframing to gently shift perspective. One missed opportunity: when patient expresses doubt ("I'm not sure how easy it'll be to keep up"), psychiatrist frames positively but does not explore underlying concern about ability to maintain change.

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall Session Score: 3.4/5**

### Key Strengths:
- Strong opening with genuine collaborative framing inviting patient to define agenda
- Consistent, specific affirmations grounded in observable patient behavior
- Accurate reflective listening with emotionally attuned reflections capturing ambivalence
- Skillful softening of sustain talk through double-sided reflections and validation without confrontation
- Practical positive outcome: patient identifies specific first step with 7/10 confidence rating
- Collaborative exploration of supporting strategies with patient generating own ideas

### Primary Areas for Improvement:
- **Over-reliance on suggestion-giving:** Psychiatrist shifts to expert role by offering specific behavioral suggestions without first exploring patient's own ideas, undermining collaboration and evocation
- **Insufficient exploration of intrinsic motivation:** Limited exploration of patient's values, past successes, or what matters most about health; missed opportunities to amplify spontaneous change talk
- **Limited explicit autonomy support:** Lacks explicit affirmations of patient's right to choose and ownership of change process; no statements like "This is entirely up to you"
- **Insufficient processing of doubt:** When patient expresses hesitation, psychiatrist reframes positively without exploring underlying concerns about ability to maintain change
- **Expert positioning:** Offers specific suggestions without first asking what patient has already considered, positioning psychiatrist as expert rather than patient as expert on own life

### Clinical Impact:
Session demonstrates moderate success in evoking movement toward change (5/10 to 7/10 confidence). However, reliance on psychiatrist's suggestions rather than patient's own ideas raises questions about sustainability and depth of commitment. Patient's change talk is somewhat reactive rather than spontaneous and self-generated. Phrase "I'm not sure how easy it'll be to keep up, but it's better than nothing, I guess" suggests possible compliance rather than genuine enthusiasm. Risk that patient may follow suggestions from authority figures (doctor, psychiatrist) rather than developing internal motivation and self-efficacy.

### Recommendations for Improvement:
1. **Prioritize evocation over suggestion-giving:** Ask patient to generate own ideas before offering suggestions
2. **Deepen exploration of intrinsic motivation:** Ask about values, past successes, and what matters most about health
3. **Strengthen explicit autonomy support:** Use language affirming patient's freedom and agency ("This is entirely up to you")
4. **Explore and process doubt more thoroughly:** When patient expresses hesitation, explore concerns rather than reframing positively
5. **Position patient as expert on own life:** Explicitly acknowledge patient's expertise about own schedule, preferences, and what works
6. **Amplify change talk strategically:** Use follow-up questions to deepen exploration of spontaneous change talk
7. **Explore past successes:** Ask about times patient successfully made changes to build confidence and self-efficacy
8. **Clarify genuine preference:** After patient identifies first step, verify it was genuine choice not compliance with suggestions
9. **Develop collaborative goal-setting:** Co-construct goals rather than psychiatrist suggesting and patient evaluating
10. **Build confidence throughout session:** Weave confidence-building affirmations throughout rather than only at end
