### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal reflective listening and no genuine effort to understand patient's perspective. When patient expresses concern about overwhelm ("feels like a lot sometimes"), clinician does not reflect or explore. Patient's fear about pushing too hard ("feels kind of scary") is invalidated rather than understood. No open-ended questions exploring patient's actual barriers or motivations. Single normalizing statement in final exchange ("Feeling tired sometimes is pretty common") cannot offset pervasive lack of empathic attunement throughout session.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2

Psychiatrist repeats nearly identical demand for "30 minutes daily exercise" across five exchanges without exploring patient's own health goals or what would feel achievable. Lacks flexibility; when patient indicates daily exercise feels unmanageable, clinician doubles down on same demand rather than exploring alternatives. Misses opportunity to build on patient-identified targets (sleep and nutrition adjustments).

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Authoritarian, expert-driven stance throughout. Psychiatrist positions self as sole authority using controlling language ("you have to," "you must," "you need to"). When patient offers own expertise about what might work ("start smaller," "most days instead of every day"), clinician explicitly rejects it: "Starting smaller just means you're not really committing." No joint problem-solving. Patient's input does not influence session direction. Power dynamic is entirely unequal; patient positioned as non-compliant recipient of clinician's wisdom rather than active partner.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Active undermining of autonomy throughout. Controlling language ("no ifs, ands, or buts," "no exceptions") leaves no room for patient choice. Fear-based persuasion used to coerce compliance ("you could develop serious problems down the line"). When patient proposes alternative ("try most days"), psychiatrist ignores stated preference and repeats demand for daily commitment. Strips patient of agency by telling her what she should feel ("you don't have to be scared"). No genuine invitations for patient to determine own path forward.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no evocation present. Psychiatrist provides reasons for change rather than drawing out patient's own motivations. No open-ended questions designed to elicit patient's perspective on health, values, or ideas about change. When patient spontaneously offers change talk ("I'm serious about wanting to be healthier"), clinician does not build on it. Patient's self-identified insights about sleep and nutrition barriers are not meaningfully explored or amplified. No curiosity about patient's strengths, resources, or past successes.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Demonstrates opposite of partnership. Psychiatrist assumes expert role for entire interaction; patient contributions are systematically dismissed. Unilateral declarations about what will and will not work ("Starting smaller...won't help you in the long run"). When patient offers expertise about herself, clinician communicates that her expertise supersedes patient's self-knowledge. No co-construction of understanding or shared decision-making. Session structure reflects hierarchical, clinician-driven model antithetical to MI principles.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Patient offers some spontaneous change talk ("I'm serious about wanting to be healthier," "I know things like sleeping more and eating better might help," "I guess I could try to go to bed a bit earlier"). However, psychiatrist does not strategically evoke or amplify this. When patient expresses willingness to try ("most days"), clinician does not accept as change talk or build on it. Prescriptive approach actually suppresses authentic change talk by demanding specific behavioral commitment rather than exploring patient's own reasons for change.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Patient expresses legitimate sustain talk (concerns about overwhelm, fear of failure, difficulty changing habits). Psychiatrist uses confrontation ("stop making excuses," "you're not really committing," "you shouldn't be so casual") which typically strengthens rather than softens resistance. Avoids most harmful responses (no mockery or sarcasm) and does acknowledge some statements before redirecting. However, sustain talk is not skillfully softened through double-sided reflection or reframing. Approach is somewhat confrontational without being severely damaging to alliance.

## Summary Assessment

**Clinical Impression:**

This session represents a fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles and spirit. The psychiatrist demonstrates pervasive judgment, control, and dismissal of patient autonomy across all nine MI domains. The patient enters the session cooperative and open; the clinician's authoritarian, prescriptive approach is likely to trigger defensiveness and undermine intrinsic motivation for change.

**Critical Deficiencies:**

- **Absence of genuine collaboration:** Psychiatrist assumes expert role, imposes specific behavioral prescription, and repeatedly demands compliance despite patient's clear indication this feels unmanageable.
- **Fundamental misunderstanding of behavior change:** Patient demonstrates sophisticated self-awareness about barriers and realistic alternatives; clinician frames these as inadequate rather than building on them.
- **Violation of core MI principle:** Motivation for change resides within the client; this clinician imposes agenda rather than evoking patient's own motivation.
- **Likely negative outcome:** Patient may comply superficially to end session but is unlikely to sustain behavior change that feels imposed rather than chosen.

**Immediate Recommendation:**

This clinician requires comprehensive retraining in Motivational Interviewing principles with focus on: (1) collaborative stance, (2) empathic reflection, (3) evoking rather than imposing motivation, (4) recognizing and amplifying change talk, and (5) eliminating confrontational language. Supervision or consultation strongly recommended before continuing MI-based clinical work.
