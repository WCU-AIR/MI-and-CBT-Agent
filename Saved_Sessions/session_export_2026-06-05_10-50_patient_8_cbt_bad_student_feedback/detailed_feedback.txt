
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish a Clear Eating Disorder Conceptualization and Treatment Plan**
- **Action**: Before the next session, develop a written case formulation that identifies: (a) the patient's eating disorder diagnosis and severity, (b) maintaining factors (restriction, exercise, ambivalence about recovery, fear of physical sensations), (c) the connection between these factors and her symptoms (fatigue, headaches, poor concentration), and (d) a specific sequence of interventions to address each factor.
- **Example**: "Valerie has anorexia nervosa, restricting type, with severe malnutrition (BMI 14.7). Her eating is maintained by: (1) ambivalence about recovery—she wants to gain weight but fears the physical sensations of eating; (2) excessive exercise—3-4 hours daily creates energy deficit; (3) lack of understanding of the connection between restriction and her symptoms. Treatment will focus on: (1) psychoeducation about eating disorders, (2) cognitive work on ambivalence, (3) behavioral activation around eating with specific caloric targets, (4) activity scheduling to reduce exercise, (5) monitoring and feedback."
- **Practice**: Review eating disorder treatment manuals (e.g., CBT for Eating Disorders by Fairburn) to develop competence in case formulation and treatment planning.

### 2. **Learn and Apply Socratic Questioning (Guided Discovery)**
- **Action**: Replace prescriptive advice with skillful questions that help the patient discover insights independently. When the patient expresses a concern or barrier, ask open-ended questions to explore it.
- **Example**: Instead of "Try to eat a little more each day," ask: "You mentioned you want to gain weight, but eating more feels difficult. What specifically makes it difficult? What happens when you try to eat more? What are you afraid will happen?"
- **Practice**: Record yourself in role-plays and count how many times you give advice vs. ask questions. Aim for a 1:3 ratio (one advice statement for every three questions). Review CBT training materials on Socratic questioning.

### 3. **Systematically Address Ambivalence About Recovery**
- **Action**: In the next session, explicitly explore the patient's fear about eating more. Use motivational interviewing techniques combined with cognitive work to help her examine the evidence for and against her fears.
- **Example**: "You said you're worried that if you eat more, you might feel uncomfortable or different. I want to understand that better. What specifically are you afraid will happen? Where does that fear come from? What evidence do you have that it will happen? What evidence do you have that it might not happen? What would need to happen for you to feel safer eating more?"
- **Practice**: Study motivational interviewing for eating disorders and practice exploring ambivalence in supervision or role-plays.

### 4. **Establish a Specific, Measurable Eating Plan**
- **Action**: Work collaboratively with the patient to develop a specific meal plan with clear caloric targets, meal times, and food choices. Make it concrete and measurable.
- **Example**: "Let's create a plan together. Right now you're eating about 600 calories per day. We need to gradually increase that. This week, let's aim for 800 calories per day, divided into three meals and two snacks. Here's what that might look like: Breakfast—toast with peanut butter and banana (300 cal), Snack—apple with cheese (150 cal), Lunch—rice and beans (350 cal), Snack—yogurt (100 cal), Dinner—chicken and vegetables (400 cal). Total: 1,300 calories. Let's start with this and see how it goes. You'll track what you eat and how you feel, and we'll review it next session."
- **Practice**: Work with a dietitian or eating disorder specialist to develop competence in meal planning. Practice presenting plans collaboratively.

### 5. **Use Behavioral Experiments to Test Eating Disorder Predictions**
- **Action**: Help the patient test her predictions about eating through structured behavioral experiments. This is a core CBT technique for eating disorders.
- **Example**: "You're worried that if you eat more, you'll feel bloated and nauseous. Let's test that prediction. This week, I'd like you to eat a small meal—say, a piece of toast with peanut butter—and notice what actually happens. Before you eat, write down: What do I predict will happen? How bad do I think it will be? Then eat the meal and write down: What actually happened? How bad was it? We'll compare your prediction to your actual experience. Often, what we predict is worse than what actually happens."
- **Practice**: Study behavioral experiments in CBT manuals. Practice designing experiments in supervision.

### 6. **Implement Systematic Monitoring and Feedback**
- **Action**: Establish clear metrics to track progress: weight (weekly), caloric intake (daily), exercise (daily), symptoms (fatigue, headaches, concentration—weekly). Review these metrics each session to provide feedback and adjust the plan.
- **Example**: "Let's track three things this week: (1) What you eat and how many calories—use this food tracking app or this sheet. (2) How much you walk or exercise. (3) Your symptoms—fatigue, headaches, concentration—rate them 0-10 each day. We'll look at these together next session and see if there are patterns. This will help us understand what's working and what needs to change."
- **Practice**: Become familiar with eating disorder monitoring tools and practice using them in sessions.

### 7. **Address Excessive Exercise Systematically**
- **Action**: Do not accept the patient's explanation that walking 3-4 hours daily is "due to lack of transportation, not for weight loss." Explore this collaboratively and develop a plan to reduce exercise to a healthy level.
- **Example**: "I hear that you walk for transportation. At the same time, walking 3-4 hours per day, combined with eating only 600 calories, creates a very large energy deficit. - **Practice**: Study activity scheduling and exercise management in eating disorder treatment.

### 8. **Provide Psychoeducation About Eating Disorders and Malnutrition**

- **Example**: "Your body needs fuel to function. When you eat only 600 calories but burn much more through walking and basic body functions, your body doesn't have enough energy. ### 9. **Assign Specific, Measurable Homework with Clear Rationale**
- **Action**: At the end of each session, assign specific homework with clear instructions, rationale, and expected outcomes. Review homework at the beginning of the next session.
