### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and genuine warmth throughout the session. Affirmations are specific and authentic (e.g., "I appreciate your openness to explore this a bit"). Ambivalence is normalized ("which is completely normal"). However, subtle directional nudges occasionally undermine full acceptance—phrases like "It's great that you're open to exploring this more without feeling pressured to decide immediately" imply that deciding soon would be preferable. Bodily autonomy concerns could be more explicitly validated as legitimate rather than reframed primarily as "personal care and control." Overall, a strong foundation of acceptance with minor opportunities to deepen validation of Jordan's autonomy concerns and vaccine hesitancy as understandable positions.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates solid reflective listening with accurate, multi-layered reflections that capture Jordan's core ambivalence and internal logic. Early reflection ("It sounds like you're feeling pretty confident in your health right now and questioning whether the flu shot really fits into your picture of staying well") effectively mirrors Jordan's position. Complex reflection about autonomy concerns and risk minimization shows understanding of multiple dimensions. However, empathy lacks depth in exploring emotional nuance and internal conflict. When Jordan expresses uncertainty about whether his vaccination would protect grandparents, the clinician reflects this but does not explore the emotional weight—potential guilt or relational conflict. Reflections remain somewhat surface-level, capturing content without fully exploring underlying emotion or the relational dimensions of decision-making. Opportunity to deepen empathic exploration of what family relationships mean and emotional stakes of the decision.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains appropriate focus on target behavior (flu vaccination decision-making) with logical session structure: exploring personal risk perception → introducing family protection angle → identifying information barriers → establishing concrete action plan → assessing confidence in follow-through. Minimal topic drift; conversation consistently returns to core question of vaccination decision. However, direction occasionally becomes subtly prescriptive rather than purely exploratory. Question "What do you think would need to happen for you to feel more certain that getting the flu shot is a valuable choice for you?" presumes Jordan *should* feel more certain and move toward "yes" rather than remaining genuinely open to "no." Clinician explores what would lead to vaccination but not what would lead to deciding *against* it. Direction could be strengthened by remaining more genuinely neutral about outcomes and exploring multiple possible conclusions Jordan might reach.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist establishes strong collaborative stance through agenda-setting ("What would you like to focus on or talk about during our session?") and consistent invitation of Jordan's input ("What do you think makes the flu shot feel less important for you personally?"). Clinician validates Jordan's role in decision-making and affirms his agency ("You're really taking charge in a way that respects both your values and the people you care about"). Language is inclusive and partnership-oriented. However, collaboration could be deepened by more fully co-constructing interpretations and solutions. When providing interpretations (e.g., "It's important for you to feel confident that your choice truly matters"), these are presented as clinician insight rather than co-constructed understanding. Action plan steps are suggested by clinician rather than generated by Jordan first. True collaboration would involve asking "How do you think you might get this information?" before offering suggestions like calling the nurse line. Opportunity to invite Jordan's problem-solving before offering clinician-generated ideas.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates exemplary autonomy support throughout the session. From opening question, clinician explicitly frames session as being "all about what matters most to you," establishing Jordan's right to determine focus and pace. Consistent affirmations of freedom to choose without pressure: "It's great that you're open to exploring this more without feeling pressured to decide immediately," "You're really taking charge in a way that respects both your values and the people you care about," "It's wonderful to see you honoring your own pace in this process." Clinician explicitly uses word "autonomy" and reinforces Jordan's ownership of decision. Notably, psychiatrist never suggests vaccination is "right" choice or that Jordan "should" get vaccinated; framing remains "What would be right for you?" When Jordan states he is "not ready to say yes right now," clinician affirms this position without subtle pressure. Action plan framed as Jordan's choice; confidence level (7/10) affirmed without suggestion it should be higher. No implicit message that higher confidence is needed before acting. This is a genuine strength of the session.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates some evocation of Jordan's own motivations through open questions ("What do you think makes the flu shot feel less important for you personally?" and "What kinds of information or experiences have helped you make health decisions in the past?"). Jordan's important motivational material emerges: care for grandparents, desire for trusted information, value of autonomy. However, evocation is inconsistent and often superficial. Clinician does not systematically explore Jordan's own reasons *for* change. When Jordan mentions grandparents' vulnerability, clinician reflects but does not deeply explore: "Tell me more about what it would mean to you if your grandparents got seriously ill" or "What does it feel like when you think about passing the flu to them?" Clinician does not strategically amplify or reinforce change talk when it emerges. When Jordan says "I guess if it's about them, maybe it's worth considering," this meaningful statement is not explored or strengthened. Clinician also misses opportunities to evoke Jordan's own problem-solving by asking for his ideas before offering framework of "clear information." Session demonstrates basic evocation but lacks strategic, consistent amplification of change talk that characterizes strong MI practice.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates strong partnership by consistently conveying that Jordan possesses important knowledge about his own situation. Clinician avoids expert role through agenda-setting, asking "What do you think?" rather than explaining, and validating Jordan's past decision-making strategies. Language is inclusive ("we've talked about," "together we've explored"). Clinician does not position himself as authority on vaccination but as facilitator helping Jordan clarify thinking. However, partnership could be strengthened by co-constructing interpretations rather than presenting clinician insight as established fact. When clinician provides interpretations ("It's important for you to feel confident that your choice truly matters"), these are presented as clinician insight rather than collaborative understanding. Clinician suggests specific action steps rather than inviting Jordan to generate solutions first. True partnership would involve asking "What feels like the best way for you to get the information you need?" before offering suggestions. Clinician also does not explicitly invite Jordan's expertise about family dynamics (e.g., "What do you think your grandparents would want you to know about this decision?"). Session maintains relatively equal power-sharing but could more consistently invite Jordan's problem-solving before offering clinician-generated ideas.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** This represents a significant weakness in the session. While Jordan produces meaningful change talk, clinician does not strategically evoke, amplify, or reinforce it. Change talk that emerges includes: "I guess if it's about them, maybe it's worth considering" (ability/commitment), "If it's mostly about me not getting sick and helping to stop passing it to them, then that might convince me more" (reasons/ability), and "I see why it makes some sense, and I want to help keep them safe" (reasons/desire). However, when these statements emerge, clinician does not pause to explore or strengthen them. When Jordan says "I guess if it's about them, maybe it's worth considering," clinician simply reflects without deepening, then pivots *away* from change talk toward sustain talk (doubt about efficacy). Clinician does not ask strategic questions to evoke robust change talk: "What would it mean to you to protect your grandparents?" "Tell me more about wanting to help keep them safe—what does that feel like?" "You mentioned it might be worth considering for them—what specifically appeals to you about that?" Clinician does not use scaling questions strategically to evoke change talk (e.g., "What would it take to move from a 5 to a 6?"). Clinician does not reinforce or celebrate change talk when it emerges. When Jordan moves from "I don't think I really need the flu shot" to "I see why it makes some sense, and I want to help keep them safe," this meaningful shift is not explicitly acknowledged or strengthened. Session demonstrates minimal strategic cultivation of change talk—a core MI competency.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates inconsistent handling of sustain talk. Jordan produces several statements against vaccination: "I'm young and healthy, so I don't think I really need the flu shot," "I don't usually get sick," "Getting a shot for something I probably won't even catch feels kinda unnecessary," "I don't like the idea of putting stuff in my body if I don't really need to." Clinician's primary strategy is accurate reflection followed by pivot toward change talk or alternative perspectives. This avoids confrontation, which is appropriate. However, pivoting is somewhat abrupt and does not fully explore or normalize concerns before shifting direction. Critical missed opportunity occurs when Jordan expresses doubt about vaccine efficacy: "I don't know if it really makes that big of a difference with me getting it or not." Clinician reflects this but then says "At the same time, you're wondering how much your getting the shot would actually impact their safety, which makes sense"—this actually *reinforces* sustain talk by validating doubt without exploring it or offering information to address it. Clinician does not use double-sided reflections to acknowledge both sustain talk and emerging change talk simultaneously (e.g., "On one hand, you're not sure you personally need the vaccine because you're young and healthy. On the other hand, you care about your grandparents and want to protect them"). Clinician does not strategically reframe sustain talk. When Jordan expresses concern about "putting stuff in my body if I don't really need to," this legitimate autonomy value could be explored: "What would help you feel confident that the vaccine is something you actually *do* need?" Session demonstrates adequate but not skillful handling of sustain talk; clinician avoids confrontation but does not strategically soften or reframe resistance.

## **SUMMARY OF CLINICAL EVALUATION**

### **Strengths:**
- ✓ **Exemplary autonomy support** with consistent, explicit affirmation of Jordan's right to choose and his pace
- ✓ **Strong acceptance** with genuine warmth, normalization of ambivalence, and unconditional positive regard
- ✓ **Solid collaboration and partnership** through agenda-setting and affirmation of Jordan's expertise
- ✓ **Competent empathy** with accurate, multi-layered reflections capturing core concerns
- ✓ **Appropriate direction** with clear focus and logical session structure
- ✓ **Non-confrontational stance** maintaining supportive tone throughout

### **Areas for Improvement:**
- ✗ **Weak change talk cultivation** (Score: 2/5) — Minimal strategic evocation, amplification, or reinforcement of Jordan's emerging motivations
- ✗ **Superficial evocation** — Limited deep exploration of emotional dimensions and relational significance
- ✗ **Inadequate softening of sustain talk** — Reflects doubt without exploring or offering information to address it
- ✗ **Subtle directional bias** — Occasional implicit suggestions that deciding soon is preferable
- ✗ **Clinician-generated solutions** — Action plan suggested rather than co-constructed with Jordan
- ✗ **Limited emotional exploration** — Reflections capture content but not emotional weight or internal conflict

### **Clinical Impact:**
Jordan leaves with a concrete plan to gather information and 7/10 confidence in follow-through—positive outcomes. However, without stronger change talk cultivation, significant risk exists that Jordan will gather information and still conclude vaccination is unnecessary for him personally. The session has not fully developed his powerful intrinsic motivation to protect his grandparents. With recommended improvements, this clinician could move from adequate to strong MI practice and significantly increase likelihood of evoking sustainable motivation for change.

### **Competency Level:**
**Adequate but not strong MI practice.** Psychiatrist excels in creating safe, respectful, non-pressuring environment but significantly underutilizes core MI competencies for evoking and amplifying intrinsic motivation. Session succeeds in engaging Jordan and avoiding resistance but does not maximize opportunity to strengthen his motivation for vaccination.
