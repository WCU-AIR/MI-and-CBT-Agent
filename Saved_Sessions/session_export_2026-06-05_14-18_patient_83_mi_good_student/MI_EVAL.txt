# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance with genuine affirmations of Lukas's strengths ("It's really impressive how you've managed to balance the demands of school and sports"). Normalizes ambivalence as thoughtful navigation. However, explicit affirmation of autonomy and client's right to make own choices regarding alcohol use is absent. Misses opportunities to validate Lukas's caution about using suggested strategies and his courage in disclosing the complexity of his situation.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates solid reflective listening and perspective-taking in exploration phase with accurate, complex reflections ("That image of walking a tightrope really captures the delicate balance you're trying to maintain"). However, empathy becomes more surface-level during action planning. Fails to explore potential anxiety about implementation, fear of disappointing friends, or emotional weight of social rejection. Complex reflections capturing both desire for connection AND fear of judgment are largely absent in latter portion of session.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains focus on target behavior (alcohol use and peer influence) early in session but direction becomes increasingly diffuse during action planning. Shifts from exploration of Lukas's own motivations to implementation of clinician-suggested solutions. Does not clarify whether target is abstinence, moderation, or harm reduction. Fails to maintain focus on exploring Lukas's values, identity as athlete/student, or what he genuinely wants his life to look like.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates partial collaboration through early open-ended questions inviting Lukas's perspective ("What do you imagine would be the ideal way for you to balance those parts of your life?"). However, collaboration deteriorates significantly during advice-giving phase. Clinician presents structured list of strategies rather than co-constructing solutions with Lukas. Power dynamic shifts—Lukas becomes consumer of expertise rather than co-creator. Final exchange about support is clinician-directed rather than collaborative.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Autonomy support is notably weak throughout session. When Lukas expresses hesitation about using suggested strategies ("I'm not promising I'll use all of them"), psychiatrist accepts but does not affirm as appropriate caution or reinforce that Lukas is expert on himself. Language becomes increasingly directive ("Starting small like this can build your confidence"). Critically absent: explicit affirmation of Lukas's right to choose, freedom to make mistakes, or right to continue drinking. No statement like "Whatever you decide is your choice, and I respect that."

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates adequate but inconsistent evocation. Strong evocative moments include asking about past experiences giving confidence ("What parts of yourself or your past experiences give you confidence?"), which generates genuine change talk. Confidence scaling question is well-timed. However, evocation becomes minimal during action planning. Misses critical opportunities: no exploration of why Lukas values friendships, what he's already tried, what he wants his life to look like, or what he thinks might work. Clinician tells rather than asks about strategies.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Partnership is substantially compromised, particularly in second half of session. While psychiatrist begins with collaborative stance, power dynamic shifts noticeably when transitioning to advice-giving. Request for permission ("Would it be okay if I shared a few ideas?") marks transition from partnership to expert-driven consultation. Lukas's role becomes evaluative rather than generative. True partnership would involve asking what Lukas has considered, what he thinks might work, and collaboratively developing ideas. Clinician becomes designated support provider rather than partner helping mobilize Lukas's own resources.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates adequate but inconsistent cultivation of change talk. Strong evocative moments generate genuine change talk ("I might be able to handle this, too"; "I've worked hard to keep my grades up"). Confidence scaling question effectively elicits change talk. However, psychiatrist misses opportunities to amplify and reinforce this talk—does not deepen exploration when Lukas expresses capability. Once advice-giving begins, change talk cultivation essentially stops. Critically absent: explicit development of commitment talk, the strongest predictor of behavior change. Lukas may reflect compliance rather than authentic commitment.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates adequate but inconsistent handling of sustain talk. Responds to Lukas's concerns about missing out and peer judgment with balanced reflections that acknowledge tension without reinforcing sustain talk. However, does not strategically soften sustain talk through double-sided reflections or reframing. Misses opportunity to explore roots of sustain talk—does not ask what specifically feels hard about saying no or what Lukas imagines would happen. Does not reframe sustain talk (concern about being judged) as evidence of Lukas's values (care for relationships).

---

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Session Score: 3.2/5 – Adequate but Inconsistent MI Competence**

**Strengths:**
- Excellent initial engagement with open-ended question setting collaborative agenda
- Accurate reflective listening in exploration phase capturing emotional complexity
- Effective use of confidence scaling generating important ambivalence information
- Appropriate affirmation of strengths and past successes building self-efficacy
- Validation of ambivalence as thoughtful rather than problematic
- Permission-seeking before advice-giving showing awareness of autonomy support

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- **Premature shift to advice-giving:** Transitions too quickly from exploration to action planning; exploration phase should be more extensive
- **Insufficient evocation:** Presents structured list of strategies rather than asking what Lukas has considered; represents fundamental departure from MI principle of evoking rather than installing motivation
- **Weak autonomy support:** Minimal explicit reinforcement of Lukas's right to choose or ownership of change process; subtly positions clinician's approach as correct path
- **Limited commitment talk:** While generating some change talk, strongest predictor of behavior change—commitment talk—is never explicitly developed
- **Insufficient exploration of underlying fears:** Does not deeply explore what specifically frightens Lukas about refusing drinks or what peer judgment means to him
- **Clinician-directed action planning:** Suggests what support Lukas needs rather than asking what he envisions; undermines partnership and autonomy support

**Impact on Client Engagement:**
Positive: Warm, respectful tone and accurate reflections help Lukas feel heard; emerging insight about ambivalence; affirmation builds self-efficacy. Limiting: Shift to advice-giving likely reduces intrinsic motivation; risks creating compliance rather than genuine commitment; weak autonomy support may undermine long-term change and independent decision-making development.

**MI Competency Level:** Basic to intermediate. Demonstrates awareness of MI concepts but does not fully embody MI spirit of drawing out client motivation. Most critical improvements needed: extend exploration, strengthen autonomy support, evoke rather than provide solutions, and develop commitment talk explicitly.
