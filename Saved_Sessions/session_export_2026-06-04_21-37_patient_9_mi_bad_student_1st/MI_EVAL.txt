# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. Judgmental language ("making excuses," "rationalizing") and explicit rejection of the client's perspective ("No, no, you don't get to negotiate here. This isn't about what you think or feel") directly contradict MI's foundational principle of accepting the client as a person of inherent worth. No affirmation of Amina's strengths, self-awareness, or willingness to attend is provided. The repeated ultimatums create a coercive atmosphere incompatible with acceptance. Amina explicitly states: "being told what to do like this just makes me push back even more" and "right now, I'm just feeling more stuck and stressed, not motivated to change"—clear evidence that the lack of acceptance is harming the therapeutic relationship.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** The session contains almost no reflective listening and demonstrates minimal effort to understand Amina's experience. The psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with "No, no..." rather than reflecting back what Amina has said. When Amina expresses that deleting apps feels "too drastic" and that she's "scared," the response is dismissal ("No, no, you're not getting it") rather than exploration. Amina's legitimate psychological needs for stress relief and connection are never acknowledged or explored with curiosity; instead, they are reframed as "excuses." There is no attempt to understand her ambivalence—the tension between wanting better focus/sleep and needing stress relief and connection. Amina's own words reveal the impact: "it feels like you're asking me to walk away from the only way I really unwind."

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** The session maintains consistent focus on social media use as the target behavior, preventing a score of 1. However, the direction is rigid and prescriptive rather than collaborative. The singular goal—"delete the apps for a week"—is repeated without variation or exploration of alternatives. There is no progressive clarification of direction that emerges from Amina's own values and goals. No strategic exploration of discrepancy between Amina's values and current behavior occurs. While focus is maintained, the direction is authoritarian rather than collaborative, lacking the strategic nuance needed to build intrinsic motivation for change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** This session is explicitly hierarchical and authoritarian, the antithesis of collaboration. The psychiatrist uses language that eliminates partnership: "You have to do exactly what I say: delete the apps for a week. No ifs, ands, or buts" and "This isn't about what you think or feel." When Amina suggests alternatives ("Maybe there's a way to strike a better balance instead?"), her input is dismissed without consideration. The psychiatrist positions himself as the sole expert with the correct answer while positioning Amina as someone who needs to be corrected and controlled. Amina explicitly recognizes this: "it feels like you're being bossed around rather than being supported." There is no joint problem-solving, no co-construction of solutions, and no seeking of Amina's input on what might work for her.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** The session actively undermines autonomy at every turn. The psychiatrist explicitly denies Amina's autonomy: "No, no, you don't get to negotiate here" and "This isn't about what you think or feel." Coercive language is used throughout: "You have to do exactly what I say" and "If you don't, you're just sabotaging your own health and future"—framing non-compliance as self-harm rather than a legitimate choice. Guilt and fear are used as motivators ("your sleep and mental health will keep deteriorating"), which is pressure, not autonomy support. Amina's explicit response reveals the impact: "I can't promise I'll actually do it without feeling like it's my decision" and "I just need to figure it out on my terms, not because someone's ordering me to." The lack of autonomy support is preventing her from engaging with change.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** This session contains virtually no evocation—it is entirely prescriptive. The psychiatrist never asks questions designed to elicit Amina's own reasons for change, values, ideas, or resources. Instead of asking "What concerns you most about your current phone use?" or "How do you imagine your life could be different?", the clinician tells Amina what should concern her. When Amina offers her own perspective—that phone use helps her relax and stay connected—it is dismissed rather than explored. True evocation would involve open-ended questions to draw out change talk, but none are asked. Amina's own change talk is minimal ("my grades slipped," "I get tired in class") because the psychiatrist never facilitates self-discovery. When Amina suggests "maybe there's a way to strike a better balance," this opportunity for evocation is dismissed rather than explored.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** The session actively conveys that expertise and wisdom reside solely within the clinician, not the client. The psychiatrist explicitly claims expert authority: "I'm here to help you change, and I'll be telling you exactly what you need to do" and "You have to do exactly what I say." When Amina expresses doubt about whether a 9 PM cutoff would work for her, the response dismisses her self-knowledge: "No, no, you can't just say 'everyone does it' and keep going like this." Phrases that undermine partnership are used repeatedly: "You're just making excuses," "You're not getting it," "You're rationalizing." These communicate that Amina's perspective is invalid and the clinician's is correct. Amina explicitly recognizes the lack of partnership: "it feels like you're being bossed around rather than being supported." There is no power-sharing, no recognition of Amina's wisdom about herself, and no collaborative problem-solving.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** The session contains minimal change talk from Amina, and the psychiatrist does nothing to evoke or reinforce it. Amina's sparse change statements ("my grades slipped," "I get tired in class," "I do want to feel better and focus more in class") are never reflected back or reinforced. When Amina says "I do want to feel better and focus more in class," the psychiatrist responds with a demand rather than exploring this motivation further. No open-ended questions are asked to evoke change talk about benefits, confidence, or strengths. When Amina expresses ambivalence, the psychiatrist responds with pressure rather than exploring the ambivalence to strengthen change talk. A skilled response would explore what would help her feel more confident, but instead, demands are made. The psychiatrist imposes reasons for change externally rather than facilitating self-discovery.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist does attempt to address Amina's sustain talk (e.g., "everyone does it," "it helps me relax," "I'd miss out"), which prevents a score of 1. However, the approach is confrontational and dismissive rather than skillful. When Amina says "everyone does it," the response is direct confrontation: "No, no, you can't just say 'everyone does it' and keep going like this." When Amina expresses that phone use helps her relax and stay connected, the psychiatrist responds: "You can't keep feeling stressed or lonely as an excuse to stay on your phone all night," framing legitimate needs as "excuses." A more skillful approach would use double-sided reflection: "So on one hand, your phone helps you relax and stay connected—that's important. And on the other hand, you're noticing it's affecting your sleep and grades. Both matter." The psychiatrist never explores what Amina would need to feel confident about change, which could help soften sustain talk by building self-efficacy.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.1/5**

**Assessment:** This session represents a fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles and demonstrates poor clinical practice that is likely to harm the therapeutic relationship and reduce the client's motivation for change. The psychiatrist employs an authoritarian, directive approach characterized by the absence of acceptance, empathy, collaboration, autonomy support, and evocation. The client explicitly states that this approach is counterproductive: "being told what to do like this just makes me push back even more" and "right now, I'm just feeling more stuck and stressed, not motivated to change."

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Complete absence of acceptance and empathy
- Authoritarian stance replacing collaboration
- Coercion and pressure undermining autonomy
- Telling rather than evoking reasons for change
- Confrontational handling of ambivalence
- Minimal change talk evocation and reinforcement

**Recommendation:** The psychiatrist should seek formal Motivational Interviewing training and supervision from a certified MI trainer before continuing to use this approach with clients. The current approach is inconsistent with evidence-based practice and is likely to harm therapeutic outcomes.
