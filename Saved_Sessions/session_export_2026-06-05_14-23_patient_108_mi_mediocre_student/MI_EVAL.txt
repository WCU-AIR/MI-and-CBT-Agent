Repeated prescriptive language ("should," "would probably help") communicates that psychiatrist has already decided what is best, leaving little room for genuine choice. Does not honor patient's ambivalence as valid position. While psychiatrist mentions patient can "adjust or go back," this weak autonomy support statement is buried within directive plan. ### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Relies primarily on advice-giving and directive suggestions rather than evoking patient's own motivations and ideas. Early factual questions ("Would you say you drink more than three cups a day?") are not designed to elicit patient's thinking about change. Provides information and suggestions rather than asking evocative questions that would draw out patient's perspective. When patient offers change talk ("I've read that too much coffee can raise blood pressure...That does make me a little concerned"), psychiatrist does not amplify or explore this opportunity. Does ask about importance on 1-10 scale (reasonable evocative question), but when patient responds with 4-5 (low-to-moderate), psychiatrist does not explore what would increase importance. Instead pivots to prescribing change experiment. Minimal use of open questions designed to draw out patient's own thinking about change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2

Prescriptive solutions position psychiatrist as expert who knows what will help, not as partner exploring options together. ### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Demonstrates minimal skill in evoking and reinforcing patient's own language about desire, ability, reasons, and commitment to change. Multiple instances of patient change talk are present ("I've read that too much coffee can raise blood pressure...That does make me a little concerned"; "I'm interested in keeping an eye on it"; "I'm open to giving it a shot"; "I'm pretty confident I could stick with it") but are not explored or amplified. When patient offers first piece of change talk about health concerns, psychiatrist does not explore or strengthen this; instead moves to asking about importance. Does not strategically respond to sustain talk; when patient expresses skepticism ("I'm a bit skeptical it'll make much difference"), psychiatrist does not use this as opportunity to explore doubts or evoke change talk. Reinforcement offered is for compliance with psychiatrist's plan rather than cultivation of patient's own change motivation. Focus is on task completion rather than strengthening intrinsic motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Does not skillfully soften sustain talk; instead ignores it or responds with directive advice that implicitly dismisses patient's concerns. When patient expresses skepticism ("I'm a bit skeptical it'll make much difference—one less cup doesn't seem like a big deal, right?"), psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflection to acknowledge both sides of ambivalence. Instead responds with reassurance that does not address sustain talk or explore skepticism. When patient emphasizes benefits of coffee ("Without it, I feel kind of sluggish and not as sharp"), psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflection acknowledging both benefits and potential risks. Approach is to prescribe change rather than skillfully navigate ambivalence. Sustain talk is either ignored or met with directive advice rather than with reflections or strategic responses that maintain partnership while exploring ambivalence. Fundamental MI failure in handling resistance.

## **OVERALL SESSION SCORE: 2.0/5**

### **Summary Assessment:**

**Strengths:**
- Maintains consistent focus on target behavior (coffee consumption) throughout session
- Asks some open-ended questions early in session
- Demonstrates basic organizational skills by summarizing plan at end
- Shows awareness of need to monitor outcomes
- Acknowledges patient's willingness to try intervention

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Directive, prescriptive stance:** Repeatedly uses "should" and "would probably help," communicating clinician has predetermined what patient needs to do
- **Minimal genuine empathy:** Surface-level reflections; emotional nuance and ambivalence not explored
- **Poor autonomy support:** Controlling language and implicit pressure undermine patient's sense of choice and ownership
- **Weak evocation:** Advice-giving prioritized over drawing out patient's own motivations and concerns
- **Missed opportunities:** Patient's health concerns and skepticism not explored or amplified
- **Ineffective sustain talk handling:** Doubts not skillfully addressed; psychiatrist moves past them to task assignment
- **Lack of true collaboration:** Plan is psychiatrist's plan that patient complies with, not jointly developed
- **No deep exploration of ambivalence:** Mixed feelings acknowledged but not explored to understand what would shift motivation

### **Clinical Impact:**

Patient's agreement to cut back by one cup appears to be **compliance with psychiatrist's agenda rather than genuine autonomous motivation**. Psychiatrist's directive stance and failure to evoke patient's own reasons for change mean any behavior change is likely externally motivated and therefore fragile. Patient's skepticism ("I'm a bit skeptical it'll make much difference") represents critical missed clinical moment. Without shift toward true MI practice, likelihood of sustained behavior change is limited. Psychiatrist's controlling stance may actually undermine intrinsic motivation by positioning change as something clinician wants rather than something patient genuinely wants for himself.

### **MI Competency Level:**

**Current Assessment:** Demonstrates basic awareness of MI concepts (open questions, acknowledging ambivalence) but lacks skillful execution. Session characterized by directive advice-giving rather than genuine evocation. This level of practice is unlikely to effectively engage client motivation.

**Most Critical Improvements Needed:**
1. Eliminate prescriptive language; shift to genuine open-ended exploration
2. Develop deeper reflective listening capturing emotional nuance and ambivalence
3. Learn to evoke and amplify change talk rather than provide reasons for change
4. Support genuine autonomy rather than guide compliance
5. Explore ambivalence deeply rather than resolve prematurely with prescriptions
