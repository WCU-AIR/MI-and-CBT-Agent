
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. The psychiatrist opens with a directive statement—"I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to get better"—which immediately establishes a hierarchical, judgmental stance rather than one of respect for the client's autonomy or perspective.

Most critically, the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses Andrei's legitimate concerns about time constraints and work pressures as "excuses." This occurs at least five times throughout the session: "You're just making excuses again," "No, you're not getting it. I don't care about your tiredness or excuses," and "No, you're missing the point. There's no room for 'small steps.'" These statements communicate profound disrespect for the client's lived experience and barriers. Rather than normalizing the struggle of balancing health with demanding work, the psychiatrist pathologizes Andrei's concerns and invalidates them.

The psychiatrist also uses confrontational language that violates MI spirit: "You have to choose: exercise tomorrow or keep risking your life," "This isn't negotiable," and "This is not optional." These statements communicate conditional regard—the psychiatrist's respect is contingent on compliance, not unconditional. There is no affirmation of Andrei's strengths (his self-awareness, his willingness to engage, his honesty about barriers), no warmth, and no genuine support for his struggle. The tone is punitive and controlling throughout.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance. The session is characterized by judgment, dismissal of client concerns, conditional regard based on compliance, and a complete absence of affirmation or warmth. This directly violates a foundational MI principle and creates an adversarial rather than supportive relationship.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening, genuine effort to understand the client's perspective, and capturing emotional nuance. This session contains almost none of these elements.

The psychiatrist never reflects back what Andrei is saying. When Andrei states, "I'm kept in your waiting room—for 45 minutes. That's not acceptable," the psychiatrist does not acknowledge this frustration or validate the irritation. When Andrei explains, "Food is just fuel for me. I'm often grazing, grabbing whatever's quick because I'm on the run," the psychiatrist does not reflect this practical reality or explore what it means to live under such time pressure. When Andrei says, "The weight is something I would like to get hold of," the psychiatrist never amplifies or explores this change talk—instead, the psychiatrist pivots immediately to demands and threats.

The psychiatrist demonstrates no effort to understand Andrei's emotional state. Andrei expresses feeling overwhelmed, pressured, and exhausted ("I'm tired—physically and mentally," "I'm already under a lot of that pressure"). The psychiatrist's response is to intensify pressure rather than understand it: "I don't care about your tiredness or excuses." This is the opposite of empathy.

There are no complex reflections that deepen insight. There are no attempts to understand the conflict between Andrei's desire for health and his genuine constraints. The psychiatrist treats Andrei's barriers as character flaws rather than real obstacles worthy of exploration. A truly empathic response might have been: "It sounds like you're caught between knowing what needs to happen and feeling completely overwhelmed by the demands on your time right now. **Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no empathic listening, no reflective responses, and no genuine effort to understand Andrei's perspective or emotional experience. This is a critical failure in MI practice and creates distance rather than connection.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

Direction in MI means maintaining strategic focus on a target behavior while remaining client-centered. This psychiatrist does maintain focus on weight loss and health behaviors—that is consistent. However, the direction is imposed rather than collaborative, and it lacks strategic nuance.

The psychiatrist repeatedly returns to the same demands: "exercise 30 minutes tomorrow" and "eat proper meals." This is repetitive rather than strategic. A more skillfully directed approach would involve exploring what small, time-efficient changes Andrei might realistically implement, then progressively building on those. Instead, the psychiatrist repeats the same ultimatum five times, which suggests either poor listening or deliberate escalation of pressure.

The psychiatrist does maintain some focus on the target behavior (weight, exercise, diet, blood pressure monitoring), but this focus is rigid and non-negotiable rather than collaboratively refined. There is no exploration of Andrei's values, no clarification of what "getting hold of his weight" means to him, and no progressive movement toward realistic goals. Additionally, the psychiatrist loses sight of the relational context. Andrei explicitly states that the approach is pushing him away: "That's pushing me away" and "If you don't work with me on how to fit this into my life, it's just going to feel impossible." A strategically directed session would adjust course when the client signals disengagement. This psychiatrist does not.

**Summary:**

## Recommendations

### 1. **Foundational: Adopt the MI Spirit and Stance**
- **Action:** Before any technique training, the psychiatrist must fundamentally shift from an expert/authority stance to a collaborative, client-centered stance. This requires genuine belief that the client's expertise about his own life is valuable and that motivation for change resides within the client, not in the clinician's threats.
- **Example:** Instead of opening with "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do," open with: "I'm glad you're here. I know you're busy and frustrated with the wait. Before we dive in, I'd like to understand what brought you in today and what your concerns are about your health. What's most important to you right now?"
- **Practice:** Reflect on your own beliefs about client autonomy, resistance, and motivation. Consider reading motivational interviewing foundational texts (Miller & Rollnick, *Motivational Interviewing: Helping People Change*) to deepen understanding of MI spirit. Practice role-plays where you deliberately adopt a collaborative stance and notice how clients respond differently.

---

### 2. **Critical: Develop Reflective Listening and Empathic Responding**
- **Action:** Learn to reflect back what the client is saying before responding with advice or directives. Use reflections to demonstrate understanding and validate the client's experience. This is the foundation of empathy in MI.
- **Example:** When Andrei says, "I'm kept in your waiting room—for 45 minutes. That's not acceptable. I haven't got a lot of time to waste here," respond with: "You're frustrated about the wait, and time is really precious for you right now. I hear that." Then: "Tell me what's going on with your health that brought you in today."
- **Example:** When Andrei says, "Food is just fuel for me. I'm often grazing, grabbing whatever's quick because I'm on the run," respond with: "So with your schedule, you're constantly moving, and eating is just about getting fuel in quickly. That's the reality of your day." Then explore: "How does that work for you? What would be different if you had more time?"
- **Practice:** Record yourself in role-plays and count the number of reflections vs. directives. Aim for at least 3 reflections before offering any advice. Practice complex reflections that capture both content and emotion: "It sounds like you're caught between knowing what needs to happen for your health and feeling completely overwhelmed by the demands on your time. That's a tough position to be in."

---

### 3. **Critical: Stop Using Threats and Coercion; Replace with Autonomy Support**
- **Action:** Eliminate all language that removes choice or uses fear to coerce compliance. Replace with language that affirms the client's right to choose and emphasizes that change must come from the client's own motivation.
- **Example:** Instead of: "You have to exercise 30 minutes tomorrow or you'll have a heart attack," say: "I'm concerned about your blood pressure and weight, and I know you are too. You've mentioned wanting to get hold of your weight. What do you think might be a realistic first step for you, given how busy you are?"
- **Example:** Instead of: "This isn't negotiable. You have to commit," say: "I want to support you in making changes that matter to you. What feels doable to you right now?"
- **Practice:** In role-plays, practice offering choices and affirming autonomy: "You get to decide what changes make sense for you," "I'm here to support whatever you decide," "What would feel manageable to you?"

---

### 4. **Critical: Learn to Evoke Rather Than Impose**
- **Action:** Shift from a "telling" approach to an "asking" approach. Use open-ended questions to draw out the client's own motivations, values, and ideas. When you hear change talk, explore it and amplify it.
- **Example:** Instead of: "You need to stop eating junk food and start exercising," ask: "You mentioned wanting to get hold of your weight. What would be different for you if you were able to do that? What matters to you about your health?"
- **Example:** When Andrei says, "I'm out of breath when I do simple things," explore: "Tell me more about that. How does that affect you? What would it mean to you to not feel that way?"
- **Example:** When Andrei says, "The weight is something I would like to get hold of," amplify: "That's important to you. What's driving that? What would change if you were able to get hold of your weight?"
- **Practice:** In role-plays, practice asking open-ended questions and then listening deeply to the client's responses. Practice reflecting back what you hear and asking follow-up questions that deepen exploration. Count the number of times you ask vs. tell. Aim for at least 80% asking.

---

### 5. **Critical: Collaborate on Realistic, Time-Efficient Solutions**
- **Action:** When the client identifies barriers (like time constraints), treat these as real obstacles to be problem-solved together, not as excuses to be dismissed. Work collaboratively to identify small, realistic changes that fit with the client's actual life.
- **Example:** Instead of: "There's no room for 'small steps' or 'realistic plans,'" say: "I hear that time is your biggest constraint. Let's think about this together. What are some small changes that might fit with your schedule? What would be realistic for you?"
- **Example:** Explore: "You mentioned that food is just fuel and you're grabbing things on the run. What if we thought about quick, healthier options you could grab? What would work for you?"
- **Example:** Explore: "You're concerned about finding 30 minutes for exercise. What if we started smaller? What about 10 minutes? Or even 5 minutes? What would feel doable?"
- **Practice:** In role-plays, when a client identifies a barrier, pause and say: "That's a real challenge. Let's think about how we might work around that. What ideas do you have?" Practice brainstorming together rather than imposing solutions.

---

### 6. **Important: Recognize and Soften Sustain Talk Without Confrontation**
- **Action:** When the client expresses reasons not to change (sustain talk), acknowledge these without confronting them. Use double-sided reflections, reframing, and exploration of ambivalence to gently move toward change talk.
- **Example:** Instead of: "You're just making excuses," use a double-sided reflection: "On one hand, you're juggling an incredibly demanding schedule. On the other hand, you've said the weight is something you'd like to get hold of. How do you make sense of that?"
- **Example:** Instead of: "I don't care about your tiredness," reframe: "It sounds like you're exhausted, and adding more demands right now feels overwhelming. That makes sense. What would need to be different for you to feel like change was possible?"
- **Example:** Explore ambivalence: "So you want to address your health, but you're worried that the typical advice won't work with your schedule. What would need to happen for you to feel confident that you could make changes?"
- **Practice:** In role-plays, when a client expresses sustain talk, practice pausing and using a double-sided reflection before responding. Practice reframing barriers as understandable rather than as character flaws.

---

### 7. **Important: Assess Readiness and Adjust Approach Based on Client Response**
- **Action:** Pay attention to how the client is responding. If the client is becoming more defensive, disengaged, or explicitly stating that your approach isn't working, adjust your approach. This is not a sign to escalate pressure; it's a sign to change strategy.
- **Example:** When Andrei says, "That's pushing me away," this is critical feedback that the current approach is counterproductive. The response should be: "I hear that my approach isn't working for you. I apologize. Let me try a different way. What would be helpful for you?"
- **Example:** When Andrei says, "If you don't work with me on how to fit this into my life, it's just going to feel impossible," this is a clear signal to shift to collaboration. Respond with: "You're right. Let's work together on this. What would feel realistic to you?"
- **Practice:** In role-plays, practice noticing when a client is disengaging and deliberately shifting your approach. Practice saying: "I notice this approach isn't working. Let me try something different. What would be helpful for you?"

---

### 8. **Important: Validate the Client's Concerns and Barriers**
- **Action:** Communicate genuine understanding and validation of the client's real-world constraints. This is not the same as agreeing that change is impossible; it's acknowledging that the barriers are real and understandable.
- **Example:** "I hear you. You're self-employed, working long hours, constantly juggling tasks. That's a demanding situation. And you're also concerned about your health and your blood pressure. You're trying to balance two important things. That's genuinely difficult."
- **Example:** "It makes sense that food is just fuel for you right now. When you're running from one thing to the next, sitting down for a proper meal feels like a luxury you don't have. I get that."
- **Practice:** In role-plays, practice validating barriers before exploring possibilities for change. Practice statements like: "That's a real challenge," "I understand why that's difficult," "That makes sense given your situation."

---

### 9. **Important: Build Self-Efficacy and Emphasize Client Competence**
- **Action:** Affirm the client's strengths, past successes, and capacity for change. Emphasize that the client is the expert on his own life and has the ability to make changes that work for him.
- **Example:** "You've shown a lot of self-awareness here. You know your weight is an issue, you understand how it's affecting your breathing and your blood pressure, and you're honest about your barriers. That's important. You're not in denial—you're being realistic."

- **Example:** "You've said the weight is something you'd like to get hold of. That tells me you have motivation. The question is how to channel that motivation in a way that works with your life."
- **Practice:** In role-plays, practice identifying and affirming client strengths. Practice statements like: "You're clearly capable of..." "You've shown that you can..." "Your honesty about this is a strength."
