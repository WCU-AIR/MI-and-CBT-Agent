When Mateo states "I do the exercises...I'm consistent," clinician questions effort ("Are you sure you're doing the exercises correctly?") rather than strengthening commitment. When Mateo expresses desire for change, clinician pressures rather than encourages. Improvement in middle section with questions inviting change talk ("How about we set some specific, achievable goals together?") and affirmation of identified exercises. ### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Softening sustain talk is largely absent with clinician using confrontation that likely strengthens Mateo's resistance. When Mateo expresses legitimate concerns about pain and difficulty changing habits, psychiatrist responds with direct confrontation ("stop making excuses," "stop feeling sorry for yourself," "you must push harder"). These responses violate MI's core principle of avoiding confrontation and are likely to trigger defensiveness. Modest improvement in middle section with validation and reframing ("It sounds like you're trying to find a balance between staying active and respecting your body's limits"). However, this skill is not sustained; final exchange returns to confrontation ("you have to stick to this plan strictly—no slacking off") likely to trigger sustain talk and defensiveness.

### Overall Clinical Summary

**Session Score: 1.8/5**

**Key Strengths:**
- Demonstrates capacity for collaborative goal-setting in middle section of session
- Shows ability to validate pain and struggle with genuine empathic statements
- Proposes realistic, graduated goals (5 minutes stretches, walks twice weekly) aligned with client capacity
- Explicitly acknowledges client expertise ("You know your body better than anyone")

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Severe inconsistency and regression:** Repeated pattern of improvement followed by regression to controlling, confrontational language undermines therapeutic alliance and confuses client
- **Confrontational responses to sustain talk:** Dismissal of legitimate pain concerns ("stop making excuses," "stop feeling sorry for yourself") violates MI principles and strengthens resistance
- **Failure to amplify change talk:** Questions client effort and moves to warnings about consequences rather than developing intrinsic motivation
- **Prescription without exploration:** Imposes specific plan without exploring readiness, capacity, or values
- **Conditional regard and judgment:** Repeated use of "you must," "no excuses," and consequences-based messaging communicates judgment rather than acceptance
- **Failure to explore meaning and values:** Does not deeply connect change to what matters most to client

**Impact on Client Engagement:**
Session demonstrates pattern likely to confuse Mateo and undermine therapeutic alliance. Early confrontational approach creates defensiveness. Middle section shows improved engagement when collaborative approach is used. However, final regression to controlling language is likely to undermine progress. Mateo's final response ("I'll try to stick to it. But I have to be honest—it's not always easy") suggests fragile commitment and anticipated difficulty with adherence.

**Recommendations for Development:**
1. Eliminate confrontational language entirely
2. Maintain collaborative stance throughout entire session without regression
3. Strategically amplify and develop change talk rather than questioning effort
4. Explore meaning and values to strengthen intrinsic motivation
5. Use double-sided reflections to soften sustain talk without confrontation
6. Consistently affirm autonomy and client ownership throughout
7. Develop self-awareness about habitual reversion to directive practice
8. Avoid warnings about consequences; instead explore client's own concerns

---

**Supervisor Review Note:** This clinician demonstrates potential for MI competence based on middle-section performance but requires focused training on consistency, elimination of confrontational language, and integration of MI principles into sustained practice. Significant work needed to overcome habitual pattern of reverting to directive approach.
