## Summary of Feedback

- **Clear target behavior identified:** Psychiatrist maintains focus on smoking cessation as presenting concern.
- **Consistent engagement:** Provider remains engaged throughout session (though engagement without skillful MI practice is insufficient).

### Critical Areas for Improvement

This session represents a **fundamental failure to implement Motivational Interviewing principles**. The psychiatrist demonstrates:

1. **Pervasive judgment and shame-induction** rather than acceptance
2. **Complete absence of empathic attunement** and reflective listening
3. **Coercive direction** rather than collaborative guidance
4. **Systematic elimination of client autonomy** through commands and pressure
5. **Zero evocation** of client's own motivations and values
6. **Expert-driven hierarchy** rather than true partnership
7. **Failure to cultivate change talk** and active suppression of emerging motivation
8. **Direct confrontation of sustain talk**, which predictably strengthens resistance
9. **Repeated use of fear-based arguments and shame** to attempt behavioral control

The psychiatrist's approach is **antithetical to MI spirit**. Rather than evoking intrinsic motivation, the provider attempts to impose external compliance through pressure, judgment, and fear. This creates psychological reactance—Ahmed becomes more defensive and entrenched in his resistance.

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change Likelihood

**This session is likely to decrease Ahmed's motivation for change.** By session end, Ahmed has moved from tentative openness ("Maybe soon") to firm refusal ("That's just not gonna happen"). Key indicators of harm:

- Ahmed explicitly states: "pushing me like this is just making me more resistant"
- Ahmed's sustain talk strengthens throughout session
- Ahmed reasserts autonomy: "I need to do this on my own terms, not because I'm being told I have to"
- Therapeutic relationship is damaged; Ahmed unlikely to return for follow-up

**Psychological reactance is operating:** When people perceive their freedom is threatened, they resist more strongly. The psychiatrist's demands and commands trigger Ahmed's need to assert autonomy, making him less likely to quit.

### Whether Session Embodies MI Spirit

**This session does NOT embody MI spirit.** It represents the opposite: confrontation instead of collaboration, judgment instead of acceptance, imposition instead of evocation, coercion instead of autonomy support. This is a textbook example of what NOT to do in MI.

## Key Recommendations for Improvement

### 1. **Fundamental Reorientation: From Expert-Driven to Collaborative Partnership**
Restructure therapeutic stance from "I will tell you what to do" to "Let's figure this out together." Genuine belief that Ahmed has wisdom about his own life is essential. Change is more likely when it comes from within rather than being imposed externally.

### 2. **Develop Genuine Empathic Listening and Reflective Responses**
Replace arguments and dismissals with reflective listening demonstrating accurate understanding. Before responding to Ahmed, reflect back what you heard. Use formula: "What I'm hearing is... Is that accurate?"

### 3. **Eliminate Shame-Based Language and Judgment**
Remove all language labeling Ahmed's perspective as invalid, dishonest, or pathological. Replace phrases like "you're making excuses," "you're fooling yourself," "you're avoiding the truth" with genuine curiosity: "Help me understand how you see this..."

### 4. **Respect and Support Autonomy—Eliminate Commands and Pressure**
Stop using command language ("You must," "You will," "This is non-negotiable") and fear-based pressure. Explicitly affirm Ahmed's right to choose and ownership of change process. Use language: "It's entirely up to you," "You're the expert on your own life," "What feels right to you?"

### 5. **Evoke Rather Than Impose: Draw Out Ahmed's Own Motivations**
Ask open-ended questions designed to help Ahmed discover his own reasons for change. Focus on his values and what matters to him:
- "What matters most to you in your life right now?"
- "You mentioned wanting to be around for your family. Tell me more about that."
- "If you did decide to quit, what would be the benefits for you?"
- "What would need to happen for you to feel ready to make a change?"

### 6. **Develop Skill in Handling Sustain Talk Without Confrontation**
Use double-sided reflections, gentle amplification, or strategic redirection rather than direct argument:
- **Double-sided reflection:** "On one hand, you see these mini-cigars as pretty harmless because you only use them occasionally. On the other hand, you know any smoking carries some health risk. How do you think about that?"
- **Gentle amplification:** "So you're saying these mini-cigars are so occasional and so different from cigarettes that there's really nothing to worry about?"

### 7. **Amplify and Reinforce Change Talk When It Emerges**
Listen carefully for statements Ahmed makes about wanting to change, being able to change, or reasons for change. When you hear these, pause and explore further. When Ahmed says "I do want to be around for my family and stay healthy," respond with: "That's really important—being around for your family. Tell me more about what that means to you."

### 8. **Explore Ahmed's Stage of Change and Meet Him There**
Recognize Ahmed is in precontemplation (not considering change). Trying to move him directly to action is developmentally inappropriate and counterproductive. Focus on moving toward contemplation by exploring ambivalence: "What would need to change for you to see quitting as something worth doing?"

### 9. **Seek Formal MI Training and Clinical Supervision**
This session demonstrates fundamental misalignment with MI principles. Seek formal MI training (e.g., through MINT—Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers) and regular clinical supervision focused on MI skill development. Record sessions (with consent) and review with supervisor.

### 10. **Develop Awareness of Your Own Countertransference**
Notice when you feel frustrated, judgmental, or like you need to "convince" the client. These are signs you've abandoned MI principles. When you feel this, pause and return to curiosity. After each session, reflect: "Did I feel frustrated or judgmental? If so, what triggered that? How could I have stayed curious instead?"
