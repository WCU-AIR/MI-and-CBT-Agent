
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

**Summary:**

## Recommendations

### 1. **Develop and Implement Systematic Case Conceptualization**
- **Action:** Before the next session, create a written case formulation that explicitly links the patient's history (critical father, perfectionist family, competitive environment) to her current symptoms (perfectionism, fear of judgment, avoidance). Identify the core beliefs maintaining her anxiety (e.g., "I am inadequate," "My worth depends on others' approval," "Mistakes are catastrophic").
- **Example:** "Mathilda's anxiety is maintained by perfectionism and fear of judgment. Her core belief is 'If I'm not perfect, people will judge me and I'm inadequate.' This belief was reinforced by her critical father and competitive family. She avoids social situations to prevent judgment, which reinforces the belief that judgment is dangerous. Treatment will involve: (1) challenging perfectionism, (2) behavioral exposure to feared social situations, (3) testing predictions about judgment, (4) building self-worth independent of others' approval."
- **Practice:** Write out the formulation and review it before each session. Use it to guide intervention selection and explain the rationale to the patient.

### 2. **Establish Structured, Collaborative Agenda-Setting**
- **Action:** At the beginning of each session, explicitly set a collaborative agenda with the patient. Identify 2-3 target problems, prioritize them together, allocate time, and establish specific session goals.
- **Example:** "Hi Mathilda. Last time we talked about your anxiety in social situations and identified perfectionism as something we want to work on. Today I'm thinking we could spend 20 minutes talking about how perfectionism keeps you stuck, and then 30 minutes planning some small steps to practice speaking up. Does that sound good? Is there anything else you'd like to focus on today?"
- **Practice:** Use a written agenda template. Share it with the patient. Check in mid-session: "We've spent 15 minutes on perfectionism. Are we on track? Should we adjust?"

### 3. **Implement Systematic Guided Discovery Using Socratic Questioning**
- **Action:** Replace direct suggestions with Socratic questions that help the patient discover insights. When the patient expresses an anxious thought, use the following sequence: (1) Identify the thought, (2) Examine evidence for and against it, (3) Consider alternatives, (4) Develop a more balanced thought, (5) Plan behavioral testing.
- **Example:** 
  - **Patient:** "I'm worried I'll say something stupid in class and everyone will judge me."
  - **Therapist:** "That's a thought that comes up for you. Let's examine it together. When you've spoken up in class before, what actually happened?"
  - **Patient:** "Well, people usually just listened and moved on."
  - **Therapist:** "Did anyone say you were stupid?"
  - **Patient:** "No."
  - **Therapist:** "So what's the evidence that people will judge you as stupid?"
  - **Patient:** "I guess... there isn't really evidence. I'm just afraid they will."
  - **Therapist:** "That's an important distinction. You're afraid they will, but it hasn't actually happened. What would it take for you to test this prediction? Could you speak up once in class and see what actually happens?"
- **Practice:** Record yourself or have a supervisor listen to sessions. Count how many times you ask questions versus make statements. Aim for 70% questions, 30% statements.

### 4. **Develop and Implement Systematic Behavioral Exposure Hierarchy**
- **Action:** Collaboratively create a detailed exposure hierarchy with the patient. Identify avoided social situations, rate them by anxiety level (0-100), and plan gradual exposure starting with lower-anxiety situations. Monitor anxiety levels and feared outcomes.
- **Example:** 
  - **Situation 1 (Anxiety 20):** Speak to cousin for 5 minutes about a neutral topic
  - **Situation 2 (Anxiety 30):** Speak to one close friend in a small group
  - **Situation 3 (Anxiety 50):** Ask a question in class
  - **Situation 4 (Anxiety 70):** Participate in group project discussion
  - **Situation 5 (Anxiety 85):** Speak up in a large class
  - **Situation 6 (Anxiety 95):** Give a presentation
- **Practice:** For each exposure, have the patient predict: "What do I think will happen?" Then after the exposure, review: "What actually happened? Was my prediction accurate? What did I learn?"

### 5. **Teach and Practice Cognitive Restructuring Systematically**
- **Action:** Introduce the thought record as a structured tool. Teach the patient to: (1) identify the situation, (2) identify the automatic thought, (3) identify the emotion and anxiety level, (4) examine evidence for and against the thought, (5) develop a more balanced thought, (6) re-rate anxiety. Practice in-session before assigning as homework.
- **Example:** 
  - **Situation:** About to speak in class
  - **Automatic Thought:** "I'll say something stupid and everyone will judge me"
  - **Anxiety Level:** 8/10
  - **Evidence For:** "I sometimes feel nervous and might stumble over words"
  - **Evidence Against:** "I've spoken before and people didn't judge me. Most people are focused on themselves. Even if I stumble, that doesn't mean I'm stupid."
  - **Balanced Thought:** "I might feel nervous, but I can speak anyway. Even if I'm not perfect, that's okay. People are usually kind."
  - **New Anxiety Level:** 5/10
- **Practice:** Have the patient complete one thought record in-session with your guidance. Then assign homework with clear instructions and follow-up.

### 6. **Address Perfectionism Directly as a Core Treatment Target**
- **Action:** Explicitly identify perfectionism as a maintaining factor of anxiety. Help the patient understand the perfectionism-anxiety cycle: perfectionism → high standards → pressure → anxiety → avoidance. Develop interventions to challenge perfectionism and practice "good enough."
- **Example:** "Mathilda, I've noticed that you try to make your answers perfect before speaking, and that actually increases your anxiety and makes you freeze. Perfectionism is keeping you stuck. What if we worked on accepting 'good enough'? You could practice speaking even when you're not 100% sure of your answer. Let's start small—maybe you could ask one question in class this week, even if you're not sure it's the perfect question."
- **Practice:** Assign behavioral experiments: "Intentionally give an imperfect answer in class and observe what happens. Does the world end? Do people judge you? Or do they just move on?"

### 7. **Establish Clear, Specific, Rationale-Explained Homework**
- **Action:** For each homework assignment, provide: (1) clear written instructions with specific format, (2) explicit rationale explaining why it will help, (3) in-session practice or modeling, (4) commitment check and problem-solving of barriers, (5) plan for review at next session.
- **Example:** 
  - **Assignment:** "Complete this thought record form when you feel anxious in a social situation. Write down: (1) What happened? (2) What did you think? (3) How anxious (0-10)? (4) What evidence is there for/against the thought? (5) What's a more balanced thought? (6) How anxious now (0-10)?"
  - **Rationale:** "We're doing this because anxiety often involves thoughts we don't notice. By writing them down and examining them, we can see if they're accurate. This helps us challenge anxious thoughts and feel less anxious."
  - **Practice:** "Let's do one together right now. Think of a social situation that makes you anxious. What thought comes up? Let's fill out the form together."
  - **Commitment Check:** "Does this feel doable? What might get in the way? How can we make it easier?"
  - **Review Plan:** "Bring your completed forms to our next session. We'll review them together and see what patterns we notice."
- **Practice:** Write out homework assignments in advance. Include specific instructions, rationale, and review plan.

### 8. **Implement Systematic Feedback Elicitation Throughout Sessions**
- **Action:** Regularly ask for feedback about the session, the therapist's explanations, and the patient's reactions. Adjust based on feedback. At the end of each session, summarize key points and ask for the patient's takeaways.
