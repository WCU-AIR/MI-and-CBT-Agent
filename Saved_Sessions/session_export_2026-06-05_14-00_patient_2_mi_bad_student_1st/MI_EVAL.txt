# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard throughout the session. The opening statement ("I'll be telling you exactly what steps you must take") signals conditional acceptance based solely on compliance. The patient's explanations are repeatedly labeled as "excuses" (6+ instances), and legitimate contextual barriers—work demands, family responsibilities, stress management—are invalidated rather than explored. Shaming language is prevalent ("You must stop fooling yourself," "Your diet is clearly poor," "Your diet is unacceptable"). No affirmation of positive behavior or acknowledgment of the patient's genuine effort to engage is offered. The psychiatrist communicates judgment and disapproval at every turn, fundamentally undermining acceptance.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective from their frame of reference. This session contains zero reflective listening and active resistance to understanding the patient's viewpoint. The patient articulates clear constraints ("by the time I get off work and handle things at home, there's barely any energy left," "I use them sometimes to relax after work, and it helps me wind down," "I need a plan that fits my life, not just orders"), yet the psychiatrist never reflects back any of these statements. No complex reflections capture emotional nuance or demonstrate understanding. Instead, the psychiatrist dismisses the patient's perspective with counter-arguments ("Stress is not a reason to keep smoking mini-cigars," "everyone can find 30 minutes a day if they really want to"). The patient explicitly requests empathic engagement ("you gotta help me see how it can fit in my life"), which is ignored entirely.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains extremely clear, consistent direction toward three specific target behaviors: tobacco cessation (mini-cigars), exercise (30 minutes, 5 days per week), and dietary improvement (fruits and vegetables daily). These domains are identified early and repeatedly reinforced throughout the session with no topic drift. However, it is critical to note that while direction is technically present, it is executed in a manner entirely inconsistent with MI spirit. Direction in MI should be collaborative and progressive; here it is imposed unilaterally and repetitively. The psychiatrist's direction is also strategically ineffective—repeating identical demands without modification despite clear evidence that this approach generates resistance and defensiveness. A truly skillful clinician would adjust strategy when the current approach is failing.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, with both parties contributing knowledge and perspective. This session is the antithesis of collaboration. The opening statement explicitly establishes an authoritarian framework: "I'll be telling you exactly what steps you must take." The psychiatrist positions himself as the sole authority on what the patient "needs to do" and what he "must take." Throughout the session, the psychiatrist issues directives without soliciting input, dismisses the patient's contributions as invalid ("you're just making excuses," "you're not understanding"), and asks only rhetorical or shame-based questions. The patient explicitly requests collaboration ("Can't we talk about how to make small changes instead?"), which is ignored. There is no joint problem-solving, no shared decision-making, and no co-construction of solutions. The psychiatrist remains entirely in the expert role while the patient is positioned as a non-compliant subject requiring correction.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support means respecting and actively fostering the client's perception of choice and self-determination. This session actively undermines autonomy at every turn. The psychiatrist uses language that explicitly removes choice: "you must," "you have to," "this is non-negotiable," "there's no other option" (appearing 15+ times). When the patient expresses ambivalence or hesitation, the psychiatrist frames it as failure of will rather than legitimate constraint ("Why haven't you started yet?" implies he could if he wanted to; "everyone can find 30 minutes a day if they really want to" implies the patient doesn't really want to). Fear-based pressure is used ("If you don't take these steps seriously, you're putting yourself at huge risk for heart disease and cancer"), which attempts to control behavior through threat rather than supporting intrinsic motivation. The patient explicitly states his need for autonomy ("It feels like you're just telling me what to do without understanding my situation"), yet the psychiatrist intensifies demands in response. An autonomy-supportive response would acknowledge the patient's perspective and explore what conditions might support readiness for change.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation is the core of MI—drawing out the client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources rather than imposing them. This session contains virtually no evocation. The psychiatrist asks only closed, rhetorical, or shame-based questions ("Are you currently smoking?", "Are you exercising regularly?", "Do you follow a healthy diet?", "Why haven't you started yet?", "Don't you want to avoid serious health problems?"). None of these questions evoke the patient's own reasons for change, values, self-efficacy, or ideas about what might work. The psychiatrist tells the patient what he should think rather than asking questions that help him discover his own reasons for change. The patient does offer minimal spontaneous change talk ("I know it's not perfect," "I don't want to get serious diseases"), but the psychiatrist never amplifies or reinforces these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist responds to ambivalence by intensifying pressure, which suppresses change talk. Genuine evocative questions would explore values, past successes, perceived barriers, and realistic first steps—none of which occur.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership means conveying understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client, and actively fostering power-sharing so that the client's contributions substantially influence the session. This session demonstrates the opposite. The psychiatrist actively assumes the expert role for the entire interaction, with the opening statement explicitly establishing this: "I'll be telling you exactly what steps you must take." All contributions are directive ("You must quit mini-cigars immediately," "You must make time for exercise," "You must eat fruits and vegetables daily"), while the patient's contributions are systematically dismissed as invalid ("you're just making excuses," "you're not understanding," "you're missing the point"). The patient's lived experience—work demands, family responsibilities, stress management needs—is treated as irrelevant obstacles rather than legitimate constraints that should shape the intervention. The patient explicitly requests partnership ("Can't we talk about how to make small changes instead?"), which is ignored. The psychiatrist maintains unilateral expertise and power imbalance throughout, with no genuine co-construction of solutions.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Cultivating change talk means actively encouraging the client's own language in favor of change and confidence for making that change. This requires strategic questioning, reflective listening, and reinforcement of change-oriented statements. The patient offers minimal spontaneous change talk: "I know it's not perfect" (acknowledgment of problem), "I don't want to get serious diseases, sure, who does?" (desire for change), and "Maybe I need to eat better" (ability/need language). However, the psychiatrist never amplifies, reinforces, or builds on any of these statements. When the patient says "I don't want to get serious diseases," the psychiatrist responds with "Don't you realize the serious risks you're taking?"—which is not amplification but rather a shame-based challenge that suppresses further change talk. Instead of evoking change talk, the psychiatrist generates and reinforces sustain talk through repeated pressure. Each directive ("you must quit immediately," "you're just making excuses," "you're not taking this seriously") generates more sustain talk from the patient. This is the opposite of cultivating change talk; the psychiatrist's approach systematically suppresses it.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Softening sustain talk means skillfully handling client arguments for the status quo without confrontation, using techniques like double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic redirection to minimize sustain talk while maintaining collaboration. This session demonstrates the opposite. The psychiatrist directly confronts the patient's sustain talk with counter-arguments and pressure, which research shows intensifies resistance. The patient's sustain talk includes minimization ("I don't think they're the same thing"), resistance to change ("I don't think quitting them right now is necessary"), ambivalence ("I'm not ready to quit right this minute"), and perceived inability ("It's not like a switch I can flip"). The psychiatrist's response to each instance is direct confrontation: "Mini-cigars are still harmful, so you really need to quit those immediately," "No, you're just making excuses," "everyone can find 30 minutes a day if they really want to." This is textbook confrontation, which increases resistance and sustain talk. Each confrontation is followed by the patient offering more sustain talk, more defensiveness, and more resistance. Skillful softening would involve double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic redirection—none of which occur. The psychiatrist's approach is entirely confrontational and systematically generates more sustain talk.

## Summary of Findings

### Strengths
- **Clear, consistent focus on target behaviors:** The psychiatrist maintains unwavering direction toward three specific health domains (tobacco, exercise, diet) throughout the session with no topic drift.
- **Persistence in addressing health concerns:** The psychiatrist repeatedly returns to identified health issues and does not allow avoidance or deflection.

### Critical Areas for Improvement

This session represents a **fundamental failure of motivational interviewing practice** across nearly every dimension:

1. **Authoritarian stance from the outset:** The opening statement establishes a non-collaborative, expert-driven framework antithetical to MI principles.

2. **Systematic rejection of the client's perspective:** Every explanation the patient offers is dismissed as "excuses" rather than explored with curiosity, communicating that his lived experience is irrelevant.

3. **Repeated use of coercive language:** "You must," "you have to," "this is non-negotiable" (15+ instances) remove autonomy and generate resistance.

4. **Absence of reflective listening:** Zero reflections throughout the entire session—a fundamental MI skill that is entirely absent.

5. **Confrontational approach to resistance:** Each time the patient expresses ambivalence or sustain talk, the psychiatrist responds with direct confrontation, which research shows increases resistance.

6. **Failure to evoke change talk:** All questions are closed, rhetorical, or shame-based. The psychiatrist tells rather than asks, imposes rather than elicits.

7. **Shame and judgment:** Language communicates disapproval and judgment, undermining acceptance and collaboration.

8. **Escalating pressure despite evidence of failure:** The psychiatrist repeats the same ineffective approach multiple times, intensifying demands each round despite clear evidence of generating defensiveness.

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change

**This session is likely to produce the opposite of the intended effect.** The patient enters with some ambivalence about his health behaviors—acknowledging that smoking "isn't great," recognizing his diet "isn't perfect," and stating "I don't want to get serious diseases." These are seeds of change talk that could be cultivated.

However, the psychiatrist's approach systematically suppresses this change talk and generates defensiveness. By the end of the session, the patient explicitly states:
- "pushing me like this just makes me shut down"
- "I'm just going to stick with what I know because it feels like there isn't any other option for me"
- "hearing it like a command makes me less likely to listen"

**The psychiatrist has transformed the patient's initial ambivalence into entrenched resistance.** The patient is now more defensive about his choices, more skeptical of the healthcare system, and less likely to follow through on any recommendations.

### Recommendation

**The psychiatrist should engage in formal motivational interviewing training and skill development before continuing to conduct behavior change counseling.** The current approach violates evidence-based principles of behavior change and is likely to harm rather than help patients. The most critical improvements needed are: (1) adopting a collaborative partnership stance, (2) developing reflective listening as a core skill, (3) replacing confrontation with curiosity, (4) evoking rather than imposing, and (5) supporting autonomy throughout.
