### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Complete absence of partnership. Authoritarian, expert-driven stance where psychiatrist assumes unilateral authority and dismisses client input entirely. Explicit restrictions on client voice ("Just yes or no answers, please"). Unilateral pronouncements ("That's exactly the problem," "It's non-negotiable"). Client's repeated invitations for partnership are rejected ("Can we talk about the waiting first?" and "meet me halfway, not come down like a hammer"). Power dynamic is entirely one-directional. Psychiatrist holds all authority; client positioned as passive recipient of orders. No shared decision-making or co-construction of goals.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Active undermining of autonomy at every turn. Controlling language throughout: "You have to," "You really need to," "You need to," "It's non-negotiable," "Do you understand?" Client explicitly signals that autonomy support would increase openness ("bossing me around like that isn't helping...I'm not some kid who just needs orders"). Psychiatrist intensifies control in response. Client's freedom to choose never acknowledged. Phrases like "Are you going to quit or not?" frame change as compliance with psychiatrist's demands rather than client's own choice. "Non-negotiable plan" communicates client has no say in own treatment.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no evocation present. Only closed, factual questions asked ("Are you currently smoking...? Do you drink...? Are you exercising...?"). Client's spontaneous change talk ignored: "I know it's a problem," "I do want that [to get better]." Psychiatrist tells client why he should change ("It's ruining your health," "These habits are dangerous") without exploring what client thinks or feels. Reasons for change imposed rather than evoked.

Client's spontaneous change talk ignored: "I know it's a problem" (recognition of need), "I do want that [to get better]" (desire for change), "Maybe if this whole system wasn't so frustrating, I'd be more ready" (implicit willingness). ## Overall Summary

### **Overall Session Score: 1.1/5**

**MI Competency Assessment:** Minimal to no MI competency demonstrated. Approach is fundamentally inconsistent with evidence-based MI practice and operates in direct opposition to MI principles across all nine domains.

**Key Findings:**

- **Authoritarian stance:** Entire session characterized by unilateral expert authority, directive language, and dismissal of client input.
- **Confrontational approach:** Shame, blame, and pressure used—techniques that research shows *increase* resistance and *decrease* behavior change.
- **Absence of empathy:** No reflective listening, no exploration of client perspective, explicit dismissal of emotional reality and legitimate concerns.
- **Suppression of change talk:** Client's spontaneous change talk ignored; confrontational approach actively suppresses further motivation.
- **Escalation of defensiveness:** Client's defensiveness escalates from frustration to explicit threat of disengagement across three iterations.
- **Violation of autonomy:** Controlling language and "non-negotiable" plan communicate client has no choice in own treatment.

**Impact on Client Engagement and Change:**

This session is **actively harmful to client engagement and likelihood of behavior change**. Client entered with some ambivalence and openness ("I know it's a problem," "I do want that"). By session end, client is threatening to disengage ("all I want to do is walk out"). Psychiatrist has successfully *decreased* motivation rather than evoking it. Research on behavior change consistently demonstrates that confrontational, directive approaches increase resistance and *decrease* sustained behavior change.

**Critical Improvements Needed:**

1. Shift from directive/expert to collaborative/evocative stance
2. Develop genuine reflective listening skills
3. Validate and address legitimate concerns rather than dismissing them as "excuses"
4. Ask open-ended questions to evoke client motivation
5. Recognize and amplify spontaneous change talk
6. Use autonomy-supportive language consistently
7. De-escalate defensiveness through validation and empathy
8. Implement structured MI opening establishing collaboration
9. Address system barriers (wait times) as legitimate clinical issue
10. Engage in formal MI training and ongoing supervision

**Recommendation:** Psychiatrist requires comprehensive MI training, supervision with recorded session feedback, and commitment to deliberate practice. Current approach is fundamentally misaligned with evidence-based practice and will result in poor behavior change outcomes and client disengagement if continued.
