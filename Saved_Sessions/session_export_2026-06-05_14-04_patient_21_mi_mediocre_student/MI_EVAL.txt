# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal acceptance of Brandon's perspective and autonomy. While opening with basic warmth, the stance quickly becomes directive and problem-focused rather than unconditionally supportive. Fails to normalize Brandon's ambivalence or validate his concerns about social belonging and stress relief. Language becomes increasingly prescriptive ("It would probably help if...," "You might want to...") rather than respectful of Brandon's right to define his own experience. Expert-driven approach undermines genuine acceptance of Brandon as a person worthy of respect regardless of drinking choices.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Empathy is superficial and inconsistently applied. Psychiatrist uses basic factual reflections ("So, you're saying you drink to unwind...") rather than accurate reflections capturing emotional experience. Misses multiple opportunities for deeper empathic understanding—when Brandon says "I'm stuck in that, I guess," indicating ambivalence and frustration, psychiatrist does not explore emotional state. Fails to use complex reflections that would demonstrate understanding of Brandon's internal conflict between stress relief/social connection and family concerns. Reflections remain factual rather than emotionally attuned to Brandon's core experience.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains general focus on alcohol consumption throughout session, demonstrating adequate directional consistency. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively established. Never explicitly asks Brandon what he wants to focus on or what his goals are; instead, assumes target is reducing drinking and steers conversation toward that endpoint. Direction becomes increasingly prescriptive by mid-session with specific solution suggestions. Successfully guides Brandon toward concrete goal (cutting back on weeknights), but goal emerges from psychiatrist's suggestions rather than Brandon's articulated values and priorities.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Collaboration is largely absent. Psychiatrist adopts expert stance from beginning, positioning himself as authority on what Brandon should do. Frequent directive suggestions ("Do you think it would be helpful if...?" "Maybe you could try...?" "You might want to...?") dominate conversation. True collaboration would involve jointly exploring problem with Brandon's ideas shaping direction. When Brandon expresses hesitation or concern, psychiatrist does not use as opportunity to explore his perspective more deeply or invite Brandon to generate solutions. Instead, psychiatrist reframes concern and offers another suggestion. Relationship is hierarchical rather than partnership-based.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Autonomy support is minimal and contradicted by directive stance. While psychiatrist includes phrases like "it's your choice how to move forward," these statements are undermined by entire session structure communicating that psychiatrist has "right" answers. Frequent use of subtle pressure language: "It would probably help if..." (5+ times), "You might want to..." (4+ times), "Maybe you should..." cumulates to guiding Brandon toward predetermined solutions rather than supporting his own decision-making. Does not explore or affirm Brandon's stated ambivalence as legitimate. Session ends with "Keep up the good work!"—reinforcing compliance with psychiatrist's agenda rather than supporting autonomous decision-making.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out Brandon's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources—is almost entirely absent. This represents the most critical MI failure. Psychiatrist tells Brandon what reasons for change should be rather than asking questions to help Brandon articulate his own. Never asks: "What matters most to you?" "What worries you most about your drinking?" "If you were to make a change, what would that look like?" "What ideas do you have?" When Brandon offers minimal change talk ("maybe I should cut back a bit"), psychiatrist does not amplify or explore. Psychiatrist generates all ideas and reasons. Brandon articulates almost no genuine change talk; statements are compliance-based ("I'll give it a shot") rather than intrinsically motivated.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership—understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within client—is fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist explicitly positions himself as expert throughout, with Brandon as passive recipient of advice. Session structure communicates non-partnership: psychiatrist asks questions, listens, then tells Brandon what he should do. Psychiatrist generates all solutions and suggestions; Brandon's role is to accept or mildly resist. Never asks: "What do you think might work for you?" "What ideas do you have?" "What feels doable to you?" Brandon is never invited to be co-creator of plan. Psychiatrist dominates with expert suggestions and directives, minimizing genuine collaboration. Relationship is expert-driven rather than partnership-based.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Change talk is almost entirely absent, and psychiatrist makes minimal effort to cultivate it. Brandon offers only weak, compliance-based statements: "maybe I should cut back a bit," "I'm aware it might help with some stuff," "I'm willing to try a bit at least." Psychiatrist does not strategically evoke or amplify any of this. When Brandon says "maybe I should cut back," psychiatrist does not ask "Tell me more about that" or explore what makes him think that might help. Psychiatrist asks about confidence and importance but immediately pivots to prescribing solutions rather than exploring responses. Final statements ("I'll give it a shot") are responses to psychiatrist's suggestions, not Brandon's own articulated motivations. True change talk would reflect Brandon's own reasons for change, which never emerges.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk—Brandon's arguments for maintaining status quo—is present throughout and not skillfully managed. Brandon's sustain talk includes: "I don't think it's a big deal," "everyone I know drinks," "drinking's the easiest way to relax," "not drinking feels awkward," "other ways to relax don't come to mind easily," "my friends all drink too," "it seems like a hassle." Psychiatrist's responses primarily acknowledge sustain talk then offer solutions, rather than skillfully redirect or reframe. Does not use double-sided reflections acknowledging both benefits of drinking and potential benefits of change. Does not gently explore whether concerns (e.g., being "odd one out") are based on actual evidence or assumption. Solution-focused approach may inadvertently trigger more sustain talk by giving Brandon reasons to explain why solutions won't work.

---

## Overall Session Score: 1.6/5

**Summary:**

This session demonstrates fundamental deficiencies in Motivational Interviewing practice. While the psychiatrist maintains courteous demeanor and keeps session focused on alcohol consumption, core MI principles—evocation, partnership, autonomy support, and genuine empathy—are largely absent. The psychiatrist adopts expert-driven, directive stance, telling Brandon what to do rather than helping him discover his own reasons for change. Change talk is minimal and compliance-based rather than intrinsically motivated. Sustain talk is not skillfully managed. The session violates MI spirit of collaboration and client-centered care.

Most critically, this approach is unlikely to increase Brandon's intrinsic motivation for change. While Brandon agrees to try cutting back, this agreement appears to be compliance with psychiatrist's suggestions rather than genuine, self-motivated change. Research demonstrates compliance-based change is less likely to be sustained than intrinsically motivated change. Brandon's ambivalence is not explored or resolved; core concerns about social exclusion are not addressed; and his own ideas and resources are not solicited or valued.

**Recommendations for Improvement:**
- Fundamentally shift from expert to evocative stance
- Develop genuine empathic reflections capturing emotional nuance and ambivalence
- Explore and validate Brandon's ambivalence rather than trying to overcome it
- Strategically evoke and amplify change talk through follow-up questions
- Use double-sided reflections acknowledging both sustain talk and change talk
- Explore Brandon's social concerns rather than dismissing them
- Establish genuine partnership by inviting Brandon to co-create solutions
- Strengthen autonomy support by genuinely affirming his right to choose
- Assess readiness for change more thoroughly before action planning
- Recognize difference between compliance and genuine change motivation
