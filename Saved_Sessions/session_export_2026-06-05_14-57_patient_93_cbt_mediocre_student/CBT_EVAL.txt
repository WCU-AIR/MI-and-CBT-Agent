## DETAILED COMMENTS BY DOMAIN

### **Agenda-Setting (Score: 2/6)**
The therapist failed to establish a structured, collaborative agenda. Opening language was vague ("talk a bit about what's been on your mind lately, just kind of get a general sense of things"). No specific target problems were identified or prioritized. When patient presented multiple concerns (pain, fatigue, sleep disruption, hopelessness, motivation loss), therapist did not help prioritize or establish session focus. Identical vague opening repeated in second session suggests no systematic approach. Most critically, therapist did not review homework from prior session before assigning new tasks—a fundamental CBT structure element.

### **Feedback Elicitation (Score: 1/6)**
Feedback elicitation was essentially absent. Therapist did not systematically check understanding, explore patient reactions to interventions, or solicit input on session helpfulness. When patient expressed doubt about cognitive restructuring ("it's just really hard...I'm not sure if it would actually stick with me"), therapist did not explore this resistance. When patient expressed significant fear about movement ("I'm afraid that moving might make the pain worse...I'm scared it'll hurt more"), therapist did not check whether patient felt heard or whether fear was adequately addressed before assigning homework. No explicit feedback solicitation at session end ("What was helpful today?" "What wasn't useful?" "Do you feel we're on the right track?").

### **Understanding/Empathy (Score: 3/6)**
Therapist demonstrated basic empathy and surface-level understanding but missed critical clinical elements. Accurately reflected explicit concerns ("the pain and feeling stuck are really tough for you") and acknowledged fear about movement. However, missed: (1) depression severity and hopelessness not explored as primary treatment target; (2) cannabis use never mentioned or assessed despite case formulation indicating daily use; (3) social isolation and avoidance not addressed; (4) catastrophic beliefs identified but not deeply explored; (5) functional relationship between pain, avoidance, depression, and cannabis use not communicated. Therapist did not convey understanding of maintaining factors or how interventions target them.

### **Interpersonal Effectiveness (Score: 4/6)**
Therapist displayed adequate warmth, concern, and professionalism. Used welcoming language ("Hello! It's good to see you today"), validated experience ("That's understandable," "which is pretty common"), normalized struggle ("It's okay if it feels difficult at first"), and offered encouragement ("You're doing good by taking this step"). Tone was warm and supportive. However, lacked deep empathic attunement to patient's profound hopelessness and distress. Repeated identical opening suggests somewhat scripted, impersonal approach. Did not establish appropriate professional boundaries around case complexity or discuss realistic expectations. Most critically, did not assess suicide risk despite patient's expressed hopelessness ("It's like there's no hope," "I feel so down that I just want to give up").

### **Collaboration (Score: 2/6)**
Collaboration was minimal and one-directional. Therapist offered directives ("You should practice," "Maybe you could try") rather than collaborative problem-solving. Patient positioned as recipient of suggestions rather than active partner in treatment design. When patient expressed fear about movement, therapist did not pause to collaboratively problem-solve: "What would need to happen for you to feel safe? What would be a truly small first step?" No collaborative empiricism—when patient expressed belief "my body is broken," therapist offered alternative thought rather than inviting patient to examine evidence together. Therapist did not invite patient to identify goals, priorities, or co-create treatment plan.

### **Pacing and Efficient Use of Time (Score: 3/6)**
Pacing was inconsistent and inefficient. Session lacked clear structure, creating aimlessness despite staying on topic. Multiple interventions (cognitive restructuring, behavioral activation, thought-monitoring) covered without adequate depth on any single intervention. Cognitive restructuring exchange lasted only a few turns before moving to behavioral activation without resolving patient's skepticism. Therapist did not prioritize among patient's multiple concerns (pain, depression, cannabis use, sleep disruption, social isolation, hopelessness). Critical assessment areas not thoroughly explored (cannabis use pattern, suicide risk, previous attempts at activity, catastrophic beliefs about pain, social support). Failed to review prior homework, missing valuable assessment opportunity. Interventions felt rushed and superficial rather than strategically developed.

### **Guided Discovery (Score: 2/6)**
Guided discovery was largely absent. Therapist relied heavily on direct suggestion and cognitive substitution rather than Socratic questioning. When patient expressed "my body is just broken now," therapist did not ask: "What makes you think that? What evidence do you have? What evidence might suggest otherwise? What would it mean if your body could heal?" Instead, therapist offered direct alternative: "pain doesn't always mean permanent damage or that sometimes things can improve." When patient expressed fear about movement, therapist did not use Socratic questions to examine fear: "What specifically are you afraid will happen? How likely is that? What evidence do you have? What would happen if you tried and it didn't hurt as much as you feared?" Instead, therapist offered reassurance: "the pain might not be as bad as you expect." Questions asked ("What do you think?") were rhetorical, following suggestions, inviting agreement rather than genuine exploration.

### **Focusing on Key Cognitions/Behaviors (Score: 3/6)**
Therapist identified some relevant cognitions (catastrophizing: "it's never going to get better," "my body is broken," "I've destroyed my body," "there's no hope") and behaviors (avoidance of movement, inactivity). However, did not distinguish between surface thoughts and core beliefs. Did not explore core beliefs about disability and self-worth, pain-harm relationships, or control. Identified behavioral avoidance but did not make it primary focus or develop systematic exposure plan. Cannabis use—identified in case formulation as critical maintaining factor—was never mentioned or targeted. Social withdrawal and rumination not addressed. Most critically, did not articulate functional relationship between cognitions and behaviors or the avoidance cycle (pain → catastrophic thought → avoidance → depression → more avoidance → reinforcement of catastrophic thought) that maintains both pain and depression.

### **Strategy for Change (Score: 2/6)**
Strategy for change was vague and unfocused. Therapist never articulated explicit case conceptualization linking presenting problems to maintaining factors. No strategic sequencing of interventions—did not establish whether to address depression first, cannabis use first, behavioral activation first, or cognitive work first. No clear goals established ("Is goal pain reduction? Mood improvement? Increased activity? Reduced cannabis use? Improved sleep? Social re-engagement?"). No measurement plan for tracking progress. Strategy did not adequately address critical maintaining factors: cannabis use completely ignored; avoidance cycle identified but not targeted systematically; social isolation not addressed; hopelessness not addressed as primary treatment target. Implicit strategy (identify catastrophic thoughts → suggest alternatives → encourage activity → monitor thoughts) is generic CBT approach, not tailored to this patient's specific maintaining factors. Lacks coherence and strategic promise.

### **Application of CBT Techniques (Score: 3/6)**
CBT techniques were applied with significant flaws. **Cognitive Restructuring:** Therapist used cognitive substitution without evidence examination. Offered new thought ("This is tough, but it might get better") without helping patient examine evidence for/against original thought ("my body is broken"). When patient expressed doubt ("it's just really hard...I'm not sure if it would actually stick"), therapist did not explore or adjust approach. **Behavioral Activation:** Lacked systematic planning. Suggestion to do "gentle stretching or short walks" was vague, without specific schedule, duration, or frequency. Patient expressed significant fear ("I'm afraid moving might make pain worse"), but therapist did not develop structured exposure plan or behavioral experiment. Therapist offered reassurance ("pain might not be as bad as you expect") rather than systematic testing of fear. **Thought-Monitoring:** Suggested without structure, format, or clear rationale. Patient does not know what to record, how to record it, or how to use information. Overall skill level was below adequate—techniques showed basic familiarity but not skilled execution.

### **Homework (Score: 2/6)**
Critical deficiency: Therapist did not review prior homework from previous session. No assessment of whether patient completed stretching or thought-monitoring, no exploration of barriers, no troubleshooting of obstacles. New homework was vague and non-specific: "notice your thoughts about the pain and movement" (no format provided), "gentle stretching when you feel able" (no specific schedule, duration, or frequency). Conditional language ("when you feel able," "when you feel up to it") unlikely to result in completion. No written tools provided (no thought record, activity log, or structured format). Inadequate rationale—therapist did not explain why homework targets maintaining factors or how it connects to treatment goals. No explicit commitment check ("Do you think you can do this?") or problem-solving about barriers. No clear measurement plan for reviewing homework ("How will we know if you did it? What will we look for?"). Homework unlikely to be completed or to produce meaningful change.

## CLINICAL RECOMMENDATIONS

### **Immediate Actions (Before Next Session):**

1. **Conduct comprehensive assessment and develop explicit case conceptualization** (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
   - Assess pain severity, location, triggers, impact on functioning
   - Assess depression severity (PHQ-9), hopelessness, suicidality
   - Assess cannabis use pattern (frequency, quantity, triggers, perceived benefits, negative consequences)
   - Assess avoidance behaviors, social isolation, catastrophic beliefs
   - Develop written case conceptualization linking problems to maintaining factors

2. **Assess and address suicide risk** (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
   - Ask directly about suicidal thoughts, plans, means
   - Develop safety plan with warning signs, coping strategies, support contacts
   - Document assessment and plan in clinical record

### **Ongoing Supervision Focus:**

- Develop competence in guided discovery and Socratic questioning
- Learn systematic behavioral activation and exposure planning
- Master structured cognitive restructuring with evidence examination
- Develop skill in comprehensive assessment and case conceptualization
- Learn to establish collaborative agendas with prioritization
- Develop skill in specific, structured homework assignment and review

### **Training Recommendations:**

- Pursue additional training in CBT for chronic pain and somatic symptom disorder
- Attend workshops on behavioral activation and exposure therapy
- Read: "Cognitive Therapy of Chronic Pain" by Beck & Budd
- Work with supervisor experienced in CBT for chronic pain and complex cases
