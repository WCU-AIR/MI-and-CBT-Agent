# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance. Affirms patient's courage ("That shows a lot of courage and hope") and validates experience of ambivalence without criticism. Normalizes struggle by acknowledging complexity of smoking being "woven into daily routine" and tied to social connections. However, missed opportunities to deepen acceptance—could have more explicitly normalized difficulty with unmet deadlines as common experience. Minimal explicit affirmation of patient's intrinsic worth independent of smoking behavior or change efforts. Warmth and support are genuine but acceptance could be strengthened through more frequent, explicit affirmations of inherent worth.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Exceptional reflective listening and accurate perspective-taking throughout session. Early reflections capture both surface content and emotional nuance: "smoking has been a long-standing part of your life, almost woven into your daily routine, but now it's also becoming a source of worry..." Sophisticated, complex reflections deepen insight rather than simply parroting content. Captures emotional undertones—fear about health, anxiety about social separation, nervousness about conversations. Reflection about vulnerability demonstrates deep empathic attunement. Reflections are accurate, timely, and serve to deepen patient's understanding of own experience. This is exemplary reflective listening practice.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains appropriate strategic focus on tobacco cessation while allowing patient to guide specific direction. Opening question establishes collaborative direction-setting rather than imposing predetermined agenda. Skillfully guides conversation toward increasingly concrete action steps: from exploring what makes quitting "more doable," to identifying importance of support, to drawing on past success with exercise, to developing specific first step. Progressive clarification and movement toward change without losing sight of target behavior. However, missed opportunities to sharpen direction—could have explored "six months and a day" timeline more explicitly to examine whether realistic or self-imposed pressure. Limited discussion of specific smoking patterns (frequency, triggers, contexts) essential for concrete cessation plan.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong collaborative stance throughout most of session. Opening question invites patient to set agenda, establishing partnership from start. Frequent use of collaborative language: "we talked about," "you've got a clear sense," "you're drawing a parallel" positions patient as active agent rather than passive recipient. Asks genuine questions inviting patient's input rather than providing solutions: "What do you think would need to be different this time?" "How important would you say having this kind of support is?" However, collaboration could be strengthened by asking patient to co-construct solutions more actively: "What would that support look like from your perspective?" Subtle expert moves—asking about past successes and drawing parallels—slightly shift power toward clinician's interpretation rather than pure collaboration.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist consistently respects and supports patient's autonomy throughout session. No pressure, prescription, or directive language. Uses autonomy-supportive language: "you're willing to take that risk," "you've got a clear sense of what could help," "you're setting yourself up for success" emphasizing patient's agency and choice. Respects patient's pace by asking about preparation and self-care rather than demanding specific behavioral changes. Validates 6/10 confidence as "quite healthy" rather than pushing for higher confidence. However, autonomy support could be strengthened by more explicitly acknowledging patient's right to choose not to quit: "This is ultimately your decision, and I want to support whatever you choose." Could have explored whether motivation is intrinsic or externally imposed regarding social pattern changes.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong evocation skills demonstrated by drawing out patient's own motivations, values, and resources. Rather than telling patient why to quit, clinician asks questions eliciting own reasons: "What do you think would need to be different this time for quitting to feel more doable or meaningful?" Skillfully evokes past success with exercise, asking patient to reflect on what helped, then facilitates connection to current challenge. Uses 0-10 importance scale effectively to prompt patient to articulate how much support matters (8/10). However, evocation could be strengthened by more directly exploring intrinsic motivations: "What are your personal reasons for wanting to quit smoking? What matters most to you about this?" Focus remains primarily on support and social factors rather than deeper personal reasons (health, finances, self-image, values). Limited explicit exploration of patient's confidence in ability to quit or beliefs about capacity for change.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong partnership demonstrated through active fostering of power-sharing and positioning patient's contributions as central to process. Uses inclusive language ("we talked about," "you've got a clear sense," "you're drawing a parallel") conveying shared ownership. Consistently validates patient's ideas and insights: "That's a powerful insight," "That sounds like a really thoughtful and manageable plan," "You're setting yourself up for success." Demonstrates partnership by following patient's lead and building on patient's connections. However, partnership could be strengthened by more explicitly acknowledging patient's expertise about own life: "You know yourself better than anyone—what do you think would work best for you?" Occasionally makes interpretations or draws connections patient hasn't explicitly stated, subtly shifting power toward clinician's framework. Doesn't explicitly invite patient to shape session structure or process.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates some skill in evoking change talk but misses significant opportunities to strategically amplify and reinforce it. Patient provides several instances of change talk (desire, reasons, need, commitment) but psychiatrist's responses are primarily reflective rather than strategically amplifying. When patient says "I'd probably say it's about an 8" regarding importance of support, clinician reflects back but doesn't ask follow-up questions deepening this change talk: "What makes it an 8 rather than a 5?" When patient identifies past success with exercise and role of social support, clinician reflects insight but doesn't explicitly ask patient to elaborate on confidence or ability to replicate success. Doesn't strategically ask questions designed to evoke specific types of change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment). When patient expresses doubt, clinician validates but doesn't explicitly ask patient to articulate reasons for trying despite doubt, which would cultivate commitment talk.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates adequate but inconsistent skill in addressing sustain talk. Patient provides several instances of sustain talk: "It's part of my life. I started when I was young," "We all just do it as a little group," "I'm giving myself six months, six months and-a-day." When patient mentions smoking is "part of my life" and tied to social group, clinician reflects accurately but doesn't strategically reframe or explore further. Could have asked: "How do you feel about smoking being such a central part of your identity? Is that something you want to change?" or "What are other ways you could stay connected with them?" Doesn't address "six months and a day" timeline as potential barrier or ambivalence. However, clinician does demonstrate some skill by consistently redirecting toward patient's strengths and past successes, implicitly suggesting patient has overcome obstacles before. Uses double-sided reflection acknowledging both difficulty and patient's capacity to reflect on it.

### **Overall MI Competency Assessment**

**Current Session Score: 3.9/5 (Intermediate to Advanced)**

**Summary of Strengths:**
- Exceptional empathic attunement with consistently accurate, complex reflections facilitating self-discovery
- Strong collaborative stance with genuine questions and inclusive language positioning patient as active partner
- Consistent respect for autonomy with validation of patient's pace; no pressure or prescription
- Skillful use of past success to build self-efficacy and connect to current challenge
- Warm, supportive tone creating safety and encouraging patient to open up
- Progressive movement toward concrete action steps with clear direction while maintaining client-centeredness
- Effective use of scaling questions (0-10) to clarify priorities and confidence

**Summary of Areas for Improvement:**
- Insufficient strategic amplification of change talk; responses primarily reflective rather than deepening motivation
- Limited exploration of intrinsic motivations; focus remains on support and social factors rather than personal reasons for quitting
- Inadequate addressing of sustain talk; minimal strategic reframing or exploration of underlying needs
- Missed opportunities to explore self-efficacy; doesn't explicitly ask about confidence in ability to quit
- Limited exploration of specific smoking patterns (frequency, triggers, contexts) needed for concrete planning
- Subtle expert positioning; occasionally makes interpretations patient hasn't explicitly stated, shifting power toward clinician's framework

**Likelihood of Evoking Sustained Client Motivation:** Moderate to Good (strong relationship and initial engagement, but insufficient depth in change talk cultivation to maximize intrinsic motivation for sustained behavior change)
