# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates profound absence of unconditional positive regard throughout session. Repeatedly communicates judgment and disapproval ("You can't let excuses like work and kids get in the way"). Patient's reality and autonomy are consistently rejected. No affirmation of patient's strengths, willingness to try, or realistic self-assessment. Declares plan "non-negotiable" with "no exceptions," communicating that patient's perspective and concerns are irrelevant. Treats patient as someone to be corrected and forced into compliance rather than as a person of inherent worth struggling with real barriers.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Reflective listening is virtually absent. Patient's stated barriers (time constraints with work and children) are framed as "excuses" rather than understood. When patient expresses ambivalence ("I'm not sure if I can realistically fit in that many days"), psychiatrist responds with pressure rather than reflection. Vulnerable disclosure of fear of failure ("I'm worried I'll just get overwhelmed and give up") is met with ultimatums rather than understanding. Zero complex reflections, zero perspective-taking, and zero attempt to understand why patient struggles. Patient's emotional state shifts from discouraged to defensive to resistant—direct result of lack of empathic attunement.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** While psychiatrist maintains clear focus on behavior change (exercise and medication adherence), direction is imposed rather than collaborative. Unilaterally decides five days per week is target and rejects patient's counter-proposals (three days, then four days) without exploration. No progressive clarification through dialogue; instead, one-way prescription. Psychiatrist fails to adjust direction despite repeated patient feedback that plan feels unrealistic. Direction is entirely clinician-driven, not co-constructed. Demonstrates rigid, prescriptive approach inconsistent with MI principles of client-centered guidance.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Complete absence of genuine joint problem-solving and shared decision-making. Psychiatrist unilaterally sets goals without exploring patient's input. Dismisses all patient proposals ("Three days a week is not enough"; "Four days won't cut it"). Never asks "What would work for you?" or "How could we design a plan that fits your life?" Power imbalance explicitly asserted through language: "This isn't optional... No exceptions. This is non-negotiable." Patient treated as passive recipient of expert advice rather than active partner. When patient explicitly requests collaboration ("Can we try that instead?"), psychiatrist refuses both times and reasserts unilateral plan.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Relentless pressure, control, and minimization of choice throughout session. Controlling language pervasive: "You really need," "You have to," "You can't let," "You must," "It's five or nothing," "Non-negotiable." Patient's statement "I'm not ready to commit to five days" met with "There's no room for 'not ready' when it comes to your health"—direct negation of autonomy. Never affirms patient's right to choose different path or make own decisions. No language like "This is your decision" or "You get to choose what feels right for you." Frames plan as something patient must do, not something patient could choose to do. Systematically undermines autonomy through ultimatums and explicit removal of choice.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Almost no evocation present; psychiatrist tells rather than asks. Never elicits patient's own reasons for change, values, ideas, or resources. Absent are open-ended questions like "What matters most to you about your health?" or "What would success look like for you?" Questions asked are closed and directive ("Do you have a gym nearby? Just say yes or no"; "How many days a week can you commit? Just give me a number"). Patient's spontaneous change talk ("I'm definitely willing to do something"; "I could aim for maybe three days") is never amplified or reinforced; instead, it is dismissed or overridden with pressure. Patient's strengths and resources are never explored or built upon. No curiosity about what has worked in past or what internal motivation exists.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Complete absence of partnership; psychiatrist actively assumes and maintains expert role throughout. Opening statement immediately establishes psychiatrist as authority: "I'll be guiding you through what you should do to get better." Unilateral decision-making evident: "I'm going to write you a plan now." Patient's knowledge of own capacity dismissed when stating realistic constraints. Controlling language ("You must," "You have to," "No exceptions," "Non-negotiable") positions psychiatrist as authority and patient as someone to be controlled. When patient requests partnership ("Can we talk about something a bit more flexible?"), psychiatrist refuses. Never conveys that patient has valuable knowledge about own life, preferences, or constraints. Patient treated as passive recipient rather than active partner in creating change.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Complete failure to cultivate change talk; psychiatrist's approach actively suppresses it. Patient offers spontaneous change talk ("I guess I know I should be doing better"; "I'm definitely willing to do something"; "I could aim for maybe three days"; "I want to improve my health"), but psychiatrist never amplifies, explores, or reinforces any of it. Instead, psychiatrist overrides: "Three days a week is not enough"; "Four days won't cut it; it's five or nothing." Never asks questions designed to evoke more change talk ("What would be the benefits?"; "How confident are you?"; "What reasons do you have?"). Psychiatrist's controlling approach creates environment where patient becomes defensive and resistant rather than motivated. Reinforces sustain talk through language like "You can't" and "It's five or nothing."

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Complete failure to soften sustain talk; psychiatrist actively reinforces and amplifies it. Patient expresses sustain talk ("I've tried before but slip back"; "It's hard to stay on track"; "It feels overwhelming"; "I'm worried I'll get overwhelmed and give up"), but psychiatrist responds with confrontation and pressure rather than empathy and curiosity. When patient says "It's hard to stay on track with everything else going on," psychiatrist responds: "You can't let excuses like work and kids get in the way. It's about willpower"—creating defensiveness rather than softening resistance. When patient expresses fear of failure, psychiatrist responds with ultimatum ("Five or nothing") that directly triggers this fear. Never uses reframing or double-sided reflection. By end of session, patient's sustain talk has intensified: "This approach just makes me shut down." Psychiatrist has amplified rather than softened resistance.

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.1/5**

**Assessment:** This session represents a fundamental failure to implement Motivational Interviewing principles. The psychiatrist operates from an expert-driven, authoritarian, controlling stance that directly contradicts the MI spirit of partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation. Across all nine domains, the psychiatrist demonstrates minimal to no competence in MI practice.

**Key Findings:**
- Psychiatrist is **not practicing Motivational Interviewing**; instead practicing directive, confrontational counseling
- Approach is likely to **increase patient resistance** and **decrease motivation for change**
- Patient begins with ambivalence and willingness; ends with active resistance and explicit statement that psychiatrist's approach makes them "shut down"
- Psychiatrist has created exact conditions that predict treatment failure
- Patient is unlikely to engage in sustained behavior change without fundamental shift in clinician approach

**Critical Recommendations:**
1. Adopt collaborative stance from outset; position patient as expert on own life
2. Implement reflective listening to demonstrate empathic understanding
3. Explore barriers with curiosity rather than dismissal
4. Amplify and reinforce all instances of change talk
5. Collaborate on goal-setting rather than imposing goals
6. Respect autonomy by offering choice and acknowledging patient's right to decide
7. Soften sustain talk through reframing and double-sided reflection
8. Replace closed, directive questions with open-ended exploratory questions
9. Actively acknowledge and build on patient strengths
10. Receive and respond to patient feedback about approach effectiveness

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation:** **Very Low.** This session is likely to decrease patient's intrinsic motivation for change. Without fundamental shift in approach, patient is unlikely to engage in sustained behavior change and will likely experience another cycle of failure, further entrenching belief that change is impossible.
