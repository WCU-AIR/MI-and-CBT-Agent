# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance with genuine affirmations ("That's a really understandable place to be" and "It's impressive that you've noticed these triggers"). Smoking is reframed as a coping mechanism rather than criticized. However, acceptance is slightly compromised when shifting to advice-giving mode, positioning the psychiatrist as the source of solutions rather than maintaining pure acceptance. Recovery is attempted through collaborative framing, but the initial expert stance moderately undermines the acceptance foundation.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Solid reflective listening with accurate two-sided reflections capturing ambivalence ("You really value feeling good physically and socially...At the same time, smoking serves as a way to unwind"). The psychiatrist demonstrates good perspective-taking and validates Hana's experience. However, empathy occasionally remains at surface level rather than exploring emotional nuance—for example, when Hana expresses uncertainty about replacing smoking, the psychiatrist could have reflected the underlying anxiety before moving to solutions. Reflections are competent but miss some opportunities for deeper emotional attunement.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains clear, appropriate focus on smoking cessation and its connection to dental health throughout the session. Direction is established early and maintained through strategic questioning that progressively clarifies Hana's relationship to change (importance scale, past attempts, triggers, action planning). However, direction could be sharper by strategically leveraging Hana's stated values (feeling good, looking presentable, having energy) as explicit pathways to the change goal. The psychiatrist also doesn't fully explore what would move her importance rating higher, representing a missed strategic opportunity.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Collaboration is present but inconsistent. Early questioning demonstrates collaborative stance ("What would you like to focus on?"). However, a significant deficit emerges when the psychiatrist shifts to providing a list of strategies (walking, deep breathing, chewing gum, finding non-smoking friends) without first asking what Hana has already considered. This represents a shift from co-construction to modified expert model. While the psychiatrist attempts recovery by asking "What stands out to you?", true collaboration would have positioned Hana as the primary source of solutions. The overall arc shows collaboration being compromised rather than consistently maintained.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Autonomy support is present but inconsistently emphasized. The psychiatrist respects Hana's choice and accepts her ambivalence (4-5 importance rating without pushing). However, autonomy support weakens significantly during advice-giving, where framing like "Some people find it helpful to..." subtly positions these as recommended strategies. More critically, the psychiatrist doesn't explicitly reinforce Hana's ownership of the change process. Stronger autonomy support would include explicit statements like "This is entirely your decision" or "You get to choose what feels right for you." The action planning is autonomy-supportive, but explicit messaging about client ownership could be substantially strengthened.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Evocation is inconsistently executed. The psychiatrist does evoke some client-generated change talk through good questions ("What do you value most about your health?"). However, evocation is significantly undermined when Hana expresses uncertainty about replacing smoking—the psychiatrist could have asked "What do you think might help?" but instead provides a list of strategies. Critical opportunities are missed: when Hana rates importance as 4-5, the psychiatrist doesn't ask "What would move that number up?"; when she mentions liking being outside, this strong change talk isn't explored further. The psychiatrist minimally cultivates or strategically amplifies existing change talk, representing a significant MI deficit in this domain.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Partnership is compromised by the psychiatrist's shift into expert-advisor mode. Early questioning honors Hana's wisdom ("What would you like to focus on?"), but partnership deteriorates when the psychiatrist seeks permission to share strategies and then unilaterally provides them. True partnership would have positioned Hana's insights as the foundation ("You've identified that you need alternatives to relax and that your social environment matters—what ideas have you had?"). While attempts are made to restore collaboration, the fundamental message shifts from "your wisdom guides us" to "I have expertise to offer." The final action planning returns to partnership, but the overall arc shows partnership being compromised rather than consistently maintained throughout.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** This is a significant weakness. While some change talk is present (desire: "I want to feel good"; ability: "going for a quick walk sounds doable"), the psychiatrist minimally cultivates or strategically amplifies it. Critical opportunities are missed: Hana's stated values (feeling good, looking presentable, confidence when smiling) are never explicitly connected to smoking cessation; when she expresses ability, this isn't explored further; when she rates importance as 4-5, the psychiatrist doesn't ask what's behind that rating. The psychiatrist accepts change talk but doesn't actively strengthen it through follow-up questions. By the end, Hana has committed to trying walking, but the psychiatrist hasn't built a strong foundation of desire, ability, reasons, and need talk that would sustain motivation. This represents a major MI deficit.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist skillfully handles sustain talk without confrontation or argumentation. Hana's sustain-talk statements (minimizing harm, status quo preference, past failure, environmental barriers, uncertainty about alternatives) are met with double-sided reflections and strategic reframing rather than confrontation. The psychiatrist normalizes her experience ("That's a very common experience") and reframes past attempts as evidence of capability rather than failure. Sustain talk is gently redirected without arguing, demonstrating solid MI technique. The one area for minor improvement: when Hana says change isn't "urgent yet," the psychiatrist could have explored "What would make it feel more urgent?" However, overall sustain talk management is skillfully executed.

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall Score: 3.4/5**

### Key Strengths
- Consistent acceptance and warm, nonjudgmental tone throughout
- Solid reflective listening with accurate two-sided reflections
- Skillful handling of sustain talk without confrontation
- Clear focus on target behavior (smoking cessation)
- Permission-seeking approach showing respect for autonomy
- Collaborative action planning with client-chosen first step

### Key Areas for Improvement
- **Insufficient change talk cultivation** – Misses opportunities to evoke and amplify Hana's own reasons for change; fails to link stated values to smoking cessation
- **Premature shift to advice-giving** – Moves to providing strategies before fully exploring Hana's own ideas, undermining evocation and partnership
- **Weak autonomy support messaging** – Doesn't explicitly reinforce that change is Hana's choice
- **Missed strategic opportunities** – Doesn't explore what would move importance rating up; doesn't ask what she's already considered
- **Limited partnership in problem-solving** – Shifts from collaborative exploration to expert-advisor mode
- **Insufficient amplification of change talk** – When Hana expresses ability or desire, these aren't explored further to strengthen motivation

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change
Hana remains engaged and cooperative, which is positive, but the psychiatrist has not built a robust motivational foundation for sustained change. The session moves Hana from contemplation toward preparation, but the motivational architecture is fragile. Without deeper exploration of her own reasons for change or explicit connection between her values and quitting smoking, Hana may revert to smoking when encountering barriers (stress, social pressure, cravings). The shift toward advice-giving, while well-intentioned, may actually reduce the likelihood of sustained behavior change by positioning the psychiatrist as the driver of change rather than Hana herself.
