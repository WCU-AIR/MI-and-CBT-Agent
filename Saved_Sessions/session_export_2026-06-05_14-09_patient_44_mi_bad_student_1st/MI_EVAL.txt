### Acceptance

Psychiatrist demonstrates explicit judgment and disapproval throughout ("Well, average isn't good enough," "That's not the attitude you should have," "You're really not taking this seriously enough"). No affirmation of client's strengths (physical activity, reasonable eating attempts, willingness to attend). Pathologizes client's autonomy and hesitation rather than acknowledging understandable ambivalence. ### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1

### Direction

Direction is maintained through authoritarian prescription and repeated commands rather than through skillful evocation and strategic questioning that helps client discover own reasons for change. Focus is on imposing specific behavioral targets (quit smoking this week, cut down drinking, eat vegetables daily) rather than exploring client's ambivalence, values, and readiness. Does not progressively clarify client's direction or readiness; instead imposes predetermined direction and demands compliance. While focus is clear, the method violates MI principles and actively undermines client engagement.

### Autonomy Support

Uses language minimizing choice: "You really should quit smoking immediately," "You must eat fruits and vegetables every day," "You have to quit smoking this week—no ifs, ands, or buts," "This lifestyle change is non-negotiable." Most critically, when client explicitly states "I don't like being told what I *have* to do without being part of the decision... I need to feel like it's my choice," psychiatrist's response demonstrates complete failure to honor this feedback. Rather than adjusting to support autonomy, psychiatrist repeats same controlling language: "You have to quit smoking this week—no ifs, ands, or buts." No acknowledgment of client's freedom to choose, no emphasis on client's ownership of change process, no exploration of what client wants for his health.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no evocation—the core of MI. Psychiatrist does not ask questions designed to elicit client's own reasons for change, values related to health, concerns about smoking, or ideas about what might help him change. Instead, psychiatrist imposes reasons for change: "It's not just about feeling fine now; it's about preventing serious diseases later" and "If you don't quit, you'll end up with serious lung problems or worse." These are psychiatrist's reasons, not client's. Provides external motivation (fear of future illness) rather than helping client discover intrinsic motivation. Asks only closed yes/no questions that demand factual answers; these do not evoke anything. Client does offer spontaneous change talk ("Yeah, I've been to the doctor about maybe quitting…" and "Maybe if we talked about what would make quitting doable for me, I could think about it"), but psychiatrist does not recognize, amplify, or build on these statements. Instead, psychiatrist continues with directives and threats.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Demonstrates opposite of partnership. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role for entire interaction and explicitly rejects notion that client has any relevant wisdom about his own change process. Opening statement establishes psychiatrist as sole expert ("I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to improve"). Subsequent statements reinforce this: "You really should quit smoking immediately," "You must eat fruits and vegetables every day," "You have to quit smoking this week—no ifs, ands, or buts," "I'm setting this plan for you, and you need to follow it exactly." Does not acknowledge that client knows his own life, barriers, motivations, or capacity for change. When client explains his perspective ("My job keeps me moving all day—I'm pretty active, so I feel fine"), psychiatrist treats this as faulty reasoning to be corrected rather than valuable information about client's experience. When client explicitly invites partnership ("Maybe if we talked about what would make quitting doable for me, I could think about it"), psychiatrist does not foster collaboration but reasserts expert authority. Phrase "Understood?" at end of final directive is demand for compliance, not genuine question seeking client's input or agreement.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** No strategic cultivation of change talk; psychiatrist's approach actively suppresses it by creating defensiveness and resistance. Client does offer spontaneous change talk ("Yeah, I've been to the doctor about maybe quitting…" and "Maybe if we talked about what would make quitting doable for me, I could think about it"), which are openings where client expresses consideration of change. Skilled MI practitioner would recognize these as change talk and amplify them with reflections and follow-up questions. Instead, psychiatrist does not acknowledge or build on these statements and continues with same directive approach, causing client to retreat into sustain talk and defensiveness. No attempt to evoke client's desire for change ("What would be good about quitting?"), ability to change ("What have you successfully changed in your life before?"), reasons for change ("How might quitting fit with your values?"), need for change ("What concerns you most about your health?"), or commitment to change ("What would it take for you to be ready?"). Psychiatrist's repeated demands ("You have to quit smoking this week") actually strengthen client's sustain talk and resistance, as evidenced by increasingly firm statements: "I'm not ready to just quit this week," "I'm not ready to follow orders," "I'm not feeling sick or anything yet."

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Demonstrates opposite of softening sustain talk. Psychiatrist directly confronts client's sustain talk with counter-arguments, which predictably strengthens it. Client's primary sustain talk is: "I feel fine," "My work is really manual labor—keeps me strong," "I'm not ready to just quit this week." Skilled MI practitioner would not argue against these statements but would explore them, reflect them, and gently introduce discrepancy. Instead, psychiatrist uses direct confrontation repeatedly: "Well, average isn't good enough," "That's not the attitude you should have," "You're really not taking this seriously enough," "You have to take this seriously." Each statement directly challenges client's perspective, causing him to defend it more firmly. By session end, client's sustain talk has intensified: "I'm not ready to follow orders—I need to feel like it's my choice" and "I'm not feeling sick or anything yet." Psychiatrist's confrontational approach has strengthened rather than softened client's resistance to change. No use of double-sided reflections, reframing, or strategic responses that minimize sustain talk.

## Summary Assessment

**Critical Findings:**

This session represents a **fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles** across all nine competency domains. The psychiatrist employs an authoritarian, expert-driven, confrontational approach that is explicitly contraindicated in evidence-based practice for behavior change.

**Most Critical Deficiency:**

The complete absence of collaboration and partnership. The psychiatrist opens with "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to improve" and maintains this stance throughout, explicitly rejecting the client's invitation to partner ("Maybe if we talked about what would make quitting doable for me, I could think about it"). This violates the fundamental MI principle that clients are experts on their own lives and change processes.

**Impact on Client Engagement:**

This session is **actively harmful** to the client's likelihood of sustainable change. The confrontational, authoritarian approach predictably increases resistance and defensiveness. Rather than evoking intrinsic motivation, the psychiatrist imposes external pressure, which typically results in either unstable compliance or resistance (as demonstrated here). By session end, the client has explicitly rejected the psychiatrist's approach and dug in on sustain talk.

**Recommendation:**

The psychiatrist must receive formal Motivational Interviewing training and ongoing clinical supervision. The current approach should not be repeated without significant intervention and retraining. This session demonstrates harm to the therapeutic alliance and decreased likelihood of sustainable behavior change.
