
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

4. Can the patient apply learnings independently?

Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS)
This document outlines a structured framework for assessing therapist competence in Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT). 
It is used for training, supervision, and evaluation. Each item is scored on a 0–6 scale:

0 = Poor
2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

2    Therapist set agenda that was vague or incomplete.
0    Therapist did not set agenda.

## Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Feedback: Therapist Evaluation

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made no attempt to establish a collaborative agenda at any point during the session. A proper agenda would have involved: (1) identifying specific, measurable treatment targets (e.g., reducing self-injurious behavior, managing anxiety around schedule changes, improving sleep); (2) prioritizing which problems to address first; (3) allocating time appropriately; and (4) establishing clear session goals that both therapist and patient understand and agree upon.

Instead, the therapist opened with generic pleasantries ("Hey there! How's it going today?") and allowed the session to drift without structure. The patient spontaneously mentioned several critical issues—self-injury (biting wrist, pinching), anxiety around schedule changes, sleep difficulties, and emotional dysregulation—but the therapist never explicitly organized these into a coherent treatment plan or asked Ashley which issue she wanted to prioritize. The session became a series of disconnected exchanges rather than a focused therapeutic intervention.

For a patient with Ashley's cognitive and communication limitations (Full Scale IQ 31, verbal IQ 23), an explicit, concrete agenda is not optional—it is essential. Ashley needs clear structure, predictability, and explicit guidance about what will happen in session. The absence of agenda-setting represents a fundamental failure to adapt CBT to this patient's neurodevelopmental profile.

**Summary:**

No agenda was established. The therapist did not identify, prioritize, or structure treatment targets. This represents a critical failure in basic CBT structure, particularly problematic given Ashley's need for explicit organization and predictability.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made minimal, inadequate attempts to elicit or respond to feedback throughout the session. True feedback elicitation in CBT involves: (1) regularly checking whether the patient understands the therapist's reasoning; (2) asking whether interventions are helpful; (3) soliciting reactions to the session; (4) adjusting approach based on patient response; and (5) summarizing key points and asking for patient input.

The therapist asked only two questions that remotely resembled feedback-seeking: "Have you tried that before?" and "What do you think?" Both were superficial and followed lengthy therapist monologues about personal anecdotes. The therapist never asked: "Is this helpful?" "Do you understand what I'm suggesting?" "How are you feeling about our conversation?" "What would be most useful for you to work on?" or "Did today's session help you?"

More critically, when Ashley provided clear feedback that contradicted the therapist's approach—"I don't really feel like things will just get better by thinking," "New things make me upset sometimes," "Smiling is hard for me"—the therapist did not adjust. Instead, the therapist repeated the same ineffective strategy (positive thinking, trying new things, behavioral activation) without acknowledging Ashley's explicit statement that this approach was not resonating.

At the end of the session, the therapist provided no structured feedback elicitation: no summary of what was discussed, no check for understanding, no solicitation of Ashley's thoughts about the session's usefulness, and no collaborative planning for next steps.

**Summary:**

Feedback was minimally elicited and largely ignored when provided. The therapist did not check understanding, adjust based on patient response, or summarize key points. This represents a significant gap in collaborative practice.

### 3. Understanding
