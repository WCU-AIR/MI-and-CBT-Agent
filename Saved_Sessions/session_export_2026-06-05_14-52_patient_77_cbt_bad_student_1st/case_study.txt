--- DISORDER ---
Pica
--- CASE STUDY ---

Thomas, an 8-year-old boy with mild to moderate intellectual developmental disorder, was brought to the emergency room by his parents due to worsening abdominal pain over the prior 24 hours. He had developed constipation, with only one bowel movement in the past week, and had vomited earlier in the day.

Teachers at his special education classroom reported difficulties since transferring from a similar school 4 months earlier. Thomas often appeared distressed, rocking, crying, and clutching his stomach. One week prior, a pediatrician diagnosed an acute exacerbation of chronic constipation; over-the-counter laxatives were ineffective. Thomas also began complaining of nighttime pain, resulting in diminished interest in hobbies such as video games and sports, instead spending time alone playing with inherited army men figurines. When not complaining, he ate well and maintained growth at the 40th percentile for height and weight.

Past medical history included constipation, stomachaches, and intermittent headaches, worsening after a family move from semirural Florida to an urban apartment. He shared a room with his 6-year-old biological brother, whom he described as his "best friend." Thomas was adopted at birth; little is known about his biological parents.

In the ER, Thomas was well-groomed, sitting on his mother's lap, crying, irritable, and refusing to speak to the examiner, repeating that his stomach hurt. Vital signs were stable, and he was afebrile. Physical examination revealed generalized abdominal tenderness, but was limited due to his crying.

Abdominal X-ray showed multiple small metallic particles, suspected to be lead paint flakes, and three 2-cm-long metallic objects in the stomach. Blood lead level was 20 μg/dL (normal <5 μg/dL). Endoscopy removed the three toy soldiers from his stomach.

Discussion: Thomas is a child with intellectual developmental disorder presenting with abdominal pain, constipation, irritability, and behavioral changes following a move. Imaging revealed ingestion of nonfood objects, consistent with pica. Pica is often comorbid with intellectual developmental disorder and can be dangerous, particularly with ingestion of lead-containing objects. Psychological stress from the move may also contribute to mood and behavioral changes.
