
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

No agenda was established at any point during this session. The therapist opened with a generic, unstructured greeting ("Hey there! How's it going today?") without collaboratively identifying session goals, target problems, or time allocation. Across both session segments presented, there is no evidence that the therapist and patient discussed what they would work on, prioritized among competing concerns, or established measurable objectives for the session.

The patient spontaneously introduced his primary concern (moving stress, perfectionism, difficulty relaxing), but the therapist never formalized this into a structured agenda. A proper CBT agenda would have included: (1) identifying that OCPD-related perfectionism and control-seeking are the core issues, (2) distinguishing between surface complaints (moving logistics) and underlying maladaptive patterns (rigid perfectionism, anxiety avoidance through hyperproductivity), (3) setting specific session goals (e.g., "Today we'll explore how your need for control affects your relationship and identify one behavioral experiment"), and (4) time management to ensure adequate coverage of key material.

The absence of agenda-setting is particularly problematic in this case because the patient presents with a personality disorder characterized by rigidity and control-seeking. A structured agenda would model flexibility, collaborative decision-making, and prioritization—all therapeutic targets for OCPD treatment. Instead, the session drifted aimlessly, with the therapist responding reactively to whatever the patient mentioned rather than guiding the work strategically.

**Summary:**

No agenda was established. The therapist failed to collaboratively identify session goals, prioritize problems, or structure the session around measurable objectives—a fundamental CBT requirement.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Summary:**

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated surface-level understanding of the patient's explicit statements but missed critical nuances and the underlying clinical picture. The therapist accurately reflected that the patient feels stressed about moving, struggles to relax, and worries about things being imperfect. However, the therapist fundamentally failed to grasp the patient's "internal reality"—the maladaptive cognitive and behavioral patterns characteristic of OCPD.

Specific failures in understanding:

1. **Missed the core pathology**: The patient explicitly stated, "If something's even a little off, I get this feeling that something bad will happen" and "If I don't control it, something important will fall apart." These are not normal moving stress—they reflect catastrophic thinking and control-seeking compulsions. The therapist normalized this ("Everyone goes through stuff like this, no big deal") rather than recognizing it as maladaptive.

2. **Misidentified the problem**: The therapist treated this as a stress-management issue requiring relaxation and positive thinking. In reality, the patient's anxiety is *maintained* by avoidance of discomfort (refusing to relax without "productivity") and safety behaviors (excessive control and organization). Standard reassurance and relaxation suggestions will likely reinforce these maladaptive patterns.

3. **Missed the functional impairment**: The patient stated, "Watching TV or just relaxing without a purpose is hard. It almost makes me anxious." This indicates that the patient's difficulty relaxing is not laziness or stress—it's anxiety avoidance. The therapist never explored this or recognized it as a treatment target.

4. **Failed to recognize relationship impact**: The patient mentioned Peter multiple times and indicated that his controlling behavior is causing relationship conflict (per the case formulation). The therapist never asked about the relationship, Peter's perspective, or how the patient's perfectionism affects his partner.

5. **Missed the work-life imbalance**: The patient stated he reads "legal journals or articles" to relax and feels anxious without productivity. This reflects the DSM-5 OCPD criterion of "excessive devotion to work and productivity at expense of leisure and relationships." The therapist treated this as a positive coping strategy rather than a symptom.

The therapist's repeated personal anecdotes ("When I was moving...," "When I was in college...") suggest the therapist was relating to the patient's experience as normal stress rather than recognizing the pathological nature of the presentation. Empathy requires understanding not just what the patient said, but *why* they're struggling and what maintains the problem.

**Summary:**

The therapist grasped explicit content but missed the underlying OCPD pathology, catastrophic thinking, anxiety avoidance, and relationship impact. Understanding remained superficial and normalized maladaptive patterns.

- **Action:** When the patient expresses catastrophic thoughts ("If something's even a little off, something bad will happen"), use cognitive restructuring. Ask: (1) "What evidence do you have that this will happen?" (2) "What evidence is there against this?" (3) "What's the most realistic outcome?" (4) "Even if something did go wrong, how would you cope?" Help the patient develop a more balanced thought.
- **Example:** Patient: "If I don't pack everything perfectly, something important will be lost." Therapist: "That's an important thought. Let's examine it. Has something important actually been lost when you didn't control packing? What's the evidence for and against this thought? What's a more balanced way to think about it?"

- **Action:** Collaboratively design behavioral experiments that test the patient's beliefs about control. For example: "Let's test your belief that something important will be lost if you don't control packing. This week, let Peter pack one box without your oversight. We'll observe what actually happens. - **Example:** "I'd like to suggest an experiment. This week, let Peter organize one closet or drawer without your input. Before you do it, predict what will happen. Then observe what actually happens. We'll discuss the results next session."
