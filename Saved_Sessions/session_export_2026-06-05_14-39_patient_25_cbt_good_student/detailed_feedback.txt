
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

This document outlines a structured framework for assessing therapist competence in Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT). 
It is used for training, supervision, and evaluation. Each item is scored on a 0–6 scale:

0 = Poor
2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Conduct Comprehensive Assessment of Suicidal Ideation and Depression Severity (URGENT)**

- **Action:** At the next session, explicitly assess for suicidal ideation, intent, and plan. Use a structured suicide risk assessment tool (e.g., Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale). Assess the full range of depressive symptoms (anhedonia, hopelessness, concentration, sleep, appetite, guilt, worthlessness).
- **Example:** "You mentioned earlier that you have thoughts of death. Can you tell me more about what those thoughts are like? Do you ever think about harming yourself? If so, how often and how serious are those thoughts?"
- **Practice:** Review suicide risk assessment protocols and practice asking directly about suicidal ideation. Role-play with a supervisor to build comfort with this essential clinical skill.

### 2. **Develop Explicit Comorbidity Treatment Plan**

- **Action:** Collaboratively establish which disorder will be the primary focus and why. Discuss how depression and eating disorder symptoms interact. Create a treatment timeline that addresses both conditions sequentially or in parallel.
- **Example:** "I notice you're struggling with both low mood and the binge-purge cycle. These often go together—when depression is worse, eating disorder symptoms often get worse too. I'd like to suggest we focus first on improving your mood and energy through some behavioral changes, which often helps with the eating disorder as well. Does that make sense to you?"
- **Practice:** Study the literature on comorbid depression and eating disorders. Develop case formulations that explicitly link the two disorders. Practice explaining this to patients in accessible language.

### 3. **Implement Behavioral Monitoring for Eating Disorder**

- **Action:** Assign a food and mood diary as homework. Have the patient record all food intake, binge-purge episodes, mood before and after, and triggering thoughts/feelings. Review this at each session to identify patterns.
- **Example:** "I'd like you to keep a record of what you eat, when you eat it, and how you're feeling before and after. If you have a binge or purge episode, note what happened right before—what were you thinking or feeling? This will help us understand the patterns better."
- **Practice:** Learn to use behavioral monitoring data to identify high-risk situations, triggers, and maintaining factors. Practice analyzing patterns with a supervisor.

### 4. **Introduce Behavioral Interventions for Bulimia Nervosa**

- **Action:** Establish regular eating patterns (e.g., three meals plus snacks at set times). Implement stimulus control (removing binge foods from the home, eating in designated areas). Plan exposure to feared foods in a structured way.
- **Example:** "One thing that helps with binge eating is having regular meals and snacks throughout the day, so your body isn't in a state of deprivation. Let's work together to plan a regular eating schedule that feels manageable for you. We can start with breakfast, which you mentioned skipping."
- **Practice:** Study evidence-based CBT protocols for bulimia nervosa (e.g., Fairburn's CBT-E). Practice designing behavioral experiments and exposure hierarchies. Role-play implementing these with patients.

### 5. **Implement Behavioral Activation for Depression**

- **Action:** Assess the patient's current activity level and valued activities. Collaboratively develop a behavioral activation plan that gradually increases engagement in activities that provide pleasure or meaning. Schedule specific activities for each day.
- **Example:** "I notice that after the binge-purge cycle, you withdraw and feel more sad. One thing that can help with depression is doing activities that give you a sense of accomplishment or joy, even small ones. What are some activities you used to enjoy or that you think might help you feel better?"
- **Practice:** Learn the behavioral activation model and how to assess anhedonia and avoidance. Practice designing graded activity schedules. Study how behavioral activation can interrupt the depression-eating disorder cycle.

### 6. **Deepen Cognitive Work on Core Beliefs**

- **Action:** Move beyond the immediate thought ("I don't deserve to treat myself") to explore core beliefs about body image, perfectionism, control, and self-worth. Use the downward arrow technique to identify underlying beliefs.
- **Example:** "When you think 'I don't deserve to treat myself,' what does that say about you as a person? What would it mean if you did treat yourself kindly?" [Listen for core beliefs like "I'm unworthy," "I must be perfect," "My body determines my value."]
- **Practice:** Study core belief work in CBT. Practice using the downward arrow technique. Develop belief change experiments that test core beliefs through behavioral evidence.

### 7. **Use Formal Thought Records**

- **Action:** Introduce a structured thought record worksheet that includes: situation, automatic thought, evidence for/against, alternative thought, and outcome. Have the patient practice this in session and assign it as homework.
- **Example:** Provide a written thought record template. Walk through a recent binge-purge episode together, filling in each column. Assign the patient to complete one thought record daily for the next week.
- **Practice:** Create multiple thought record templates tailored to different situations (eating-related, mood-related). Practice completing them yourself to model the process. Role-play with a supervisor.

### 8. **Systematically Check Understanding Throughout Sessions**

- **Action:** After introducing any new concept, technique, or intervention, explicitly check for understanding. Ask the patient to summarize what they heard or explain how they'll apply it.
- **Example:** After explaining the relationship between depression and eating disorder: "I've shared a lot here. Can you tell me back what you understood about how your mood and eating are connected?"
- **Practice:** Develop a habit of pausing after each major point to check understanding. Practice asking open-ended questions that require the patient to demonstrate comprehension.

### 9. **Explore Ambivalence About Change**

- **Action:** When the patient reports low confidence (5/10) or hesitation, explicitly explore this ambivalence. Use motivational interviewing techniques to understand barriers and build intrinsic motivation.
- **Example:** "You mentioned you're about 50% confident about trying the pause-and-breathe practice. Tell me about the 50% that's hesitant. What concerns do you have?"
- **Practice:** Study motivational interviewing principles. Practice exploring ambivalence without judgment. Learn to elicit change talk and strengthen commitment to change.

### 10. **Establish Clear Session-to-Session Continuity**

- **Action:** At the end of each session, explicitly plan what will be reviewed at the next session. Ask the patient to bring the homework (food diary, thought records) and plan to review it together.
- **Example:** "Next time we meet, I'd like to review your food and mood diary to see what patterns we can identify. I'd also like to hear how the pause-and-breathe practice went. Please bring your diary with you."
- **Practice:** Develop a system for tracking homework completion and session-to-session progress. Practice reviewing homework in a way that reinforces learning and identifies barriers.
