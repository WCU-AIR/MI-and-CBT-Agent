# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance. Aizada's legitimate health concerns are repeatedly labeled as "excuses," "stubbornness," and "wasting time." There is no affirmation of her strengths, no warmth, and no genuine support. Acceptance is entirely conditional on compliance with the prescribed exercise regimen. When Aizada expresses fear about exercise exacerbating pain, the response is pressure rather than understanding. The authoritarian tone and dismissive language create an environment of disapproval rather than unconditional positive regard, fundamentally violating MI principles.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal empathy with zero reflective listening statements throughout the entire session. When Aizada articulates her cancer fear, it is dismissed rather than explored. When she expresses fear about exercise worsening pain, the response is pressure rather than curiosity. The psychiatrist fails to capture emotional nuance—reducing Aizada's complex experience of fear, pain, exhaustion, and feeling unheard to simple "stubbornness." Most critically, when Aizada directly states "it feels like you're not really listening to how bad it hurts sometimes," the psychiatrist does not pause, reflect, or demonstrate understanding. Instead, the response is to repeat the same directive pressure. This represents a fundamental failure to understand the client's perspective.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a clear focus on exercise and diet as primary targets for change, demonstrating some organizational clarity. However, the execution is problematic and inconsistent with MI principles. Direction is rigid and imposed rather than collaboratively developed. The target behavior (daily exercise) is prescribed without exploring Aizada's goals, values, or readiness. Additionally, the focus may be strategically misaligned with the client's actual stage of change and primary concern. Aizada's presenting problem is health anxiety and somatic symptoms; her primary barrier is fear of serious illness. The psychiatrist focuses on behavioral change without addressing the underlying ambivalence about whether symptoms are psychosomatic or organic. Consistency is achieved through repetition and pressure rather than skillful exploration.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates an entirely authoritarian, expert-driven stance with no genuine collaboration. The opening statement explicitly positions the psychiatrist as sole authority: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to get better." Repeated use of directive language ("You really should," "You must," "You have to") reflects commands, not invitations for joint problem-solving. When Aizada offers a compromise ("Maybe I could try a little walking when the pain is less"), it is rejected: "Just a little walking won't cut it." There is no solicitation of her input on solutions, no shared decision-making, and explicit rejection of her contributions. The repeated question "Will you commit to exercising daily starting tomorrow?" is a demand for compliance framed as a question, not a collaborative inquiry.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist actively undermines autonomy throughout the session. Repeated use of "You have to," "You must," and "You really should" minimizes perceived choice. The psychiatrist frames the choice as binary and non-negotiable: either comply with the prescribed plan or face health decline ("If you don't start exercising now, your health will decline for sure"). This is a threat designed to coerce compliance, not support autonomy. Aizada's stated preference for a gradual approach is dismissed as insufficient, sending the message that her autonomy—her right to choose a pace that feels safe—is not respected. There is no affirmation of her right to make her own decisions, no acknowledgment that she is the expert on her own body, and no support for her self-efficacy in determining what is manageable.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no evocation; the approach is entirely prescriptive. The psychiatrist tells Aizada what she needs to do rather than asking about her own reasons for change, values, or ideas about what might help. There is no exploration of her motivation, values, ideas, or resources. When Aizada offers a small piece of change talk ("Maybe I could try a little walking when the pain is less"), it is not amplified or explored; instead, it is dismissed as insufficient. The psychiatrist also fails to evoke Aizada's own observations about her condition—that symptoms fluctuate with stress—which are valuable change talk seeds that could be explored and amplified. The approach is entirely directive, with no effort to draw out the client's own motivations or resources.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist actively assumes the expert role for the entire interaction, explicitly stating: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to get better." The psychiatrist positions herself as the sole source of knowledge and authority. Throughout the session, contributions are directives, not collaborative explorations. When Aizada offers input—her fear about exercise, her preference for gentle movement, her need for reassurance about serious illness—these contributions are dismissed or overridden rather than incorporated. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge Aizada's expertise about her own body. There is no moment where the psychiatrist says "You know your body better than I do" or "What have you learned about what helps?" These would be partnership statements. Instead, unilateral authority is maintained throughout, with no power-sharing and no substantial influence of client input on session direction.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Aizada offers minimal change talk in this session, and when she does, the psychiatrist fails to amplify or reinforce it. When she says "Maybe I could try a little walking when the pain is less," this tentative change talk is dismissed: "Just a little walking won't cut it." When she states "I want to get better" (multiple times), this desire for change is ignored. When she acknowledges the stress connection ("Maybe it does sometimes"), this opening is not pursued. When she says "Maybe once I'm sure it's nothing dangerous, I'd feel safer to try more exercise," this condition for change is dismissed: "You can't wait for tests forever." The psychiatrist also fails to evoke change talk through strategic questioning. There are no open-ended questions designed to elicit the client's own reasons for change, confidence, or commitment. Instead, there are only closed questions and directives.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist's response to all sustain talk is confrontational and dismissive. When Aizada expresses cancer fear, the response is direct confrontation: "You really need to stop worrying about cancer right now." This typically strengthens sustain talk. When she says pain prevents exercise, the psychiatrist responds: "Why are you making excuses about pain? It's just a barrier you need to overcome." This frames her pain as invalid. When she requests more tests, the psychiatrist says: "You can't wait for tests forever." When she hesitates about daily exercise, the psychiatrist says: "You're just being stubborn and wasting time." The psychiatrist never uses double-sided reflections, never reframes sustain talk in a way that opens possibility, and never strategically responds to minimize it. This confrontational approach typically strengthens resistance and is fundamentally inconsistent with MI principles.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Level:** Pre-MI / Non-Competent

**Session Embodies MI Spirit:** No

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation for Change:** Actively Counterproductive

---

### Key Findings:

This session represents a **comprehensive failure of Motivational Interviewing practice** across all nine MI domains. The psychiatrist demonstrates:

- **Zero reflective listening statements** throughout the entire session
- **Entirely directive approach** with no evocation of client motivation
- **Confrontational responses** to client resistance and health anxiety
- **Judgmental and dismissive language** that violates MI principles of acceptance
- **Unilateral authority** with no genuine collaboration or partnership
- **Active undermining of autonomy** through pressure and threats
- **Dismissal of all client contributions** to the session

**Impact on Client Engagement:**

By the end of the interaction, Aizada remains unmoved and has explicitly stated she is "not ready to commit to a full exercise plan." The psychiatrist's approach has likely:
- Strengthened resistance and defensiveness
- Damaged the therapeutic alliance
- Intensified health anxiety
- Reduced perceived autonomy
- Decreased likelihood of behavior change

The client leaves the session more entrenched in her position, feeling unheard and pressured, with no increase in intrinsic motivation for change.

---

### Critical Recommendations:

1. **Seek formal MI training immediately** through MINT-certified trainers
2. **Develop reflective listening skills** as the primary intervention
3. **Shift from expert-driven to collaborative partnership** stance
4. **Address health anxiety through exploration**, not dismissal
5. **Evoke rather than impose** motivation for change
6. **Amplify and reinforce change talk** when offered
7. **Handle sustain talk skillfully** without confrontation
8. **Support autonomy explicitly** through language and approach
9. **Develop genuine empathy and acceptance** for client perspective
10. **Participate in ongoing supervision** with MI-trained supervisors

**Prognosis for Change with Current Approach:** Poor. This approach is actively counterproductive and likely to worsen outcomes.
