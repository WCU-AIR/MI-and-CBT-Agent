# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates profound lack of acceptance throughout the session. Judgmental language ("Drinking to wind down is just a bad habit you need to break") and dismissal of client concerns ("There's no excuse that's good enough") are evident. Minimal unconditional positive regard. Only one moment of genuine acceptance emerges in the third turn ("I appreciate you sharing that"). Overall stance is authoritarian and disapproving rather than warmly supportive.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal empathic skill demonstrated. When client expresses emotional barriers ("I'd be facing a lot of things all at once"), psychiatrist responds with dismissal rather than reflection. Lacks accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand client's perspective. Only one accurate empathic reflection occurs in third turn ("It sounds like quitting completely feels like a big leap for you"). Multiple instances of minimizing and overriding client's stated concerns and emotional experience.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains consistent focus on alcohol reduction as target behavior. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively established. Repeatedly insists on complete abstinence as only acceptable goal, rather than exploring client's own direction. Inconsistent oscillation between abstinence-only and harm reduction approaches undermines strategic clarity. Brief collaborative clarification in third turn is immediately undermined by return to prescriptive direction.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Demonstrates expert-driven, hierarchical stance throughout most of session. Prescriptive language ("you need to," "you have to," "you must") establishes power dynamic incompatible with collaboration. Client's self-generated strategies are dismissed rather than built upon. Only one genuinely collaborative turn ("Let's work together to identify some practical strategies") is immediately abandoned. Overall pattern: psychiatrist operates as expert issuing directives rather than as partner co-creating solutions.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Actively undermines client autonomy throughout session. Pressures commitment ("Will you do that?") rather than inviting choice. Dismisses client's concerns about readiness ("you can't keep waiting for the 'perfect' time"). Frames prescriptions as non-negotiable directives rather than collaborative options. Message communicated: client's concerns don't matter; autonomy is secondary to clinical judgment. Minimal respect for client's right to choose and determine own readiness.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Demonstrates minimal genuine evocation. Questions asked are primarily rhetorical devices designed to highlight psychiatrist's view rather than genuinely draw out client motivation. One evocative question ("What are some things you've tried that helped you reduce your drinking?") is immediately undermined by prescriptive response. Does not ask about client's values, vision for future, or internal motivation for change. Relies on extrinsic pressure and prescription rather than drawing out intrinsic motivation.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Actively assumes expert role and minimizes client expertise throughout session. Positions psychiatrist's clinical judgment as superseding client's self-knowledge and lived experience. When client articulates self-understanding, psychiatrist dismisses it as "rationalization" rather than treating it as valuable insight. Client's contributions are minimized or dismissed rather than substantially influencing session direction. One brief collaborative turn is immediately undermined by reassertion of expert authority.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Client spontaneously offers significant change talk (cutting down, improving communication, avoiding triggering situations). Psychiatrist does not cultivate or reinforce this. Minimizes client's progress ("That's a good start, but really, you need to quit drinking altogether"). Does not reinforce self-efficacy or ask follow-up questions to deepen change talk. One evocative question invites change talk, but psychiatrist immediately responds with prescriptive language that undermines confidence. Overall: progress is minimized rather than amplified.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates inconsistent skill in handling sustain talk. Primarily uses confrontational approach (direct challenge: "you can't keep waiting," "maybe your current mindset is holding you back") which can strengthen resistance through psychological reactance. However, one moment of sophisticated reframing occurs in third turn ("quitting completely feels like a big leap for you right now, and you're interested in exploring ways to cut down safely as a step forward"). This skillful response is immediately followed by return to confrontation. Lacks consistent use of double-sided reflections or strategic reframing.

### Summary of Key Findings

#### **Critical Strengths:**
- Maintains consistent focus on target behavior (alcohol reduction)
- Demonstrates one moment of genuine MI skill in third turn with accurate empathic reflection and collaborative language
- Asks some open-ended questions
- Acknowledges client's existing efforts in third turn

#### **Critical Areas for Improvement:**

**Fundamental Violation of MI Spirit:** The psychiatrist operates from an expert-driven, authoritarian stance that directly contradicts MI principles. Repeated use of prescriptive language ("you must," "you have to," "you need to") establishes hierarchical power dynamic incompatible with MI partnership.

**Minimal Acceptance and Empathy:** Demonstrates judgment, dismissal, and invalidation rather than unconditional positive regard and accurate understanding. Phrases like "you need to stop rationalizing" and "you have to toughen up" communicate disapproval rather than acceptance.

**Prescriptive Rather Than Evocative:** Tells client what to do rather than drawing out client's own motivations, values, and resources. This undermines intrinsic motivation and client self-efficacy.

**Undermines Client Progress:** When client articulates strategies and progress, psychiatrist minimizes efforts rather than reinforcing them, reducing confidence in ability to change.

**Inconsistent MI Application:** One moment of genuine MI skill is immediately followed by return to directive, non-collaborative responses, suggesting intellectual understanding of MI principles but lack of consistent application and genuine commitment to MI spirit.

**Confrontational Approach to Ambivalence:** Uses direct challenge rather than skillfully softening sustain talk through validation and reframing, which can strengthen resistance through psychological reactance.

---

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change

**Likelihood of Evoking Sustained Change Motivation: LOW**

This session is likely to **reduce rather than increase** client's intrinsic motivation for change. Client enters with genuine change talk and willingness to work on goals. Psychiatrist's authoritarian, dismissive approach communicates that client's concerns don't matter, progress isn't sufficient, and autonomy is secondary to clinical judgment.

Client's responses show increasing defensiveness and entrenchment in ambivalence: "I'm not sure I'm ready for that yet"; "Maybe I need more support or tools before I try a full break." These indicate psychological reactance strengthening rather than movement toward commitment.

Client will likely leave session feeling misunderstood, judged, and pressured rather than supported and empowered, reducing probability of follow-through on change goals and increasing risk of relapse or disengagement from treatment.

---

### Primary Recommendations for Improvement

1. **Fundamental shift from expert to partner stance** – Replace prescriptive language with collaborative language; treat client as expert on own life
2. **Develop genuine acceptance and unconditional positive regard** – Communicate respect for autonomy; normalize struggle of change; avoid judgment
3. **Build genuine empathic reflections** – Capture emotional nuance and underlying concerns; reflect both content and feeling
4. **Shift from prescriptive to evocative approach** – Ask questions that draw out client's own motivations, values, and resources
5. **Cultivate and reinforce change talk** – Respond to client progress by reinforcing, deepening, and amplifying it
6. **Handle ambivalence skillfully** – Use double-sided reflections and reframing rather than confrontation
7. **Affirm existing strengths and progress** – Build on what client has already accomplished
8. **Explore readiness before prescribing change** – Collaboratively problem-solve barriers rather than imposing goals
9. **Develop consistency in MI application** – Extend the one skillful moment throughout entire session
10. **Address underlying barriers to MI implementation** – Reflect on what drives shift to directive mode; develop strategies to maintain MI stance

---

### Recommendation for Training

**Comprehensive MI training with emphasis on MI spirit (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation) is strongly recommended.** Supervision with experienced MI trainer is essential. Psychiatrist should record and review sessions regularly to identify moments of genuine MI skill and extend those throughout entire sessions. Without significant training and practice, current approach is likely to harm rather than help clients with ambivalence about change.
