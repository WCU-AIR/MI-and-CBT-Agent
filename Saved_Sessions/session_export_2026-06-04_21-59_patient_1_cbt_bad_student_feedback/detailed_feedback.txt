
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made no attempt to establish a collaborative agenda for the session. There was no discussion of session goals, prioritization of problems, time allocation, or what Dylan hoped to accomplish during their time together. A proper CBT agenda-setting would have involved explicit conversation such as: "Dylan, I know you're dealing with a lot since the accident. Dylan has specific, concrete problems: nightmares with racing heart, inability to concentrate on homework, avoidance of cars and loud environments, and anger outbursts. A proper agenda would have prioritized which symptom cluster to address first (e.g., "Should we focus on your sleep problems, your anxiety around cars, or your irritability?"). This would have provided structure, communicated competence, and given Dylan a sense of control—particularly important for trauma survivors who often feel powerless.

The session drifted without direction, with the therapist repeatedly offering generic advice about positive thinking and hydration rather than collaboratively identifying and targeting specific treatment goals. No agenda was revisited, adjusted, or summarized.

**Summary:**

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist's responses suggest a fundamental misunderstanding of trauma psychology. ### 5. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration was minimal and largely absent. True collaboration in CBT involves: (1) shared decision-making about treatment goals and methods, (2) soliciting patient input on what's most important to address, (3) explaining the rationale for interventions, (4) inviting the patient to be an active problem-solver, and (5) treating the patient as a partner in the therapeutic work.

The therapist did none of these systematically. Instead, the therapist adopted an expert-advice-giver stance, repeatedly telling Dylan what to do: "Maybe try to think positive," "Maybe you could try that?" "Maybe you could try to get a bit more rest?" "Maybe you could try that?" The repeated "maybe" language is superficially collaborative but actually represents the therapist offering solutions without genuine partnership.

Dylan was positioned as a passive recipient of advice rather than an active collaborator. ### 1. **Immediate Priority: Learn Trauma-Specific CBT**
- **Action:** Obtain specialized training in trauma-focused CBT (TF-CBT) or Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT). These are evidence-based treatments for PTSD/ASD with strong empirical support. Do not continue treating trauma patients without this training.
- **Example:** Enroll in a TF-CBT certification program (available through the National Child Traumatic Stress Network). Read "Cognitive Processing Therapy for PTSD" by Resick & Schnicke or "Trauma and Recovery" by Judith Herman to build foundational knowledge.
- **Practice:** Attend a workshop on trauma assessment and treatment planning. Practice conducting a thorough trauma history and identifying maintaining factors (avoidance, catastrophic thoughts, safety behaviors).

### 2. **Establish a Structured Assessment and Case Conceptualization Process**
- **Action:** Before the next session, conduct a comprehensive trauma assessment including: (a) detailed trauma history (what happened, when, where, who was involved), (b) current symptoms mapped to DSM-5 criteria, (c) triggers and avoidance patterns, (d) catastrophic thoughts and beliefs, (e) functional impairment (school, relationships, activities), (f) protective factors and strengths.
- **Example:** Use a structured tool like the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) or the Acute Stress Disorder Scale (ASDS) to systematically assess symptoms. Ask Dylan: "What situations make your anxiety worse? What do you avoid? What are you afraid will happen? What does the accident mean to you about yourself or the world?"
- **Practice:** Develop a written case conceptualization that links Dylan's symptoms to underlying trauma processes. For example: "Dylan's intrusive headlight images and hypervigilance are maintained by avoidance of trauma reminders and catastrophic thoughts ('I'm not safe in cars'). His anger reflects a sense of powerlessness and threat. Treatment will involve gradual exposure to reminders and cognitive restructuring to reduce threat perception."

### 3. **Eliminate Excessive Self-Disclosure; Maintain Professional Boundaries**
- **Action:** Stop sharing personal experiences in session. Self-disclosure should be rare, brief, and clearly in service of the patient's treatment (e.g., "I've worked with many teens after accidents, and what you're experiencing is a normal trauma response"). Do not compare Dylan's trauma to your exam stress or childhood fears.
- **Example:** Instead of "When I was stressed about exams, I just reminded myself to think positive," say: "Many people try to manage trauma by thinking positive or distracting themselves. - **Practice:** In supervision or role-play, practice responding to patient distress with empathy and clinical expertise rather than personal anecdotes. Ask yourself: "Does this self-disclosure serve the patient's treatment, or does it shift focus to me?"

- **Action:** In the next session, provide clear, age-appropriate psychoeducation about how trauma affects the brain and why Dylan's symptoms make sense. Explain the fight-flight-freeze response, why intrusive memories occur, why avoidance maintains symptoms, and why processing is necessary for recovery.

- **Practice:** Develop a handout or visual aid explaining trauma response. Practice delivering this explanation in a way that normalizes Dylan's symptoms while conveying hope and a clear path forward.

### 5. **Conduct Thorough Cognitive Assessment; Identify Catastrophic Thoughts**
- **Action:** Systematically identify Dylan's automatic thoughts and core beliefs related to the trauma. Ask: "What goes through your mind when you see a car? When you hear a loud noise? - **Practice:** Use a thought record to help Dylan identify the relationship between situations, thoughts, feelings, and behaviors. For example: Situation: "Hearing a car horn" → Thought: "That's the crash happening again" → Feeling: "Terror, heart racing" → Behavior: "Jump, avoid going outside."

### 6. **Develop a Graded Exposure Plan**
- **Action:** Create a hierarchy of trauma reminders from least to most anxiety-provoking. Work with Dylan to gradually face these reminders in a controlled way, starting with the least threatening. This is the core intervention for reducing hypervigilance and avoidance in ASD.
