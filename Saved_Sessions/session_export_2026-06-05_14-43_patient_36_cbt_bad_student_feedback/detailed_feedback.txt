
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish Structured Session Agenda and Treatment Planning (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Before the next session, meet with the caregivers to conduct a comprehensive functional assessment. Collaboratively identify 2-3 specific, measurable target behaviors (e.g., "Adriana will check back with caregiver before approaching unfamiliar adults," "Adriana will wait for permission before physical contact with strangers," "Adriana will respect peer boundaries during play"). - **Example:** "Today we're going to work on three things: (1) understanding why Adriana approaches strangers so quickly, (2) teaching her to check back with mom or dad first, and (3) practicing a safer way to say hello. We'll spend about 10 minutes on each. Does that sound good?"
- **Practice:** Use a written session agenda template for every session. Share the agenda with caregivers at the beginning and review progress at the end. Practice prioritizing the most dangerous behaviors first (stranger approach, willingness to leave with unfamiliar adults).

### 2. **Develop Accurate Diagnostic Conceptualization of DSED (CRITICAL)**
- **Action:** Study the neurobiology and etiology of Disinhibited Social Engagement Disorder. Understand that this is NOT typical shyness or social anxiety—it is a trauma-related attachment disorder resulting from early deprivation, institutional care, and disrupted attachment formation. Recognize that the symptoms (indiscriminate approach, overly familiar behavior, diminished checking back) are adaptive responses to early neglect that are now maladaptive and dangerous.
- **Example:** In your mind, reframe the case: "Adriana's indiscriminate approach to strangers is not a sign of friendliness or confidence—it's a sign that she did not develop secure attachment in infancy due to institutional deprivation. She approaches all adults equally because she did not learn to discriminate safe from unsafe caregivers. This is dangerous and requires intervention."
- **Practice:** Read current literature on attachment disorders and institutional deprivation (e.g., Zeanah & Gleason, 2015; Smyke et al., 2007). Consult with an attachment-focused therapist or supervisor. Never normalize DSED symptoms as typical development.

### 3. **Eliminate Excessive Self-Disclosure and Model Professional Boundaries (CRITICAL)**
- **Action:** Stop sharing personal anecdotes about your own childhood, sleep habits, or emotional experiences. A child with poor boundaries needs a therapist who models clear, professional boundaries. Your self-disclosure teaches the patient that personal sharing is appropriate in therapeutic relationships and blurs the distinction between peer and professional.
- **Example:** Instead of "When I was little, I used to run up to anyone who smiled at me," say: "I'm here to help you learn safer ways to say hello to new people. Let's practice together."
- **Practice:** Record yourself in role-plays and count instances of self-disclosure. Aim for zero. When you feel the urge to share a personal story, pause and ask: "Does this serve the patient's treatment goals, or does it blur boundaries?" If the latter, redirect to the patient's experience.

### 4. **Implement Caregiver-Involved Treatment Planning and Behavioral Intervention (CRITICAL)**
- **Action:** Involve the adoptive parents as active treatment partners. Conduct a joint session to: (1) educate them about DSED and its etiology, (2) assess current safety management strategies, (3) develop a behavioral safety plan (e.g., rules about stranger contact, checking back procedures, consequences for boundary violations), (4) teach them to coach Adriana in appropriate social behavior, and (5) establish a reinforcement system for appropriate boundary-respecting behavior.

Here's how we can practice that together..."
- **Practice:** Develop a written behavioral plan with specific rules, teaching strategies, and reinforcement procedures. Schedule regular caregiver consultation sessions (not just child sessions). Teach parents to use behavioral rehearsal, role-play, and contingency management.

- **Action:** Replace generic reassurance ("things will get better," "people are usually nice") with Socratic questions that help the patient discover insights. Ask questions that explore the patient's thoughts, feelings, and observations about her behavior and its consequences.
