# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance

**Comments:** Minimal unconditional positive regard demonstrated. Repeated use of prescriptive language ("should," "probably," "maybe you should") communicates subtle judgment about Eric's smoking choices. Dismisses Eric's rationalization about work-related fitness without exploring its validity. Conditional approval contingent on accepting clinician's agenda rather than genuine affirmation of autonomy or effort. ### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Surface-level reflective listening present but emotionally flat and factually focused. Misses critical opportunities for deeper empathic exploration—particularly when Eric identifies smoking as stress relief (key emotional regulation function). Fails to explore ambivalence or validate difficulty of change. Does not investigate Eric's statement about limited coping resources ("nothing else works as well"), which reveals potential hopelessness. Reflections restate content without exploring meaning or feeling. No strategic response to strengthen emerging change talk.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clear focus on smoking reduction/cessation maintained throughout session with consistent directional consistency. However, direction is pursued through clinician-imposed goals rather than collaborative exploration. Leading questions designed to plant reasons for change in Eric's mind rather than allowing his own motivations to emerge organically. Maintains focus when stress management introduced as barrier, but responds with prescriptions rather than exploring Eric's current coping repertoire or evoking his own ideas.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Fundamental absence of true collaboration. Psychiatrist adopts expert-driven, hierarchical stance throughout, making unilateral decisions about session focus, goals, and interventions. False choice presented in Turn 1 ("Does that sound okay?") with agenda already predetermined. Prescriptive language throughout ("you should," "maybe you should," "you might want to") positions clinician as authority. No genuine power-sharing or co-construction of change plan. Eric positioned as passive recipient of expert advice, not equal partner in problem-solving.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session actively undermines autonomy support. Prescriptive language throughout subtly communicates clinician knows what's best and Eric should comply. Repeated "should try," "should consider," "probably help if you" conveys disapproval of current choices. When Eric expresses low importance (3-4/10) and moderate confidence (5/10), clinician immediately prescribes solution without exploring what these numbers mean or what Eric thinks would help. Assigns specific homework without asking if Eric wants to do this or prefers different approach. Minimizes choice throughout.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Almost entirely absent. Psychiatrist repeatedly imposes reasons for change rather than evoking them. Turn 4 tells Eric what to think about rather than asking what he thinks. Turn 6 prescribes stress-management alternatives without exploring what Eric has tried or values. Critical statement about limited coping resources ("nothing else works as well") receives prescriptive response, not empathic exploration. No strategic amplification of change talk produced by Eric. No exploration of values, family meaning, or what matters most to him. Missed opportunities to ask open-ended evocation questions throughout.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role and positions Eric as passive recipient of clinical guidance. Unilateral agenda-setting in Turn 1 framed as question but predetermined. Consistent pattern of assess-diagnose-prescribe throughout. Language consistently positions clinician as authority ("you should," "maybe you should," "we can set a goal" where "we" means clinician decides). Interprets Eric's ambivalence and prescribes solution without asking what Eric thinks would work. Assigns homework and closes with praise for compliance. No recognition that Eric's wisdom about his own life is central to change.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no skill demonstrated in evoking, recognizing, or strategically reinforcing change talk. Eric produces natural change talk ("I've thought about cutting down," "I'm open to trying stuff," "I guess I could try cutting down") but clinician fails to amplify or strengthen it. No follow-up questions to deepen emerging change talk. No strategic questioning designed to evoke desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment. When Eric says "I'm open to trying stuff," clinician immediately prescribes interventions rather than asking what appeals to him. Change talk statements met with prescriptions rather than reflections and deepening questions.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill in handling sustain talk. While avoiding overt confrontation, clinician uses leading questions and directive responses that may trigger defensiveness rather than exploration. Turn 4 directly contradicts Eric's sustain talk ("I feel fine") with "sooner than you think" language—confrontational approach. Sustain talk statements about minimized risk ("not coughing," "feeling fine") met with leading questions rather than empathic exploration or double-sided reflection. Turn 7 sustain talk about limited coping alternatives receives prescriptive response rather than exploration. Absence of overt confrontation is not same as skillfully softening sustain talk. Missing double-sided reflections, validation of difficulty, and strategic pivoting that acknowledges sustain talk without reinforcing it.

---

### **Overall MI Competency Assessment**

**Current Session Score: 1.4/5**

**Summary:** This session demonstrates **poor motivational interviewing practice** representing fundamental departure from MI principles and spirit. Psychiatrist operates from traditional medical/expert model, making unilateral decisions while using prescriptive language that undermines autonomy and collaboration. While basic rapport established and tone non-confrontational, nearly all core MI domains are deficient.

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Directive prescriptions disguised as collaboration
- Imposition of reasons for change rather than evocation
- Failure to explore ambivalence or develop discrepancy
- Minimal change talk cultivation and amplification
- Sustain talk not softened through empathic exploration
- Expert-driven agenda without genuine collaboration
- Superficial empathy missing emotional attunement

**Likelihood of Sustained Change: Low**

Eric's agreement appears to be compliance with clinician's agenda rather than genuine motivation. Stated importance (3-4/10) and confidence (5/10) remain low. Ambivalence unresolved. Stress management barrier unaddressed. No genuine increase in intrinsic motivation. Without significant approach changes, Eric likely to attempt plan briefly, encounter barriers, and disengage or relapse.

**Most Critical Improvements Needed:**
1. Shift from expert to collaborative stance (foundational)
2. Develop evocation skills—ask rather than tell
3. Explore ambivalence empathically rather than prescribe
4. Recognize and amplify change talk strategically
5. Replace directive language with autonomy-supportive language

**Recommendation:** Formal MI training including didactic instruction, supervised practice with feedback, role-plays, observation of expert practitioners, and regular feedback on recorded sessions. MI is learnable; with focused training, this clinician can develop genuine competence and significantly improve client outcomes.
