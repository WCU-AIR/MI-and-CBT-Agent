
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

- **Practice**: Review the literature on OCD treatment, particularly for patients with delusional-intensity beliefs and absent insight. Consult with an OCD specialist or supervisor. Use the Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) to systematically assess obsessions and compulsions. Use the Brown Assessment of Beliefs Scale (BABS) to assess insight level.

### 2. **URGENT: Assess insight level and consider pharmacological augmentation**
- **Action**: Explicitly assess the patient's insight using the BABS or clinical interview. Given delusional-intensity beliefs and absent insight, consult with a psychiatrist about pharmacological augmentation (e.g., adding an antipsychotic to the SSRI). Standard SSRI monotherapy is often insufficient for OCD with poor insight.
- **Example**: "Trevor, I want to better understand how certain you feel about the contamination in your home. On a scale of 0-100, how certain are you that COVID-19 is present in your home right now? What evidence do you have for this? What evidence might suggest it's not present? [Based on responses, assess insight level and discuss pharmacological options with psychiatry.]"
- **Practice**: Familiarize yourself with the BABS and its interpretation. Review literature on OCD with poor insight and pharmacological augmentation strategies. Establish a consultation relationship with a psychiatrist experienced in OCD treatment.

### 3. **Implement Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) as the primary treatment modality**
- **Action**: Replace distraction and cognitive restructuring with systematic ERP. Develop an exposure hierarchy with the patient, starting with less anxiety-provoking exposures and progressing to more challenging ones. For each exposure, the patient will deliberately tolerate anxiety while refraining from compulsions (response prevention). Explain the rationale: anxiety naturally decreases over time (habituation) when the patient remains in the situation without performing compulsions.
- **Example**: "Trevor, research shows that the most effective treatment for OCD is called Exposure and Response Prevention, or ERP. The idea is that when you avoid cleaning or wash less, your anxiety will initially increase. But if you stay with that anxiety without giving in to the urge to wash, your brain will gradually learn that the feared outcome doesn't happen and the anxiety will naturally decrease. We'll start with exposures that are challenging but manageable, and gradually work up to more difficult ones. For example, we might start by having you touch a doorknob and wait 30 minutes before washing, then gradually increase the time or the 'contamination.' Does this make sense? What questions do you have?"
- **Practice**: Complete training in ERP for OCD (e.g., through the International OCD Foundation, the Anxiety and Depression Association of America, or specialized CBT training programs). Practice developing exposure hierarchies and conducting exposures in session. Consult with an OCD specialist to review your ERP implementation.

### 4. **Eliminate reassurance-seeking and teach the patient about reassurance cycles**
- **Action**: Stop providing reassurance ("You're doing your best," "You're doing important work"). Instead, psychoeducate the patient about how reassurance-seeking maintains OCD. Explain that reassurance provides temporary relief but reinforces the cycle. Help the patient understand that tolerating uncertainty is part of recovery.
- **Example**: "Trevor, I want to talk about something important. In OCD, when we feel anxious about contamination, we often seek reassurance—like asking 'Did I wash enough?' or 'Am I really clean?' The reassurance feels good temporarily, but it actually keeps the OCD going. It teaches your brain that the worry is real and needs reassurance. To get better, we need to break this cycle by tolerating the uncertainty and anxiety without seeking reassurance. I know that's hard, but it's how your brain learns that the feared outcome doesn't happen. So going forward, I won't be providing reassurance, and I'll help you resist the urge to seek it. Does that make sense?"
- **Practice**: Review literature on reassurance-seeking in OCD and its role in maintaining symptoms. Practice delivering this psychoeducation in a compassionate, non-judgmental way. Role-play with a supervisor or colleague.

- **Action**: In the next session, conduct a detailed assessment of the patient's obsessions, compulsions, triggers, and maintaining factors.
