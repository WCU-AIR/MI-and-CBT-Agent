### Empathy

**Comments:** The psychiatrist uses strong reflective listening and perspective-taking, with complex reflections that capture Adrian's ambivalence: "It sounds like you're weighing two important sides here—the clear discomfort and worry that this infection has brought up, which pushes you to think about change, alongside the value you place on your weekends." Some reflections become lengthy and somewhat didactic, shifting focus from Adrian's experience to the clinician's interpretation.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains general focus on Adrian's methamphetamine use and its connection to his infection, beginning with an appropriately collaborative opening. However, the direction becomes increasingly diffuse as the session progresses. Critically, the clinician never explicitly clarifies what change Adrian is considering—is the goal abstinence, reduced use, safer use practices, or harm reduction? The session drifts between these possibilities without establishing shared understanding. When Adrian says "I'm still not totally convinced it's worth all that effort just yet," the clinician doesn't pause to explore what "effort" Adrian is actually considering or what specific change goal would be worth that effort. The focus on "reaching out to your buddy" as a first step subtly shifts the target from Adrian's drug use to social connection without explicit discussion or negotiation.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates strong collaborative stance with frequent invitations for Adrian's input: "What's on your mind?" "What do you think might be the connection?" "Can you tell me about a time...?" The use of scaling questions invites Adrian to define his own readiness rather than imposing an assessment. However, subtle moments of steering emerge. Statements like "It's great that you noticed how good it felt to take care of yourself" contain implicit approval that could guide Adrian toward a predetermined conclusion. When Adrian identifies reaching out to his buddy as a first step, the psychiatrist doesn't explicitly negotiate whether this is Adrian's genuine priority or whether it emerged from the clinician's questions. The clinician asks "How confident do you feel about taking that step?" but not "Is this actually something you want to do?"

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently emphasizes Adrian's freedom to choose with language like "when the time feels right," "without pressure," and "giving yourself some space to do it when you feel ready." The clinician avoids prescribing change and positions Adrian as the expert on his own change process. However, repeated affirmations of Adrian's planning ("That sounds like a really thoughtful and manageable first step") could be experienced as approving a predetermined direction rather than genuinely supporting autonomous choice. Most importantly, the psychiatrist never explicitly addresses Adrian's stated ambivalence: "I'm still not totally convinced it's worth all that effort just yet." Rather than exploring what would need to happen for Adrian to feel the effort is worth it, the clinician moves forward with action planning, representing subtle pressure toward change that may undermine true autonomy.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asks evocative questions ("What do you think might be the connection?" "What would you say makes you consider whether or not to change?" "Can you tell me about a time in the past when you made a change?") and Adrian produces meaningful change talk: "I don't want to deal with that again," "not knowing what's in the stuff definitely makes me pause," and "I don't want to end up with something worse." However, after Adrian produces change talk, the clinician typically reflects it back without strategically amplifying or deepening it. When Adrian says "I don't want to deal with that again," the clinician could have asked follow-up questions: "What specifically about the infection was hardest for you?" or "How important is it to you to avoid that happening again?" The psychiatrist also misses opportunities to evoke Adrian's values and self-perception. When Adrian hints at shame about his use, this powerful motivator goes unexplored. Questions designed to evoke specific types of change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need) are largely absent.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates some partnership elements but frequently assumes an expert or guide role that subtly undermines true power-sharing. While the clinician uses questions and invites Adrian's input, the psychiatrist interprets Adrian's experience in ways that position the clinician as the expert interpreter: "That sense of it being manageable for now seems important to you." These interpretations, while accurate, represent the clinician explaining Adrian to himself rather than Adrian discovering his own understanding. The psychiatrist doesn't genuinely collaborate on what social support might look like; instead, the clinician guides Adrian toward a specific action (contacting his buddy) and then affirms this plan repeatedly. The clinician also doesn't share uncertainty or acknowledge limits of expertise. When Adrian expresses nervousness about contacting his friend, the psychiatrist responds with reassurance rather than genuinely exploring Adrian's concern. True power-sharing and co-creation of session structure are limited.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist evokes some change talk and reflects it back appropriately, but doesn't consistently amplify, deepen, or strategically strengthen Adrian's own motivations for change. When Adrian produces meaningful change talk ("I don't want to deal with that again," "I don't want to end up with something worse"), the clinician reflects this but doesn't ask follow-up questions to strengthen this motivation. The psychiatrist doesn't strategically ask questions designed to evoke specific types of change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment). When Adrian produces sustain talk, the clinician reflects the ambivalence but doesn't use techniques like double-sided reflection or reframing to shift the balance toward change talk. The psychiatrist doesn't explicitly reinforce Adrian's change talk with enthusiasm or emphasis. Action planning at the end does represent an attempt to cultivate commitment talk, but this emerges more from the clinician's guidance than from Adrian's own articulation of commitment.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** This represents a significant weakness in the session. Adrian produces substantial sustain talk throughout ("it's mostly just weekend stuff," "Not like it's taking over or anything," "It's just something to do on weekends," "Don't want to overthink it too much," "I'm still not totally convinced it's worth all that effort just yet"), and the psychiatrist rarely uses MI-consistent techniques to soften or redirect it. When Adrian minimizes his use, the clinician reflects this back without strategically redirecting. The psychiatrist could have used double-sided reflections: "On one hand, you see it as just weekends and manageable, and on the other hand, you're worried about what's in the stuff and don't want another infection." Reframing could have been employed: "So you're saying it's 'just weekends'—and at the same time, you mentioned the infection was really uncomfortable and you don't want to go through that again. How do those two things fit together?" Amplified reflection could have prompted Adrian to soften his own sustain talk: "So it sounds like you're completely convinced this isn't a problem and there's no reason to change at all?" When Adrian states "I'm still not totally convinced it's worth all that effort just yet," the clinician doesn't explore what "effort" Adrian is referring to or what would need to happen for Adrian to feel the effort is worth it. Instead, the clinician moves toward action planning, which subtly pressures Adrian toward change without fully addressing his ambivalence.

## **Overall Session Score: 3.4/5**

### **Summary of Strengths:**
- Strong reflective listening with accurate, complex reflections capturing Adrian's ambivalence
- Collaborative opening that appropriately invites Adrian to set the agenda
- Effective validation of Adrian's mixed feelings and normalization of tension between relief and health concerns
- Skillful exploration of past successes, evoking self-efficacy and identifying concrete supports
- Autonomy-supportive language emphasizing Adrian's choice and freedom
- Appropriate use of scaling questions without imposing judgment
- Concrete, manageable action plan that Adrian identifies as doable (confidence level 6/10)

### **Summary of Areas for Improvement:**
- **Insufficient softening of sustain talk:** Adrian's minimization ("it's just weekends," "not like it's taking over") is accepted without strategic redirection through double-sided reflections, reframing, or amplified reflections
- **Shallow evocation of change talk:** While evocative questions are asked, follow-up questions to strengthen and elaborate Adrian's motivation are largely absent
- **Unclear change goal:** The session never explicitly establishes what Adrian is considering changing (abstinence, reduced use, safer use, harm reduction), undermining strategic direction
- **Missed exploration of shame and vulnerability:** Adrian hints at shame and fear, but these aren't deeply explored with empathy and acceptance
- **Subtle expert positioning:** Despite collaborative language, the psychiatrist frequently interprets Adrian's experience and guides him toward predetermined conclusions, subtly undermining true partnership
- **Limited exploration of values:** The clinician doesn't deeply explore what Adrian values about his current pattern or his life more broadly, limiting Adrian's ability to make a fully autonomous choice
- **Action planning without full ambivalence resolution:** The session moves toward action planning without fully resolving Adrian's stated ambivalence ("I'm still not totally convinced it's worth all that effort")

### **Clinical Significance:**
This session demonstrates adequate foundational MI competence with solid rapport-building and collaborative stance. However, the session may not sufficiently strengthen Adrian's intrinsic motivation for change. Adrian's stated readiness (3/10) and explicit ambivalence suggest he is not yet ready for significant change. By accepting his minimization without strategic redirection and moving toward action planning without fully resolving ambivalence, the psychiatrist risks Adrian's commitment wavering once he leaves the session. The action plan may represent the clinician's agenda more than Adrian's genuine priority. If Adrian's ambivalence isn't more fully explored and his sustain talk isn't softened, he may not follow through on the action step or may quickly return to his previous pattern.
