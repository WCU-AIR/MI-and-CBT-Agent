# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance throughout. Effectively normalizes ambivalence early ("That balance shows you have a grounded sense of where you're at, which is a strong foundation to build from") and validates patient's concern about family harmony. However, could deepen acceptance by more fully exploring underlying fears about disruption when patient expresses hesitation about family involvement. Past attempts at change could be more actively reframed as evidence of commitment rather than implicitly framed as failures.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates strong reflective listening with several complex, emotionally attuned reflections. Particularly skilled moment: "There's a real pull between wanting to care for yourself and wanting to fit in with your loved ones, which must feel like walking a tightrope." Accurately captures both factual barriers and emotional/relational tension. However, misses opportunities to explore deeper emotional weight of concerns—such as shame or fear of judgment underlying "I don't want to be the odd one out," and disappointment/demoralization accompanying repeated setbacks.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintains clear, strategic focus on weight loss goal and barrier reduction throughout. Logical progression: explores barriers → reasons for change → confidence level → past successes → concrete next steps. Effectively redirects when appropriate (e.g., "what do you think might be one small step you could try that feels doable?"). Could strengthen direction by more explicitly prioritizing which barriers to address first and tying interventions directly to patient's identified strengths rather than addressing barriers diffusely.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates strong collaborative stance by consistently inviting patient input and treating him as equal partner. Seeks permission before offering strategies ("Would it be okay if I shared some information...?") and builds on patient's own ideas rather than imposing solutions. Could deepen collaboration by more explicitly positioning patient as expert on his own life and inviting his problem-solving before offering clinician-generated solutions. When patient hesitates about family involvement, could more explicitly invite patient to problem-solve rather than simply reflecting.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently respects and affirms patient's right to choose throughout. Validates boundary about complexity ("Thanks for being open to exploring options and for sharing what feels doable for you") and accepts hesitation about family involvement without pressure. Could strengthen autonomy support by more explicitly framing choices as patient's own and avoiding subtle suggestions that certain approaches are "right." When patient settles on snack prep, could have explicitly affirmed: "This is your choice, and you're the expert on what will work in your life."

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates basic evocation of patient's motivations and past successes but inconsistently. Effectively asks "What do you think has helped you reach that six before?" However, shifts into advice-giving mode ("One approach that many find helpful is building small, manageable habits...") rather than consistently drawing out patient's own ideas. Misses opportunities to deepen change talk through follow-up questions when patient articulates reasons for change ("I want to keep up with them—play with the kids without getting winded"). Could strengthen by asking patient to generate solutions before offering clinician-generated strategies.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates strong partnership by treating patient as equal collaborator rather than assuming expert role. Uses inclusive language ("we can talk about what might fit for you"), seeks permission before offering information, and invites patient input on decisions. Tone is warm and collegial rather than hierarchical. Could strengthen partnership by more consistently positioning patient as expert on his own life and inviting his expertise before offering clinician information. When patient hesitates about family involvement, could ask: "You know your family better than anyone. What do you think would need to happen?"

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates inconsistent cultivation of change talk. Some effective moments (e.g., asking about past successes generates change talk about planning and exercise). However, misses critical opportunities to amplify and strengthen change talk. When patient articulates reasons for change ("I want to be healthier...for my kids and my wife"), psychiatrist reflects but doesn't deepen through follow-up questions. When patient identifies snack prep as action step, doesn't strengthen commitment language. Doesn't strategically respond to sustain talk or explicitly reinforce patient's confidence increase (6→7) or ask what contributed to it.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates basic softening of sustain talk through reflection and redirection without reinforcement. Adequately handles some instances (e.g., when reflecting on consistency doubt, pivots toward change talk about past successes). However, misses opportunities to use sophisticated techniques like double-sided reflection or gentle challenging. When patient says "I just know how hard it's going to be," could respond: "It sounds really hard, and at the same time, you've had success before when you've planned ahead. What made those times different?" Doesn't explore whether concern about family involvement might reflect limiting assumptions rather than genuine barriers.

## Summary Assessment

### Strengths
- ✓ Consistent empathy and acceptance with genuine affirmation of patient's values
- ✓ Strong collaborative approach treating patient as equal partner
- ✓ Clear direction and logical progression toward concrete action steps
- ✓ Effective autonomy support respecting patient's boundaries
- ✓ Skillful use of confidence scaling (6→7) to assess readiness
- ✓ Successfully identifies specific, doable first step (healthy snack prep)

### Areas for Improvement
- ✗ **Insufficient evocation of change talk** – shifts into advice-giving rather than consistently drawing out patient's own motivations and ideas
- ✗ **Limited amplification of change talk** – reflects but doesn't deepen through follow-up questions
- ✗ **Inconsistent softening of sustain talk** – misses opportunities for double-sided reflection and gentle challenging
- ✗ **Missed opportunities to strengthen commitment** – doesn't explore what contributed to confidence increase or ask implementation-focused questions
- ✗ **Subtle expert positioning** – occasionally positions self as expert with knowledge about "what works" rather than positioning patient as expert on own life

---

## Clinical Impact

This session maintains patient engagement and generates modest motivation increase (confidence 6→7), but falls short of the motivation amplification that MI aims to achieve. Patient leaves with concrete action step but may not develop the deep intrinsic motivation needed to sustain change through inevitable challenges. Given patient's history of relapse and stated doubt about maintaining changes, a more consistently evocative approach would likely generate stronger commitment and greater likelihood of sustained behavior change.
