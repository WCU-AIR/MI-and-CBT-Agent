
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. What CBT-specific techniques were used (not just general therapy)?
2. What critical CBT components are missing?
3. Would this lead to measurable symptom improvement?
4. Can the patient apply learnings independently?

2. What was missing (specific techniques that should have been used)
3. Why missing elements matter for patient outcomes

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

No agenda was established at any point during this session. A proper CBT session with a 12-year-old presenting with DMDD and ADHD requires explicit collaborative agenda-setting, particularly given the complexity of his presentation and the need to prioritize among multiple potential targets (anger management, sleep disruption, academic stress, social withdrawal, video game frustration). The therapist opened with a vague, open-ended question ("How have you been doing lately? Anything new or interesting going on?") but never transitioned this into a structured agenda. There was no discussion of session goals, no prioritization of problems, no time allocation, and no reference back to an agreed-upon focus throughout the session. The patient repeatedly expressed core concerns—the inability to control anger, feeling stuck in cycles of frustration, social isolation fears, and the pervasive nature of his irritability—yet the therapist never organized these into actionable session targets.

A proper agenda would have sounded like: "Wyatt, I hear that anger is a big issue for you, and you're also struggling with sleep and feeling stuck. Today we have about 45 minutes. What would be most helpful to focus on? Should we start by understanding what happens right before you get really angry, or would you rather talk about what happens after?" This would establish collaboration, prioritization, and structure—none of which occurred.

**Summary:**

The therapist completely failed to establish a session agenda, leaving the session structurally aimless and reactive rather than goal-directed. This is a fundamental CBT failure that undermines all subsequent work.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made minimal, inadequate attempts to elicit feedback from Wyatt. Across the entire session, there was only one direct question about the patient's experience: "Have you been sleeping okay?" This is insufficient for CBT, which requires ongoing feedback loops to ensure understanding, gauge the patient's reactions to interventions, and adjust course accordingly.

The therapist never asked: "Does that make sense?" "How does that land with you?" "What do you think about trying that?" "Is there anything I said that didn't quite fit your experience?" Most critically, the therapist never solicited feedback about the session itself—no end-of-session summary check, no "What was most useful today?" no "Is there anything we should do differently next time?" **Summary:**

Feedback elicitation was virtually absent. The therapist failed to check understanding, gauge reactions to suggestions, or invite the patient's perspective on session usefulness—all critical CBT competencies.
