
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## CRITICAL PRELIMINARY NOTE

This session represents a **fundamental departure from evidence-based CBT** and constitutes poor clinical practice that is likely to harm this patient. The therapist engaged in excessive self-disclosure, provided generic reassurance without clinical structure, failed to conduct any cognitive-behavioral assessment or intervention, and did not establish a treatment plan. ## 1. Agenda

### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made no attempt to establish a collaborative agenda at any point in the session. A proper CBT agenda would have included: (1) explicit identification of session goals, (2) prioritization of problems (flying phobia vs. animal phobia vs. alcohol use), (3) time allocation for specific interventions, and (4) agreement on what would be accomplished by session end.

Instead, the therapist opened with a vague, open-ended question ("How have you been holding up lately?") and allowed the session to drift into unfocused discussion. When the patient mentioned flying anxiety and family events, the therapist never clarified whether this would be the session focus or how it connected to treatment goals. The patient's mention of alcohol use (three glasses of wine nightly) was acknowledged but never incorporated into a coherent treatment plan or agenda.

The session lacked structure entirely. There was no discussion of: What specific problem would be targeted today? What CBT techniques would be used? What would constitute progress? How would homework connect to session work? The patient left without understanding the treatment strategy or what to expect next.

**Summary:**

No agenda was established. The session drifted without direction, priorities, or explicit goals. This violates a foundational CBT principle and leaves the patient uncertain about treatment direction.

## 2. Feedback

### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made minimal, inadequate attempts to elicit feedback. Across the entire session, there was only one implicit check: "What do you think?" after suggesting positive thinking. This single, superficial attempt does not constitute adequate feedback elicitation.

A therapist scoring 4+ on this criterion would: (1) regularly check understanding of explanations, (2) ask whether interventions are making sense, (3) solicit reactions to suggestions, (4) verify that homework instructions are clear, and (5) summarize key points and ask for patient input.

This therapist did none of these. When suggesting "focus on the good parts," the therapist did not check whether the patient understood this advice or found it helpful. When recommending water intake, sleep hygiene, fresh air, and music, no follow-up questions verified comprehension or buy-in. The patient's explicit statement—"It's like no matter how much I want to look at the positive side, my body just won't listen"—should have triggered clarifying questions about why positive thinking wasn't working, but the therapist instead repeated the same ineffective advice.

Most critically, there was no feedback elicitation at session end. The therapist did not ask: "What was most helpful today?" "Do you understand what we discussed?" "What questions do you have?" "How are you feeling about the plan?" The patient left without confirming understanding or commitment to any specific interventions.

**Summary:**

Feedback was essentially absent. One vague question does not meet the threshold for adequate feedback elicitation. The therapist failed to verify understanding, check reactions, or confirm patient commitment to any recommendations.

## 3. Understanding

### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated limited understanding of the patient's internal experience and missed critical clinical information. ## 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness

### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist displayed adequate warmth and concern but with significant interpersonal problems that undermined therapeutic effectiveness. While not hostile or demeaning, the therapist's approach was problematic in several ways:

**Excessive self-disclosure**: The therapist repeatedly shared personal experiences ("When I was feeling down," "I used to have this habit," "I once had a week," "I once had a friend," "I had nights like that too"). While some self-disclosure can build rapport, this frequency and depth violates professional boundaries and shifts focus from the patient to the therapist. The patient came seeking expert help, not to hear about the therapist's personal struggles.

**False equivalence**: By repeatedly comparing the therapist's experiences to the patient's, the therapist implied that a 51-year-old man's specific phobia with significant functional impairment is equivalent to the therapist's general stress or sleep issues. **Reassurance without substance**: The therapist repeatedly offered reassurance ("It'll get better, for sure," "things will improve," "You've got this," "You're stronger than you think") without providing any concrete evidence, rationale, or plan. **Positive aspects**: The therapist was warm, non-judgmental, and created a safe environment. The patient felt heard enough to disclose personal information and ended the session feeling "a little more hopeful." However, warmth alone does not constitute effective CBT.

**Summary:**

## 5. Collaboration

### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration was minimal and ineffective. True collaboration in CBT requires: (1) shared decision-making about treatment targets, (2) soliciting patient input on treatment approach, (3) working as partners to develop and test hypotheses, and (4) patient actively engaged in generating solutions.

This therapist did none of these. Instead, the therapist:

1. **Prescribed solutions unilaterally**: "Try to think positive," "focus on the good parts," "keep telling yourself 'this too shall pass,'" "try stepping outside for fresh air," "listen to your favorite music." These were presented as directives, not collaborative suggestions.

2. **Did not elicit patient preferences**: The therapist never asked, "What have you already tried?" "What approaches appeal to you?" "What barriers do you anticipate?" "What would be most helpful to focus on first?"

3. **Ignored patient feedback**: When the patient explicitly stated, "It's like no matter how much I want to look at the positive side, my body just won't listen," the therapist should have recognized this as feedback that positive thinking alone is insufficient and collaborated on a different approach.
