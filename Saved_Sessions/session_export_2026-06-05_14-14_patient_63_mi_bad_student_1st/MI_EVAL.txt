# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Complete absence of unconditional positive regard. Psychiatrist demonstrates explicit judgment and disapproval through character attacks ("You're just making excuses again," "stop feeling sorry for yourself"). Opens with authoritarian stance ("I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do") that establishes non-accepting framework. Dismisses patient's legitimate life stressors and emotional experience. No warmth, affirmation of worth, or normalization of struggle demonstrated. Violates foundational MI principle of acceptance throughout entire session.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Zero empathic responses and no reflective listening demonstrated. Psychiatrist makes no genuine effort to understand patient's perspective or emotional experience. When patient connects smoking to broader life overwhelm ("How am I supposed to focus on quitting when I can't even keep a job?"), psychiatrist dismisses as excuse-making rather than exploring with curiosity. Fails to recognize vulnerable disclosure about self-efficacy ("I don't even know if I believe I'm the kind of person who can quit for good"). Responds to expressions of feeling trapped with judgment rather than understanding. No validation of patient's struggle or acknowledgment of legitimate barriers.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains consistent focus on smoking cessation but uses coercive direction rather than collaborative guidance. Repeatedly attempts to move patient toward immediate action ("Are you going to start today?") through authority and pressure rather than strategic evocation. Multiple restarting of conversation (asking "do you smoke?" repeatedly) suggests either failure to maintain coherent direction or punitive reset when patient doesn't comply. Provides no exploration of patient's own goals, values, or reasons for change—only external pressure and consequences. Direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration entirely absent. Psychiatrist explicitly rejects collaborative stance in opening statement: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do." Expert-driven, authoritarian approach positions psychiatrist as sole source of knowledge and patient as passive recipient. Never seeks patient's input, explores his ideas about change, or co-constructs solutions. When patient offers perspective (patches made him feel worse, overwhelming circumstances make quitting difficult), psychiatrist dismisses rather than incorporates. Treats patient as object to be fixed rather than partner in problem-solving. Zero collaborative elements demonstrated.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy actively undermined throughout session. Repeated use of coercive language: "You have to quit immediately," "You have to stop this habit now," "You just have to have the willpower," "You're just choosing to harm yourself." Language of "have to" and "must" removes choice and frames psychiatrist's agenda as non-negotiable. Frames patient's current behavior as deliberate self-destruction, removing agency and replacing with shame. No acknowledgment of patient's freedom to choose differently. Repeated demands for immediate commitment ("Are you going to start today?") opposite of autonomy support. Pressuring approach likely to produce superficial compliance or resistance rather than internalized motivation.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation—core MI principle of drawing out client's own motivations and resources—entirely absent. Psychiatrist engages in pure imposition rather than exploration. Multiple openings for evocation missed: patient states "I wish I could just commit" and "Yeah, I want to quit smoking, or at least not feel trapped by it"—these are change talk sparks that should be amplified. Instead, psychiatrist ignores these statements and responds to sustain talk with lectures and pressure. Never asks exploratory questions: "What would it mean to you to quit?" "What's important about your health?" "What would be different if you weren't smoking?" Approach entirely tell-based rather than ask-based. Zero evocation of intrinsic motivation.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership completely absent. Psychiatrist explicitly assumes expert role and maintains it throughout: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do." Never positions patient as expert on his own life or seeks his wisdom about what has/hasn't worked. When patient explains patches made him feel worse, this expert knowledge about his own experience is dismissed as excuse-making. When patient connects smoking to broader life overwhelm, this insight into his own context is invalidated. Maintains hierarchical stance where psychiatrist knows what's best and patient's job is compliance. Repeated conversation resets suggest punitive approach rather than collaborative problem-solving.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Multiple instances of change talk present in patient's statements but never cultivated, amplified, or reinforced: "I know I should quit or cut back," "I wish I could just commit," "Yeah, I want to quit smoking, or at least not feel trapped by it." These genuine expressions of desire, ability, reasons, and need for change receive no reflective amplification. Psychiatrist ignores change talk entirely and responds to surrounding sustain talk with lectures and pressure. When patient says "I wish I could just commit," psychiatrist demands immediate commitment rather than exploring wish. Judgmental, pressuring approach actively suppresses rather than cultivates change talk. Patient's defensive position strengthens as session progresses.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk present throughout ("Every time I try, something goes wrong," "Nothing changes no matter what I do," "I don't believe I'm the kind of person who can quit"). Psychiatrist attempts to redirect away from sustain talk but uses confrontation and pressure rather than MI-consistent techniques like double-sided reflections or strategic questioning. Responds to "nothing changes no matter what I do" with "stop feeling sorry for yourself"—confrontational response that invalidates rather than softens. Repeated demands for immediate action ("Are you going to start today?") actually reinforce sustain talk by creating resistance. One positive element: psychiatrist does attempt to redirect focus by emphasizing health consequences, though done through fear and pressure rather than MI-consistent methods. Approach partially effective but actively creates resistance.

## Overall Session Assessment

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.2/5**

**Summary:** This session represents fundamental violation of Motivational Interviewing principles and MI spirit. Psychiatrist operates from expert-driven, authoritarian, judgmental stance that is actively counterproductive to evoking client motivation for change. Across all nine MI domains, session scores critically low (mostly 1/5), with minimal direction (2/5) and weak attempts to redirect sustain talk (2/5).

**Current MI Competency Level:** **No competence in Motivational Interviewing demonstrated.** This is not MI with minor gaps—this is systematic violation of core MI principles at nearly every turn. Characterized by judgment rather than acceptance, lectures rather than evocation, authority rather than partnership, dismissal rather than empathy, and suppression rather than amplification of change talk.

**Critical Findings:**
- Character attacks and confrontational language violate acceptance
- Zero reflective listening violates empathy
- Coercive direction violates collaboration and autonomy support
- Imposition of solutions violates evocation and partnership
- Suppression of change talk violates fundamental MI technique
- Repeated conversation resets suggest frustration or punitive approach

**Impact on Client Engagement:** Session is actively counterproductive. Rather than increasing intrinsic motivation, psychiatrist's approach increases defensiveness, reinforces hopelessness, and damages therapeutic alliance. Patient enters with ambivalence and sparks of change talk; exits more entrenched in position ("I don't think I'm starting with them today"). Research demonstrates that judgment, pressure, and loss of autonomy increase sustain talk and decrease change talk—exactly what occurred here.

**Recommendations for Practice:**
1. Complete formal MI training (MINT-certified)
2. Engage in regular supervision with MI expert
3. Record and review sessions to identify patterns of judgment and control
4. Practice reflective listening and evocative questioning in role-plays
5. Develop genuine acceptance and compassion for patients struggling with behavior change
6. Replace "have to" language with choice-supporting language
7. Amplify and reinforce all instances of change talk
8. Explore ambivalence with curiosity rather than confrontation
9. Connect behavior to broader life context
10. Eliminate repeated resets and punitive approaches

**Likelihood of Evoking Motivation for Change:** Very low. Current approach violates fundamental MI principle that motivation must be evoked from within client, not imposed from without. Without significant intervention, psychiatrist will continue to increase shame, defensiveness, and hopelessness rather than evoking intrinsic motivation for change.
