### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains basic focus on medication education and decision-making but direction becomes diffuse. Repeatedly redirects patient to external resources (doctor, pharmacist) rather than exploring patient's own ambivalence and readiness. Questions asked ("Have you started taking it?" "What might happen if you don't take it?") show some attempt at direction but lack strategic sequencing to build understanding of stage of change. Final plan constructed primarily by psychiatrist rather than co-created, directing toward compliance with external advice rather than clarified internal motivation.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Collaboration significantly compromised despite collaborative language. Psychiatrist assumes expert role with prescriptive advice dominating: "You might want to ask your doctor," "Maybe you should write down questions," "You might want to start keeping a diary." Pattern of "should" statements reflects unilateral problem-solving. Limited exploration of patient's own ideas or resources. Session plan largely constructed by psychiatrist with patient complying rather than co-creating. Power dynamic heavily weighted toward clinician as expert; genuine partnership absent.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Autonomy support is weak throughout. While not explicitly coercive, overall tone emphasizes compliance with external authority ("really important to follow your doctor's advice closely"). Repeated deflection to external resources leaves patient dependent on others rather than empowered to decide. Confidence expressed ("I'm confident you can handle this") subtly prescriptive—assumes patient will take medication rather than genuinely supporting right to choose. No explicit affirmation that decision ultimately belongs to patient. Framing of side effects as problems to solve rather than legitimate considerations in autonomous decision-making.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation nearly absent—fundamental MI failure. Psychiatrist rarely draws out patient's own motivations, values, or resources. Multiple missed opportunities: fails to explore reasons for considering medication beyond mood issues; never asks about values related to health or quality of life; doesn't follow up on confidence scaling with "What would move you from 5 to 7?"; no exploration of past successes or ambivalence; no amplification of change talk ("I'm willing to try if it can help"). Prescriptive responses to barriers replace evocative questioning. No strategic questions designed to help patient discover own reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Partnership significantly compromised. Psychiatrist positions self as expert with knowledge and solutions; patient positioned as recipient of advice. Expert-driven direction-setting throughout with minimal incorporation of patient's input or ideas. Asymmetrical knowledge sharing—psychiatrist's expertise valued over patient's self-knowledge. Unilateral session summary constructed by psychiatrist. Limited shared decision-making; psychiatrist doesn't ask "What feels right to you?" or "What matters most in your decision?" Implicit hierarchy maintained throughout; implicit pressure toward medication adherence despite stated openness to patient choice.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Change talk cultivation nearly absent—critical MI failure. Patient produces multiple change talk statements ("I'm willing to try if it can help," "I want benefits to outweigh risks," "I appreciate having a plan," "I want to be careful but also open to trying") but psychiatrist acknowledges without amplifying. No follow-up questions to deepen change talk. Confidence scaling used but not strategically explored ("What would move you from 5 to 7-8?"). Prescriptive responses to barriers replace evocative questioning that would strengthen patient's own motivation. No reflection of change talk in ways that deepen or reinforce commitment. Patient's intrinsic motivation for change remains underdeveloped.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist adequately avoids reinforcing sustain talk and doesn't argue with patient's concerns—appropriate and avoids righting reflex. Acknowledges concerns through simple reflection. Provides information-seeking suggestions to address underlying sustain talk about lack of knowledge. However, doesn't actively soften sustain talk through strategic techniques: no double-sided reflection acknowledging both concern and willingness; doesn't reframe caution as strength showing responsible health behavior; doesn't explore meaning underlying concerns; doesn't normalize worry as typical response to new medication; doesn't ask about confidence in managing side effects. Handling is passive and competent rather than actively skillful.

## **Overall Session Score: 1.9/5**

---

### **SUMMARY ASSESSMENT**

**Fundamental Issue:** This session violates core motivational interviewing principles. While the psychiatrist is warm, respectful, and provides practical advice, the approach prioritizes expert guidance over genuine partnership and evocation. The psychiatrist assumes expert role, provides prescriptive solutions, and fails to draw out patient's own motivations, values, and resources.

**Critical Deficiency:** The psychiatrist must shift from expert problem-solver to collaborative partner. This requires fundamental reorientation—from telling to asking, from providing solutions to evoking patient's thinking, from directing to partnering.

**Risk:** Without genuine MI practice, this approach risks creating compliance without commitment. Patient may follow the plan because psychiatrist suggested it, but has not developed own conviction that citalopram is right choice. If barriers arise, patient may abandon medication because motivation was externally driven rather than internally generated.

**Recommendation:** Commit to genuine MI training and practice. Record sessions, identify patterns of advice-giving and expert positioning, deliberately practice evoking questions. Seek feedback from MI trainer. Develop skill of genuine partnership and evocation—the heart of motivational interviewing.
