### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Collaboration is severely compromised throughout most of session. Opening intervention establishes explicitly hierarchical, non-collaborative stance with language positioning psychiatrist as expert with answers and client as passive recipient. Repeated directives ("You really should," "You have to," "You need to commit fully") reinforce authoritarian dynamic rather than partnership. When client expresses perspective, psychiatrist argues against it rather than collaboratively exploring. Questions function as challenges rather than genuine inquiries. Significant shift occurs mid-session with language "work together" and invitation for client to define manageable steps. Subsequent interventions continue collaborative tone. However, inconsistency and late emergence of collaboration represent significant deficiency in MI practice.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Autonomy support is severely compromised throughout session. Psychiatrist repeatedly pressures client toward predetermined outcome while minimizing right to choose alternative path. Opening language ("I'm going to tell you what you need to do") removes autonomy entirely. When client articulates preference for gradual reduction, psychiatrist does not respect this choice but argues against it. Demand to "promise me you'll stop drinking completely right now" pressures client toward specific outcome rather than supporting freedom to choose. Mid-session improvement evident when psychiatrist asks "What might feel like a manageable first step for you?" and affirms client-generated ideas. However, critical failure is that autonomy support should have been present from beginning. Initial violation of autonomy may cause client to feel preferences were dismissed and later support is conditional.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out client's own motivations, values, and reasons for change—is largely absent in opening exchanges. First intervention relies entirely on telling rather than asking. Psychiatrist provides external justifications (liver disease, heart issues) without exploring what client values or what might motivate him intrinsically. Early questions are primarily factual or confrontational rather than evocative, functioning as interrogation reinforcing clinician's predetermined agenda. Meaningful shift occurs mid-session with questions inviting client to explore own thinking about change. Client responds by articulating own ideas: cutting back during week, moderating weekends, alternative stress-management strategies, involving friends for accountability. Subsequent interventions continue evocative approach asking about confidence, barriers, and preferred strategies. Critical deficiency is that evocation should have been primary strategy from beginning, not introduced mid-session.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Partnership—conviction that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within client—is fundamentally violated in opening. Psychiatrist explicitly assumes expert role ("I'm going to tell you what you need to do") communicating that clinician possesses answers and client's role is compliance. When client expresses perspective, psychiatrist does not treat as valid contribution but argues against it. Early questions function as challenges to client's judgment rather than genuine inquiries into client's wisdom. Significant shift occurs mid-session with genuine fostering of partnership through language "work together" and invitation for client to define manageable steps. Subsequent interventions continue partnership stance with explicit statement "Remember, this is your journey, and I'm here to support you." However, critical failure is that partnership should have been established from beginning. Initial expert-driven stance undermines collaborative relationship and may cause client to feel contributions only valued when aligned with clinician's predetermined agenda.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2.5/5
**Comments:** Cultivating change talk—actively encouraging client's own language in favor of change and confidence—is largely absent in opening and only emerges in later portions. First three interventions provide reasons for change without strategically evoking or reinforcing client's own change talk. When client offers change talk ("I know drinking every day isn't exactly great for me"), psychiatrist does not amplify or build on statements but continues arguing for complete abstinence. Early questions do not strategically evoke change talk but function as factual inquiries or confrontations. Meaningful shift occurs when psychiatrist asks "What might feel like a manageable first step for you?" inviting client to articulate own ideas. Client responds with change talk about cutting back during week and moderating weekends. Psychiatrist reinforces through reflection. Subsequent interventions continue cultivating change talk through questions about confidence and preferred strategies. Client articulates multiple change talk statements. Psychiatrist demonstrates some skill in reinforcing through reflections and affirmations. However, critical deficiency is that change talk cultivation should have been primary strategy from beginning, not introduced mid-session.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3/5
**Comments:** Softening sustain talk—skillfully handling client arguments for status quo without confrontation—is moderately managed with notable inconsistencies. Client offers multiple sustain talk statements throughout session regarding relaxation, social connection, and not seeing drinking as serious problem. In opening exchanges, psychiatrist does not skillfully soften sustain talk but responds with direct contradiction triggering defensiveness. However, psychiatrist demonstrates improved skill mid-session using double-sided reflection validating concerns while implicitly acknowledging tension between benefits of drinking and risks. When client articulates sustain talk about confidence and barriers, psychiatrist does not argue or confront but asks collaborative questions inviting problem-solving. Psychiatrist demonstrates skill in redirecting sustain talk toward change talk by asking about helpful strategies. Client then generates own solutions. Psychiatrist could have been more strategic in softening sustain talk through deeper exploration of client's perspective with curiosity rather than immediate argument. Overall, psychiatrist demonstrates some skill but misses opportunities for deeper exploration of client's ambivalence.

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall Score: 2.1/5 – Below Average MI Competence**

### Key Findings:

**Critical Deficiency:** Session begins with fundamentally non-MI approach, explicitly adopting expert-driven, prescriptive stance that violates core MI principles of collaboration, autonomy support, and evocation. Opening three interventions characterized by directive language, confrontational questioning, and dismissal of client's perspective.

**Significant Strength:** Meaningful and important shift beginning in fourth intervention where psychiatrist transitions to collaborative, client-centered approach. From this point forward, clinician demonstrates improved reflective listening, genuine curiosity, and support for client-generated solutions. Client articulates multiple self-generated strategies and expresses increased willingness to engage in change.

**Critical Issue:** Inconsistency and timing. MI principles should be present from beginning, not introduced midway. Early non-MI approach may have damaged therapeutic relationship and triggered unnecessary defensiveness. While later collaborative stance is skillful and effective, it cannot fully compensate for initial violation of MI principles.

**Client Engagement Outcome:** Despite problematic opening, client ultimately engages in collaborative process and articulates own change goals and strategies, suggesting mid-session correction was sufficient to redirect interaction toward productive path. However, client-centered approach from beginning would likely have resulted in stronger engagement and more robust change talk.

### Recommendations for Improvement:

1. **Establish collaborative partnership from outset** using language communicating genuine curiosity about client's perspective
2. **Elicit client goals before prescribing outcomes** allowing client to define what change looks like
3. **Replace confrontation with curiosity** using questions inviting exploration rather than implying judgment
4. **Develop complex, emotionally attuned reflections** capturing emotional meaning and ambivalence
5. **Strategically evoke and amplify change talk** through targeted questions and reinforcement
6. **Respect and support client autonomy** explicitly affirming right to choose own path
7. **Avoid premature prescription of specific outcomes** allowing goals to emerge collaboratively
8. **Soften sustain talk through validation and exploration** not argument
9. **Maintain consistency in MI approach throughout session** from opening to close
10. **Deepen exploration of ambivalence** recognizing it as normal and valuable

---

**Conclusion:** Psychiatrist demonstrates capacity to apply MI principles effectively but shows significant deficiency in consistency and timing. Primary work ahead is developing consistent application of MI principles throughout entire session, recognizing that opening moments are critical for establishing collaborative partnership MI requires.
