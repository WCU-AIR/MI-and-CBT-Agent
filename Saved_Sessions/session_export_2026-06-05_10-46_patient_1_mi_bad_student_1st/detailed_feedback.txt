
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a complete absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly uses judgmental language, labeling Adrian's use as "a serious problem you need to fix right away" and later stating "That's exactly the kind of thinking that's holding you back." Rather than communicating respect for Adrian as a person, the clinician conveys disapproval and contempt for his perspective. Most critically, the psychiatrist explicitly rejects Adrian's self-assessment ("No, you don't get it," "That's not good enough," "No, that's not acceptable"), which communicates fundamental disrespect for the client's autonomy and lived experience.

The psychiatrist also normalizes Adrian's struggle by framing it as mere "excuse-making" rather than as a genuine expression of ambivalence—a core feature of the contemplation stage. When Adrian expresses fear ("it feels overwhelming, and honestly, kind of scary"), the clinician dismisses this emotional reality entirely: "There's no room for 'overwhelming' or 'scary' when your life is at stake." This is not acceptance; it is invalidation. The repeated use of "you have to," "you must," and "you need to" creates a coercive, authoritarian dynamic that is antithetical to MI spirit.

Additionally, the psychiatrist's opening statement—"I'm here to tell you what you need to do to improve your health, so no beating around the bush"—explicitly positions the clinician as the expert with answers, not as a partner respecting Adrian's worth and capacity for self-direction. There is no warmth, no affirmation of Adrian's strengths (e.g., his willingness to attend, his honesty about his ambivalence), and no genuine support for his struggle.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates zero acceptance. Judgmental language, dismissal of Adrian's perspective, invalidation of his emotions, and an authoritarian stance throughout create an environment of disrespect and coercion entirely inconsistent with MI principles.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective from their internal frame of reference. This psychiatrist demonstrates virtually none of this. Instead of reflecting Adrian's ambivalence—his recognition of risk alongside his fear of quitting, his need for the drug as a coping mechanism, his sense of being overwhelmed—the clinician repeatedly dismisses Adrian's statements as excuses.

When Adrian says, "It's just—like I said—this is the only thing I have that feels like a break or a change from the routine," the psychiatrist responds with "No, you have to stop justifying your use," rather than exploring what Adrian is communicating: that meth serves a psychological function (relief from boredom, routine, mental stagnation). A empathic response might be: "So it sounds like weekends without meth feel pretty empty or stuck for you—like you need something to break up the monotony. Help me understand what that's like."

Similarly, when Adrian expresses fear ("it feels overwhelming, and honestly, kind of scary"), the psychiatrist responds with "There's no room for 'overwhelming' or 'scary' when your life is at stake"—a response that invalidates rather than explores Adrian's emotional experience. Empathy would involve acknowledging the fear and exploring it: "It sounds like the idea of quitting feels really scary to you. What's the scariest part about it?"

The psychiatrist also fails to recognize or reflect Adrian's change talk. Adrian states: "I do care, or else I wouldn't be here talking to you" and "I don't want to die or mess up my life." These are important statements of concern and motivation that should be amplified through reflection. Instead, they are ignored entirely, and the focus remains on Adrian's "excuses."

There are no complex reflections, no emotional attunement, no genuine curiosity about Adrian's internal world. The psychiatrist is listening to respond with directives, not to understand.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no empathy. There is no reflective listening, no effort to understand Adrian's perspective, and active invalidation of his emotional experience. Critical change talk is ignored entirely.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

While the psychiatrist does maintain a clear focus on meth use and quitting, the *manner* in which direction is maintained is fundamentally inconsistent with MI. Direction in MI means strategically guiding the conversation toward change goals while remaining client-centered and collaborative. This psychiatrist maintains direction through coercion and demand, not through skillful evocation.

The psychiatrist does stay focused on the target behavior (meth use) and does not allow the conversation to wander. However, the approach is entirely prescriptive: "You need to quit immediately," "You have to stop making excuses," "This isn't up for negotiation." This is directive in the worst sense—it is authoritarian rather than collaborative.

A more MI-consistent approach to direction would involve: exploring Adrian's own reasons for change, asking about his values and what matters to him, clarifying discrepancies between his stated concerns (the infection) and his behavior, and allowing Adrian to articulate his own goals. Instead, the psychiatrist imposes a goal (immediate cessation) and demands compliance.

The repeated demand for a "yes or no" answer about quitting today is particularly problematic. It forces a false binary that ignores Adrian's genuine ambivalence and his stage of change (contemplation). Rather than using direction to help Adrian explore his own motivation, the psychiatrist uses it to demand capitulation.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior but does so through coercion and demand rather than collaborative exploration. Direction is present but executed in a way that undermines MI principles and client autonomy.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have valuable knowledge. This psychiatrist explicitly rejects collaboration from the opening statement: "I'm here to tell you what you need to do to improve your health" and "I'll be telling you exactly how to do it."

The power dynamic is entirely unequal. The psychiatrist holds all the authority and expertise; Adrian is positioned as a passive recipient of advice who is failing to comply. This is the opposite of collaboration.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates zero collaboration. The stance is explicitly expert-driven and authoritarian, with repeated dismissal of Adrian's perspective and no joint problem-solving or shared decision-making.

### 5. Autonomy Support
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Autonomy support means respecting the client's right to choose and actively fostering their perception of choice. This psychiatrist does the exact opposite—actively pressuring Adrian and minimizing his autonomy at every turn.

The language is consistently controlling: "You have to," "You must," "You need to," "You can't," "This isn't up for negotiation." These phrases communicate that Adrian does not have a choice, that the psychiatrist will decide what Adrian does, and that Adrian's preferences are irrelevant.

There is no acknowledgment that Adrian is the expert on his own life, that he has the right to make his own decisions (even decisions the psychiatrist disagrees with), or that change must come from Adrian's own motivation, not from external pressure. The entire approach assumes that the psychiatrist's job is to control Adrian's behavior, not to support Adrian's autonomy in making his own choices.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates zero autonomy support. The stance is explicitly controlling and pressuring, with repeated denial of Adrian's right to choose and active minimization of his agency.

### 6. Evocation
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Evocation is the core of MI—drawing out the client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources rather than imposing them. This psychiatrist does virtually no evocation. Instead, the psychiatrist tells Adrian what to do and why.

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Softening sustain talk means skillfully handling the client's arguments for the status quo without confrontation, using techniques like double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk while maintaining the collaborative relationship.

This psychiatrist does the exact opposite—the psychiatrist directly confronts Adrian's sustain talk, which strengthens it and damages the relationship.

Adrian's sustain talk includes:
- "I only do meth on weekends" (minimization)
- "It's not like I'm using every single day or anything" (comparison to worse use)
- "I just snort it a couple of times, maybe just to hang out with friends and have something to do" (normalization)
- "It's, like, manageable" (minimization)
- "I'm careful about when and how I use it" (illusion of control)
- "Quitting completely just sounds like overkill" (minimization)
- "Maybe one day, but not today" (delay)
- "I gotta figure things out at my own pace" (autonomy assertion)

Rather than softening this sustain talk through skillful reflection and reframing, the psychiatrist directly attacks it:

- "No, you don't get it. You can't use 'hard' or 'not ready' as an excuse."
- "No, you have to stop justifying your use."
- "Why are you still defending this habit when it's clearly harming you?"

Each of these responses is a direct confrontation that communicates: "Your perspective is wrong, and I'm right." In MI, this is known as the "confrontation-escalation cycle"—when the clinician confronts the client's sustain talk, the client typically escalates their sustain talk to defend their position. This is exactly what happens in this session.

Adrian's sustain talk becomes progressively more entrenched:

A MI-consistent approach to softening sustain talk might involve:

3. **Exploring the function:** "Help me understand what meth does for you on weekends. What would you be doing instead if you weren't using?"

Instead, the psychiatrist uses confrontation, which strengthens Adrian's defenses and his commitment to sustain talk.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates zero skill in softening sustain talk. Direct confrontation of Adrian's arguments for the status quo strengthens his defenses and entrenchment, creating a confrontation-escalation cycle that damages the therapeutic relationship and reduces motivation for change.
