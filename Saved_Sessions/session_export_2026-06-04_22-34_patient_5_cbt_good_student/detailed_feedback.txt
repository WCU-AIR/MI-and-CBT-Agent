
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

This document outlines a structured framework for assessing therapist competence in Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT). 
It is used for training, supervision, and evaluation. Each item is scored on a 0–6 scale:

0 = Poor
2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Develop a Comprehensive, Multimodal Treatment Strategy**
- **Action:** Before the next session, create a written case formulation that explicitly addresses: (a) physiological withdrawal symptoms, (b) cognitive factors (automatic thoughts, beliefs), (c) emotional factors (guilt, anxiety, fear), (d) behavioral factors (drinking context, avoidance), (e) environmental factors (marital stress, work stress), and (f) relapse prevention. Ensure the treatment strategy addresses all of these domains, not just cognition.
- **Example:** "Nicholas's drinking is maintained by: (1) physiological withdrawal (tremor, anxiety) that he manages with alcohol; (2) automatic thoughts ('I need a drink to calm down,' 'I'm not strong enough'); (3) guilt and shame that intensify the urge to drink; (4) avoidance of difficult emotions; (5) high-risk situation (sitting in front of TV after work); (6) marital conflict and fear of divorce. Treatment will address all of these through: cognitive restructuring, relaxation/mindfulness, emotion-regulation skills, behavioral activation, stimulus control, communication skills, and relapse prevention."
- **Practice:** Review the patient's presentation and identify all maintaining factors. Write out a one-page formulation that guides your treatment planning. Share this formulation with your supervisor or a peer for feedback.

### 2. **Implement Relaxation and Mindfulness Techniques for Physiological Withdrawal**
- **Action:** Teach the patient specific techniques to manage tremor, anxiety, and diaphoresis without alcohol. Options include: progressive muscle relaxation, diaphragmatic breathing, grounding exercises (5-4-3-2-1 technique), or brief mindfulness meditation. Provide a rationale: "Your body is experiencing withdrawal symptoms that feel very uncomfortable. Alcohol temporarily relieves these symptoms, but we can teach your body to calm down on its own. These techniques will help."
- **Example:** "Let's try a simple breathing exercise right now. Breathe in slowly for a count of 4, hold for 4, and breathe out for 4. Notice how your body feels. This is something you can do anytime you feel shaky or anxious, and it will help calm your nervous system without alcohol."
- **Practice:** Learn 2-3 relaxation techniques thoroughly so you can teach them skillfully. Practice them yourself so you can model them authentically. In the next session, teach the patient one technique and have him practice it with you. Assign it as homework with specific instructions (e.g., "Practice this breathing exercise 3 times per day for 2 minutes each time").

### 3. **Assign a Structured Thought Record as Written Homework**
- **Action:** Provide the patient with a written thought record (or "thought worksheet") that he can use to practice cognitive restructuring outside the session. The form should include columns for: (1) Situation/Trigger, (2) Automatic Thought, (3) Emotion/Intensity (0-10), (4) Evidence For, (5) Evidence Against, (6) Alternative Thought, (7) New Emotion/Intensity. Ask the patient to complete one thought record per day, focusing on situations where he feels the urge to drink.
- **Example:** "Here's a worksheet that will help you practice what we did today. When you feel the urge to drink, write down: What happened? What thought went through your mind? How anxious did you feel (0-10)? What evidence supports that thought? What evidence challenges it? What's a more balanced thought? How anxious do you feel now? Bring this to our next session so we can review it together."
- **Practice:** Create or find a simple, clear thought record template. Practice using it yourself with a personal example so you can explain it clearly. In the next session, review the patient's completed thought records and reinforce the skill. Troubleshoot any difficulties.

### 4. **Develop Specific, Detailed Behavioral Coping Plans**
- **Action:** Move beyond vague homework ("try a distraction") to specific, detailed plans. For each high-risk situation, develop a concrete action plan. Example: "When you feel the urge to drink after work, you will: (1) Pause for 10 minutes, (2) Use your alternative thought ('I'm stronger than my cravings'), (3) Do 5 minutes of breathing exercises, (4) Go for a 15-minute walk around the block, (5) Call your wife and tell her you're struggling, (6) If the urge is still strong, call your sponsor/therapist/crisis line." Write this down and have the patient carry it with him.
- **Example:** "Let's create a specific plan for tomorrow after work. You'll be sitting in front of the TV around 6 PM, and that's when you usually feel the urge. Here's what you'll do: First, you'll pause and notice the urge without acting on it. Then you'll remind yourself, 'I'm stronger than my cravings.' Then you'll do the breathing exercise we just practiced for 5 minutes. Then you'll go for a walk. If the urge is still strong, you'll call me or your wife. Let's write this down so you have it."
- **Practice:** For each patient, identify 2-3 high-risk situations and develop detailed action plans for each. Write them down. Review them with the patient and ensure he understands and commits to them. In the next session, ask specifically about each situation: "Did you encounter that situation? Did you use the plan? What happened?"

### 5. **Explicitly Address Emotion-Regulation and Avoidance**
- **Action:** Explore the emotion-regulation function of drinking more deeply. Ask: "What emotions do you drink to escape?" "What would happen if you didn't drink and just sat with those feelings?" "What emotions are hardest for you to tolerate?" Then teach specific emotion-regulation skills: distress tolerance (TIPP skills, self-soothing), emotion acceptance (mindfulness), or problem-solving for solvable problems. Explain: "Alcohol is a way to escape difficult emotions, but it doesn't solve the problem. We need to teach you how to tolerate and manage emotions without alcohol."
- **Example:** "You mentioned feeling guilty before you drink, and then drinking to forget the guilt. That makes sense—alcohol temporarily takes away the guilt. But it comes back, and then you feel more guilt about drinking. Instead, we can learn to sit with the guilt, understand what it's telling you, and decide what to do about it. For example, if you feel guilty about letting your wife down, maybe you could talk to her about it, or do something to make amends. That would actually reduce the guilt, rather than just escaping it."
- **Practice:** Learn emotion-regulation skills from DBT or other evidence-based sources. Practice teaching them to patients. In the next session, teach the patient one emotion-regulation skill and assign it as homework.

### 6. **Develop Explicit Relapse Prevention Planning**
- **Action:** Given the patient's history of failed quit attempts, develop a detailed relapse prevention plan. Identify: (1) High-risk situations (after work, weekends, marital conflict, etc.), (2) Early warning signs of relapse (increased cravings, isolation, negative thinking, etc.), (3) Coping strategies for each high-risk situation, (4) What to do if a lapse occurs (how to prevent it from becoming a full relapse), (5) Support resources (therapist, AA, crisis line, wife, etc.). Write this down and review it regularly.
- **Example:** "You've tried to quit before and it didn't work. That's important information. Let's figure out what happened those times and make sure we do things differently this time. What were the situations where you relapsed? What were you thinking and feeling? What could you have done differently? Let's make a plan so that if you encounter those situations again, you're prepared."
- **Practice:** Learn relapse prevention planning thoroughly. For each patient with substance use disorder, develop a written relapse prevention plan. Review it with the patient and ensure he understands it. Revisit it regularly in sessions.

### 7. **Increase Feedback-Seeking About the Therapeutic Process**
- **Action:** Systematically elicit feedback about the session and the therapeutic approach. Ask questions like: "How are you feeling about our approach so far?" "Is this making sense?" "Does this feel helpful?" "What's your reaction to the alternative thought we developed?" "Is there anything you'd like to do differently in our sessions?" "On a scale of 0-10, how helpful has this session been?" Adjust your approach based on the patient's feedback.
- **Example:** "Before we wrap up, I want to check in with you about how this session felt. Did it make sense? Was it helpful? Is there anything you'd like to do differently next time? Your feedback helps me be a better therapist for you."
- **Practice:** At the end of each session, ask for explicit feedback about the session. Listen carefully and adjust your approach accordingly. This demonstrates respect for the patient's perspective and increases engagement.

### 8. **Establish Clear Session Structure and Time Allocation**
- **Action:** At the beginning of each session, explicitly allocate time to agenda items. Example: "We have 50 minutes today. Let's spend 20 minutes reviewing your homework and how the week went, 20 minutes working on a new skill, and 10 minutes planning homework for next week. Does that work for you?" This helps manage time efficiently and ensures important topics are covered.
- **Example:** "Today I want to: (1) Check in on how the breathing exercises went (10 minutes), (2) Work on communication skills with your wife (25 minutes), (3) Plan homework (10 minutes), (4) Answer any questions (5 minutes). Does that sound good?"
- **Practice:** For each session, create a written agenda with time allocations. Share it with the patient at the beginning of the session. Adjust as needed based on patient priorities. This demonstrates structure and professionalism.

### 9. **Explicitly Check Understanding and Commitment to Homework**
- **Action:** Before ending the session, ask the patient to summarize the homework in his own words. Example: "Tell me back what you're going to do this week so I know we're on the same page." Also ask: "On a scale of 0-10, how confident are you that you'll do this homework?" If confidence is low, problem-solve barriers or adjust the homework to be more manageable.
- **Example:** "Okay, let's make sure we're on the same page about your homework. Can you tell me what you're going to do this week? ... Great. And how confident are you that you'll actually do it? ... A 5? What would help you feel more confident? What might get in the way?"
- **Practice:** At the end of each session, ask the patient to summarize homework. If he can't, clarify it. If confidence is low, problem-solve. This increases the likelihood that homework will actually be completed.

### 10. **Coordinate with Medical Management for Withdrawal**
