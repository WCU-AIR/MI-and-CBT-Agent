### Collaboration

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistent respect for Eric's right to choose with permissive language ("If you decided to try," "If you're gonna try this"). The clinician affirms Eric's self-knowledge and avoids prescriptive language. However, a subtle gap exists: the psychiatrist assumes commitment to change rather than explicitly affirming Eric's right to remain undecided or choose not to change. Language could more explicitly honor that "if you decide this isn't the right time, that's okay too."

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Moderate evocation with skillful use of past-success exploration, but significant missed opportunities to draw out Eric's own reasons for change and values. The psychiatrist generates reasons (health, wife's concern, future strength) rather than evoking them. Critical gaps include: insufficient exploration of what Eric himself feels about his wife's concerns, no inquiry into Eric's core values, and limited questions about Eric's own perspective on smoking risks. The session lacks deep exploration of intrinsic motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong partnership with genuine incorporation of Eric's preferences and acknowledgment of his self-knowledge. The opening question establishes partnership, and the clinician validates Eric's expertise about himself. However, clinician-generated solutions are presented before fully exploring Eric's own ideas, subtly positioning the clinician as expert. A more partnership-oriented approach would draw Eric's ideas first: "What do you think might help you handle those times when you usually smoke?"

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Moderate cultivation of change talk with some strategic amplification (e.g., highlighting the "maybe" as showing openness). However, significant gaps exist: insufficient exploration of intrinsic desire for change, weak amplification when Eric says "maybe I could cut back" (could deepen with "What makes you think you could actually do this?"), and no explicit commitment language. The psychiatrist relies on Eric's spontaneous change talk rather than strategically evoking it through targeted questions about reasons, ability, and commitment.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Moderate skill in softening sustain talk through double-sided reflections and gentle reframing. However, significant gaps include: insufficient exploration of sustain talk content (e.g., when Eric says "I feel fine" multiple times), weak response to minimization patterns, and limited use of strategic questioning to introduce doubt. The psychiatrist acknowledges sustain talk but doesn't deeply challenge it. Questions like "What does the doctor see that concerns them?" or "What would happen if you continued smoking for 10 more years?" could gently introduce doubt without confrontation.

## **Overall Clinical Summary**

**Current Competency Level: 3.7/5 (Adequate to Good)**

### Strengths
- Strong collaborative stance with consistent invitation of client input and incorporation of feedback
- Consistent acceptance and warmth maintaining nonjudgmental, supportive tone throughout
- Strategic use of scaling questions to clarify importance and confidence
- Skillful exploration of past successes and connection to current situation
- Appropriate pacing that respects ambivalence without pushing too hard
- Good reflective listening with accurate perspective-taking

### Critical Areas for Improvement
1. **Insufficient evocation of intrinsic motivation:** The psychiatrist presents reasons for change rather than drawing them from Eric. Deeper exploration of Eric's own reasons, values, and what matters most is needed.

2. **Premature action planning:** After Eric rates importance at only 3-4, the session quickly moves to action planning. More time exploring ambivalence and values would be more strategically sound.

3. **Weak amplification of change talk:** When Eric expresses change talk, validation occurs but strategic deepening is absent. Follow-up questions to strengthen statements are needed.

4. **Insufficient softening of sustain talk:** Eric's minimization ("I feel fine," "nothing too bad") is acknowledged but not explored. Strategic questioning could gently introduce doubt.

5. **Limited values exploration:** The psychiatrist never asks what matters most to Eric—his health, relationships, work ability, independence? Connecting smoking to core values would increase motivation.

6. **Clinician-generated solutions:** Strategies are presented before fully exploring Eric's own ideas, subtly positioning the clinician as expert rather than co-creator.

### Impact on Client Engagement
The psychiatrist's approach maintains Eric's engagement and prevents defensiveness, supporting the therapeutic relationship. However, the session is unlikely to significantly increase intrinsic motivation for change. Eric leaves with a plan to cut back but with low importance (3-4) and tentative commitment ("I could give that a shot"). While this represents progress with a pre-contemplative client, deeper ambivalence exploration and values clarification would likely result in stronger motivation and more genuine commitment.

### Recommendations for Development
- Prioritize evoking over educating; ask questions that help Eric discover his own reasons for change
- Extend ambivalence exploration before action planning; ask "What would move you from a 3 to a 5?"
- Draw out client's own solutions first before offering clinician-generated options
- Strategically amplify change talk with follow-up questions: "Tell me more about that"
- Use strategic questioning to soften sustain talk rather than reflection alone
- Explicitly explore values and connect smoking to what matters most to Eric
- Check explicit commitment rather than assuming agreement means commitment
- Increase ratio of questions to statements; aim for 70% questions, 30% statements
