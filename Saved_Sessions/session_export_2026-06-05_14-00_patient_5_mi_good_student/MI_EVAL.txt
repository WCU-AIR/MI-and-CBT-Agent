### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist makes some effort to evoke Aizada's own motivations and resources but does so inconsistently. Good evocative moments include "What would you say motivates you most to keep advocating for yourself?" which draws out intrinsic motivation (wanting life back, family connection). The question "Can you tell me about a time when you've managed difficult symptoms before?" successfully evokes coping resources. However, evocation is limited in critical ways. The psychiatrist doesn't systematically explore reasons for change or what would be different if symptoms improved. Most significantly, when Aizada mentions that pain improved during her son's wedding and worsens when her husband is away, the psychiatrist validates but doesn't deeply explore: "What do you make of that pattern? What does that tell you about what might be happening?" This would evoke her own insight rather than the psychiatrist interpreting it. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to ask "What ideas do you have?" before offering suggestions.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates some partnership elements but falls short of true power-sharing. While open questions and invitations for perspective are present, the psychiatrist frequently interprets Aizada's experience rather than co-constructing understanding. Examples include "It sounds like you've been feeling dismissed and maybe even a bit invalidated," "It's like there's this push and pull between what you feel physically and what you wonder about emotionally," and "You're holding two important truths here." While accurate, these represent the psychiatrist's interpretation rather than genuine co-construction. True partnership would involve more back-and-forth: "How would you describe what's happening for you?" The psychiatrist doesn't explicitly acknowledge Aizada's expertise about her own body. The overall stance is more "expert interpreter of your experience" than true partnership where both parties' knowledge is equally valued.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist elicits some change talk but does so inconsistently and doesn't strategically amplify or reinforce it. Aizada produces important change talk: "I want to be well again," "I want my life back," "I want to enjoy time with my family without worrying all the time," and "I'm willing to give it a shot." However, the psychiatrist's response is minimal. When Aizada says "I want to be well again," the psychiatrist reflects but doesn't amplify with deeper questions like "Tell me more about what having your life back would look like." When Aizada mentions improved symptoms during her son's wedding, the psychiatrist validates but doesn't amplify: "So when you were focused on something meaningful and joyful, your body responded differently. What does that suggest to you?" The psychiatrist doesn't explicitly reinforce or celebrate change talk when it emerges. Change talk is present but underutilized; the psychiatrist could draw out much more motivation through deeper exploration and explicit reinforcement.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates some skill in gently redirecting away from sustain talk but doesn't do so consistently or strategically. Aizada produces several sustain talk statements: "It's hard for me to just accept that it might not be something physical," "I just don't believe it," "I'm worried the pain will get worse if I push myself too much," and "I'm afraid of being told it's all in my head." When Aizada expresses worry about making symptoms worse, the psychiatrist validates this concern rather than exploring whether this worry is based on evidence. The psychiatrist doesn't address Aizada's fear of being dismissed or told "it's all in your head"—a significant sustain talk barrier that could be softened by explicit reassurance. When Aizada expresses worry about failure, the psychiatrist doesn't directly address this. The approach is more "validate and move on" rather than "strategically soften the sustain talk." The psychiatrist could use more double-sided reflections to help Aizada examine competing evidence.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 3.6/5 (Adequate to Good)**

**Session Strengths:**
- Strong reflective listening with several complex, accurate reflections capturing ambivalence
- Consistent validation without judgment; maintains respectful, nonjudgmental stance throughout
- Good autonomy support through open questions and non-prescriptive language
- Appropriate pacing allowing Aizada to share her story and gradually explore new perspectives
- Concrete behavioral step collaboratively developed (walks with husband) building on her observations
- Avoids confrontation about health anxiety; doesn't dismiss concerns or push psychological explanations prematurely
- Identifies key motivations (wanting life back, family connection) that could drive change

**Areas for Improvement:**
- Insufficient evocation and amplification of change talk; validates motivations but doesn't deeply explore them
- Limited strategic reinforcement of change statements; misses opportunities to strengthen motivation
- Inadequate softening of sustain talk; worries are validated but not strategically explored or reframed
- Interpretation rather than co-construction; frequently interprets Aizada's experience rather than inviting her to describe it
- Limited partnership; doesn't fully position Aizada as expert on her own experience
- Incomplete exploration of stress-symptom connection; validates observations but doesn't deeply explore what patterns mean
- Vague change goal; session doesn't explicitly clarify what is being worked toward
- Insufficient reassurance about exploring psychological factors without invalidating physical symptoms

**Likelihood of Evoking Sustained Client Motivation for Change: Moderate**

Aizada leaves with a concrete behavioral step and some openness to exploring stress, which is positive. However, her core ambivalence remains largely unresolved. Without deeper exploration of motivations and strategic softening of sustain talk, she may not sustain behavioral change or develop genuine openness to psychological factors. The warm, validating approach is essential for rapport but must be combined with more strategic evocation to create meaningful momentum toward change.

**Recommendations for Development:**
1. Systematically evoke and amplify change talk through deeper exploratory questions
2. Explicitly reassure about exploring psychological factors without invalidating physical symptoms
3. Deepen exploration of stress-symptom patterns she has already noticed
4. Co-construct understanding rather than interpret her experience
5. Strategically soften sustain talk by exploring evidence and reframing concerns
6. Explicitly clarify and collaboratively agree on change goals early in sessions
7. Use scaling questions more strategically for evoking additional change talk
8. Invite Aizada's ideas before offering suggestions or behavioral steps
9. Strengthen affirmation and celebration of change talk and effort
10. Develop skill in exploring discrepancies between observations and beliefs without confrontation
