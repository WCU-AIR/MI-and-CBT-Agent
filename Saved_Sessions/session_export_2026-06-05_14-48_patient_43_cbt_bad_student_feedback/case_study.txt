--- DISORDER ---
Gambling Disorder

--- CASE STUDY ---

Tomás Zambrano, a 36-year-old married first-generation Hispanic man, presented to a gambling clinic at a major medical center for evaluation and treatment. He is a high school football coach and has a 5-year-old son. He denied prior psychiatric or substance use disorders.

Mr. Zambrano was appropriately dressed and groomed, expressed himself fluently in English and Spanish, spoke in normal tone and volume, and demonstrated intact cognitive function and average intelligence.

He had been betting on sports and playing cards since childhood, noting that gambling "is part of our culture." His father frequently played poker, and Mr. Zambrano shared these early poker sessions as bonding experiences. He increasingly used poker as a way to relieve work-related stress, enjoying the excitement, intellectual challenge, and competition.

Over the past 2 years, he began losing more money than he could afford and escalated both the frequency and stakes of his poker games. He chased losses, believing that odds would favor him, and continued playing when winning, convinced he was "on a streak." Losses made him feel unworthy, stupid, and irritable, yet he persisted, attempting to fine-tune his strategy. Attempts to cut back caused irritability and preoccupation, leading him to resume gambling.

By the time of presentation, nightly poker led to daytime fatigue and poor job performance. He was preoccupied with his next game, and his family life suffered. His wife discovered that he had used his son’s college fund and accumulated $30,000 in credit card debt. Feeling sad and desperate after his wife threatened divorce, he sought treatment.

Discussion: Mr. Zambrano demonstrates preoccupation with gambling, recurrent chasing of losses, escalation of bets, lying to his wife, and jeopardized work and family relationships. He meets at least five of nine DSM-5 criteria for gambling disorder. He denied manic symptoms, ruling out gambling secondary to bipolar disorder. Cultural and familial influences, including a father who gambled, may have contributed to his disorder. Assessment of comorbidity is recommended, although he denies other psychiatric symptoms.
