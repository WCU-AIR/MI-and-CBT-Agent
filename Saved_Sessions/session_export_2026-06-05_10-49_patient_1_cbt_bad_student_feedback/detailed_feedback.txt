
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Feedback: Therapist Evaluation

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made no attempt to establish a collaborative agenda at the beginning of the session or at any point thereafter. A proper CBT agenda would involve: (1) explicitly reviewing the presenting problem and DSM-5 ASD symptoms Dylan is experiencing, (2) collaboratively prioritizing which symptoms to address first (intrusive memories, hypervigilance, avoidance, sleep disturbance, concentration problems), (3) establishing time-limited, measurable goals for the session, and (4) allocating time across topics. Instead, the therapist opened with a vague, unstructured greeting ("Hey there! How's it going today?") and allowed the session to drift reactively through Dylan's spontaneous disclosures without ever establishing priorities or structure.

The session lacks any evidence of strategic planning. Dylan presents with multiple ASD symptoms—intrusive imagery, nightmares, hypervigilance, avoidance behaviors, sleep disturbance, irritability, and concentration problems—yet the therapist never identifies which symptom cluster to target first or why. There is no discussion of treatment goals, timeline, or what success would look like. The therapist does not return to an agenda after Dylan's initial disclosure, meaning the session becomes entirely patient-led without therapeutic direction. By the second session opening ("Hey there! It's good to see you. How have you been holding up since we last talked?"), the therapist again fails to set an agenda, simply asking an open-ended question without establishing what will be accomplished in that session.

This is a critical failure in CBT structure. Agenda-setting is foundational to evidence-based CBT and serves multiple functions: it provides structure, ensures collaborative decision-making, maximizes efficient use of time, and communicates to the patient that the therapist has a coherent treatment plan. Without it, sessions become unfocused and fail to build toward measurable change.

**Summary:**

No agenda was established in either session opening. The therapist failed to collaboratively prioritize Dylan's multiple ASD symptoms or establish session goals, resulting in reactive, unstructured sessions without strategic direction.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist elicited virtually no feedback from Dylan about the session itself, his understanding of interventions, or the helpfulness of suggestions. Proper feedback elicitation in CBT involves: (1) asking Dylan if he understands the rationale for interventions, (2) checking whether suggestions feel relevant or feasible, (3) soliciting his reactions to the session content, (4) asking what he found most/least helpful, and (5) adjusting approach based on his input.

Instead, the therapist made repeated suggestions (breathing exercises, warm tea, calming music, positive thinking, getting outside, talking to friends, resuming hobbies) without ever asking Dylan whether these felt realistic, whether he understood why they might help, or whether he had concerns about implementing them. When Dylan explicitly expressed doubt ("Deep breathing sounds like something I could try—I'm just not sure if it'll actually calm me down"), the therapist did not explore this concern or adjust the recommendation. When Dylan said "Saying 'this too shall pass' feels... kind of empty, honestly," the therapist did not acknowledge this feedback or modify the approach—instead, the therapist continued offering similar platitudes in subsequent exchanges.

The therapist never asked Dylan to summarize what he learned, never checked his commitment to suggested activities, and never asked whether the session was helpful. At the end of the first session, the therapist simply concluded with encouragement ("Keep your head up and try to focus on the positives") without eliciting Dylan's thoughts on the session or his confidence in the recommendations. This represents a fundamental failure to engage Dylan as a collaborative partner and to gather critical information about whether interventions are landing effectively.

**Summary:**

The therapist did not elicit feedback about session content, Dylan's understanding of recommendations, or his reactions to suggestions. Dylan's expressed doubts were not explored or addressed, representing a missed opportunity for collaborative problem-solving.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated surface-level understanding of Dylan's explicit statements but consistently missed the deeper meaning and emotional reality of his experience. Dylan repeatedly communicated specific, clinically significant information that the therapist failed to grasp or respond to appropriately.

**What was understood:** The therapist accurately reflected that Dylan is experiencing sleep problems, anxiety around cars, difficulty concentrating, and avoidance of discussion. These explicit statements were acknowledged.

**What was missed—critical gaps:**

1. **Intrusive imagery and trauma processing:** Dylan stated, "Every time I hear a car or a loud noise, I get all jumpy. I keep seeing that crash over and over in my head. It's like I can't escape it." He later elaborated: "When I'm standing by the driveway waiting to cross the street or something, my heart just pounds and I can't stop thinking about if another car is gonna hit me." And crucially: "Like the accident happening over and over" and "flashes of my friend getting hurt." These are hallmark ASD symptoms requiring trauma-focused intervention. The therapist never acknowledged the intrusive nature of these images, never explored their frequency or intensity, and never explained that these are normal trauma responses requiring specific treatment (not just positive thinking).

2. **Hypervigilance and threat perception:** Dylan described being "stuck on this constant alert mode, and I can't seem to turn it off." This is hypervigilance—a core ASD symptom. The therapist's response ("just try to think positive") completely misses that Dylan's nervous system is dysregulated and perceiving threat everywhere. Positive thinking cannot override a hypervigilant nervous system; this requires psychoeducation about trauma responses and evidence-based interventions like gradual exposure or cognitive processing.

3. **Avoidance as safety behavior:** Dylan stated, "I try to avoid busy streets or places with lots of cars" and "I don't want to keep feeling like this forever." The therapist did not recognize that avoidance, while understandable, maintains ASD symptoms and prevents habituation. No discussion occurred about the relationship between avoidance and symptom persistence.

4. **Shame and social withdrawal:** Dylan disclosed, "I haven't really talked to my friends about it. I guess I don't want them to think I'm weak or something. And with my family, I get kind of snappy when they ask if I'm okay, so I just shut down or get annoyed." This reveals shame, isolation, and irritability—all ASD symptoms. The therapist's response ("it's really brave of you to share that") was generic empathy without exploring the shame or its impact on his support system.

5. **Anhedonia and behavioral withdrawal:** Dylan said, "I used to like playing video games and skateboarding, but since the accident, I just don't feel like doing any of that... It's frustrating because I want to enjoy those things again, but it feels like my brain is stuck on this constant alert mode." This is anhedonia and behavioral avoidance—not laziness. The therapist's suggestion to "force yourself to do little things" shows some understanding but lacks the clinical sophistication to recognize this as a symptom requiring structured behavioral activation, not willpower.

6. **Emotional dysregulation and irritability:** Dylan's irritability toward family ("I get kind of snappy") is an ASD symptom, not a character flaw. The therapist never explored this or explained it as a normal trauma response.

The therapist's repeated self-disclosure ("I remember when I was stressed," "When I was feeling down," "I remember once I had a really bad week") further demonstrates misunderstanding. Dylan's trauma is not equivalent to the therapist's stress or bad week. This conflation minimizes his experience and suggests the therapist does not grasp the severity of ASD or its distinction from normal stress.

**Summary:**

The therapist understood Dylan's surface complaints but missed the clinical significance of intrusive imagery, hypervigilance, avoidance patterns, shame, anhedonia, and emotional dysregulation. Repeated self-disclosure suggested the therapist did not differentiate trauma from normal stress.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Implement Trauma-Focused CBT (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Immediately shift to evidence-based trauma treatment. Select either Prolonged Exposure (PE), Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT), or trauma-focused CBT based on training and resources. These are the gold standard for ASD and PTSD.
- **Example:** "Dylan, I want to explain what's happening in your brain after the accident. Your brain is stuck in a threat-detection mode—it's constantly scanning for danger because of what you experienced. This is a normal response to trauma, but we need to help your brain learn that you're safe now. We're going to do something called Prolonged Exposure. This means we'll gradually help you face the situations and memories that are causing you anxiety, so your brain can learn that they're not actually dangerous. This is the most effective treatment for what you're experiencing. Does that make sense?"
- **Practice:** Obtain training in PE or CPT through evidence-based resources (e.g., ISTSS, VA/DoD guidelines). Practice with supervision. Role-play the psychoeducation component with a colleague.

### 2. **Provide Psychoeducation About ASD and Trauma Responses**
- **Action:** Educate Dylan about Acute Stress Disorder, explaining that his symptoms (intrusive imagery, hypervigilance, avoidance, sleep disturbance, irritability) are normal responses to trauma, not signs of weakness or pathology. Explain the neurobiological basis: the amygdala (threat-detection center) is overactive, the prefrontal cortex (rational thinking) is underactive, and the body is stuck in fight-flight-freeze mode.
- **Example:** "After a traumatic event like the accident you experienced, your brain's threat-detection system gets stuck in overdrive. Your amygdala—the part of your brain that detects danger—is constantly sending alarm signals, even when you're safe. That's why you jump at loud noises, why you have nightmares, and why you feel anxious around cars. This is a normal response to trauma, and it's not your fault. The good news is that there are specific treatments that help your brain learn that you're safe. We're going to work together to help your brain reset."
- **Practice:** Develop a written psychoeducation handout for Dylan. Practice explaining trauma responses in age-appropriate language. Use diagrams or analogies (e.g., "Your threat-detection system is like a smoke alarm that's too sensitive—it's going off even when there's no fire").

### 3. **Establish Clear Treatment Goals and Measurement**
- **Action:** Collaboratively establish specific, measurable treatment goals. Identify baseline symptom severity using standardized measures (e.g., PTSD Checklist for DSM-5, Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale). Track progress throughout treatment.
- **Example:** "Let's talk about what we want to accomplish. One goal might be: 'By the end of treatment, Dylan will be able to sit in a car without significant anxiety.' Another might be: 'Dylan will be able to practice driving on quiet streets.' Another might be: 'Dylan will sleep through the night without nightmares at least 4 nights per week.' We'll also track how many nights you sleep well and how often you have nightmares. This way, we can see if the treatment is working and adjust if needed."
- **Practice:** Familiarize yourself with trauma-specific outcome measures. Practice collaboratively setting SMART goals (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound). Create a tracking sheet for Dylan to monitor symptoms weekly.

### 4. **Develop a Symptom Hierarchy and Exposure Plan**
- **Action:** Create a hierarchy of feared situations and trauma reminders, ranked by anxiety level (0-100). Plan graduated in vivo exposure, starting with lower-anxiety situations and progressing to higher-anxiety situations.
- **Example:** "Let's make a list of situations that make you anxious. We'll rank them from least to most anxiety-provoking. We'll start with the least anxiety-provoking situation and gradually work our way up. Each time you face a situation, your anxiety will decrease over time. This is called habituation. We'll practice each situation until your anxiety drops by at least 50% before moving to the next one."
- **Practice:** Learn the principles of exposure therapy. Practice creating hierarchies with other patients or in role-play. Understand the rationale: repeated exposure to feared situations in a safe context allows the brain to learn that the situation is not actually dangerous, reducing anxiety over time.

### 5. **Plan Imaginal Exposure for Trauma Memory Processing**
