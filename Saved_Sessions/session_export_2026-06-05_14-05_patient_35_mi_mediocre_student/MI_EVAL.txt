# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal acceptance demonstrated. While the opening is warm, the clinician quickly pivots to a change agenda without validating Dylan's perspective or concerns about the referral. Dylan's strengths (gym attendance, academic management) are glossed over. No genuine affirmation of worth or acknowledgment of his resistance as normal. The clinician prioritizes the change agenda over relationship-building, failing to establish unconditional positive regard.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Empathy is largely absent. The clinician asks factual questions rather than reflecting Dylan's perspective back to him. Key statements—"I don't see why I need to change," "I'm almost 21," "I just want to have a good time with my friends"—are never explored or reflected. When the clinician attempts reflection ("Yeah, I get that it's part of college life"), it is immediately followed by advice, undermining genuine empathic understanding. Dylan's values and concerns remain unexamined.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Direction exists but is generic and disconnected from the presenting problem. The clinician maintains focus on health and lifestyle changes but never directly addresses the student conduct violation that prompted the referral. The session treats this as general wellness optimization rather than addressing the specific incident. No exploration of what the student conduct office is concerned about or why Dylan was referred. Direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration is virtually absent. The clinician unilaterally sets the agenda without asking Dylan what he wants to discuss. Questions are rapid-fire and factual, leaving no space for Dylan's priorities to emerge. Solutions are prescribed without exploration ("You might want to try cutting back," "Maybe you should set goals"). When Dylan expresses low motivation, the clinician argues rather than collaborates. Dylan is positioned as a passive recipient of clinical guidance, not an equal partner.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy is actively suppressed. Prescriptive language dominates ("should," "would probably help," "you might want to"). Dylan's stated position that he doesn't need change is not respected; instead, the clinician argues for change. When Dylan expresses ambivalence or uncertainty, this is glossed over rather than explored. No explicit affirmation of Dylan's right to choose. The clinician subtly pressures Dylan toward predetermined outcomes while minimizing his agency and self-determination.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation is essentially absent. The clinician provides reasons for change rather than asking Dylan to generate his own. Dylan's values (friendship, belonging, enjoyment) are never explored. No questions designed to elicit Dylan's own motivations, strengths, or resources. The clinician tells Dylan what would help rather than asking what Dylan thinks might help. Dylan is never given the opportunity to discover his own reasons for change, which is the heart of MI.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership is fundamentally absent. The clinician assumes the expert role throughout, determining the agenda, asking questions, offering advice, and making decisions about Dylan's treatment. Dylan is positioned as a subject to be evaluated, not a partner with valuable knowledge about his own life. Unilateral goal-setting, clinician-driven accountability, and expert-centered language reinforce hierarchy rather than partnership. No genuine sharing of expertise or honoring of Dylan's self-knowledge.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk is minimal and not strategically cultivated. Limited change talk emerges ("I guess having a couple of booze-free days doesn't sound too bad," "I'll give it a shot"), but it is not amplified or reinforced through follow-up questions. The clinician relies on advice-giving rather than evoking Dylan's own reasons for change. No strategic use of open questions to elicit change talk. When Dylan rates confidence at 5/10, this ambivalence is not explored to identify what would increase motivation.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk is present throughout ("I don't see why I need to change," "I'm almost 21," "Everyone drinks at college parties," "When my friends are drinking, it's hard not to join in") but is not skillfully softened. The clinician sometimes argues against sustain talk ("But maybe you should think about..."), which typically strengthens resistance. Double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic exploration are absent. Sustain talk is either ignored or inadvertently validated, allowing it to persist unchallenged.

---

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall Score: 1.6/5**

### Strengths
- Warm, non-confrontational opening establishes basic rapport and safety
- Maintains calm demeanor throughout, avoiding harsh judgment or escalation
- Shows some organizational structure by maintaining general focus on health/lifestyle changes
- Accepts Dylan's low motivation without escalation and offers modest, achievable goals

### Critical Deficiencies
- **Expert-centered rather than client-centered:** Clinician drives agenda, asks questions, provides advice, and makes decisions without genuine partnership
- **No evocation of intrinsic motivation:** Clinician provides reasons for change rather than inviting Dylan to discover his own
- **Presenting problem not addressed:** Session never directly addresses the student conduct violation that prompted the referral
- **Sustain talk not softened:** Dylan's resistance and reasons against change are never strategically addressed
- **Autonomy minimized:** Clinician subtly pressures Dylan toward predetermined outcomes
- **Empathy limited:** Clinician asks questions and offers advice but rarely reflects Dylan's perspective back to him
- **Collaboration absent:** Dylan is positioned as passive recipient rather than equal partner

### Impact on Client Engagement
Dylan's agreement to try "a couple of no-drinking days" represents compliance, not genuine motivation. His commitment is tentative ("I'm not really sure if I'd stick with it," "No promises though"). Underlying resistance remains unaddressed—Dylan still doesn't see a need for change, still minimizes the incident, and still values his social life and drinking with friends. The psychiatrist's expert stance may have actually increased Dylan's defensiveness.

### Key Recommendations
1. Directly address the student conduct violation and Dylan's perspective on it
2. Shift from advice-giving to evocation through open-ended questions
3. Explore and validate Dylan's values (friendship, enjoyment, autonomy)
4. Use double-sided reflections to soften sustain talk without confrontation
5. Amplify and reinforce change talk when it emerges
6. Explicitly support Dylan's autonomy and right to choose
7. Develop genuine empathic reflections that demonstrate understanding
8. Help Dylan discover discrepancy between his values and behavior
9. Establish true collaboration and partnership in problem-solving
10. Seek supervision and record sessions to monitor MI fidelity
