--- DISORDER ---

Major depressive disorder (MDD)

Mild neurocognitive disorder due to multiple etiologies

--- CASE STUDY ---

Betty Drucker, a 76-year-old white woman, presented with sad mood, loss of interest in previously enjoyed activities, neglect of personal appearance and household responsibilities, feelings of insecurity in social interactions, and difficulties with concentration and word finding. She reduced social contacts, stopped attending her senior center, and discontinued her weekly card game due to lack of pleasure and poor concentration. She lost 7 pounds over 2 months and experienced insomnia with ruminative thoughts. This was her first psychiatric episode.

She had retired 10 years prior after a successful legal career and had been divorced for 30 years. Her two children and three grandchildren lived nearby, and she had been interacting with them frequently before symptom onset.

Medical history included hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and coronary artery disease treated with a stent. Current medications were hydrochlorothiazide, olmesartan, atorvastatin, and low-dose aspirin. She smoked half a pack per day for ~30 years.

On examination, Ms. Drucker appeared disheveled and drained. She was slow to respond, had a depressed and anxious mood, and preoccupation with finances, though she could be temporarily reassured. Cognitive testing showed: recall of 2/4 objects at 3 minutes, poor semantic fluency, impaired alternating word generation, uneven spacing in clock drawing with correct hands, MMSE score of 24, and Stroop Test 22/22. Neurological exam was unremarkable except for mild left mouth droop. Head CT revealed pronounced periventricular and subcortical white matter hyperintensities.

Discussion: Ms. Drucker demonstrates late-life depression with executive dysfunction, which may reflect depression-executive dysfunction syndrome, often influenced by vascular risk factors. Cognitive impairment may be partially secondary to depression but may also reflect early-stage mild neurocognitive disorder. Her vascular history, white matter changes, psychomotor retardation, and executive dysfunction suggest a vascular contribution. Alzheimer's disease or mixed-etiology neurocognitive disorder is possible; Lewy body disease, Parkinson’s disease, and frontotemporal dementia are less likely given absent extrapyramidal signs, autonomic instability, or personality changes.
