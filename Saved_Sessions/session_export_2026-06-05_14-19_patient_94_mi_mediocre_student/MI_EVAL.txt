### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Autonomy support is minimal and inconsistently applied. While avoiding overt coercion, persistent subtle pressure undermines patient's sense of choice and agency. Prescriptive language ("But maybe you'd feel better if...," "Maybe you should try...") signals disagreement with patient's stated preferences. When patient states low priority, clinician overrides with "maybe you should still think about it, because it would probably help." One moment of autonomy affirmation ("Keep it simple and take it at your own pace") comes only after significant pressure applied. Never explicitly states "This is your decision" or "You're in control here."

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out patient's own motivations, values, and resources—is almost entirely absent. Clinician tells rather than asks. Missed opportunities abound: when patient mentions enjoying pub, clinician could explore what's important about that but instead prescribes exercise; when patient expresses low priority, clinician could ask what would need to change but instead applies pressure. No strategic questioning to help patient discover own reasons for change or explore values and goals. Most problematically, never explores actual presenting concern—alcohol use—at all. Zero evocation of patient's thoughts, feelings, or motivations regarding drinking.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership is fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist assumes and maintains expert role throughout with no genuine power-sharing or co-creation. Clinician decides what problem is (lack of exercise), what solution is (short walks, physiotherapy), and uses authority-positioning language ("Maybe you should," "It would probably help," "You might want to"). Frames patient's tentative agreement as validation of clinician's plan. Never invites patient into problem-solving with statements like "What do you think might help?" or "What feels doable to you?" Most critically, completely ignores actual reason for referral (alcohol use), suggesting either lack of clinical attention or assumption that clinician knows better what patient needs to address.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Change talk is neither evoked nor cultivated. Psychiatrist fails to recognize or reinforce limited change talk that emerges ("I suppose maybe," "I could probably manage," "I'm willing to have a go"). When patient produces ability statements, clinician does not strategically amplify through reflection or questioning. Most critically, completely neglects primary clinical domain (alcohol use) where change talk is essential. No exploration of patient's thoughts or concerns about drinking. Limited change talk that does emerge is weak, tentative, and conditional. Skilled clinician would recognize insufficient commitment and strategically evoke stronger change talk through careful questioning.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk is present throughout ("I don't mind the aches," "I'm not really convinced exercise will help," "It's pretty low on my list") and inadequately addressed. Psychiatrist sometimes acknowledges sustain talk but then applies pressure with "but" statements that directly contradict and invalidate patient's perspective. Uses gentle pressure ("But maybe...") and prescriptive advice rather than skillfully reframing or using double-sided reflections. Avoids direct confrontation (positive), but approach does not skillfully redirect language toward change—simply applies pressure, which can strengthen resistance. Missing opportunities for strategic reframing: "On one hand you're not convinced, and on the other hand you're willing to try. Tell me more about that."

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.6/5 – POOR MI COMPETENCY**

### Critical Clinical Failure:
The most significant deficiency is **complete omission of the primary clinical concern for which patient was referred: alcohol use**. Referral explicitly notes concerns about drinking (7 drinks/week, increased since retirement, possible link to fall) and son's concerns. Psychiatrist never addresses this. Instead, entire session focuses on exercise for aches and pains—topic patient never raised and not clinically indicated. This represents fundamental failure of clinical attention and MI application.

### Fundamental MI Deficiencies:
- **Expert-driven stance:** Assumes expert role; makes unilateral clinical decisions without collaboration
- **Prescriptive approach:** Tells patient what to do rather than evoking own motivations and ideas
- **Minimal evocation:** Almost no strategic questioning to draw out values, concerns, or reasons for change
- **Weak autonomy support:** Subtle pressure undermines patient's sense of choice despite non-coercive tone
- **No change talk cultivation:** Limited change talk neither recognized nor reinforced
- **Missed empathic opportunities:** No exploration of emotional experience, values, or concerns
- **No partnership:** Unilateral decision-making; patient treated as passive recipient of advice

### Strengths:
- Warm, non-confrontational opening tone
- Avoids aggressive confrontation tactics
- Maintains consistent focus once topic introduced
- Allows patient space to express views
- Uses soft language ("maybe," "might") rather than harsh directives

### Impact:
This approach is unlikely to increase patient's intrinsic motivation for change. Subtle pressure and expert-driven stance may increase resistance. By failing to address alcohol use—the actual clinical concern—psychiatrist missed critical opportunity to help patient explore ambivalence about drinking and develop own motivation to reduce consumption. Patient leaves with vague commitment to try walks but no exploration of drinking concerns or reasons for potential change.

### Recommendation:
**This clinician requires formal MI training and supervision to develop competency in this evidence-based approach.** Current session represents significant departure from MI principles and clinical best practice. Priority actions: (1) Address alcohol use in next session; (2) Shift from expert-driven to collaborative stance; (3) Develop strategic evocation skills; (4) Strengthen reflective listening; (5) Seek formal MI training and supervision.
