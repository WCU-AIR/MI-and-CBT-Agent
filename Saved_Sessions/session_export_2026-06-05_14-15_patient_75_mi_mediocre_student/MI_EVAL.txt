**Comments:** Collaboration nearly absent. Session occurs entirely from expert stance with patient as passive recipient of advice. ### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Evocation almost entirely absent. Clinician tells rather than asks; prescribes rather than explores. Critical failures: (1) No exploration of values—patient mentions twice worrying about children and not wanting them to smoke; these profound values never deepened; (2) No elicitation of change talk—when patient expresses concern about role modeling, clinician accepts low importance rating without exploring what would increase it; (3) All solutions generated by clinician; patient never asked "What have you thought?" or "What would help?"; (4) No exploration of past successes or strengths; (5) No curiosity about patient's perspective—closed questions focused on barriers, never open questions like "What do you think?" or "What would need to happen?"; (6) Missed opportunity with ambivalence—patient's tension between worrying about kids and enjoying smoking never explored; treated as separate problems to solve with advice rather than internal conflict to explore.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Change talk cultivation almost entirely absent. Multiple missed opportunities to amplify patient's concerns about role modeling. Patient offers clear change talk: "I'm worried about my children," "I feel like I'm setting a bad example," "I do worry about my kids sometimes and the example I'm setting." Clinician's response inadequate to each: after turn 2, clinician asks about barriers and diet/exercise, completely bypassing change talk offered; after turn 6, clinician accepts low importance rating without exploring what would increase it or deepening concern expressed. Clinician should have amplified ("Tell me more about that"), explored ("What specifically worries you?"), deepened ("What goes through your mind?"), and strengthened ("How do you sit with that tension?"). Instead, clinician moved past opportunities without developing them. No building of confidence or self-efficacy when patient rates confidence at 2/10.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Sustain talk not softened; confronted with prescriptive advice, which typically strengthens resistance. Patient's sustain talk includes: "I enjoy smoking," "It helps me relax," "I don't have any plans to quit," "I'm not sure I can," "I don't see how those things could replace what smoking does for me," "I'm not really convinced," "I don't really see myself doing that." Clinician's responses: turns 4 & 6 respond with more prescriptive advice ("Maybe like a hobby or exercise?")—confrontational, implicitly arguing against patient's position without exploring it; turn 8 again suggests "small steps" despite clear sustain talk; turn 9 finally accepts sustain talk but only after entire session arguing against patient's position. Clinician should have explored with curiosity ("What is it about smoking that works so well?"), used double-sided reflection ("On one hand...on the other hand..."), reframed without confrontation ("If you could find something that worked just as well..."), explored ambivalence, and avoided prescriptive advice. By responding to sustain talk with advice, clinician inadvertently strengthened it and increased patient entrenchment.

## Overall Assessment

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.4/5**

**Summary:**

This session represents a significant departure from Motivational Interviewing principles and practice. The clinician operates from a traditional medical expert model rather than an MI collaborative model. While basic courtesy and warmth are present, fundamental MI principles of collaboration, evocation, autonomy support, and partnership are violated throughout. The clinician's approach is more consistent with traditional directive counseling than with Motivational Interviewing.

**Critical Findings:**

- **Expert-driven rather than client-centered:** Clinician identifies problem, generates solutions, and repeatedly prescribes despite patient's clear statements of non-readiness.
- **Prescriptive advice despite client refusal:** Patient explicitly states four times she is not interested in cutting back; clinician continues prescribing, fundamentally disrespecting autonomy.
- **Missed opportunities to evoke change talk:** Patient offers clear change talk about concerns for children; clinician never deepens these statements or uses them to help patient discover own motivation.
- **Failure to explore ambivalence:** Patient's ambivalence evident (cares about role modeling but enjoys smoking); clinician treats as separate problems rather than real tension to explore.
- **Confrontation of sustain talk:** Clinician responds to patient's reasons for maintaining smoking with prescriptive advice rather than curiosity, inadvertently strengthening resistance.
- **No affirmation or validation:** Session contains zero affirmations of patient's strengths, no validation of concerns as understandable, no normalization of ambivalence.
- **Unilateral agenda-setting:** Clinician never asks what patient wants to focus on; assumes smoking is target and pursues relentlessly.

**Impact on Client Engagement and Change:**

This session is likely to **decrease** patient's motivation for change and damage therapeutic relationship. Repeated prescriptive advice despite clear refusal triggers psychological reactance—defensive response where people become more entrenched in position to reassert autonomy. Patient likely leaves more convinced she doesn't want to change, not less. Missed critical opportunity: patient's genuine concern about being good role model could have been foundation for exploring own motivation for change. Instead, concern acknowledged but never deepened. Therapeutic alliance likely damaged; patient may be less likely to return or engage in future sessions.

**Recommendations for Improvement:**

1. **Shift from expert to collaborative stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)** - Reframe role from expert prescribing solutions to collaborative partner helping client discover own solutions. Mindset shift required.
2. **Amplify and deepen change talk when it emerges** - Pause and explore deeply rather than moving past it.
3. **Use double-sided reflections to honor ambivalence** - Reflect both sides without judgment.
4. **Explore sustain talk with genuine curiosity rather than confrontation** - Respond with questions showing interest, not suggestions arguing against.
5. **Ask open questions rather than prescribing solutions** - Replace prescriptive statements with open questions inviting client to generate ideas.
6. **Affirm client strengths and validate perspective** - Actively look for opportunities to affirm what's working and validate concerns as understandable.
7. **Assess and respect readiness; don't push against it** - Accept non-readiness as valid information, not obstacle to overcome.
8. **Use readiness scales strategically, not as endpoints** - Use scales as springboards for deeper conversation.
9. **Establish collaborative goals at beginning of session** - Ask what client wants to focus on; let client's priorities guide session.
10. **Develop comfort with ambivalence and non-readiness** - Recognize these as normal; job is to help explore, not resolve.

**Path Forward:**

Clinician should engage in deliberate practice of MI skills with particular focus on foundational shift from expert to collaborative stance. Recording and reviewing sessions, seeking supervision from MI-trained clinician, and practicing specific techniques (open questions, reflective listening, amplifying change talk) would significantly improve outcomes. Foundation present (basic warmth and respect) but substantial development needed in MI-specific skills and philosophy.
