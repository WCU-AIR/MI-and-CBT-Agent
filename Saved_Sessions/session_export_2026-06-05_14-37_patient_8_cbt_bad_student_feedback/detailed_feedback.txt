
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

2 = Barely Adequate

6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

## Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Feedback: Therapist Evaluation

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

No agenda was established at any point during this session. The therapist did not collaborate with the patient to identify specific treatment targets, prioritize problems, or establish time-bound goals for the session. This is a critical omission in CBT practice. The session drifted without structure—beginning with general check-in questions but never transitioning to explicit problem identification or session planning. For a patient with anorexia nervosa presenting with multiple interconnected symptoms (fatigue, headaches, poor concentration, postprandial nausea, low caloric intake, amenorrhea), establishing a clear agenda is essential to focus intervention efforts.

The therapist made no attempt to ask: "What would be most helpful to focus on today?" or "Of your headaches, fatigue, and eating difficulties, which feels most urgent to address?" or "How can I best use our time together today?" Instead, the session remained reactive and unstructured, following the patient's lead without therapeutic direction.

**Summary:**

The therapist completely failed to establish a collaborative agenda. No session structure, priorities, or goals were identified. This represents a fundamental violation of CBT protocol.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made minimal attempts to elicit feedback and did not systematically check for understanding throughout the session. There was one implicit acknowledgment ("that makes complete sense"), but this was superficial validation rather than genuine feedback-seeking. The therapist never asked: "Does this make sense?" "How are you feeling about what we've discussed?" "Is there anything you'd like me to clarify?" "What's your reaction to this approach?" or "What are your thoughts on trying this?"

Most critically, at the end of the session, the therapist provided no structured closing feedback process. There was no attempt to summarize key points, ask what the patient found helpful, check understanding of any recommendations, or solicit the patient's perspective on the session's usefulness. The final exchange ("Alright, I'm glad we had this chat today...") was a dismissal, not a feedback-gathering moment.

For a patient with anorexia nervosa who may have limited insight into her condition and who expressed confusion about her eating difficulties ("It's confusing because I want to get better, but at the same time, eating feels uncomfortable"), systematic feedback-checking is essential to ensure comprehension and engagement.

**Summary:**

Minimal feedback elicitation; no systematic checking for understanding or satisfaction. No closing feedback process. This represents inadequate adherence to CBT structure.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated surface-level empathy but failed to demonstrate genuine understanding of the patient's internal experience or the clinical complexity of her presentation. While the therapist acknowledged the patient's statements ("that makes complete sense"), these were generic validations rather than evidence of deep comprehension.

Critical missed opportunities for demonstrating understanding:

1. **Failure to recognize the eating disorder cycle:** The patient explicitly stated, "It's like a cycle I can't get out of" and "I'm afraid that if I eat more, I'll get sick or feel worse afterward." The therapist never explored this cycle, never asked clarifying questions about the relationship between restriction, physical symptoms, and anxiety, and never demonstrated understanding that postprandial nausea and bloating are common physiological consequences of severe restriction that perpetuate the disorder.

2. **Misattribution of symptoms:** The therapist suggested hydration and sleep as primary interventions for fatigue and headaches, without acknowledging that these symptoms are likely secondary to severe malnutrition (BMI 14.7, ~600 calories/day). The therapist did not demonstrate understanding that the patient's fatigue, poor concentration, and headaches are medical consequences of anorexia nervosa, not simply stress-related symptoms amenable to positive thinking.

3. **Failure to recognize ambivalence:** The patient expressed clear ambivalence: "I want to get better and have more energy, but it feels hard to break the pattern" and "I want to feel better...but it's hard when I'm so tired and my body feels weak." The therapist never explored this ambivalence, never asked about barriers to eating, and never demonstrated understanding of the conflict between the patient's stated desire to gain weight and her behavioral restriction.

4. **Cultural and contextual factors ignored:** The case study notes cultural factors influence her presentation and that she recently immigrated. The therapist made no attempt to understand her cultural background, religious dietary restrictions, financial limitations, or how these factors interact with her eating disorder.

5. **Repetitive generic responses:** The therapist repeated nearly identical responses across multiple patient statements ("I remember when I was feeling down last year..."), suggesting the therapist was not truly listening to the unique content of each disclosure but rather applying a template response.

**Summary:**

Surface-level empathy without genuine comprehension of the eating disorder, its physiological consequences, or the patient's ambivalence. Missed critical opportunities to demonstrate understanding of clinical complexity.

### 5. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration was minimal and superficial. While the therapist used some collaborative language ("What do you think?" "Maybe you could try...?"), true collaborative empiricism—the hallmark of CBT—was entirely absent.

Critical failures in collaboration:

1. **No shared problem formulation:** The therapist never worked with the patient to develop a shared understanding of her eating disorder. No exploration of: "What do you think is maintaining your eating restriction?" "How do your thoughts about food relate to your eating?" "What happens in your body when you try to eat more?" 2. **Therapist-directed, not collaborative:** The therapist suggested interventions (drink more water, get more sleep, watch a movie, think positive) without collaborative discussion of whether these were appropriate, feasible, or addressing the core problem. The therapist never said, "Let's think about this together" or "What if we tried something and observed what happens?" or "What evidence do we have for and against this idea?" The patient's stated fear ("I'm afraid that if I eat more, I'll get sick or feel worse afterward") was never explored collaboratively to test its accuracy.

### 2. **Develop Eating Disorder-Specific Conceptualization**
- **Action:** Create a clear formulation of the patient's eating disorder that identifies: (1) precipitating factors (immigration, cultural factors, financial limitations), (2) maintaining factors (restriction → malnutrition → nausea/bloating → fear of eating → continued restriction), (3) cognitive factors (fear of weight gain, perfectionism, body image concerns, or in this case, lack of fat phobia but other maintaining cognitions), (4) behavioral factors (restriction, avoidance, high activity).
- **Example:** "Valerie, I want to make sure I understand what's keeping your eating restricted. ### 3. **Replace Self-Disclosure with Socratic Questioning**
- **Action:** Eliminate personal self-disclosure about your own experiences. Instead, use Socratic questions to help the patient discover insights. When tempted to share your experience, ask yourself: "What question could I ask instead?"
- **Example:** Instead of "I remember when I was feeling down, I just told myself to think positive," ask: "What do you think is causing your fatigue? What have you noticed about when it's worse or better? What do you think your body needs to have more energy?"
- **Practice:** Record yourself in role-plays and count instances of self-disclosure vs. Socratic questions. Aim for zero self-disclosure and 10+ Socratic questions per session. Practice with a supervisor.

### 4. **Address Central Eating Disorder Cognitions Through Cognitive Restructuring**
- **Action:** Identify the patient's key eating disorder thoughts and systematically address them through cognitive restructuring. For this patient: "If I eat more, I'll get sicker," "I'm weak," "I can't break this cycle."

- **Action:** Assign specific, concrete behavioral experiments to test eating disorder beliefs. For this patient: gradual increase in caloric intake with monitoring of actual vs. feared consequences.

- **Practice:** Design behavioral experiments for eating disorder cases. Practice explaining rationale clearly. Practice troubleshooting barriers. Role-play with supervisor.

### 6. **Implement Systematic Feedback-Checking Throughout Session**
- **Action:** Regularly check for understanding and elicit feedback. Ask at least 5-10 times per session: "Does this make sense?" "What are your thoughts?" "How are you feeling about this?" "Is there anything you'd like me to clarify?"

- **Action:** Stop using normalizing statements like "Everyone goes through this" or "It's totally normal." Instead, convey appropriate concern about the seriousness of anorexia nervosa while maintaining hope.
