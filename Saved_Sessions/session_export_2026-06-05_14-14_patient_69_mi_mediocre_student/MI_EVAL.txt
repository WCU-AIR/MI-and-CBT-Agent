# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal unconditional positive regard. While opening is courteous, clinician quickly shifts to directive problem-solving mode. Fails to validate Kevin's embarrassment or normalize ambivalence. No affirmation of Kevin's strengths, loyalty to friendships, or self-awareness regarding drinking-injury connection. Expert-driven prescriptive approach ("You might want to try," "It would probably help if you") contradicts MI's foundational principle of acceptance. Superficial acknowledgment of pain without genuine understanding of emotional experience.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflective listening is present but superficial and factual. When Kevin describes drinking as primary relaxation method, psychiatrist reflects content but misses emotional depth and underlying stressors. Fails to explore fear of social exclusion when Kevin states "I don't really want to be that guy who sits there nursing one drink." Kevin's statement "I'm worried it might make things less fun" reveals genuine ambivalence; psychiatrist's response minimizes concern rather than exploring it. No complex reflections that deepen insight or demonstrate understanding of Kevin's internal conflict. Reflections are transactional rather than relational.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains clear focus on reducing alcohol use and developing alternative coping strategies, which is appropriate to referral. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. Clinician assumes target behavior (alcohol reduction) and drives toward it without asking Kevin's own goals or values. Direction lacks strategic depth in evoking Kevin's reasons for change. By session end, Kevin has agreed to hiking/biking and drinking limits, but these are psychiatrist's suggestions, not Kevin's self-generated goals. Kevin's commitment language is weak ("I can try," "I'll keep an open mind"), suggesting direction has not resonated with his own motivation.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates profound absence of genuine collaboration. Psychiatrist operates entirely from expert-driven, hierarchical stance. Repeated prescriptive language ("Maybe you should try," "It would probably help if you," "You might want to") appears in nearly every clinician response, positioning psychiatrist as expert with answers and Kevin as passive recipient. When Kevin expresses ambivalence, psychiatrist offers solutions rather than exploring collaboratively. Kevin's ideas do not substantially shape session direction. No moment where Kevin's perspective is genuinely sought as essential to solution. Power dynamic is entirely unequal; psychiatrist holds expertise and solutions; Kevin holds compliance or polite resistance. Antithesis of MI collaboration.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is almost entirely absent. Prescriptive language functions as subtle directives communicating psychiatrist's agenda rather than supporting Kevin's freedom to choose. When Kevin expresses ambivalence or resistance—legitimate expressions of autonomy—psychiatrist does not respect these as valid choices. Fails to explicitly affirm Kevin's right to choose whether to change, how to change, or at what pace. When Kevin rates confidence at 4/10, psychiatrist reframes moderate ambivalence as insufficient, subtly pressuring greater commitment. No language like "It's entirely up to you" or "You're the expert on what will work for you." Kevin's responses reflect compliance rather than autonomous choice ("Yeah, okay. I can try"), indicating language of agreement with expert recommendations, not self-determined change.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out Kevin's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources—is minimal and poorly executed. Psychiatrist relies heavily on telling rather than asking. Early questions ("Do you think the drinking is a problem?" "Have you tried cutting back?") are assessment questions, not evocative. When Kevin provides change talk ("I don't want to mess up again like with my ankle, so it makes sense to have some limits"), psychiatrist does not amplify or deepen; instead pivots to new topic. No questions like "What concerns you most?" "What would be different if you cut back?" "What matters most to you?" "What would need to happen for you to feel ready?" Session dominated by advice-giving rather than evocative questioning. Kevin's own change talk is not strategically amplified, representing significant missed opportunities.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership—understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within client—is fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role throughout with no meaningful power-sharing or recognition of Kevin's expertise about his own life. Repeated prescriptive language communicates psychiatrist has answers; Kevin is recipient of advice. When Kevin identifies barriers ("When everyone's drinking, it feels weird to be the one holding back"), psychiatrist does not explore Kevin's wisdom about social dynamics; offers solutions instead. Never asks what Kevin thinks would work, what he's tried before, or what he believes about his capacity. Kevin's contributions do not substantially shape session. Session direction, goals, and strategies are all clinician-generated. No recognition that Kevin's perspective should substantially influence session. Psychiatrist is expert; Kevin is problem to be fixed.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk is present but neither strategically evoked nor skillfully reinforced. Kevin generates spontaneous change talk including regret ("That was really stupid"), problem recognition ("I know drinking probably played a part"), and reasons for change ("I don't want to mess up again like with my ankle"). However, psychiatrist's responses do not strategically cultivate more change talk. When Kevin expresses need and ability ("it makes sense to have some limits"), psychiatrist validates then redirects rather than deepening. No follow-up questions to strengthen Kevin's own reasons for change. When Kevin expresses ambivalence ("I'm not sure how well I'd stick to it"), psychiatrist offers solutions rather than exploring doubt. Session ends with weak, compliance-based change talk ("I'm still a little skeptical about it being as fun, but I'll keep an open mind"), reflecting agreement with psychiatrist's plan, not genuine internal motivation.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk appears throughout session and is handled poorly. Kevin's sustain talk includes benefits of drinking ("it's fun"), difficulty of change ("it's tough when everyone's drinking"), fear of social exclusion ("I don't really want to be that guy"), doubt about capacity ("I'm not sure how well I'd stick to it"), and ambivalence ("I'm not really sure I'm ready to give it up"). Psychiatrist's typical response is to offer solution or redirect rather than explore or soften. When Kevin expresses core ambivalence at 4/10 confidence, psychiatrist reframes as insufficient rather than exploring what 4 means or validating ambivalence as normal. No use of double-sided reflections, reframing, or exploration of competing values. Psychiatrist never normalizes ambivalence or uses techniques like "So on one hand... on the other hand." Approach leaves sustain talk unaddressed, potentially strengthening resistance rather than softening it.

## Overall Session Assessment

**Overall Session Score: 1.6/5**

---

### Summary of Strengths
- Clear focus on referral concern (alcohol use and injury)
- Basic courtesy and absence of confrontation
- Some acknowledgment of Kevin's perspective through occasional reflections
- Structured session flow addressing multiple relevant topics

---

### Summary of Areas for Improvement

**Fundamental MI Spirit Violation:** Session is dominated by expert-driven advice-giving rather than collaborative exploration. This is the core problem undermining all MI principles.

**Absence of Genuine Evocation:** Psychiatrist tells rather than asks, missing repeated opportunities to draw out Kevin's own motivations and reasons for change.

**Minimal Autonomy Support:** Kevin's ambivalence and resistance are not respected as valid expressions of autonomy; instead, they are managed or redirected.

**Superficial Empathy:** Reflective listening is present but shallow, factual, and used to redirect rather than deepen understanding.

**No Power-Sharing:** Psychiatrist assumes expert role throughout with no recognition that Kevin's wisdom should substantially influence session.

**Weak Cultivation of Change Talk:** Kevin's own reasons for change are not strategically amplified or deepened.

**Unaddressed Ambivalence:** Kevin's legitimate concerns about losing fun, social connection, and identity are not explored or softened; they are redirected.

---

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change

This session is likely to produce short-term compliance but not sustained change. Kevin may try suggested strategies because psychiatrist recommended them, but without genuine internal motivation, adherence is unlikely. Kevin's ambivalence remains unaddressed and unresolved. His core concerns—losing fun, feeling excluded, losing primary coping mechanism—remain unexplored. Psychiatrist's expert stance may activate Kevin's reactance (psychological resistance to perceived control), making him less likely to follow through. Session does not embody MI spirit of partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation. Unlikely to increase Kevin's intrinsic motivation for change or support autonomous decision-making about drinking.

---

### Critical Recommendations

1. **Shift from expert-driven advice to collaborative exploration** – Eliminate prescriptive language; replace with genuine evocative questions
2. **Develop genuine evocative questioning skills** – Learn to ask questions drawing out Kevin's own motivations, values, and reasons for change
3. **Validate and explore ambivalence** – Use double-sided reflections and exploration rather than redirecting
4. **Develop deep, complex reflections** – Move beyond factual reflections to capture emotional nuance and meaning
5. **Explicitly support autonomy** – Communicate clearly that Kevin is expert on his own life and change is his choice
6. **Use scaling questions to explore, not pressure** – Follow up with genuine curiosity about what numbers mean
7. **Strategically amplify change talk** – When Kevin generates change talk, pause and deepen it through follow-up questions
8. **Establish true partnership** – Involve Kevin in developing treatment plan; make his ideas central, not peripheral
9. **Develop skill in softening sustain talk** – Use double-sided reflections and strategic questioning without confrontation
10. **Receive formal MI training and supervision** – This session demonstrates psychiatrist is not yet competent in MI; formal training, deliberate practice, and supervision are essential

---

### Final Recommendation

**Do not continue conducting MI sessions without formal training and supervision.** Current approach is likely to activate client resistance and undermine change motivation. Psychiatrist must fundamentally shift from telling to asking, from expert-driven to collaborative, from redirecting ambivalence to exploring it. This requires formal MI training, deliberate practice with feedback, and ongoing clinical supervision.
