
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **URGENT: Implement Suicide Risk Assessment and Safety Planning**
- **Action:** In the next session, conduct a comprehensive suicide risk assessment using a structured tool (Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale or similar). Assess frequency, intensity, and specificity of suicidal thoughts; presence of plan or intent; access to means; protective factors; and previous attempts.
- **Example:** "I want to understand your thoughts about death better. You mentioned thinking of death sometimes. How often do these thoughts occur? Do you have any thoughts about how you might hurt yourself? Do you have access to means? What keeps you safe?"
- **Practice:** Review suicide assessment protocols. Practice asking directly about suicidal ideation without fear or judgment. Develop a safety plan template that includes crisis contacts, coping strategies, and means restriction.

### 2. **Establish a Coherent CBT Treatment Strategy and Case Conceptualization**
- **Action:** Before the next session, develop a written case conceptualization that identifies: (1) presenting problems, (2) maintaining factors (cognitive and behavioral), (3) treatment targets, and (4) specific CBT interventions for each target. Share this with the patient collaboratively.
- **Example:** "Based on what you've shared, I think your depression is maintained by three things: (1) thoughts that you're not good enough, which leads to guilt and self-blame; (2) avoiding activities because you feel too tired, which actually makes depression worse; and (3) isolating from your husband because you think you're a burden. We're going to work on each of these. Here's how..."
- **Practice:** Study CBT case conceptualization models. Practice writing clear, patient-friendly formulations. Learn to explain the cognitive model and how specific interventions target specific problems.

### 3. **Eliminate Inappropriate Therapist Self-Disclosure**
- **Action:** Stop using personal anecdotes ("When I was stressed," "I remember when I felt"). Instead, redirect focus to the patient's experience. If tempted to share, ask yourself: "Does this advance the patient's treatment, or does it center my experience?"
- **Example:** Instead of "When I was stressed out, I'd remind myself that everyone goes through rough patches," say: "I'm curious—what do you think might help you get through this? What have you tried before that helped, even a little?"
- **Practice:** Record yourself in practice sessions and count instances of self-disclosure. Aim for zero. When you feel the urge to share, pause and ask a question instead.

### 4. **Implement Structured Agenda-Setting in Every Session**
- **Action:** Begin each session by collaboratively setting a 3-5 item agenda with time allocation. Write it down and refer to it throughout the session. End by reviewing what was accomplished.
- **Example:** "Today I'd like to check in on how you've been, review what we talked about last time, work on understanding your thoughts about not being good enough, and plan some activities for this week. Does that sound okay? Anything else you want to add?"
- **Practice:** Use an agenda template. Practice prioritizing with patients. Learn to tactfully redirect when patients want to discuss peripheral issues.

### 5. **Develop Systematic Behavioral Activation Plan**
- **Action:** Conduct a behavioral analysis with the patient. Identify avoidance patterns (TV watching, staying in bed, isolating). Create a graded activity schedule with specific, measurable, achievable activities. Link activities to mood monitoring.
- **Example:** "Let's look at what you're doing now and what you'd like to be doing. Right now you're watching TV most evenings. What activities used to bring you pleasure or a sense of accomplishment? Let's start with something small—maybe a 10-minute walk on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday at 5 PM. We'll track your mood before and after to see what happens."
- **Practice:** Learn behavioral activation protocols. Practice creating activity schedules. Understand the rationale: inactivity maintains depression; activity breaks the cycle.

### 6. **Use Socratic Questioning to Facilitate Guided Discovery**
- **Action:** Replace advice-giving with questions. When the patient expresses a belief or concern, ask questions that help her examine the evidence and reach her own conclusions.
- **Example:** Patient: "I'm not good enough." Therapist: "What makes you feel that way? What evidence do you have? Can you think of any times when you did something well? What would it mean to be 'good enough'? How would you know?"
- **Practice:** Study Socratic questioning techniques. Practice asking open-ended questions. Record yourself and count how many times you tell vs. ask. Aim for 80% questions, 20% statements.

### 7. **Implement Cognitive Restructuring for Core Beliefs**
- **Action:** Identify the patient's core beliefs (inadequacy, perfectionism, burden). Use thought records to examine evidence for and against these beliefs. Help the patient develop more balanced, realistic thoughts.
- **Example:** "You said 'I'm not quite enough.' Let's examine that thought. What evidence do you have that you're not enough? What evidence contradicts that? What would a friend say? What's a more balanced way to think about this?"
- **Practice:** Learn thought record procedures. Practice identifying automatic thoughts and core beliefs. Practice evidence examination and developing alternative thoughts.

### 8. **Systematically Review and Adjust Homework**
- **Action:** Begin each session by reviewing the previous homework. Ask what the patient noticed, what worked, what didn't. Adjust based on feedback. Assign new homework with clear rationale and specific instructions.
- **Example:** "Last week you listened to 'Blackbird' a few times. What did you notice? Did your mood change? How did you feel? I'm wondering if we should try something different—maybe a short walk instead, or listening to music while doing something else. What do you think?"
- **Practice:** Develop homework review protocols. Practice assigning specific, measurable homework. Practice adjusting based on patient feedback.

### 9. **Provide Psychoeducation About Bipolar II and Depression**
- **Action:** Educate the patient about bipolar II disorder, the cognitive model of depression, the role of behavior in mood, and why CBT works. Help her understand the connection between her thoughts, behaviors, and mood.
- **Example:** "You have bipolar II disorder, which means you experience periods of depression and periods of elevated mood. Right now you're in a depressive episode. Depression tells us lies—it tells us we're not good enough, that we're a burden, that nothing will help. But these are symptoms, not facts. CBT helps us identify these thoughts and test them against reality."
- **Practice:** Study bipolar II psychoeducation materials. Practice explaining the cognitive model in patient-friendly language. Learn to normalize symptoms while emphasizing the importance of treatment.

### 10. **Elicit and Respond to Patient Feedback Throughout Sessions**
- **Action:** Regularly ask for feedback: "Does this make sense?" "How does that land with you?" "Is there anything that didn't feel helpful?" "What would be more useful?" Adjust based on feedback.
- **Example:** "I notice you seem hesitant about trying walks. What's your concern? What would feel more manageable?"
- **Practice:** Build feedback-seeking into your session structure. Practice responding non-defensively to critical feedback. Learn to adjust approach based on patient input.

### 11. **Establish Clear Collaboration and Shared Decision-Making**
- **Action:** Frame interventions as collaborative experiments, not directives. Invite the patient's input on what to try, when, and how. Emphasize that the patient is the expert on her own experience.
- **Example:** "I have some ideas about what might help, but I want to hear your thoughts first. What do you think might make a difference? What have you tried before? What feels doable to you?"
- **Practice:** Practice collaborative language. Learn to invite patient input before offering suggestions. Understand that collaboration increases engagement and adherence.

### 12. **Monitor Mood Patterns and Bipolar II Cycling**
- **Action:** Implement systematic mood monitoring (daily mood ratings, sleep hours, energy level, irritability). Track patterns to identify depressive and hypomanic episodes. Adjust treatment based on mood state.
- **Example:** "Let's track your mood, sleep, and energy each day. This will help us understand your patterns and know when to adjust our approach. If we see your mood starting to elevate or your sleep decreasing, we'll know to be careful about overstimulation."
- **Practice:** Learn mood monitoring tools (mood charts, sleep logs). Practice identifying bipolar II patterns. Understand the importance of sleep and activity regulation in bipolar disorder.
