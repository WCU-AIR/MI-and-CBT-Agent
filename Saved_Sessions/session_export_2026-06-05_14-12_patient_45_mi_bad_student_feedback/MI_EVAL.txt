# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Early responses severely compromised acceptance through judgment and pressure ("You must quit," "Why haven't you done that already?"). Psychiatrist labeled patient concerns as "excuses," explicitly rejecting and dismissing his experience. Later responses (4-7) demonstrate marked improvement with genuine affirmation ("You know yourself best," "It's really encouraging to hear you're open to trying"). However, minimal explicit affirmation of patient's inherent worth independent of behavior change. Overall acceptance remains below average due to significant early deficiencies that likely damaged therapeutic alliance.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Early session shows minimal empathic effort. When patient expressed discouragement about self-comparison and self-doubt, psychiatrist responded with directive advice rather than reflection. Dismissal of patient's concerns as "excuses" demonstrates lack of understanding of internal experience. Later responses (4-7) show improvement with basic empathic listening ("I appreciate you sharing that") but lack emotional depth and complexity. Psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore patient's shame, fear of failure, and ambivalence more thoroughly. Reflections remain relatively surface-level rather than capturing full emotional nuance of patient's experience.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Early responses establish clear but imposed direction without exploring patient's own goals or priorities. Direction exists but is unilateral rather than collaborative. Mid-to-later responses (4-7) show significant improvement with specific, achievable goals (three gym visits weekly, one candy bar swap) emerging from patient's input rather than psychiatrist prescription. Psychiatrist maintains appropriate focus on target behaviors while remaining flexible about implementation. However, could more strategically connect patient's intrinsic motivators (back pain relief, improved clothing fit) to behavior change goals to deepen motivation and maintain direction.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Early responses adopt authoritarian, expert-driven stance with consistently directive language ("I'm going to share some ideas," "You must quit," "You have to push through," "From now on, follow this simple plan"). Power dynamic is entirely one-directional; psychiatrist makes unilateral decisions without seeking patient input. Later responses (4-7) show marked improvement with collaborative language ("Let's think about," "Let's brainstorm," "Would you like to explore?"). Psychiatrist invites patient's input and builds on his ideas. However, even in later responses, psychiatrist still leads agenda and asks most questions. True power-sharing could be deeper with more explicit invitation for patient to lead problem-solving.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Early responses systematically undermine autonomy through controlling language ("You must," "You have to," "You can't," "You really need to"). Psychiatrist uses fear-based pressure ("If you don't, you're risking serious health problems") to coerce compliance. Final statement "Are you going to do it or not?" presents false binary pressuring commitment rather than supporting autonomous choice. Later responses (4-7) substantially improve with language emphasizing patient choice and agency ("You know yourself best," "What do you think?", "Be patient with yourself"). However, could more explicitly affirm patient's right to make mistakes, change his mind, and normalize ambivalence about change.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Early responses show no genuine evocation. Psychiatrist provides reasons for change without exploring patient's own motivations, values, or what would be meaningful to him. When patient spontaneously offers change talk ("My pants feel a little better," "My back doesn't hurt nearly so much," "I'm glad we talked last time"), psychiatrist does not amplify or deepen it. Later responses (4-7) include exploratory questions ("What do you think makes it hard?", "What have you tried?", "What usually helps you relax?") that invite patient to articulate experience. However, psychiatrist does not consistently amplify change talk or use strategic questioning to deepen intrinsic motivation. Misses opportunities to explore patient's own reasons for change, values, and confidence.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Early responses actively assume expert role and position psychiatrist as authority ("I'm going to share some ideas on how you can improve them"). Psychiatrist prescribes specific behaviors and pressures compliance; when patient offers perspective, psychiatrist overrides rather than treating it as valuable input. Hierarchical, expert-driven dynamic rather than partnership. Later responses (4-7) show significant improvement with explicit affirmation of patient expertise ("You know yourself best") and genuine invitation for input throughout. Psychiatrist builds on patient's ideas rather than dismissing them. However, psychiatrist still leads most conversation and asks most questions. Could more explicitly acknowledge patient as expert on his own life and invite him to lead agenda.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Early responses do not cultivate change talk; psychiatrist provides reasons for change without exploring patient's own. Spontaneous change talk is not reflected, amplified, or reinforced. Instead, psychiatrist pivots to directive advice. Focus on fear and consequences may evoke sustain talk or resistance rather than change talk. Later responses (4-7) begin to ask questions inviting patient to articulate motivations and confidence ("How confident do you feel?", "What are some small wins you've noticed?"). However, psychiatrist does not consistently amplify or reinforce patient's change talk when it emerges. Misses multiple opportunities to deepen desire talk, ability talk, reasons talk, need talk, and commitment talk through strategic questioning and reflection.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Early responses do not soften sustain talk; instead, psychiatrist confronts it directly. When patient expresses concern that strictness might backfire, psychiatrist responds with "you really should just push through those feelings" and "You need to be stricter." Direct confrontation of patient's concerns rather than skillful softening. Labeling concerns as "excuses" likely strengthens sustain talk rather than softens it. Later responses (4-7) show improvement by exploring barriers collaboratively ("Let's brainstorm some coping strategies") and normalizing struggle ("Change takes time, so be patient with yourself") rather than pressuring through it. However, psychiatrist does not use more advanced MI techniques like double-sided reflection or strategic reframing to deepen softening of sustain talk.

---

### Overall Session Assessment

**Overall Score: 2.6/5**

**Summary:**

This session presents a **critical case study in the consequences of non-MI-consistent practice and the power of course correction**. The psychiatrist's first three responses are **fundamentally inconsistent with MI spirit**, employing confrontation, directive advice-giving, fear-based pressure, and autonomy-undermining language that actively damages therapeutic alliance and increases client defensiveness. These early responses violate core MI principles of partnership, acceptance, autonomy support, and evocation.

The psychiatrist demonstrates significant responsiveness to the patient's feedback and shifts toward genuine MI practice in Responses 4-7. By the end of the session, the psychiatrist demonstrates collaborative problem-solving, validation, and support for the patient's autonomy. This course correction likely prevented further alliance damage and created some foundation for engagement.

**Critical Assessment:** While the later portion of the session shows improvement, the early deficiencies are severe and likely to have lasting negative impact on the patient's motivation and follow-through. A client who feels judged, pressured, and controlled in the first portion of a session may maintain defensiveness and reduced commitment even if the later portion improves. The psychiatrist's MI competency is **inconsistent and below the threshold for competent MI practice**.

**Most Critical Improvements Needed:**
1. Eliminate confrontational, directive language from the outset
2. Establish genuine partnership and collaboration from the opening
3. Strategically evoke and amplify change talk throughout the session
4. Develop deeper, more emotionally-attuned empathic reflections
5. Explicitly support autonomy and normalize ambivalence from the beginning

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation for Change:** While the patient expresses commitment at the end ("I'm ready to give it my best this week"), the early alliance damage may undermine sustained motivation. The concrete goals and strategies identified are helpful, but without a solid foundation of genuine partnership and autonomy support, the patient may struggle to maintain commitment when obstacles arise. Significant additional training and practice in MI is necessary to develop consistent, skillful practice.
