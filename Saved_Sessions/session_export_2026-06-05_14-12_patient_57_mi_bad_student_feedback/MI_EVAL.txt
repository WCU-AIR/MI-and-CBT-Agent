### Direction

Opening question appears open but is immediately followed by pivot to clinician's agenda. Session demonstrates improvement but loses significant ground in first three exchanges by not establishing shared direction from outset.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Autonomy actively undermined in first three interventions through controlling language minimizing client choice: "you really need to quit"; "you have to stop"; "You must push through"; "You have to be firm with yourself—no excuses." These statements frame change as mandatory rather than chosen. When client expresses hesitation about setting quit date, psychiatrist responds with "You have to commit to quitting immediately" and "What's stopping you from setting that date today?"—language that pressures rather than supports autonomy. Phrase "It's about willpower, and you must push through the discomfort" particularly problematic as it suggests client's reluctance is character flaw rather than legitimate concern about readiness. Critically, when client explicitly states "that kind of pressure makes me feel even more stuck," psychiatrist does not immediately validate or adjust approach; instead continues with directive language. Shift occurs at intervention 5: "Maybe we can explore together what small steps might feel manageable for you." Word "manageable" respects client's capacity to determine pace. Question "What do you think might be a realistic first step for you?" explicitly invites client's choice. Subsequent interventions reinforce autonomy with explicit affirmation: "Remember, this is your journey, and you're the expert on what works best for you." Early violations are serious and represent fundamental MI principle breach.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Evocation largely absent in first three interventions. Psychiatrist tells client what should motivate them rather than exploring what client themselves sees as connection. Question "Why didn't you make changes sooner?" is accusatory rather than evocative; assumes client has failed rather than exploring internal motivation. When client articulates ambivalence ("I'm not saying I'm not open to change, but it has to feel right"), psychiatrist responds with prescription rather than evocation, dismissing client's stated need for internal alignment. Questions "Have you tried any stress management techniques before? Why haven't you committed to quitting yet?" are investigative and judgmental rather than evocative; do not draw out client's own reasons for change or explore internal resources. Evocation begins to emerge at intervention 5: "Maybe we can explore together what small steps might feel manageable for you at this point, and how those could support your goal of being present for your family." This explicitly links potential change to client's stated value and invites exploration. Question "What do you think might be a realistic first step for you?" evokes client's own ideas. Subsequent interventions demonstrate stronger evocation: "How might cutting back in those less necessary moments help you feel more connected to your family?" draws out client's own reasoning. "What support or resources do you think would help you?" invites client to identify own resources. Critical early opportunities to evoke change talk are missed.

## Overall Session Assessment

**Overall MI Competency Score: 2.8/5**

---

### Key Strengths

- **Mid-session course correction:** Psychiatrist demonstrates ability to recognize that directive approach is not working (evidenced by client's explicit statement: "that kind of pressure makes me feel even more stuck") and pivots to more collaborative, MI-consistent approach.
- **Explicit affirmation of client expertise:** By end of session, psychiatrist clearly states "you're the expert on what works best for you"—strong MI principle affirmation.
- **Collaborative goal-setting:** From intervention 5 onward, psychiatrist successfully co-constructs manageable plan with client, linking it to client's stated value of family happiness.
- **Reflective listening improvement:** Later interventions demonstrate accurate reflections capturing both content and emotion: "It sounds like you're feeling pressured and uncertain...and that's understandable."
- **Client engagement maintained:** Despite early MI violations, client remains engaged and eventually expresses willingness to try plan and hopefulness about moving forward.

---

### Critical Areas for Improvement

- **Premature agenda-setting:** Psychiatrist enters with predetermined focus on smoking/drinking cessation without first exploring what client wants to work on or how this connects to stated values. Opening question appears open but is followed by immediate redirection to clinician's agenda.
- **Early confrontation and pressure:** First three interventions use controlling language ("you have to," "you must," "you really need to") that pressures client and undermines autonomy. Client explicitly states this makes them feel "stuck."
- **Failure to evoke client motivation:** Rather than drawing out client's own reasons for change, psychiatrist tells client why they should change. This imposes external motivation rather than evoking internal motivation.
- **Dismissal of client concerns:** When client expresses legitimate concerns about readiness, stress management, and complexity of change, psychiatrist responds with prescription rather than exploration. Client's concerns are treated as obstacles to overcome rather than important information about readiness.
- **Missed early alliance-building:** First three interventions damage therapeutic alliance by positioning clinician as expert and client as someone needing correction. This makes later course correction necessary but means critical early opportunities for engagement are lost.
- **Inconsistent MI application:** Session demonstrates psychiatrist has MI knowledge but applies it inconsistently. Early interventions violate core MI principles; later interventions demonstrate solid MI practice. Suggests psychiatrist may revert to directive approaches under pressure or when frustrated with client ambivalence.

---

### Impact on Client Engagement and Motivation

Early directive approach likely reduced client's intrinsic motivation and increased defensiveness. Client's responses show increasing resistance to psychiatrist's pressure ("I'm not saying I'm not open to change, but it has to feel right"; "that kind of pressure makes me feel even more stuck"). However, psychiatrist's mid-course correction and shift to collaboration appears to have recovered alliance. By end of session, client expresses moderate confidence about trying plan and hopefulness about moving forward. Client's final statement—"It feels good to have some space to figure this out on my terms while still keeping my family's happiness in mind"—suggests collaborative approach has successfully engaged intrinsic motivation. However, session would likely have achieved stronger commitment and more robust change talk if MI principles had been applied consistently throughout. Client's willingness to try plan appears conditional on collaborative approach continuing; if psychiatrist reverts to directive pressure in future sessions, client's motivation may decrease.

---

### Recommendations for Supervisor Review

1. **Establish genuine shared agenda** before prescribing change; explore client's priorities first
2. **Replace controlling language** ("you have to," "you must") with collaborative language consistently throughout sessions
3. **Evoke rather than impose** motivation; draw out client's own reasons for change through open questions
4. **Validate and explore** client concerns rather than confronting them; treat ambivalence as important information about readiness
5. **Link potential changes to client values** explicitly throughout session
6. **Respond immediately** when client gives explicit feedback that approach is not working
7. **Develop consistency** in MI application through self-awareness and intentional practice
8. **Use double-sided reflections** to soften ambivalence without judgment
9. **Establish collaborative plan-making** process with client input at every step
10. **Seek supervision or consultation** on directive tendencies to identify triggers and develop strategies for maintaining MI consistency

---

### Conclusion

This session demonstrates a psychiatrist with foundational MI knowledge who applies it inconsistently, resulting in a session that begins with significant MI violations but recovers through mid-course correction. The early interventions directly contradict MI spirit and damage the therapeutic alliance. However, the psychiatrist's ability to recognize this and shift to collaborative practice demonstrates capacity for MI-consistent work. With intentional focus on consistency and the specific recommendations above, this clinician could significantly improve MI competency and effectiveness in evoking client motivation for sustainable behavior change.
