## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.4/5 – Poor MI Practice**

**Key Strengths:**
- Maintains respectful, non-hostile tone throughout
- Consistent focus on presenting problem (anxiety and avoidance)
- Basic acknowledgment of client statements ("I understand," "I get that")
- Verbal affirmation of autonomy ("It's up to you to make these changes")

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Expert-driven rather than client-centered:** Entire session follows medical model; clinician diagnoses and prescribes while client remains passive
- **Repeated prescriptions without exploration:** Multiple suggestions offered without exploring what client has tried, prefers, or believes is manageable
- **Minimal evocation:** Almost no strategic effort to draw out client's own reasons for change, values, past successes, or strengths
- **Inadequate handling of ambivalence:** Client's low importance rating (4-5/10) and repeated doubt not explored; prescriptions typically increase resistance in ambivalent clients
- **Pressure and subtle judgment:** Repeated "should" language and statements like "push harder" carry implicit judgment that client is not trying hard enough
- **Minimal change talk cultivation:** Very little change talk from client; no strategic amplification or reinforcement
- **Inadequate empathy:** Reflections are factual and surface-level; multiple missed opportunities for complex, emotionally attuned reflections

**Impact on Client Engagement and Change:**
This session is likely to **decrease** intrinsic motivation and **increase** resistance. Pressure backfires with ambivalent clients. Autonomy undermined by prescriptive approach. Self-efficacy not strengthened. Ambivalence unresolved. Client's final commitment ("I'll try to keep at it") is tentative and vague with low likelihood of follow-through.

**Likelihood of Behavior Change: Low to Very Low**

## Recommendations for Improvement

**Priority 1 – Shift from Expert-Driven to Client-Centered Approach:**
Replace prescriptions with open-ended questions that invite client to be expert on their own life. Ask "What ideas do you have?" rather than "Have you tried...?" Ask "What feels manageable to you?" rather than "You should try..."

**Priority 2 – Develop Genuine Empathy Through Complex Reflections:**
Move beyond factual restatements to reflections capturing emotional nuance and validating struggle. Reflect both content and emotion: "It sounds like no matter what you try, the anxiety pulls you back—and that must feel really frustrating and defeating."

**Priority 3 – Evoke Client's Own Reasons for Change:**
Replace prescriptions with strategic questions: "What would be good about making this change?" "What matters to you that this anxiety is getting in the way of?" "Tell me about a time you faced something difficult and got through it."

**Priority 4 – Strategically Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk:**
Listen for client's own language about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment. When heard, amplify through follow-up questions: "Tell me more about that. What else? How would that help?"

**Priority 5 – Use Double-Sided Reflections for Ambivalence:**
Acknowledge both sides: "On one hand, you've struggled with follow-through. On the other hand, you're here, you're willing to talk about this, and you want to get better—those are signs of capability."

**Priority 6 – Replace Prescriptive Language with Autonomy Support:**
Eliminate "should," "would probably help," "it's important to." Use instead: "What feels right to you?" "What do you think might help?" "You're the expert on what you need."

**Priority 7 – Explore Ambivalence Rather Than Prescribe:**
When client rates importance as 4-5/10, explore both sides: "What keeps it from being higher? What would need to happen for it to feel more important?"

**Priority 8 – Build Self-Efficacy Through Past Successes:**
When client expresses doubt, ask: "Tell me about a time you faced something difficult and got through it. What did you do? What does that tell you about yourself?"

**Priority 9 – Clarify Goals Collaboratively:**
Ask client to articulate their own vision: "What would you like to be different? What's one small step that feels doable to you?" rather than summarizing clinician's formulation.

**Priority 10 – Seek Formal MI Training and Supervision:**
Attend 2-day MI workshop through MINT. Seek ongoing supervision with MI-trained clinician. Record and review sessions. Practice with role-plays before applying with clients.

---

**Conclusion:**

This session demonstrates **significant departures from MI principles** across nearly all core competencies. The psychiatrist maintains respectful rapport but operates in expert-driven mode fundamentally inconsistent with MI philosophy. A critical shift is needed: from prescribing solutions to evoking the client's own motivation for change. Without this shift, likelihood of sustained behavior change is minimal, and client may leave feeling more stuck, judged, and pressured—opposite of MI's intended impact.
