
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

## Recommendations

### 1. **URGENT: Recognize Psychiatric Emergencies and Refer Appropriately**

**Action:** Immediately develop competence in identifying psychiatric emergencies and knowing when to refer for emergency psychiatric evaluation rather than provide outpatient psychotherapy.

**Example:** When a patient presents with:
- Fixed delusional beliefs (especially about harming others)
- Severe sleep deprivation
- Pressured speech and flight of ideas
- Psychomotor agitation
- Postpartum onset with psychiatric history

The appropriate response is: "I'm concerned about the severity of what you're describing. These symptoms—especially the beliefs about harming your baby and not being able to sleep—require immediate evaluation by a psychiatrist. I'd like to help arrange that for you today. Have you been to an emergency room, or would you like me to help coordinate that?"

**Practice:** Review DSM-5 criteria for manic episodes with psychotic features and postpartum psychosis. Study cases of postpartum psychiatric emergencies. Develop protocols for emergency referral. Consult with psychiatry colleagues about appropriate triage.

---

### 2. **Establish Collaborative Agenda at Session Start**

**Action:** Begin every session by explicitly setting a collaborative agenda with the patient.

**Example:** "I'd like to make sure we use our time well today. From what you've shared, I'm hearing several things: your mood swinging between high and very low, racing thoughts that won't stop, difficulty sleeping, and scary thoughts about your baby. Which of these feels most important to focus on today? And how much time would you like to spend on each?"

**Practice:** Use agenda-setting in every session, even brief ones. Write the agenda down. Refer back to it during the session. Practice saying: "We agreed to focus on X. Should we continue with that, or do you need to shift?"

---

### 3. **Apply Specific CBT Techniques Rather Than Generic Advice**

**Action:** Replace generic wellness suggestions with evidence-based CBT techniques tailored to the patient's specific symptoms and diagnosis.

**Example:** For a patient with intrusive thoughts about harming her baby:
- **Reality-testing:** "You mentioned the thought that you've hurt your baby. Let's examine this carefully. What evidence do you have that this thought is true? What evidence contradicts it? Have you actually harmed your baby?"
- **Behavioral experiment:** "Let's check on your baby together right now. What do you observe? Is she safe? Is she unharmed?"
- **Thought record:** "Let's write down this thought. What triggered it? What evidence supports it? What evidence contradicts it? What's a more realistic thought?"

Rather than: "Try to think positive" or "Try distraction."

**Practice:** Study CBT manuals for specific disorders. Practice applying techniques in role-plays. Receive supervision on technique application. Record sessions and review for technique use.

---

### 4. **Use Guided Discovery (Socratic Questioning) Rather Than Advice-Giving**

**Action:** Replace directive advice with Socratic questions that help patients discover insights independently.

**Example:** Instead of "You should try to get more sleep," ask:
- "What happens when you try to sleep?"
- "What goes through your mind as you're lying in bed?"
- "What have you already tried to help yourself sleep?"
- "What do you think is preventing sleep right now?"
- "If you could sleep, what would be different?"

**Practice:** Study Socratic questioning techniques. Practice in role-plays. Record sessions and count the ratio of questions to statements. Aim for 70%+ questions, 30% or less statements/advice.

---

### 5. **Assess and Address Safety Explicitly**

**Action:** When a patient expresses beliefs about harming self or others, conduct a thorough safety assessment rather than normalizing or minimizing.

**Example:** "You mentioned the belief that you've hurt your baby. I need to understand this better. Do you believe this happened in the past, or are you worried it might happen? Have you actually done anything to harm your baby? Are you having urges to harm her? Is your baby currently safe? Do you have access to means that could be used to harm her?"

**Practice:** Learn suicide and homicide risk assessment. Develop protocols for safety planning. Know your duty to warn and duty to protect obligations. Consult with supervisors when safety concerns arise.

---

### 6. **Eliminate Excessive Self-Disclosure; Maintain Professional Boundaries**

**Action:** Minimize personal anecdotes and self-disclosure. Keep focus on the patient's experience, not the therapist's.

**Example:** Instead of "I remember one time I was so tired but couldn't sleep because my mind was racing too," say:
"That sounds really difficult. Tell me more about what that's like for you. When you try to sleep, what happens?"

**Practice:** Review recordings of sessions and count instances of therapist self-disclosure. Aim for zero or minimal self-disclosure. When tempted to share a personal experience, pause and redirect to the patient: "That reminds me to ask you more about your experience..."

---

### 7. **Elicit and Respond to Patient Feedback Throughout Session**

**Action:** Regularly check for understanding, adjust based on feedback, and solicit reactions to interventions.

**Example:**
- "Does that make sense?"
- "How does that land for you?"
- "I suggested trying a bath before bed. Is that something you think you could actually do, or are there barriers?"
- "What's your reaction to what we've discussed so far?"
- "Is this helpful, or would you like to focus on something different?"

**Practice:** Add feedback-checking to every session. Aim for 3-5 explicit feedback checks per session. When patient indicates something isn't working, pause and problem-solve collaboratively rather than repeating the same suggestion.

---

### 8. **Develop Coherent Treatment Strategy Based on Conceptualization**

**Action:** Develop an explicit case conceptualization and treatment strategy that guides all interventions.

**Example:** "Based on what you've shared, here's how I understand your situation: You're experiencing a manic episode with racing thoughts and intrusive beliefs about harming your baby. These beliefs are causing significant distress and isolation. My approach will be to: (1) help you reality-test these beliefs, (2) develop strategies to manage racing thoughts, (3) gradually increase your activity and connection with your husband, and (4) monitor your sleep and mood. Does that make sense? Do you have questions about this plan?"

**Practice:** For each patient, write a one-page conceptualization including: presenting problems, relevant history, maintaining factors, and treatment strategy. Share this with the patient. Refer back to it regularly.

### 9. **Assign Specific, Structured Homework with Clear Rationale**

**Action:** Replace vague suggestions with specific, written homework assignments that include rationale and review plan.

**Example:** "I'd like to assign you a homework task. Here's what I want you to do: Each time you have the thought 'I've hurt my baby,' I want you to write it down on this thought record. The reason I'm asking you to do this is that writing helps us examine our thoughts more carefully and often reveals that our scary thoughts aren't as true as they feel. Can you commit to doing this? When should we review it—at our next session?"

**Practice:** Create homework assignments for every session. Make them specific and measurable. Provide written instructions. Always review homework at the next session. Adjust based on what the patient actually completed.
