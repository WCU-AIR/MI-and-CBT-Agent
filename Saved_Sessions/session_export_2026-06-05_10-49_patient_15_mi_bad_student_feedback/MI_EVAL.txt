**Comments:** Minimal acceptance demonstrated throughout majority of session. Clinician employs overtly directive, pressuring language ("Don't make excuses," "you really should," "you're just letting your health slide") that communicates disapproval and judgment rather than unconditional positive regard. ### Empathy

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Maintains clear target behavior (weight loss through diet and exercise) but pursues it inconsistently with MI principles. Exchanges 2-5 show imposed, prescriptive direction ("You must prioritize health over work. Just commit to walking 30 minutes daily") without exploring client's own goals or what direction he might naturally move toward. Exchanges 6-7 demonstrate improved collaborative direction-setting ("What do you think might be one small, manageable step?") where client identifies own steps (water swap, hourly stretches) and clinician helps clarify and reinforce. Problem is inconsistency—skilled MI clinician would maintain collaborative direction from beginning, asking early: "What have you thought about trying?" rather than imposing solutions.

### Collaboration

Language like "You really should," "You must prioritize," "Don't make excuses," "you have to commit" reflects hierarchical, non-collaborative approach where clinician's expertise is imposed rather than shared. Client's responses reflect breakdown in collaboration: "Just isn't going to work for me," "that feels more like pressure than help," indicating perception of clinician as adversarial rather than collaborative. Therapeutic alliance is damaged. Exchanges 6-7 show substantial improvement with genuine power-sharing ("What do you think might be one small, manageable step?"), but earlier non-collaborative exchanges cause significant damage. True partnership would begin with curiosity and respect for client's expertise, not prescription and pressure.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Nearly absent from session. Clinician repeatedly pressures client to commit to specific behaviors and frames non-compliance as failure or irresponsibility ("you really should," "you must," "you have to commit," "Can you promise me you'll start walking 30 minutes a day tomorrow?"). Uses fear-based pressure ("If you don't start now, your blood pressure will only get worse... serious complications") that frames health information as threat to coerce compliance rather than support self-determination. Never acknowledges client's right to choose differently—no language like "It's ultimately your decision" or "You get to decide what feels right for you." Client explicitly rejects pressure: "that feels more like pressure than help" and "I won't commit to walking 30 minutes a day tomorrow." Minimal improvement in exchanges 6-7 with no explicit affirmation of freedom to choose or acknowledgment that change is client's decision to make.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Largely absent from first five exchanges. Rather than asking client about own reasons for change, clinician tells him why he should change ("your blood pressure will only get worse," "you're risking serious problems like heart disease or stroke"). This is opposite of evocation—attempts to install extrinsic motivation through fear and pressure rather than elicit intrinsic motivation. Client spontaneously offers change talk ("The weight is something I would like to get hold of," "Obviously, I get out of breath… maybe that's probably contributing to it") that clinician fails to evoke or amplify. Skilled clinician would ask follow-up questions to deepen this change talk; instead, clinician ignores openings and continues with directive pressure. Exchanges 6-7 show genuine evocation improvement ("What do you think might be one small, manageable step?") where client generates own ideas (water swap, stretching breaks). However, even here, evocation could be deepened with questions like "What makes you think the water swap might work for you?" Overall pattern shows insufficient curiosity about client's internal motivations.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5
**Comments:** Fundamentally violated throughout most of session. Clinician actively assumes expert role, positioning themselves as authority who knows what client "must" do, dictating solutions (30 minutes daily walking, eliminate sugary drinks and fast food, commit immediately) without genuine inquiry into client's expertise about own life, schedule, and what might work. Client possesses crucial expertise about work demands, schedule constraints, eating patterns, and what has/hasn't worked—clinician dismisses this knowledge ("Why haven't you tried walking before if it's that simple?" "Don't make excuses about being too busy"). Power dynamic heavily skewed toward clinician; client's contributions met with pressure and redirection rather than genuine consideration. Exchanges 6-7 show substantial improvement with genuine power-sharing ("What do you think might be one small, manageable step?" "How do you feel about setting a goal?") where client's ideas substantially influence plan direction. However, earlier damage is severe; truly partnered clinician would begin with curiosity and respect for client's expertise, not prescription and pressure.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Largely absent from session. Client spontaneously offers change talk on multiple occasions, but clinician fails to evoke, amplify, or reinforce it. Missed opportunities include: (1) "The weight is something I would like to get hold of"—clinician does not follow up with curiosity; (2) "I get out of breath… maybe that's probably contributing to it"—clinician does not explore this insight; (3) Exchanges 2-5 show clinician's directive, pressuring approach actually suppresses change talk, triggering defensive sustain talk instead ("I won't commit to walking 30 minutes a day tomorrow"). Exchanges 6-7 show improved cultivation with question "What do you think might be one small, manageable step?" and client responds with change talk ("I could give that a go for a week," "I could try swapping out one sugary drink"). Clinician affirms this ("That's a great start!"). However, cultivation remains basic; clinician could deepen by asking follow-up questions strengthening confidence and commitment: "What makes you think you can do this?" or "How would managing this for a week feel to you?" Overall pattern shows inadequate amplification of client's spontaneous change talk.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Poorly executed throughout most of session. Clinician's approach actively reinforces and amplifies client's sustain talk rather than strategically softening it. When client offers sustain talk ("I really haven't got time," "Food is just fuel," "I haven't tried walking regularly because I literally don't have the time"), clinician responds with confrontation and pressure rather than strategic redirection ("Why haven't you tried to fit in even a few minutes of walking?"). This focuses directly on sustain talk and challenges it confrontationally, typically strengthening resistance rather than softening it. Clinician uses fear-based arguments ("you're risking serious problems like heart disease or stroke") that trigger defensive sustain talk. Never reframes or uses double-sided reflection of barriers; skilled response to "I don't have time" would be: "On one hand, your schedule is genuinely packed. On the other hand, you mentioned wanting to get hold of your weight. How do those two things sit together for you?" Exchanges 6-7 show improvement with strategic questioning ("What do you think might be one small, manageable step?") and normalization ("Forgetting to stretch sometimes is normal when you're busy"). However, clinician could still be more skillful in reframing sustain talk as information about what might work rather than barriers to dismiss.

## Overall MI Competency Assessment

**Overall Session Score: 1.8/5**

**Summary:**

This session demonstrates critical failure in MI practice during first four exchanges, followed by significant improvement in final two exchanges. Clinician begins with directive, pressuring, confrontational approach that violates core MI principles of acceptance, autonomy support, collaboration, and evocation. This approach triggers defensive resistance and damages therapeutic alliance. However, clinician demonstrates capacity to shift toward genuine MI practice in exchanges 6-7, adopting collaborative, evocative, autonomy-supporting stance that re-engages client's motivation for change.

**Current MI Competency Level:** Below-average to minimally adequate. While late-session exchanges show solid MI practice, majority of session demonstrates significant deficiencies that harm client engagement and motivation. Inconsistency is particularly problematic—suggests clinician reverts to directive, expert-driven practice under pressure or when faced with client resistance, rather than maintaining MI principles consistently.

**Most Critical Improvements Needed:**
1. Eliminate directive pressure and confrontation (exchanges 2-5 actively counterproductive)
2. Develop genuine empathic reflection capturing client's experience without judgment
3. Amplify spontaneous change talk rather than missing opportunities
4. Maintain collaborative stance consistently from beginning to end
5. Support autonomy explicitly rather than pressuring compliance

**Recommendation:** Clinician requires focused MI training and supervision to develop consistent, internalized MI competence. Potential is present, but execution currently inadequate for effective client engagement.
