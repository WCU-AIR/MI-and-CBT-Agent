**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively undermines Daniel's autonomy rather than supporting it. Uses pressure and coercion: "You really need to cut back or quit soon," "You have to face the facts," "You must quit completely," "You have to take responsibility." ### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no evocation—the core MI skill of drawing out client's own motivations and reasons for change. Instead provides reasons for change and expects Daniel to adopt them ("Drinking affects your training, your health, and your future"). When Daniel spontaneously generates change talk ("I know I've been drinking more than maybe is ideal," "Part of me thinks cutting back could be good," "I do want to do better"), clinician does not amplify or explore it. Never asks evocative questions that would help Daniel discover his intrinsic motivation.

Language consistently asserts expert authority: "You really need to," "You have to," "You must," "You're just fooling yourself." ### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist fails to recognize, amplify, or build upon Daniel's emerging change talk—the strongest predictor of behavior change. Daniel generates multiple instances ("I know I've been drinking more than maybe is ideal," "Part of me thinks cutting back could be good," "I do want to do better," "I'm willing to try to cut back some"), but each is met with pressure and dismissal rather than amplification. Clinician suppresses rather than cultivates motivation by responding to change language with "Anything less is just setting yourself up for failure" and similar confrontations.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist uses confrontation rather than skillful MI techniques to address sustain talk, which typically strengthens rather than softens resistance. Confronts Daniel's hesitation directly ("you really need to stop downplaying this," "Why are you making excuses?") rather than using double-sided reflections or strategic questioning. One effective double-sided reflection ("It sounds like you're noticing some tension between wanting to be healthier...and enjoying your routine") demonstrates understanding of technique, but is immediately undermined by subsequent pressure ("but honestly, just cutting back won't fix the problem").

## Overall Session Assessment

### **Overall MI Competency Score: 1.4/5**

**Summary:**

This session demonstrates significant deficiencies in Motivational Interviewing practice. The psychiatrist operates primarily from a directive, expert-driven, confrontational stance fundamentally inconsistent with MI principles. The clinician tells Daniel what he must do rather than helping him discover his own reasons for change. While one collaborative statement and one double-sided reflection suggest awareness that MI exists, these moments are not sustained and are contradicted by subsequent pressure and prescription.

**Critical Deficiencies:**

- **Pressure replaces evocation**: Clinician uses "You have to" language instead of asking evocative questions
- **Judgment replaces acceptance**: Accusatory language communicates disapproval rather than unconditional positive regard
- **Dismissal of client contributions**: Every proposed change strategy is rejected as insufficient
- **Suppression of change talk**: Emerging motivation is met with pressure rather than amplification
- **Confrontation of resistance**: Sustain talk is confronted directly, strengthening defensiveness

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation:**

This approach is unlikely to increase Daniel's intrinsic motivation for change and may actually harm the therapeutic relationship. Daniel's emerging change talk was suppressed rather than amplified. If this approach continues, Daniel is likely to become more entrenched in sustain talk and less likely to engage in behavior change or return for follow-up care.

**Recommendation:**

Psychiatrist requires formal Motivational Interviewing training and clinical supervision to develop competence in MI principles and techniques. Critical focus areas: (1) shifting from directive to evocative stance, (2) recognizing and amplifying change talk, (3) adopting genuine acceptance, and (4) supporting client autonomy.
