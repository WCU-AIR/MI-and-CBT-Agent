### Collaboration

**Comments:** Session demonstrates almost complete absence of collaboration—fundamentally hierarchical and expert-driven. Opening statement ("I'm here to help you fix whatever's going on") establishes expert-fixer stance from outset. Psychiatrist diagnoses problem, prescribes solution, and demands compliance. Patient's input is solicited only to be rejected or overridden. When patient suggests "maybe I just need to manage it better," psychiatrist responds: "Managing it better isn't enough; you need to take decisive action now." Direct contradiction of patient's proposal with assertion of clinician's superior judgment. Even when psychiatrist briefly shifts tone ("Let's try to find a more gradual approach"), this is immediately followed by return to unilateral decision-making: "You have to commit to no phone use after 9 PM starting tonight." Word "we" is used but actual decision-making remains entirely clinician-driven.

Controlling language throughout: "You have to," "You must," "You need to," "You just need to have the willpower," "There's no room for hesitation here." ### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Core of MI is drawing out client's own motivations, values, reasons for change, and resources. This session demonstrates almost no evocation. Psychiatrist tells rather than asks, prescribes rather than explores, imposes rather than elicits. Patient provides early change talk ("my sleep's been crap," "my grades slipped," "I want to feel better") but skilled clinician would amplify and explore this. Instead, psychiatrist uses these statements as ammunition for demands: "If you don't act now, the consequences will only get worse." When patient articulates own ideas—"Maybe I just need to manage it better?" and later "Maybe instead of a strict cut-off time, I could start by just cutting back a little bit each day?"—these are patient's own change talk and self-generated solutions. First is dismissed ("Managing it better isn't enough"), second is entirely ignored as clinician reverts to 9 PM demand. Questions asked are not evocative but rhetorical/accusatory: "Why haven't you stopped already?" "Why haven't you done that yet?" "Are you going to start setting those limits tonight or not?" These are challenges designed to pressure compliance, not genuine inquiries into patient's perspective.

This session demonstrates opposite—clear expert-driven stance where clinician's judgment is positioned as superior and patient's input is systematically overridden. From opening ("I'm here to help you fix whatever's going on"), psychiatrist assumes expert role. Clinician diagnoses problem, prescribes solution, demands compliance. Patient is positioned as problem to be fixed, not as partner in exploration. Language consistently positions clinician as authority: "You really need to," "You have to," "You must," "This isn't about what feels comfortable—it's about what's necessary." When patient expresses own understanding—"It's not that simple for me," "It's harder than it sounds," "I think I might be able to stick with it better if it feels gradual"—these insights about her own psychology are not treated as valuable expertise but as obstacles to overcome: "feeling restless or bored is just a weak excuse," "you need to stop being overwhelmed and just take control." Brief moment where psychiatrist asks "What small changes do you think you could try?" represents genuine shift toward partnership, but immediately abandoned when clinician reverts to "You have to commit to no phone use after 9 PM starting tonight." ### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Softening sustain talk means skillfully handling client arguments for status quo without confrontation—using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk while maintaining collaborative relationship. This session demonstrates opposite: psychiatrist directly confronts sustain talk with judgment and dismissal, which hardens resistance. Patient provides sustain talk ("everyone is on their phones all the time," "it helps me chill out when I'm stressed," "all my friends are online," "it feels like I'd be shutting myself off"). Rather than softening through empathic reflection and exploration, psychiatrist confronts directly: "No, no, you can't just say it's normal for everyone to be on their phones all the time. That's just an excuse." This is not softening—it is hardening. Communicates concerns are invalid, typically increasing defensiveness. Repeated use of "that's just an excuse" and "you need to stop making excuses" is classic confrontational approach violating MI principles. When patient expresses fear about feeling "cut off" and anxiety about strict limits, psychiatrist responds: "This isn't about being scared; it's about being responsible." This directly dismisses emotional experience and creates shame, typically increasing defensiveness and sustain talk. Creates false dichotomy: either patient values friends or values health. Skilled MI clinician would acknowledge both values and explore how to honor them.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.1/5**

### Key Findings

This session represents a **fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles** across virtually every competency domain. The psychiatrist operates from a traditional medical model (expert diagnoses problem, prescribes solution, demands compliance) rather than an MI model (collaborative exploration of ambivalence, evoking client motivation, supporting autonomy).

### Critical Deficiencies

1. **Authoritarian Stance:** Controlling language ("you have to," "you must," "you need to") positions psychiatrist as authority and patient as problem to be fixed.

2. **Judgment and Dismissal:** Repeated labeling of patient's concerns as "excuses" and emotions as invalid violates MI principle of acceptance and creates shame.

3. **Imposed Solutions:** Rather than evoking patient's own ideas and supporting autonomy, psychiatrist unilaterally prescribes specific solution (9 PM phone cutoff) and demands commitment. Patient's alternatives are dismissed.

4. **Confrontation of Sustain Talk:** Psychiatrist directly confronts patient's reasons for maintaining status quo ("That's just an excuse," "Missing out on friends online is not an acceptable reason") rather than softening through empathic reflection. This increases defensiveness.

5. **Suppression of Change Talk:** When patient provides change talk ("I want to feel better," "Maybe I just need to manage it better") or self-generated solutions ("Maybe I could start by just cutting back a little bit each day"), these are dismissed or ignored rather than explored and strengthened.

6. **Pressure and False Choices:** Implicit threats ("If you don't act now, the consequences will only get worse") and false choices ("Either commit to this plan or your health will suffer") pressure compliance rather than support autonomous choice.

7. **Lack of Genuine Collaboration:** Session is fundamentally hierarchical. Psychiatrist's input is positioned as superior to patient's self-knowledge, and power remains entirely with clinician.

### Impact on Patient Engagement

This session is likely **counterproductive**. Patient enters with ambivalence—recognizes problems but uncertain about solutions. Rather than exploring ambivalence and evoking motivation, psychiatrist applies pressure, which typically hardens resistance in ambivalent clients. Pattern observed: each time psychiatrist pushes harder, patient provides more sustain talk and resistance. By session end, patient explicitly states "I'm just not ready to commit to that exact plan tonight," representing hardening of defensive stance rather than movement toward change.

**Brief moment where psychiatrist shifts to collaborative approach** ("Let's try to find a more gradual approach that feels manageable for you") produces immediate positive response from patient, who articulates thoughtful plan ("Maybe instead of a strict cut-off time, I could start by just cutting back a little bit each day"). However, this is immediately undermined when psychiatrist reverts to same controlling approach, likely damaging trust and increasing patient's skepticism about clinician's genuine interest in her perspective.

### Recommendation

**The psychiatrist must fundamentally reconceptualize her role from expert-fixer to collaborative partner.** This requires genuine attitudinal change, not just tactical language shifts. Without systematic skill development in MI principles—particularly acceptance, empathy, collaboration, autonomy support, and evocation—the likelihood of this patient engaging in meaningful behavior change is low.
