### **Item 9: Strategy for Change (Rating: 3/6)**

**Stated Strategy:**
1. Help patient develop more balanced thought about being noticed
2. Assign behavioral homework to track moments of being noticed and express gratitude

**Coherence:**
The strategy is logically coherent and grounded in CBT principles (cognitive restructuring + behavioral reinforcement). The logic is: If patient recognizes she is sometimes noticed and valued, her mood will improve and distress will decrease.

**Critical Problem: Fundamental Mismatch to Patient's Diagnosis**

The strategy assumes distress stems from a **cognitive distortion** (believing she is never noticed when evidence suggests otherwise). However, the patient's actual problem is a **personality-level pattern** in which she has organized her identity, relationships, and coping strategies around obtaining attention through drama, seduction, and emotional volatility.

**Why This Strategy Will Likely Fail:**

1. **It reinforces the problem:** Asking patient to track moments when she feels noticed and express gratitude may actually reinforce her belief that external validation is the solution to her distress. This does not address the underlying need for self-validation and healthier interpersonal patterns.

2. **It ignores personality disorder dynamics:** HPD is maintained by reinforcement from others' attention. Standard CBT cognitive restructuring is insufficient because the patient's thoughts are not primarily distorted—they reflect her actual interpersonal reality (people do sometimes ignore her). The problem is her maladaptive response to this normal human experience.

3. **It lacks a coherent model of change:** The therapist has not articulated why changing this one thought would lead to sustained mood improvement or reduced attention-seeking behavior. There is no clear theory of how the homework connects to the broader pattern.

4. **It may be iatrogenic:** The homework may inadvertently strengthen the maladaptive pattern by reinforcing the patient's focus on external validation.

**What Should Have Been Done:**

A more appropriate strategy for HPD would involve:

- **Functional analysis:** Understanding what triggers attention-seeking, what it accomplishes, what emotions it regulates
- **Exposure to discomfort:** Gradually tolerating being unnoticed without resorting to drama or seduction
- **Values clarification:** Exploring what kind of relationships and life the patient actually wants, independent of attention
- **Interpersonal effectiveness training:** Teaching alternative ways to connect with others and meet needs
- **Distress tolerance:** Building capacity to sit with uncomfortable emotions (invisibility, emptiness, shame) without acting out
- **Addressing suicidal threats:** Recognizing these as manipulative attention-seeking and developing alternative coping strategies
- **Identity work:** Helping patient develop sense of self not dependent on others' attention

**Example of More Appropriate Strategy:**

"I'm noticing that you've organized a lot of your life around making sure people notice you. You use drama, your appearance, and even threats to get attention. While this sometimes works in the short term, it seems to be creating problems in your relationships and keeping you stuck in a cycle of feeling desperate and invisible. What if we worked on a different approach? Instead of trying to grab attention, what if we focused on: (1) understanding what you're really needing when you feel invisible, (2) learning to tolerate that feeling without acting out, (3) connecting with people in more authentic ways, and (4) building a sense of yourself that doesn't depend on others' attention?"

**Recommendation:** Completely revise treatment strategy. Conduct comprehensive functional analysis. Develop strategy targeting personality-level patterns, not surface-level thoughts. Consider whether standard individual CBT is sufficient or whether DBT or schema therapy might be more appropriate for this patient.

### **Item 10: Application of Cognitive-Behavioral Techniques (Rating: 4/6)**

**Techniques Applied:**

1. **Cognitive Restructuring:** Therapist guided patient through examining evidence for/against automatic thought, identified counterevidence, and introduced balanced alternative thought. This is a core CBT technique and was executed reasonably well. Therapist used open-ended questions ("What would you say was the evidence...?") rather than lecturing.

2. **Behavioral Experiment/Homework:** Therapist assigned homework to track moments of being noticed and express gratitude, which is a behavioral activation technique designed to test the balanced thought in real life.

3. **Mood Rating Scale:** Used 0-10 scale to quantify mood, providing baseline for tracking change.

4. **Socratic Questioning:** Throughout session, therapist used questions to guide discovery rather than providing direct advice.

**Strengths:**
- Cognitive restructuring was executed with moderate skill
- Socratic questioning was appropriate and effective
- Homework was concrete and specific
- Techniques were appropriately timed

**Significant Limitations:**

1. **Shallow Application:** Cognitive restructuring was relatively brief and surface-level. Therapist did not explore:
   - Origins of the thought
   - Connection to core beliefs
   - Function of the thought in patient's life
   - Example: Did not ask "Where did you learn that you need to be the center of attention to have value?" or "What would it mean about you if people didn't notice you?"

2. **No Behavioral Analysis:** Therapist did not conduct functional analysis of attention-seeking behavior—understanding what triggers it, what it accomplishes, what maintains it. This is essential for personality disorder work.

3. **No Exposure or Values Work:** Given patient's discomfort with being unnoticed, exposure-based approach (gradually tolerating being in background) might be more effective than cognitive restructuring alone. Therapist did not consider this. No values clarification work.

4. **Limited Technique Variety:** Session relied almost entirely on cognitive restructuring and basic behavioral homework. No other CBT techniques employed:
   - Behavioral experiments to test predictions
   - Thought records
   - Activity scheduling
   - Assertiveness training
   - Emotion regulation skills
   - Distress tolerance skills

5. **Technique-Diagnosis Mismatch:** Techniques applied are appropriate for mood disorders and anxiety but insufficient for personality disorder pathology, which typically requires longer-term, more intensive intervention targeting interpersonal patterns and identity.

6. **Insufficient Challenge:** Therapist did not use behavioral experiments to challenge the patient's maladaptive patterns. Example: "This week, try having a conversation with your husband where you simply share something about your day without dramatizing it or seeking his attention. Notice what happens—does he respond differently? What emotions come up for you?"

**Recommendation:** 

- Deepen cognitive work by exploring origins and function of thoughts
- Conduct functional analysis of attention-seeking behavior
- Implement exposure-based interventions for discomfort with being unnoticed
- Add values clarification work
- Teach emotion regulation and distress tolerance skills
- Use behavioral experiments to challenge maladaptive patterns
- Consider whether DBT or schema therapy might be more appropriate than standard CBT

### **Item 11: Homework (Rating: 5/6)**

**Homework Assigned:**
1. Track moments of being noticed or cared for (journaling or phone notes)
2. Express thanks to someone once or twice in next few days
3. Use specific cues as reminders (after phone calls, arriving home from work)

**Strengths:**

1. **Specific:** Clear target behaviors (track moments, express thanks)
2. **Concrete:** Specific cues provided (after phone calls, arriving home)
3. **Rationale-Based:** Therapist explained purpose—to bring positive interactions into focus and test balanced thought
4. **Collaborative:** Patient generated initial ideas (journaling, expressing thanks); therapist refined them together
5. **Feasibility-Checked:** Therapist assessed confidence (6/10) and explored potential obstacles ("What might get in the way?")
6. **Clear Instructions:** Therapist summarized homework at end to ensure understanding
7. **Patient Engagement:** Patient demonstrated comprehension and commitment: "I'll try to keep my journal going and see how it feels to say thanks more"

**Limitations:**

1. **Limited Rationale Depth:** While therapist explained homework's purpose, connection to broader treatment strategy was not explicitly articulated. Therapist did not explain: "This homework will help us test whether your balanced thought is actually true in your daily life, and it will also help you practice connecting with people in a different way."

2. **No Contingency Planning:** Therapist did not develop detailed plan for what to do if homework is not completed or if obstacles arise. Asked "What might get in the way?" but did not develop specific solutions. Example: "If you forget to journal, what could you do to remind yourself? Should we set a phone alarm?"

3. **No Follow-Up Protocol:** Therapist did not explicitly state how homework will be reviewed next session or what data will be collected. Example: "Bring your journal so we can look at the moments you noticed together. We'll also talk about how it felt to express thanks."

4. **Questionable Therapeutic Value:** While homework is well-structured, its therapeutic value for this patient is questionable. Tracking moments of being noticed may reinforce patient's belief that external validation is the solution, rather than addressing core maladaptive pattern. For HPD patient, this homework may strengthen rather than weaken the attention-seeking pattern.

5. **Missed Opportunity:** Homework could have been designed to build distress tolerance and alternative skills. Example: "Notice moments when you feel invisible but don't do anything to grab attention. Just observe what happens. Also, practice one direct communication with your husband about a need you have, without dramatizing."

**Recommendation:**

- Deepen rationale explanation: "This homework will help us test your balanced thought AND help you practice connecting with people in a different way. We'll review it together next session."
- Develop contingency plans: "If you forget to journal, what could remind you? Should we set a phone alarm? What if those moments don't happen—what will you do?"
- Establish follow-up protocol: "Bring your journal next session. We'll look at the moments together and talk about how it felt to express thanks."
- Consider revising homework for personality disorder work: "Notice moments when you feel invisible but don't do anything to grab attention. Just observe what happens. Also, practice one direct communication with your husband about a need you have, without dramatizing or using seduction."
