--- DISORDER ---
Major neurocognitive disorder due to Alzheimer's disease

--- CASE STUDY ---

Arthur Cullman, a 71-year-old married man, was referred to a psychiatrist for evaluation of depressive symptoms unresponsive to medication trials. His wife reported that symptoms began around age 68, approximately one year after his retirement. He gradually stopped engaging in hobbies such as golf and cards, refused social interactions, and spent most of his time on the couch worrying about finances and the future. Despite these changes, he denied sadness, suicidal ideation, or homicidal ideation.

Previous medication trials included sertraline (100 mg/day for 8 months) and sustained-release venlafaxine (150 mg twice daily for over 1 year), both without improvement. Past psychiatric history included a brief depressive episode in his 20s, which resolved without treatment. Family history was notable for a brother with a major depressive episode and a mother with late-onset dementia.

Personal history revealed normal development, college graduation, a successful corporate career, and a stable 45-year marriage with three children and four grandchildren. Medical history included hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and type 2 diabetes mellitus. Medications at evaluation included lisinopril, metformin, simvastatin, and venlafaxine.

Mental status examination revealed an alert, cooperative man, neatly dressed, with a slow but steady gait. He displayed psychomotor slowing, soft speech, and a restricted range of emotional expression. He acknowledged reduced energy and activity, attributing these changes to retirement, and denied self-blame, hopelessness, or suicidal thoughts.

Cognitive examination showed orientation deficits (except for date), impaired short-term memory (remembered 1/3 objects at 2 minutes), impaired attention and calculation (3/5 serial 7 subtractions), naming deficits (4 common objects), and visuospatial deficits (incorrect clock hands placement). Neurological and physical exams were otherwise noncontributory; vital signs included BP 142/82 and pulse 84.

Discussion: Mr. Cullman presents with gradual social withdrawal, apathy, hypersomnia, psychomotor slowing, and cognitive deficits across memory, executive function, and visuospatial domains. Symptoms are progressive over three years and unresponsive to antidepressants, suggesting major neurocognitive disorder rather than primary depression. Visuospatial deficits (abnormal clock drawing) further support Alzheimer’s disease as the likely etiology.
