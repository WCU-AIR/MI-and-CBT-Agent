## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths

This session demonstrates solid foundational MI competence with genuine warmth, consistent reflective listening, and appropriate pacing. The psychiatrist successfully creates a safe, validating environment where Elira feels heard and respected. The use of scaling questions is skillful, and the evocation of the school project example effectively highlights client capability and past success. The clinician appropriately avoids confrontation and maintains a collaborative spirit throughout. The session structure is logical and easy to follow, progressing from problem exploration to strength identification to concrete planning. These elements reflect genuine MI spirit and create conditions for engagement.

### Areas for Improvement

However, the session demonstrates significant gaps in advanced MI competence that limit its effectiveness in evoking sustained motivation for change. Most critically, the psychiatrist gradually shifts from true partnership to an expert-driven stance, particularly when introducing the "if-then" plan. This undermines collaboration and autonomy support. The clinician elicits change talk but does not consistently amplify or deepen it through follow-up questions, missing opportunities to strengthen intrinsic motivation. Sustain talk (doubt, fear, skepticism) is acknowledged but not strategically reframed or redirected, allowing resistance to persist without being addressed. The exploration of intrinsic motivation remains surface-level—the psychiatrist asks *what* Elira wants to change but does not deeply explore *why* it matters or what values drive change. Solutions are primarily clinician-generated rather than co-constructed with the client, which reduces client ownership and self-efficacy. Finally, while the psychiatrist affirms Elira's bravery and self-awareness, there is insufficient explicit recognition of her broader strengths, resilience, and capacity for change.

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change

These gaps have meaningful implications for client engagement and likelihood of sustained change. While Elira leaves the session with a concrete plan (the two-minute task), her readiness for change remains at 4-5/10, and her final statement—*"I'm still nervous about it"*—suggests lingering doubt and ambivalence. The psychiatrist's expert positioning may have inadvertently reduced Elira's sense of ownership over the change process, potentially increasing the likelihood that she will attribute success or failure to the clinician's plan rather than to her own capability. The insufficient amplification of change talk means that Elira's own motivations for change have not been fully explored or strengthened. The unaddressed sustain talk (particularly her doubt about self-trust and fear of failure) remains a significant barrier that could undermine follow-through. While the session is warm and supportive, it does not fully embody the evocative, partnership-based approach that research shows is most effective in strengthening intrinsic motivation and sustaining behavior change.

### Recommendations for Development

1. **Shift from Expert to Collaborative Partner:** Before offering intervention ideas, explicitly invite the client to generate solutions first using language like "You know yourself best. What do you think might help?"

2. **Deepen Exploration of Intrinsic Motivation:** When clients identify reasons for change, ask follow-up questions exploring deeper meaning: "What would that feel like? How would your life be different?"

3. **Strategically Amplify Change Talk:** After each instance of change talk, ask at least one follow-up question that deepens or strengthens it. Track frequency and aim to increase.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk Using Double-Sided Reflections:** Acknowledge both sustain talk and change talk simultaneously, or reframe concerns as part of the change process rather than barriers.

5. **Explicitly Affirm Client Autonomy:** Regularly use language emphasizing choice and self-determination: "This is just an idea. You get to decide what feels right for you."

6. **Invite Collaborative Refinement of Plans:** After any plan emerges, ask: "How does that feel to you? Is there anything you'd want to adjust?"

7. **Increase Recognition of Client Strengths:** Actively identify and affirm specific strengths, capacities, and positive qualities observed, linking these to the change process.

8. **Explore Ambivalence More Deeply:** Resist the urge to problem-solve immediately. Instead, explore both sides of ambivalence more fully before moving to solutions.

9. **Use Scaling Questions More Strategically:** Follow up scaling questions with evocative questions exploring the reasoning behind the number and what would move the client forward.

10. **Establish Clearer, More Specific Change Goals Collaboratively:** Early in the session, ask: "When you imagine yourself no longer avoiding these tasks, what does that look like? What would be different in your life?"
