
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish Clear CBT Structure: Agenda-Setting and Session Planning**
- **Action:** Begin every session by collaboratively setting an agenda. Ask: "What would be most helpful to focus on today? We have limited time, so let's prioritize."
- **Example:** "Wallace, I know you're dealing with tobacco use, sleep problems, and work stress. These are all connected. Today, I'd like to focus on understanding your tobacco use—specifically, what it does for you and when you're most likely to smoke. Does that sound okay? We'll also plan some concrete steps for next time."
- **Practice:** Role-play agenda-setting with a supervisor. Practice saying: "I want to make sure we use our time well. What's most pressing for you right now?" Practice prioritizing when multiple issues are mentioned.

### 2. **Eliminate Inappropriate Self-Disclosure and Maintain Professional Boundaries**
- **Action:** Stop sharing personal experiences, coping strategies, or anecdotes. The session is about the patient's experience, not the therapist's past.
- **Example:** Instead of "When I was stressed, I used to chew gum," say: "I'm curious—when you feel the urge to smoke, what's happening in your body and mind at that moment? What are you feeling?"
- **Practice:** Record yourself and count instances of self-disclosure. Aim for zero personal anecdotes per session. When tempted to share a personal experience, pause and ask yourself: "How does this serve the patient's treatment?" If the answer is "it doesn't," don't say it.

### 3. **Conduct Functional Analysis of Tobacco Use**
- **Action:** Systematically explore what tobacco use accomplishes for the patient. Use guided discovery to help him identify triggers, consequences, and maintaining factors.
- **Example:** "You mentioned tobacco feels like 'the only relief sometimes.' Let's understand that better. Think about the last time you smoked. What was happening right before? What were you feeling? What happened after you smoked? How did you feel then?"
- **Practice:** Develop a functional analysis template: Trigger → Thought/Feeling → Behavior (smoking) → Consequence. Use this to organize information about the patient's tobacco use.

### 4. **Develop a Coherent Treatment Strategy with Clear Rationale**
- **Action:** Articulate a specific treatment plan that addresses the presenting problem. Explain the logic to the patient.
- **Example:** "Here's how I understand your tobacco use: It's your primary way of managing stress and anxiety. But it's disrupting your sleep, which increases stress, which leads to more smoking. Our strategy will be to: (1) understand what tobacco is doing for you, (2) develop alternative ways to manage stress that won't disrupt sleep, (3) gradually reduce tobacco use as you build new skills. Does that make sense?"
- **Practice:** Write out a treatment plan for this patient before each session. Include: (1) problem formulation, (2) treatment goals, (3) specific interventions, (4) expected timeline.

### 5. **Apply Specific CBT Techniques: Start with Behavioral Experiments**
- **Action:** Design behavioral experiments to test whether alternatives to smoking can provide similar stress relief.
- **Example:** "Let's test something. This week, when you feel the urge to smoke, try taking a 10-minute walk first. Notice what happens. Does the urge decrease? Do you feel calmer? We'll review what you discover next session."
- **Practice:** Learn and practice behavioral experiments. Understand the rationale: experiments help patients discover that alternatives work, which builds motivation to change.

### 6. **Use Guided Discovery Instead of Advice-Giving**
- **Action:** Ask questions that help the patient reach insights independently. Avoid telling him what to do.
- **Example:** Instead of "You should take walks," ask: "You mentioned feeling stuck in a cycle with smoking. What do you think would happen if you did something different when you feel stressed—like taking a walk instead of smoking? What might change?"
- **Practice:** For every piece of advice you want to give, convert it to a question. Practice asking: "What do you think?" "What have you tried before?" "What might work for you?"

### 7. **Assign Specific, Measurable Homework with Clear Rationale**
- **Action:** Assign homework that is specific, measurable, and directly connected to treatment goals. Explain the rationale and check commitment.
- **Example:** "I'd like you to try something this week. Each evening at 7 PM—when you usually smoke after work—take a 15-minute walk instead. Before the walk, notice what you're feeling. After the walk, notice if anything changed. Write down what you observe. We'll review this next session to see if walks can help with stress. Does that feel doable?"
- **Practice:** Write out homework assignments before sessions. Include: (1) specific behavior, (2) frequency/timing, (3) what to observe/measure, (4) rationale, (5) check for commitment.

### 8. **Elicit and Respond to Feedback Throughout the Session**
- **Action:** Regularly check understanding and solicit reactions. Ask: "Does this make sense?" "How are you feeling about this?" "What's your reaction?"
- **Example:** After explaining the stress-tobacco-sleep cycle, ask: "Does that match your experience? What do you think about that pattern?"
- **Practice:** Build feedback checks into your session structure. After each major intervention, pause and ask for feedback. At session end, ask: "What was most helpful today? What would you like to focus on next time?"

### 9. **Review Homework and Track Progress Systematically**
- **Action:** Begin each session by reviewing previous homework. Ask what the patient tried, what happened, and what was learned.
- **Example:** "Last time we talked about taking walks when you feel stressed. Did you try that? What happened? What did you notice?"
- **Practice:** Create a progress tracking sheet. Document: (1) homework assigned, (2) homework completed, (3) results/observations, (4) adjustments for next week.

### 11. **Establish Clear Measurement and Progress Monitoring**

- **Example:** "Let's track your tobacco use. How many cigarettes per day are you smoking now? Let's aim to reduce by 2-3 per week. We'll track this each session."
