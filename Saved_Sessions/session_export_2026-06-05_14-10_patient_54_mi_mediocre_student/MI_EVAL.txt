Phrases like "Maybe you should try to push yourself a bit" and "it would probably help if you just do something small" communicate that the psychiatrist has decided what's best, not that Isabella has genuine choice. The problem is unilaterally defined without inviting Isabella to define it. Solutions are repeatedly offered by the psychiatrist ("Maybe you should reach out to someone," "Maybe you should set aside some time," "Maybe you should write down some places") rather than co-created with Isabella. ### Critical Areas for Improvement

**Fundamental MI Spirit Violation:**
This session represents a significant departure from Motivational Interviewing principles. The psychiatrist operates from an expert-driven, directive stance that is antithetical to MI. Rather than evoking Isabella's own motivations and collaboratively exploring her ambivalence, the psychiatrist prescribes solutions and applies subtle pressure through repeated "should" statements. This approach communicates: "I know what's best for you, and you should do it"—which undermines autonomy, collaboration, and partnership.

**Key Deficits:**
- Expert-driven stance with no genuine partnership or power-sharing
- Repeated prescriptive language ("should," "maybe you should") that undermines autonomy
- Dismissal of stated barriers (lack of support, overwhelm, low motivation) rather than validation and exploration
- Minimal evocation; advice-giving replaces asking
- Surface-level empathy missing critical emotional content about isolation and overwhelm
- Low motivation (4/10) not respected; pressure maintained despite clear signal
- Sustain talk met with pressure rather than strategic softening
- Change talk minimally evoked and not amplified
- Session prioritizes psychiatrist's agenda over Isabella's own goals and values

---

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change

Isabella's agreement to the micro-goal appears compliant rather than motivated. Her brief, hesitant responses throughout suggest she is not genuinely engaged but rather complying with the psychiatrist's agenda. The likelihood of follow-through is low given her pattern of inaction and lack of genuine motivation cultivation. If she does not follow through, the psychiatrist's response will likely be further pressure, creating a cycle of non-compliance and frustration. This session demonstrates how well-intentioned advice-giving can paradoxically decrease motivation for change and may trigger reactance—a psychological response where people resist being told what to do.

---

### Recommendations for Improvement

1. **Abandon Directive Stance:** Shift from expert-driven to collaborative partnership. Begin by asking what Isabella wants to focus on, not by assuming the referral concern is her concern.

2. **Evoke Rather Than Prescribe:** Replace advice-giving with genuine evocation. Ask open questions that draw out her own thinking: "What do you think?" "What would be good about...?" "Tell me more about..."

3. **Respect Low Motivation:** When Isabella rates importance at 4/10, explore this with curiosity rather than pressure. Ask: "What keeps it at a 4? What would need to change for this to feel more important?"

4. **Validate and Explore Barriers:** When Isabella expresses barriers, validate these as real and important, then explore with curiosity rather than dismissing or problem-solving.

5. **Develop Complex Reflections:** Move beyond surface-level reflections to capture both sides of ambivalence: "Part of you sees value in getting involved, but another part feels really overwhelmed..."

6. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk:** When Isabella offers change talk, deepen it through follow-up questions rather than moving forward with the next directive.

7. **Use Double-Sided Reflections:** Soften sustain talk by acknowledging both concerns and potential motivation without confrontation.

8. **Explore Values and Goals:** Ask open questions about what matters to Isabella and what she wants her life to look like, rather than imposing goals.

9. **Eliminate "Should" Language:** Replace prescriptive language with collaborative alternatives. Audit recordings for "should" statements and aim for zero.

10. **Develop Genuine Empathy:** Explore emotional content more deeply. When Isabella mentions overwhelm or isolation, ask follow-up questions that help you understand her internal experience.

11. **Respect Autonomy:** Ensure Isabella has genuine choice throughout. Offer options rather than prescriptions.

12. **Seek Formal MI Training:** This session demonstrates significant departures from MI principles. Seek formal MI training (through MINT—Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers) and regular supervision with an MI-trained clinician.

---

### Conclusion

This session demonstrates fundamental misalignment with Motivational Interviewing principles and spirit. The psychiatrist's well-intentioned advice-giving is inconsistent with MI and represents a significant clinical gap. Immediate and substantial changes in approach are necessary. Without formal MI training and supervision, future sessions with this client and others are likely to be ineffective in evoking sustainable motivation for change. The psychiatrist should prioritize developing the collaborative, evocative stance that characterizes skillful MI practice.
