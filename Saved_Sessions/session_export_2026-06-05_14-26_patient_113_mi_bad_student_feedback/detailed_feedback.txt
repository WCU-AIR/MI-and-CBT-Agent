
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

The psychiatrist demonstrated minimal acceptance, particularly in the first four exchanges. During the initial interactions, the clinician used judgmental language and conveyed disapproval of the patient's ambivalence. Phrases like "You really should stop smoking now," "Why haven't you done that yet?" and "You can't keep making excuses" communicated conditional regard—acceptance only if Rosa complied with the clinician's directive. The statement "Your job or friendships can't be more important than your health" dismissed Rosa's real-world constraints and communicated judgment about her priorities.

The statement "I realize now that pushing you to set a quit date this week might have felt overwhelming and not supportive of your situation" showed genuine acceptance and validation. The language became warmer: "I'm glad you shared that idea," "That's great to hear," and "Remember, this is your journey, and taking it step by step is perfectly okay" conveyed authentic respect and affirmation. This late-session pivot demonstrates the clinician can access acceptance but failed to maintain it consistently throughout most of the session.

**Summary:**

Early session acceptance was poor and judgmental; late-session acceptance improved significantly but came too late to repair the damage from earlier confrontation. Overall acceptance remains below adequate due to the extended period of conditional regard and dismissal of client concerns.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 2/5**

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration was virtually absent throughout the majority of the session. MI collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring "between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful." ## Recommendations

### 1. **Eliminate Prescriptive, Directive Language (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Replace "you have to," "you must," "you need to," and "you should" with collaborative, open-ended language that invites client input and respects autonomy
- **Example:** Instead of "You really should stop smoking now because it's harming your health," say: "I'm hearing that your doctor's warning about future health risks really got your attention. What concerns you most about those potential risks?"
- **Practice:** Record yourself in role-plays and count instances of prescriptive language. Set a goal of zero per session. Practice reframing directives as open questions: "You need to exercise more" becomes "What role might physical activity play in your quit plan?"

### 2. **Develop Genuine Evocative Questioning Skills**
- **Action:** Learn to ask open-ended questions that draw out client's own motivations, values, and resources rather than prescribing solutions. Focus on "what" and "how" questions that invite exploration
- **Example:** Instead of "Have you considered using nicotine replacement therapy?" ask: "What have you learned about yourself from your previous quit attempts? What worked, even a little bit? What would you want to do differently this time?"
- **Practice:** Before each session, prepare 5-7 evocative questions targeting: (1) values ("What matters most to you about your health?"), (2) past successes ("When have you successfully made a difficult change?"), (3) self-efficacy ("What strengths do you have that could help you?"), (4) vision ("What would life look like if you quit?")

### 3. **Amplify and Reinforce Change Talk Consistently**
- **Action:** When clients offer change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment), pause and explore it rather than pivoting to barriers or solutions. Use reflections and follow-up questions to deepen and strengthen their own motivation
- **Example:** When Rosa said "I know I've got to stop," the clinician should have responded: "You feel like you've got to stop. Tell me more about what's driving that feeling. What happened when your doctor talked to you about the future risks?" (This deepens the change talk rather than moving past it)
- **Practice:** In role-plays, identify every instance of client change talk and practice responding with: (1) a reflection that captures the statement, (2) a follow-up question that deepens it, (3) affirmation of the client's own motivation

### 4. **Use Strategic Double-Sided Reflections to Soften Sustain Talk**
- **Action:** When clients express reasons against change, acknowledge both sides of their ambivalence rather than confronting or dismissing. Use reflections that validate the concern while gently opening space for change talk
- **Example:** Instead of "Weight gain and feeling cranky are temporary. You'll feel better once you push through," say: "So on one hand, you're worried that quitting will lead to weight gain and feeling cranky—those are real concerns based on your past experience. And on the other hand, you're concerned about your long-term health and what the doctor warned you about. That's a tough spot to be in."
- **Practice:** For each sustain talk statement, write a double-sided reflection that: (1) validates the concern, (2) acknowledges the competing value or change talk, (3) avoids "but" (use "and" instead)

### 5. **Establish True Collaboration and Partnership**
- **Action:** Explicitly invite client input into goal-setting and planning. Use language that positions the client as expert on their own life. Seek permission before offering suggestions. Share power in decision-making
- **Example:** Instead of "Setting a quit date this week is non-negotiable," say: "I'm wondering what timeline feels realistic to you. You know your life better than I do—what would actually work with your job and your relationships?"
- **Practice:** In each session, use phrases like: "What do you think?", "You know yourself best—what would work?", "Can I share a thought, or would you rather explore this more first?", "How do you see us working together on this?"

### 6. **Develop Genuine Empathic Accuracy Through Complex Reflections**
- **Action:** Move beyond simple reflections to complex reflections that capture emotional nuance, underlying values, and meaning. Reflect not just content but the feeling and significance behind it
- **Example:** Instead of "You're worried about weight gain," try: "So smoking has been part of your identity for so long that quitting feels like losing a piece of yourself. And at the same time, you're scared about what your health might look like in the future if you don't change. That's a real conflict—wanting to protect who you are while also wanting to protect your future health."
- **Practice:** After each client statement, pause and ask yourself: "What is the deeper feeling or value here? What does this mean to this person?" Practice writing complex reflections that capture 2-3 layers of meaning

### 7. **Respect and Explore Client Autonomy and Real-World Constraints**
- **Action:** When clients mention obstacles (job demands, financial limitations, social concerns), treat these as valid information about their life, not as excuses. Explore how to work within their real constraints rather than dismissing them
- **Example:** Instead of "Your job or friendships can't be more important than your health," say: "It sounds like your job and your friendships are really important to you, and you're trying to figure out how to make changes without losing those things. That makes sense. Let's think together about how we can work with your real life, not against it."
- **Practice:** When you hear an obstacle, respond with curiosity: "Tell me more about that," "How does that fit into your life?", "What would need to happen for that to work?" rather than dismissing it

### 8. **Recognize and Repair Ruptures in the Therapeutic Alliance**
- **Action:** Develop awareness of when your approach is creating resistance or defensiveness. When you notice the client becoming more defensive or entrenched, pause and acknowledge the shift. Use statements like "I notice I might be pushing too hard" or "Let me step back—I want to understand your perspective better"
- **Example:** The psychiatrist did this effectively in exchange 6: "I realize now that pushing you to set a quit date this week might have felt overwhelming and not supportive of your situation." This acknowledgment and course correction likely preserved the alliance
- **Practice:** After each session, reflect: "Were there moments when the client became more defensive? What was I doing? How could I have responded differently?" Build this reflection into your routine

### 9. **Avoid Pseudo-Empathy and Conditional Acceptance**
- **Action:** When you acknowledge a client's feeling, don't immediately contradict it with "but" statements that override their experience. True acceptance means validating their perspective even when you disagree with their choice
- **Example:** Instead of "I understand you're worried about weight gain, but you really need to push through those feelings," say: "Weight gain is a real concern for you based on what happened before. That's worth taking seriously as we think about how to approach this."
- **Practice:** Review your reflections and remove "but" statements that negate what came before. Replace with "and" to hold both sides of ambivalence

### 10. **Implement MI-Consistent Session Structure**
- **Action:** Structure sessions using the MI framework: (1) Establish collaboration and agenda, (2) Explore ambivalence and evoke change talk, (3) Develop discrepancy between current behavior and values, (4) Roll with resistance rather than confronting it, (5) Support self-efficacy and autonomy, (6) Summarize and plan collaboratively
- **Example:** Begin with "What would be most helpful to talk about today?" rather than "We need to set a quit date." End with "What feels like a realistic next step for you?" rather than "You have to commit to quitting this week"
- **Practice:** Use a session planning template that includes: (1) evocative questions prepared in advance, (2) anticipated sustain talk and planned double-sided reflections, (3) collaborative goal-setting approach, (4) autonomy-supportive language
