When client explicitly states boundary twice ("It's not really any of your business"), psychiatrist does not respect this or acknowledge right to decline. ### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates almost no evocation—the core of MI. Psychiatrist repeatedly tells client what she should think/feel/do and asks leading questions confirming clinician hypotheses rather than exploring client perspective. Examples: "Maybe you don't eat well?" (leading), "Do you find it hard...because of time, motivation, or maybe something else?" (multiple-choice limiting options), "Maybe motivation is a bit low?" (clinician interpreting silence as confirmation). True evocation requires open-ended questions like "What matters most to you about your health?" or "What have you noticed about your health that concerns you?" No change talk elicited because no genuine exploration of client's own motivations occurs.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.1/5**

**Strengths:**
- Maintains calm, non-hostile tone throughout session
- Does not become overtly confrontational or argumentative
- Maintains clear focus on health behavior change
- Offers concrete, specific suggestions (e.g., "five-minute walk," "choosing water instead of soda")
- Attempts to normalize future contact ("We can plan a bit more next time")

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**

This session represents a **fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles and spirit**. The psychiatrist operates from an expert-driven, directive model that directly contradicts MI's core values of partnership, autonomy support, and evocation. The clinician repeatedly overrides the client's explicit boundaries, makes unilateral decisions about session focus, uses leading questions to confirm clinician hypotheses rather than explore client perspective, and prescribes solutions without understanding the client's own motivations or readiness for change.

**Most Critical Issue:** The psychiatrist fails to recognize that the client is in **precontemplation** and is not ready to engage with the clinician's agenda. Rather than adapting the approach to build rapport and explore readiness, the clinician persists with the same directive strategy, which is likely to **increase defensiveness and resistance**. The client's statement "It's not really any of your business" is a clear boundary that should be respected and explored, not overridden.

**Current MI Competency Assessment:** The psychiatrist demonstrates **minimal competency in Motivational Interviewing**. The session shows reliance on traditional expert-driven counseling, which is contraindicated for this client presentation. Formal MI training and supervision are strongly recommended.

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation for Change:** **Very Low.** This session is likely to increase Amina's defensiveness and resistance rather than evoke intrinsic motivation for change. The repeated boundary violations, directive language, and failure to build rapport create an adversarial dynamic antithetical to MI spirit. A client in precontemplation who is already resistant requires a fundamentally different approach—one focused on engagement, acceptance, and exploration rather than prescription and persuasion.
