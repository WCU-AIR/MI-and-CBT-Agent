
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Softening sustain talk requires skillfully handling client arguments for the status quo without confrontation, using techniques like double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses. This session contains explicit sustain talk from the client ("It's not really any of your business," stated twice), which represents a clear argument against engaging with the clinician's agenda.

The psychiatrist's response to this sustain talk is to acknowledge it minimally and then override it: "Okay, I understand you might feel that way. While the clinician does not directly confront or argue with the client, the approach is still problematic because it communicates that the client's boundary is not valid and should be overcome.

- Reflecting the sustain talk: "It sounds like you feel like this is private and not something you want to discuss right now."

- Double-sided reflection: "On one hand, you feel like this is your business and not mine. On the other hand, you're here, which suggests some part of you is open to talking."

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish Genuine Rapport and Acceptance Before Pursuing Change Agenda**
- **Action:** In the opening of the session, spend 5-10 minutes building rapport and communicating unconditional positive regard before introducing any change-focused agenda. Use open-ended questions to learn about the client as a person, not just as a bearer of health problems.
- **Example:** "Hi Amina, thanks for coming in today. Before we talk about anything specific, I'd like to get to know you a bit. What's been going on with you lately?" or "I'm wondering what brought you in today—what's on your mind?"
- **Practice:** Practice opening sessions with genuine curiosity about the client's life, perspective, and experience. Record yourself and listen for how much you're asking vs. telling. Aim for 80% questions, 20% statements in the opening phase.

---

### 2. **Respect Explicit Boundaries and Explore Them with Curiosity Rather Than Persistence**
- **Action:** When a client states "It's not really any of your business," treat this as valuable information about their comfort level and readiness, not as an obstacle to overcome. Reflect it back, validate it, and explore it with genuine curiosity.
- **Example:** Instead of "Okay, I understand you might feel that way, but just to help you better..." try: "I hear you—this feels private to you. I'm curious about that. What's your concern about talking with me about this?" or "It sounds like you're not sure you want to discuss this with me. That makes sense. What would need to be different for you to feel comfortable?"
- **Practice:** In role-plays, practice responding to client resistance with reflection and exploration rather than persistence. When a client says "no" or sets a boundary, pause and ask yourself: "What is this client telling me about their readiness or comfort level? How can I explore this with curiosity rather than override it?"

---

### 3. **Use Open-Ended Questions to Evoke Client Perspective Rather Than Leading Questions to Confirm Clinician Hypotheses**
- **Action:** Replace leading questions ("Maybe you don't eat well?", "Maybe motivation is a bit low?") with genuine open-ended questions designed to understand the client's actual perspective and concerns.
- **Example:** Instead of "Do you find it hard to keep up with exercise or eating healthy because of time, motivation, or maybe something else?" try: "What's your experience been with health and wellness? What matters to you about your health?" or "If you were to think about your health right now, what comes to mind?"
- **Practice:** Practice asking open-ended questions and then *listening* without interrupting or interpreting. When the client gives a minimal response ("Mm-hmm," "Okay"), resist the urge to fill the silence with your own interpretation. Instead, ask a follow-up question: "Tell me more about that" or "What do you mean by that?"

---

### 4. **Avoid Prescriptive Language and Instead Invite Collaboration on Solutions**
- **Action:** Replace "You might want to..." and "It would probably help if you..." with collaborative language that invites the client's input and positions them as the expert on their own life.
- **Example:** Instead of "You might want to consider starting with something easy, like a five-minute walk or choosing healthier snacks," try: "I'm wondering what you think might be possible for you. What, if anything, have you thought about trying?" or "If you were to make one small change, what do you think that might be?"
- **Practice:** In supervision or role-plays, practice replacing prescriptive statements with collaborative questions. Count how many times you use "you should," "you might want to," or "it would help if you" and aim to reduce this to zero. Replace each with a question that invites the client's own thinking.

---

### 5. **Recognize and Adapt to Client Readiness Stage (Precontemplation)**
- **Action:** Assess the client's stage of change early in the session. When a client is in precontemplation (not acknowledging or discussing concerns), the goal is not to move them to action—it is to build rapport, explore ambivalence, and increase awareness. Adjust your approach accordingly.
- **Example:** With Amina, who is clearly in precontemplation and resistant, the appropriate goal for this first session is: "Build rapport, communicate acceptance, explore her perspective on why she's here, and understand her concerns about the process." NOT: "Get her to commit to a specific health behavior change."
- **Practice:** Before each session, identify the client's likely stage of change and set stage-appropriate goals. For precontemplation clients, focus on engagement and exploration. For contemplation clients, focus on evoking and exploring ambivalence. For preparation/action clients, focus on planning and support.

---

### 6. **Use Reflective Listening to Demonstrate Empathy and Build Alliance**
- **Action:** When the client provides minimal responses, use reflective listening to show that you're trying to understand their perspective. Reflect back what you hear, including the emotional tone, and invite them to correct or expand.
- **Example:** When Amina says "It's not really any of your business," reflect: "It sounds like you feel like this is private and you're not sure you want to talk about it with me" or "I'm hearing that you're not comfortable with me asking about this right now." Then pause and listen.
- **Practice:** Practice reflective listening in role-plays. After each client statement, pause and offer a reflection before asking another question. Aim for a ratio of 2 reflections for every 1 question. Record yourself and listen for how often you're reflecting vs. asking.

---

### 7. **Communicate Genuine Autonomy Support by Affirming Client Choice and Control**
- **Action:** Explicitly communicate that the client has the right to choose what to discuss, whether to engage, and what changes (if any) to pursue. Use language that emphasizes their control and ownership.
- **Example:** "You get to decide what we talk about today. I'm here to support you, but it's your choice what we focus on" or "Whether you make any changes is entirely up to you. I'm not here to convince you—I'm here to support you if you want to explore something."
- **Practice:** In each session, include at least 2-3 explicit statements affirming the client's autonomy and choice. Practice saying "It's your choice," "You get to decide," "You're in control here" and notice how it feels. Record yourself and listen for how genuine these statements sound.

---

### 8. **Explore Ambivalence Rather Than Pushing for Commitment**
- **Action:** When a client is ambivalent (showing both resistance and minimal openness), explore both sides of the ambivalence rather than pushing toward change. Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge the push-pull.
- **Example:** "On one hand, you feel like this is private and not something you want to discuss. On the other hand, you're here, which suggests some part of you is open to talking. I'm curious about both of those parts."
- **Practice:** In role-plays with resistant clients, practice offering double-sided reflections. Acknowledge the client's resistance while also noting any small signs of openness. Avoid pushing toward one side or the other.

---

### 9. **Develop Comfort with Silence and Minimal Client Responses**
- **Action:** When a client gives minimal responses ("Mm-hmm," "Okay," "Fine"), resist the urge to fill the silence or interpret it as agreement. Instead, pause, sit with the silence, and ask a follow-up question that invites elaboration.
- **Example:** After Amina says "Okay," instead of assuming agreement and moving forward, try: "What does 'okay' mean to you?" or "Tell me more about that" or simply pause and wait for the client to elaborate.
- **Practice:** In role-plays, practice tolerating 5-10 seconds of silence after a client's minimal response. Notice your urge to fill the silence and practice resisting it. Ask yourself: "What is this silence telling me? What question could I ask to understand the client's actual perspective?"
