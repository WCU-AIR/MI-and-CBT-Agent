### Summary of Key Findings

#### **Strengths:**
- ✓ Maintains clear focus on presenting concern (substance use and recovery)
- ✓ Establishes basic rapport with warm opening greeting
- ✓ Acknowledges client's three months of progress
- ✓ Attempts to structure session with clear plan and follow-up

#### **Critical Deficiencies:**
- ✗ **Expert-driven stance dominates:** Repeated prescriptive language ("you should," "it would probably help if") undermines collaboration and autonomy
- ✗ **Superficial reflective listening:** Consistently misses emotional content; reflections are factual and transactional
- ✗ **Failure to evoke:** Relies on advice-giving rather than drawing out client's own motivations, values, and strengths
- ✗ **Absent collaboration:** No genuine power-sharing; psychiatrist's agenda drives session
- ✗ **Undermined autonomy:** Subtle pressure and prescriptive language minimize client's sense of choice and control
- ✗ **Ineffective handling of ambivalence:** Does not skillfully address client's fundamental doubt about whether change is self-directed

#### **Impact on Client Engagement:**
This session is likely to **decrease intrinsic motivation for change**. While client enters with change talk and openness, psychiatrist's prescriptive approach risks triggering psychological reactance and reinforcing client's fear that change is being imposed rather than chosen. Session ends with compliance ("I'll try to stay with the plan") rather than genuine commitment—concerning for sustained change.

#### **Current MI Competency Level:**
This session reflects **supportive counseling** rather than true motivational interviewing. Psychiatrist demonstrates basic clinical skills but lacks specific MI competencies required to effectively evoke client motivation for change.

### Priority Recommendations for Improvement

**1. HIGHEST PRIORITY – Shift from Expert-Driven to Collaborative Stance**
- Eliminate prescriptive language ("you should," "it would probably help if")
- Replace with genuine inquiry: "What do you think would work best for you?"
- Position client as expert on his own life
- Practice: Count prescriptive statements in next session; goal = zero instances

**2. SECOND PRIORITY – Develop Complex Reflective Listening**
- Move beyond factual reflections to capture emotional nuance and meaning
- When client expresses emotion (shame, regret, ambivalence), reflect the emotion
- Example: Instead of "So you mentioned drinking and pills—are those the main things?" say "It sounds like that relapse brought back all those painful feelings of having let yourself down, and that's weighing heavily on you right now."

**3. Strategically Evoke and Amplify Change Talk**
- When client produces change talk, pause and explore it with open questions
- Ask: "Tell me more about that," "What does that mean to you?" "What would it feel like?"
- Reflect back change talk to strengthen it
- Example: When client says "I want things to be different — for me and for my family," explore: "Your family is really important to you. Tell me about what you want for them."

**4. Explore and Address Ambivalence Using Double-Sided Reflections**
- Acknowledge both sides of conflict without judgment
- Example: "Part of you wants to make this change for yourself, and another part isn't sure if you're doing it because you want to or because you feel you have to. Tell me more about that."
- Create space for exploration rather than pressure

**5. Support Autonomy by Emphasizing Client Choice**
- Explicitly affirm client's right to choose
- Use language: "What feels right to you?" "You get to decide." "What do you think?"
- Avoid language implying obligation: "you should," "you need to," "you have to"

**6. Seek Formal MI Training and Supervision**
- Attend MI workshop or certification program
- Receive individual supervision from MI-trained clinician
- Review session recordings with supervisor
- Commit to minimum 10 hours MI-specific training before next session with this client
- Resource: Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers (MINT): www.motivationalinterviewing.org

---

### Recommendation for Clinical Practice

**This psychiatrist should not continue conducting MI-focused sessions without formal training and supervision.** While basic clinical competence is evident, significant gaps in MI-specific competencies create risk of undermining client motivation and triggering psychological reactance. Formal MI training is essential before continuing this therapeutic approach.
