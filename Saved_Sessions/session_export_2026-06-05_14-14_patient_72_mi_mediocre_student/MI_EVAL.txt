#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates severe and consistent violation of autonomy support—a core MI pillar. Psychiatrist repeatedly pressures Laura toward specific behavioral changes while minimizing her right to choose. Directive advice throughout with "should" or "might want to" used at least 8 times. Directly overrides Laura's stated preferences: when Laura says she's "not a morning person" and hasn't tried morning yoga, clinician suggests it anyway. Prescriptive planning without co-creation. Fundamental violation of MI principles severely limiting likelihood of sustainable change.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal evocation—the core MI skill of drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources. Relies heavily on telling and advising instead. Asks "Have you tried any ways to overcome those challenges before?" but immediately pivots to offering solutions rather than exploring Laura's problem-solving capacity. Fails to evoke deeper motivations: never asks "What is it about feeling grounded that matters so much?" or "What would it feel like to have your actions match your values?" Never evokes Laura's resources and strengths despite evidence of self-awareness and willingness. Limited strategic amplification of change talk present in session. When Laura says "I'm willing to give it a shot," clinician responds with support but doesn't deepen evocation. Fails to explore ambivalence through evocation—provides reassurance instead when Laura expresses doubt.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates fundamental failure to establish true partnership. Psychiatrist consistently positions themselves as expert with solutions and Laura as recipient of advice, contradicting MI principle that expertise about change resides mostly within client. Expert-driven stance pervasive: "Maybe you should try shorter sessions," "you might want to work on building up confidence," "It's important to not expect too much too soon." Unilateral decision-making: clinician decides focus, strategies, and plan without Laura's input. Power imbalance controls session direction, generates all solutions, makes final recommendations. Laura's role is to respond to suggestions, not shape session. Dismisses Laura's contributions: when she offers information about herself ("I'm not a morning person," "I worry about satisfaction"), clinician overrides rather than treating as valuable self-expertise. Clinician positioned as judge of Laura's capability. No shared decision-making. Inclusive language absent—uses "you should," "I think," "I'm confident" rather than "we," "together," "your ideas." Critical failure in MI practice undermining client autonomy and intrinsic motivation.

## Summary of Clinical Findings

### Strengths
- Clear, warm opening that creates initial safety and inviting stance
- Maintains topical consistency on Laura's health and yoga practice throughout session
- Demonstrates basic interpersonal warmth and supportive language at times
- Attempts readiness assessment using confidence scale (1-10)

### Critical Deficiencies

**This session fundamentally fails to embody the spirit of Motivational Interviewing.** The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, directive stance that directly contradicts core MI principles. Rather than evoking Laura's own motivation, the clinician tells her what to do. Rather than supporting autonomy, the psychiatrist prescribes specific behavioral changes. Rather than establishing partnership, the clinician maintains hierarchical control.

**Most significant deficiencies:**

1. **Pervasive directiveness:** Prescriptive language ("you should," "you might want to") used at least 8 times, positioning clinician as expert with solutions rather than supporting Laura's own motivation and wisdom

2. **Failure to establish partnership:** Unilateral decision-making about what Laura should do; clinician generates all solutions; Laura's ideas, preferences, and self-expertise not solicited or valued

3. **Systematic violation of autonomy support:** Repeated pressure toward specific changes; overriding of Laura's stated preferences (e.g., suggesting morning yoga despite "not a morning person"); undermining of intrinsic motivation

4. **Minimal evocation:** Advice-giving dominates rather than drawing out Laura's own motivations, ideas, and resources—the core MI skill

5. **Inadequate empathy:** Focus on factual problem-solving rather than understanding emotional experience, ambivalence, and internal struggle; complex reflections absent

6. **Poor handling of ambivalence:** When Laura expresses doubt or concern, clinician responds with reassurance and more advice rather than exploring and softening ambivalence

7. **Insufficient cultivation of change talk:** While Laura provides some change talk, clinician does not strategically evoke, amplify, or reinforce it; advice-giving actually undermines intrinsic motivation

### Impact on Client Engagement and Sustainable Change

**This approach significantly reduces likelihood of sustainable change.** While Laura expresses willingness to try suggested strategies, her motivation is likely compliance-based (following clinician's recommendations) rather than intrinsic (her own reasons for change). Research consistently shows intrinsic motivation predicts sustained behavior change; compliance-based motivation is fragile and easily abandoned.

By overriding Laura's stated preferences, psychiatrist sets her up for failure. Laura's realistic concerns about confidence and follow-through are dismissed rather than explored, potentially increasing hopelessness about change. Session misses critical opportunities to deepen Laura's connection to her values. Power imbalance established may reduce Laura's willingness to be honest about struggles in future sessions.

### Priority Recommendations

1. **Eliminate prescriptive language** and replace with collaborative questions: "What do you think might work?" instead of "You should try..."

2. **Solicit and value Laura's ideas** before offering suggestions; treat her as expert on her own life

3. **Respect stated preferences and concerns** as important information about what will work, not obstacles to overcome

4. **Develop empathic reflections** capturing emotional content and ambivalence; use complex reflections

5. **Strategically evoke change talk** through open questions about values, reasons, past successes, and vision for change

6. **Soften sustain talk through exploration** rather than reassurance; use double-sided reflections

7. **Establish true partnership** through collaborative plan-making and shared decision-making

8. **Provide genuine affirmations** recognizing Laura's strengths, values, and self-awareness

9. **Normalize struggle** to communicate acceptance and reduce shame

10. **Engage in intensive MI training** with feedback on recorded sessions; focus on developing collaborative stance, autonomy support, and evocation skills

---

## Conclusion

The psychiatrist must fundamentally shift from expert-driven, advice-giving approach to collaborative, evocative approach. This requires eliminating prescriptive language, soliciting Laura's ideas, supporting autonomy, and strategically evoking her own motivation for change. Without this shift, the psychiatrist will continue practicing supportive advice-giving rather than Motivational Interviewing, significantly reducing likelihood of sustainable behavior change. Intensive MI training with recorded session feedback is essential for developing competent MI practice.
