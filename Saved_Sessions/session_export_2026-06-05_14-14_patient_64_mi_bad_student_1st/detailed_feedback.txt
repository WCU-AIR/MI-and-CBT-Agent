
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance or unconditional positive regard throughout this session. Instead, the clinician repeatedly employs judgmental language and labeling that directly contradicts MI spirit. Most notably, the psychiatrist explicitly accuses Jordan of "making excuses" (repeated 4+ times across the session), "not trying hard enough," "being weak," and "fooling yourself." These statements communicate disapproval and judgment rather than respect for the client's inherent worth and struggle.

The psychiatrist also dismisses Jordan's legitimate contextual concerns—peer influence, stress management, difficulty with previous quit attempts—as invalid reasons rather than exploring them with curiosity and acceptance. When Jordan expresses ambivalence and uncertainty ("I'm stuck," "I don't feel ready"), the clinician responds with pressure and criticism rather than normalizing the difficulty of behavior change. There is no affirmation of Jordan's strengths (e.g., his willingness to engage, his acknowledgment that smoking is harmful, his previous attempts to quit). The repeated refrain "You really should quit now" and "You must set one immediately" conveys an authoritarian stance that communicates conditional regard—"I will respect you only if you comply with my directive."

Most damaging: When Jordan explicitly states "Just telling me to be stronger doesn't really help," the psychiatrist responds by repeating the exact same message ("You need to be stronger and decide to quit for good"), demonstrating a complete failure to hear and respect the client's feedback about what is and isn't working.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates pervasive judgment, dismissal of client concerns, and conditional regard. There is no genuine warmth, affirmation, or normalization of struggle. This approach actively undermines the therapeutic relationship and violates core MI principles.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Adopt the MI Spirit: Partnership Over Expert Authority**
- **Action:** Fundamentally shift from "I will tell you what to do" to "Let's explore this together." Begin sessions with explicit collaboration: "I'm here to understand your perspective and work with you on this. You know your life better than anyone."
- **Example:** Instead of "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to quit," say: "I'd like to understand what smoking means to you and what's made it hard to quit before. What's your take on all this?"
- **Practice:** Record yourself and count how many times you use directive language ("you must," "you have to," "you need to") vs. collaborative language ("we," "let's explore," "what do you think"). Aim for 80%+ collaborative language.

---

### 2. **Eliminate Judgmental Language and Labeling**
- **Action:** Remove all judgmental statements ("making excuses," "being weak," "not trying hard enough," "fooling yourself"). Replace with curiosity and acceptance.
- **Example:** Instead of "Sounds like you're making excuses. Stress or friends are not valid reasons," say: "So stress and your friends smoking are real factors that make quitting hard. Tell me more about how that works for you."
- **Practice:** Before each session, write down judgmental phrases you commonly use and replace them with curious, validating alternatives. Review recordings and identify every instance of judgment; commit to eliminating them.

---

### 3. **Develop Genuine Empathy Through Reflective Listening**
- **Action:** After the client speaks, pause and reflect back what you heard—both content and emotion. Use complex reflections that capture ambivalence, values, and deeper meaning.
- **Example:** When Jordan says "It's like this pull that I can't shake off," reflect: "So part of you really wants to quit, but there's this strong pull toward smoking—especially when you're stressed or with your friends. That's a real tension you're experiencing."
- **Practice:** In each session, aim for at least 5-7 reflections before asking another question. Practice complex reflections that capture both sides of ambivalence. Record sessions and count reflections vs. questions (should be roughly equal or more reflections).

---

### 4. **Assess Stage of Change and Readiness Before Prescribing Action**
- **Action:** Before suggesting a quit date or action plan, assess where the client is: Are they in precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, or maintenance? Tailor your approach accordingly.
- **Example:** Instead of immediately demanding a quit date, first explore: "Where are you at right now with quitting? Are you thinking about it, or are you ready to make a change soon?" This tells you whether to focus on building motivation (contemplation) or planning (preparation).
- **Practice:** Learn the stages of change model thoroughly. In each session, explicitly identify the client's stage and adjust your approach. For contemplation (where Jordan is), focus on evoking motivation, not action planning.

---

### 5. **Evoke Rather Than Impose Motivation**
- **Action:** Ask open questions designed to draw out the client's own reasons, values, and motivations for change. Never tell the client why they should change; ask them.
- **Example:** Instead of "Smoking is very harmful. You're destroying your lungs and heart," ask: "What concerns you most about your smoking?" or "How does smoking fit with what matters to you in life?" or "What would be good about quitting?"
- **Practice:** Develop a repertoire of evocative questions. Before each session, write down 5-10 open questions designed to elicit change talk. Use them strategically throughout the session.

---

### 6. **Validate and Explore Barriers Rather Than Dismissing Them**
- **Action:** When the client identifies barriers (peer influence, stress, previous failed attempts), treat these as important information to explore, not as excuses to dismiss.
- **Example:** Instead of "Stress or friends are not valid reasons to keep smoking," say: "So your friends smoking and using cigarettes to manage stress are real obstacles. Let's talk about those. What would help you handle those situations differently?"
- **Practice:** When you hear a barrier, pause and ask: "Tell me more about that" or "How does that work?" rather than immediately dismissing it. Explore the barrier with genuine curiosity.

---

### 7. **Amplify and Reinforce Spontaneous Change Talk**
- **Action:** When the client spontaneously expresses change talk ("I want to quit," "I know it's bad"), recognize it, explore it, and strengthen it. Never meet change talk with pressure to act immediately.
- **Example:** When Jordan says "I want to quit, I really do," respond with: "Tell me more about that. What's making you want to quit?" or "What would be different if you did quit?" Then listen and reflect back what you hear.
- **Practice:** In each session, identify every instance of change talk and respond with an open question to explore and strengthen it. Never immediately pivot to action planning when you hear change talk; first deepen it.

---

### 8. **Use Double-Sided Reflections to Soften Sustain Talk**
- **Action:** When the client expresses sustain talk (reasons against changing), acknowledge both sides of their ambivalence rather than confronting one side.
- **Example:** Instead of confronting "My friends all smoke" with "That's not a valid reason," use a double-sided reflection: "So you want to quit, and you also recognize that your friends smoking and the social aspect of it are real obstacles. That's a tough spot to be in."
- **Practice:** Practice double-sided reflections in role-plays. When you hear sustain talk, pause and construct a reflection that validates both the desire to change and the barriers to change.

---

### 9. **Respect Autonomy and Avoid Premature Action Planning**
- **Action:** Never pressure the client to set a quit date or commit to action before they express readiness. Respect their timeline and their right to choose.
- **Example:** Instead of "You must set a quit date right now," say: "Some people find it helpful to set a quit date when they feel ready. Where are you at with that? What would need to happen for you to feel ready?"
- **Practice:** Notice your urge to push for action. When you feel it, pause and ask yourself: "Is the client ready? Have I explored their ambivalence? Have I evoked their own motivation?" If the answer is no, continue exploring rather than pushing for action.

---

### 10. **Respond to Client Feedback About What's Not Working**
- **Action:** When the client explicitly tells you something isn't working ("Just telling me to be stronger doesn't help"), listen and adjust your approach immediately. This is critical feedback.
- **Example:** When Jordan says "If you wanna help me, maybe we should talk about how I can handle the stuff that makes quitting so hard," respond with: "You're right. Let's do that. Tell me about the situations where it's hardest not to smoke. What's going on in those moments?"
- **Practice:** Treat client feedback as gold. When a client says something isn't working, stop and shift approach. This demonstrates respect and partnership.

---

### 11. **Build Collaborative Agenda-Setting**
- **Action:** At the beginning of sessions, explicitly invite the client to help set the agenda. Ask what they want to focus on, what matters to them, what they're struggling with.
- **Example:** "I want to make sure we're talking about what's most important to you. What would be most helpful to focus on today?"
- **Practice:** In each session, spend the first 5 minutes on collaborative agenda-setting. Ask open questions and listen to what the client prioritizes.

---

### 12. **Develop Self-Awareness About Your Own Biases and Urgency**
- **Action:** Recognize that your sense of urgency about the client's health ("You must quit now") is your own value, not necessarily the client's readiness. Examine where this urgency comes from and how it affects your approach.
- **Example:** Notice when you feel frustrated that the client isn't "taking it seriously" or "trying hard enough." This frustration often leads to confrontation, which backfires. Instead, pause and remind yourself: "My job is to evoke their motivation, not impose mine."
- **Practice:** After each session, reflect: "Did I impose my agenda or did I evoke theirs? Did I communicate acceptance or judgment? Would the client want to come back?" Use this reflection to adjust your approach.
