**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.2/5**

The psychiatrist employs a confrontational, directive, expert-driven approach that violates nearly every core MI principle and is **actively harmful to client motivation**.

**Critical Deficiencies:**

- Complete absence of empathy and reflective listening
- Authoritarian expert role explicitly rejecting partnership
- Coercive pressure violating autonomy support
- Failure to evoke intrinsic motivation; instead imposing external reasons
- Dismissal of legitimate barriers as "excuses"
- Ignoring and failing to amplify spontaneous change talk
- Reinforcing sustain talk through confrontation
- Failure to respond to explicit client feedback about ineffective approach

**Client Engagement Trajectory:** Patient entered session with some change talk and openness to help. By final exchanges, patient is clearly frustrated, disengaged, and defensive ("I'm tired of hearing..."). Psychiatrist's approach has triggered psychological reactance—the response to perceived threats to autonomy—which typically **strengthens resistance to change** rather than evoking it.

**Likelihood of Behavior Change:** This session is **actively undermining** Jordan's motivation and likelihood of successful smoking cessation. Research demonstrates that confrontational, directive approaches like this are associated with **worse outcomes** for behavior change, particularly for ambivalent clients. Without significant changes to clinician's approach, likelihood of patient successfully quitting smoking is substantially reduced.

**Immediate Action Required:** Psychiatrist requires intensive, immediate training in Motivational Interviewing principles and supervised practice. Current approach, despite good intentions, is evidence-based **contraindication** for supporting behavior change. Client's wellbeing depends on clinician's willingness to fundamentally shift from expert-directive to collaborative-evocative stance.
