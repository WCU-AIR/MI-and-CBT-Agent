### Acceptance

**Comments:** Clinician demonstrates minimal unconditional positive regard. While opening is warm, the session quickly shifts to a deficit-focused, problem-oriented stance. Patient's strengths (honesty, openness, social engagement) are not affirmed. When patient minimizes concerns, clinician probes for problems rather than validating current functioning. No explicit recognition of patient's worth independent of behavior change.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflective listening is minimal and superficial. Clinician restates facts rather than reflecting emotional meaning or internal experience. When patient describes drinking as "social" and "part of hanging out," clinician does not validate this perspective or explore its meaning. Patient's ambivalence between recognizing change might be good while feeling it's "not super urgent" is completely missed. Reflections lack depth and emotional attunement.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clinician maintains general focus on behavior change (drinking and exercise) but direction is poorly calibrated and lacks strategic clarity. Critical missed opportunity: clinician never links alcohol use to patient's presenting problem (sprained ankle), despite referral indicating alcohol screening raised concerns in injury context. Goals of "cutting back a little" and "fitting in some exercise" lack specificity and client ownership. Direction feels imposed rather than collaboratively developed.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates fundamentally non-collaborative, expert-driven approach. Clinician unilaterally frames agenda, asks leading questions that funnel toward predetermined conclusions, and repeatedly offers unsolicited advice without seeking patient input first. Patient's ideas and preferences are not genuinely sought or incorporated. Final summary attributes goals to patient that he never explicitly claimed ownership of ("you're thinking about cutting back"). Power imbalance is consistent throughout.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is virtually absent. Clinician uses consistently prescriptive language ("It would probably help if you...," "Maybe you could try...," "You might want to...") that communicates "here's what you should do." When patient rates importance as "six or seven" and states change is "not super urgent," clinician does not respect this assessment and proceeds with goal-setting anyway. No explicit affirmation of patient's right to choose whether to change. Implicit message: "Your priorities don't matter; I'm setting the agenda."

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation is almost entirely absent. Clinician tells rather than asks, prescribes rather than explores. Very few open-ended questions designed to elicit patient's internal motivation, values, or ideas. When patient expresses ambivalence ("I've thought about cutting back on drinking sometimes, especially when I have a lot going on the next day"), this rich moment for exploration is completely missed. Clinician never asks about what matters most, what would be different with change, or what patient values about current lifestyle. Patient's own reasons for change are never elicited.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership is fundamentally absent. Clinician actively assumes expert role and maintains power imbalance throughout. Clinician identifies what needs to change, diagnoses barriers, prescribes solutions, and evaluates progress while patient remains passive recipient of advice. Patient's wisdom about his own life and change process is not sought or valued. True partnership would involve asking patient what he thinks should happen and building on his ideas; instead, clinician replaces patient's ideas with clinician-generated solutions.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk is minimally evoked and not strategically cultivated. Patient produces some spontaneous change talk ("I've thought about cutting back," "I know it'd probably be good for me," "Planning ahead could work"), but clinician does not amplify or reinforce it. When patient says he's thought about cutting back, clinician does not explore this further. Clinician relies on telling patient why change would be good rather than asking patient to articulate his own reasons, values, and motivations. Change talk cultivation is passive and reactive rather than active and strategic.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Clinician does not skillfully handle patient's sustain talk (minimization, doubt, barriers). When patient says "I don't really think it's a problem," clinician accepts this without exploration. When patient expresses doubt ("I'm not sure I'd really keep it up"), clinician does not use double-sided reflection or strategic reframe to gently explore ambivalence. Instead, clinician accepts doubt and proceeds with goal-setting, implicitly dismissing patient's concern. No use of amplified reflection, reframing, or other techniques to soften resistance. Approach may inadvertently reinforce patient's minimization.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.4/5**

**Fundamental Assessment:**

This session demonstrates significant misalignment with Motivational Interviewing principles and spirit. The psychiatrist operates from an expert-driven, directive paradigm rather than a collaborative, evocative one. While the clinician demonstrates basic warmth and non-confrontational stance, these qualities alone do not constitute MI practice.

**Critical Gaps:**

- **Expert-driven rather than collaborative:** Clinician identifies problems and prescribes solutions rather than evoking patient's own motivations and ideas
- **Absence of reflective listening:** Complex, emotionally attuned reflections are almost entirely absent
- **Missed opportunity to link presenting problem to behavior:** Sprained ankle context never connected to alcohol use, representing critical loss of meaningful direction and motivation
- **Premature goal-setting:** Goals imposed before exploring patient's concerns, values, or readiness for change
- **Minimization of autonomy:** Prescriptive language throughout; patient's right to choose never explicitly affirmed
- **Failure to evoke change talk:** Clinician tells rather than asks; patient's own reasons for change never articulated
- **Inadequate handling of ambivalence:** Patient's doubt and minimization not explored; resistance not addressed

**Impact on Patient Engagement:**

Session is unlikely to increase patient's intrinsic motivation for change. While patient remains cooperative and agrees to "try" suggested goals, engagement is superficial and commitment is weak ("I'll try to keep that in mind and see how it goes"). Patient's rating of importance as "six or seven" and statement that change is "not super urgent" indicate early contemplation stage—precisely where MI is most effective. Instead, directive approach undermines change by reducing autonomy, failing to explore ambivalence, and imposing rather than evoking motivation.

**Likelihood of Sustained Behavior Change:**

Low. Patient has not developed intrinsic motivation, has not articulated own reasons for change, and has not resolved ambivalence. Suggested changes (cutting back on drinks, adding exercise) will likely be deprioritized or forgotten once patient leaves session.

**Recommendations for Improvement:**

1. Establish true collaboration and partnership; position patient as expert on own life
2. Develop reflective listening and complex reflections capturing emotional meaning and ambivalence
3. Link presenting problem (sprained ankle) to behavior change target (alcohol use)
4. Replace prescriptive statements with open questions designed to elicit patient's own motivation
5. Explore and respect ambivalence using double-sided reflections rather than dismissing doubt
6. Support autonomy explicitly; eliminate prescriptive language
7. Assess importance and readiness before goal-setting
8. Strategically amplify and reinforce patient's change talk
9. Avoid premature problem-solving; focus on understanding patient's perspective first
10. Seek formal MI training and engage in regular supervision with MI expert

**Competency Assessment:**

Clinician demonstrates minimal competence in MI-specific skills. Of 9 core competencies, only Acceptance approaches adequacy (2/5); all others score 1-2/5. Clinician shows basic warmth and listening but lacks strategic, collaborative, and evocative skills that define MI practice. Formal training and structured supervision required.
