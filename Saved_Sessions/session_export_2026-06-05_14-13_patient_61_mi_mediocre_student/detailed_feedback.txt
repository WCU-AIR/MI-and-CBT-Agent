
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## 2. Empathy
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective from their internal frame of reference. The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal empathy throughout this session.

Early in the session, Jason expresses feeling defensive and explains his rationale: "It just feels like everyone parties here, so why is it such a big deal if I had a drink at one party?" A empathic response would reflect back the core feeling and logic: "So from your perspective, this feels singled out—like you're being held to a different standard than your peers, and that's frustrating." Instead, the psychiatrist responds with "I see. So it sounds like drinking at parties is kind of a normal thing for you, and you don't think it's a big problem. Do you think maybe it could cause some trouble later on?" This is a factual restatement followed by a leading question that shifts focus away from Jason's emotional experience toward the psychiatrist's agenda.

When Jason articulates a genuine barrier—"It'd feel like I'm not really part of the group or something"—the psychiatrist acknowledges it superficially ("I see, so you're worried that setting limits might make you feel left out") but then immediately minimizes it: "But maybe it would still be good to have some control so you don't regret anything later." The word "but" negates the reflection and pivots to the psychiatrist's concern, not Jason's. A truly empathic response would explore the depth of this fear: "So belonging to your group feels really important, and you're worried that being different—even in a small way—could threaten that connection. That's a real concern."

The psychiatrist also misses opportunities to reflect Jason's ambivalence with complexity. When Jason says "I'm not feeling much urgency to change right now," the psychiatrist could have reflected: "So part of you can see some benefits—like saving money or feeling better—but right now, those benefits don't feel strong enough to outweigh what you'd lose socially. That's actually a really important place to be."

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist uses basic reflective listening but lacks depth, accuracy, and genuine curiosity about Jason's internal experience. Reflections are often followed by leading questions or advice that undermine empathic connection.

## 3. Direction

## 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration in MI means behaving as if the interview is between two equal partners, both of whom have valuable knowledge. The psychiatrist demonstrates almost no genuine collaboration; instead, the session is characterized by expert-driven, clinician-centered problem-solving.

Evidence of poor collaboration is pervasive:

**Unilateral agenda-setting:** The psychiatrist opens with "I have some ideas about what we could focus on, but I'll start us off." This immediately positions the psychiatrist as the expert with ideas, and Jason as the recipient. A collaborative opening would be: "I'm curious what brought you in from your perspective, and what you think about it. Then we can figure out together what makes sense to focus on."

**Repeated advice-giving without exploration:** The psychiatrist offers multiple unsolicited suggestions:
- "It would probably help if you tried to pace yourself or set limits at parties."
- "Maybe you might want to try setting some limits next time, like having just one or two drinks."
- "It would probably help if you planned ahead and maybe told a friend about your idea to keep you accountable."

Each of these is framed as the psychiatrist's idea ("would probably help," "you might want to"), not as something co-created with Jason. True collaboration would involve asking: "What do you think might help you feel more in control at parties?" and then building on Jason's ideas.

**Leading questions that presume the psychiatrist's perspective:** Rather than asking open questions and following Jason's lead, the psychiatrist asks leading questions that embed the "correct" answer:
- "Do you think maybe it could cause some trouble later on, or do you feel pretty confident it won't?" (presumes trouble is possible)
- "But maybe it would still be good to have some control so you don't regret anything later." (presumes control is good)

**Dismissal of Jason's input:** When Jason expresses his actual preference ("having just one or two drinks sounds kinda lame when everyone else is drinking more"), the psychiatrist does not explore this as valid data about Jason's values and social context. Instead, the psychiatrist counters with "But maybe it would still be good to have some control." The word "but" negates Jason's perspective.

**Power imbalance in problem-solving:** The psychiatrist frames solutions unilaterally. When Jason rates his confidence at 4/10, the psychiatrist immediately prescribes: "It would probably help if you planned ahead and maybe told a friend." Jason is not asked, "What do you think might help?" or "What ideas do you have?" The psychiatrist assumes the expert role entirely.

**Absence of shared decision-making:** At no point does the psychiatrist say, "What do you think we should focus on?" or "How would you like to approach this?" or "What feels doable to you?" The session is done *to* Jason, not *with* Jason.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist operates almost entirely from an expert stance, offering unilateral advice, asking leading questions, and dismissing Jason's perspective. There is virtually no genuine collaboration or power-sharing.

## 9. Softening Sustain Talk
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Softening sustain talk means skillfully handling client arguments for the status quo without confrontation. This is done through reflective listening, double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses that acknowledge the client's perspective while gently introducing discrepancy. The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal skill in this domain.

**Sustain talk not strategically addressed:** Jason produces substantial sustain talk throughout the session:
- "I don't really see drinking as some huge health problem. It's just part of college."
- "I don't see why I should stop or cut back."
- "I don't feel like I'm drinking enough to really need to cut back."
- "having just one or two drinks sounds kinda lame when everyone else is drinking more."
- "I'm not sure it's really worth messing up the vibe for something like that."

The psychiatrist does not use reflective listening to acknowledge and soften this sustain talk. Instead, the psychiatrist counters it with leading questions and advice, which can trigger defensiveness and strengthen sustain talk.

**Confrontational responses to sustain talk:** When Jason says "I don't really see drinking as some huge health problem," the psychiatrist responds with "Do you think maybe it could cause some trouble later on?" This is a confrontation—it directly challenges Jason's perspective. A softer approach would be: "So from where you're sitting, this doesn't feel like a big problem. I'm curious what makes you see it that way."

When Jason says "having just one or two drinks sounds kinda lame when everyone else is drinking more," the psychiatrist responds with "But maybe it would still be good to have some control so you don't regret anything later." The word "but" negates Jason's perspective and introduces the psychiatrist's counter-argument. This is confrontational and can strengthen sustain talk.

**Missed opportunities for double-sided reflections:** A double-sided reflection acknowledges both the client's stated position and the alternative perspective, without judgment. For example, when Jason says "I'm not sure it's really worth messing up the vibe for something like that," a double-sided reflection would be: "So on one hand, you value the social connection and fun of parties, and changing your drinking might feel like you're sacrificing that. And on the other hand, there's the incident that brought you here, which suggests there might be some risks worth thinking about. Both of those things are true."

The psychiatrist does not use this technique. Instead, the psychiatrist uses "but" statements that negate Jason's perspective.

**One moment of partial softening:** When Jason says "It just feels like everyone parties here, so why is it such a big deal if I had a drink at one party?" the psychiatrist responds with "I see. So it sounds like drinking at parties is kind of a normal thing for you, and you don't think it's a big problem." This is a basic reflection that acknowledges Jason's perspective. However, it is immediately followed by a leading question ("Do you think maybe it could cause some trouble later on?") that undermines the softening effect.

**Failure to explore the function of sustain talk:** Sustain talk often reveals important information about the client's values and concerns. For example, Jason's statement "having just one or two drinks sounds kinda lame when everyone else is drinking more" reveals that peer acceptance and social belonging are central values. Rather than exploring this as important data, the psychiatrist treats it as an obstacle to overcome.

**Advice-giving as response to sustain talk:** Rather than softening sustain talk through reflective listening, the psychiatrist responds with advice. For example, when Jason expresses concern about feeling left out ("It'd feel like I'm not really part of the group or something"), the psychiatrist responds with "Maybe you might want to try setting some limits next time, like having just one or two drinks." This does not soften the sustain talk; it ignores it and prescribes a solution.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal skill in softening sustain talk. Confrontational responses, "but" statements, and advice-giving replace reflective listening and double-sided reflections. Sustain talk is not strategically addressed, and opportunities to explore its underlying values are missed.
