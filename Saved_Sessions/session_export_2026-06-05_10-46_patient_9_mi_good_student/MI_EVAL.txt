# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance throughout. Affirms Amina's self-awareness ("That self-awareness is a real strength") and validates her ambivalence as legitimate rather than pathologizing hesitation. However, acceptance could be deepened by more explicitly validating her hesitation and difficulty of change before pivoting to problem-solving. When Amina expresses doubt about readiness, a fuller exploration of that hesitation before moving to capability-building would strengthen acceptance.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates strong reflective listening and accurate perspective-taking. Complex reflections capture both emotional and practical dimensions—e.g., "You're picturing a version of yourself with more energy and focus, which could boost your schoolwork, but you're also feeling hesitant because that quick, familiar escape with your phone feels like a safety net you're not ready to lose." However, empathy could deepen by more fully exploring emotional dimensions such as loneliness from living away from home and vulnerability about self-control before moving toward solutions. Clinician is more solution-focused than purely empathic at times.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains clear, appropriate focus on social media use and its impact on wellbeing and academics. Session progresses logically: exploration of ambivalence → assessment of importance → exploration of past attempts → identification of barriers → collaborative goal-setting. Opening question is open-ended and client-centered. However, direction could be sharper by exploring underlying stressors driving phone use more deeply (academic pressure, social anxiety, homesickness) and by explicitly building discrepancy between Amina's values and current behavior—a classic MI technique for building motivation.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist establishes strong collaborative stance with opening invitation: "I want to make sure our time together focuses on what feels most important to you." Uses collaborative language throughout ("What do you think?" "What do you imagine?"). Amina's ideas substantially shape session direction; when she suggests 10 p.m. cutoff, clinician affirms this rather than imposing alternatives. However, collaboration could be strengthened by reducing subtle expert positioning (e.g., "That's a thoughtful and realistic approach") and more fully co-constructing solutions rather than evaluating them.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist consistently respects Amina's autonomy and right to choose. Avoids prescriptive language; frames change as her decision. Validates her realistic self-assessment about capacity ("You're being honest about where you feel you are right now"). Does not pressure or minimize her concerns about follow-through. However, autonomy support could be enhanced by reducing subtle expert evaluation (e.g., "It's great to hear you're thinking about ways to make this manageable") and more fully trusting her self-assessment without affirming the quality of her thinking.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist asks some evocative questions ("What do you think you'd like to explore?" "What do you imagine might be different?") but misses systematic opportunities to draw out Amina's own motivations, values, and intrinsic reasons for change. When Amina mentions slipped grades and classroom fatigue, clinician reflects but doesn't explore: "What does that mean to you about your future?" or "What are your hopes for your academic performance?" Session is somewhat clinician-driven in problem identification. Limited exploration of past successes, values, or identity beyond immediate phone use issue. Clinician prompts goal-setting rather than asking more open questions like "What ideas do you have about how to approach this?"

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates genuine partnership and power-sharing. Actively fosters collaboration by inviting Amina's perspective, validating her ideas, and allowing her contributions to shape session direction. Does not assume expert role in way that diminishes Amina's expertise about her own life. Uses inclusive language ("together," "let's explore"). However, partnership could be strengthened by reducing language that positions clinician as evaluator ("That's thoughtful," "It's great to hear," "I appreciate") and more consistently positioning clinician as co-explorer rather than assessor of Amina's thinking.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist elicits some change talk spontaneously (Amina: "My grades slipped," "I'd feel less wiped out," "I'd focus better in class"), but doesn't systematically amplify or reinforce it. When Amina expresses change talk, clinician reflects rather than asking follow-up questions to deepen and elaborate. Limited strategic evocation of different types of change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment). Misses opportunities to explore: "What would you like to be different?" "What strengths could help you?" "What are your main reasons?" Session ends with Amina at 6/10 confidence—moderate but not strong. More skillful approach would strengthen commitment language and confidence through strategic evocation and reinforcement.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist validates sustain talk and appropriately avoids confrontation. However, clinician doesn't strategically soften sustain talk through double-sided reflections or exploration of barriers. When Amina expresses worry about ignoring reminders ("I worry I'd just ignore those reminders"), clinician reflects but doesn't explore: "What would make the difference between ignoring a reminder and actually pausing?" When Amina expresses doubt about readiness ("Maybe I'm just not ready yet?"), clinician validates but doesn't ask: "What would need to happen for you to feel more ready?" Approach is somewhat passive—reflecting and validating sustain talk without strategically working to shift perspective or explore what might overcome barriers.

## Overall Summary

### Strengths:
- Strong collaborative stance with genuine partnership and power-sharing
- Consistent acceptance, warmth, and unconditional positive regard
- Accurate, complex reflective listening capturing emotional and practical dimensions
- Appropriate direction and focus on social media use and consequences
- Genuine autonomy support; respects client's right to choose
- Concrete, manageable goal-setting (10 p.m. cutoff) that client generated

### Areas for Improvement:
- **Underdeveloped evocation:** Misses systematic opportunities to draw out Amina's own motivations, values, and self-generated solutions
- **Inconsistent change talk cultivation:** Doesn't strategically amplify, reinforce, or elaborate on change talk; misses opportunities to explore different types (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment)
- **Passive sustain talk handling:** Validates but doesn't strategically soften through double-sided reflections or barrier exploration
- **Subtle expert positioning:** Uses evaluative language ("That's thoughtful," "It's great to hear") that positions clinician as assessor rather than co-explorer
- **Limited exploration of underlying drivers:** Doesn't deeply explore specific stressors driving phone use or core values and identity
- **Missed discrepancy-building:** Doesn't explicitly explore gap between Amina's stated values (academic success, good health, sleep) and current behavior

---

### **Overall MI Competency Score: 3.7/5**

**Clinical Recommendation:** Psychiatrist demonstrates basic to intermediate MI competence with strong foundational skills in collaboration, acceptance, and reflective listening. To advance toward advanced competence, focus on: (1) systematic evocation of change talk through strategic questioning, (2) strategic softening of sustain talk using double-sided reflections, and (3) explicit values clarification and discrepancy-building to strengthen intrinsic motivation. While Amina leaves with a concrete goal and moderate confidence (6/10), more skillful evocation would likely have strengthened her commitment and intrinsic motivation for sustained behavior change.
