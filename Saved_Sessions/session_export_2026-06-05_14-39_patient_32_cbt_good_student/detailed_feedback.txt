
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

--- CASE STUDY ---

Itsuki Daishi was a 23-year-old Japanese male engineering student referred to his university mental health clinic by a professor concerned about irregular class attendance. Mr. Daishi reported being distracted by “listening devices” and “thought control machines” allegedly placed in his apartment. Symptoms began 3 months prior, after returning from Japan. Early signs included a classmate sneezing oddly and noticing strangers outside his apartment. He developed persecutory delusions involving television reporters, thought insertion, and grandiose beliefs about becoming prime minister of Japan. He became socially withdrawn, slept little, and avoided classes, while maintaining personal hygiene and normal eating.

He denied manic, depressive, or violent ideation. Past cannabis use was limited to one episode during a trip to Japan; he reported no substance use since. Family and guardian reported no prior psychiatric history.

On examination, Mr. Daishi was well-groomed, cooperative, and goal-directed in speech. Mood was “afraid,” affect range and mobility normal. He denied hallucinations, guilt, suicidal thoughts, or worthlessness. Cognitive functions were intact, but insight was absent regarding his delusional beliefs. Laboratory tests, CT scan, and urine drug screen were normal.

Discussion: Mr. Daishi’s persistent delusions (persecutory, referential, thought insertion, grandiose) without hallucinations or negative symptoms meet criteria for delusional disorder, mixed type. Duration of 3 months rules out brief psychotic disorder (<1 month) and schizophrenia (>6 months). Absence of mood symptoms excludes bipolar disorder, MDD with psychotic features, or schizoaffective disorder. Environmental factors, such as jet lag and prior cannabis use, were insufficient to explain persistence of symptoms.
