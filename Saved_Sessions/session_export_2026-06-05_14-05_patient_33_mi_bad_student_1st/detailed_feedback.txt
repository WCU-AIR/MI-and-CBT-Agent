
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a complete absence of unconditional positive regard and contains multiple instances of explicit judgment and disrespect toward the client. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels Derrick's behavior as "making excuses," calls him "lazy," and dismisses his emotional experience ("stop thinking about your 'feelings'"). Rather than communicating respect for Derrick's autonomy and worth, the clinician uses authoritarian language ("you have to," "you must," "mandatory") that conveys contempt for the client's perspective.

Most critically, the psychiatrist demonstrates no effort to normalize Derrick's struggle or affirm his inherent worth. When Derrick expresses legitimate concerns about isolation during his previous six-month sobriety attempt—a real barrier to sustained change—the clinician dismisses this as irrelevant ("you're not going to get anywhere if you keep worrying about losing your social life"). This response invalidates Derrick's lived experience and communicates that his concerns don't matter. The repeated, nearly identical opening questions (asked at least 5 times across the transcript) suggest the clinician is not listening to or respecting Derrick's responses, further undermining acceptance.

The tone throughout is cold, dismissive, and punitive rather than warm and supportive. There is no genuine affirmation of Derrick's strengths (e.g., his willingness to attend, his insight about needing rides instead of driving drunk, his recognition of peer pressure as a barrier).

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates zero acceptance. The session is characterized by judgment, dismissal of client concerns, and authoritarian control rather than respect for client worth and autonomy. This directly violates the foundational MI principle of unconditional positive regard.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective. This psychiatrist demonstrates neither. Derrick repeatedly articulates his core concern—managing the DUI consequences while navigating peer pressure and the isolation he experienced during sobriety—yet the clinician never reflects back understanding of this dilemma. Instead, the psychiatrist dismisses Derrick's perspective as "excuses" and "resistance."

When Derrick says, "I went six months without drinking… I was doing really good, but I felt isolated… My girlfriend and I argued a lot, it didn't work out," this is critical change talk revealing his ambivalence and the real barriers to sustained abstinence. A clinician demonstrating empathy would reflect: *"So you managed to stay sober for half a year, which shows real commitment, but the loneliness and relationship strain made it really hard to keep going. That's a genuine struggle, not an excuse."* Instead, the psychiatrist responds with: "You're not going to get anywhere if you keep worrying about losing your social life or your girlfriend."

There are zero complex reflections in this session. There are zero attempts to capture emotional nuance. The psychiatrist never explores what it feels like to be caught between legal consequences and social belonging, or what isolation felt like during the sobriety attempt. The repeated, scripted opening questions show no curiosity about Derrick's actual experience.

Derrick explicitly states his ambivalence and willingness to explore smaller steps ("Maybe if we focus on one thing at a time, I'd have a better shot"), yet the clinician ignores this and demands all-or-nothing commitment. This is the opposite of empathic understanding.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no empathy. There are no reflections, no exploration of client perspective, and active dismissal of Derrick's stated concerns and emotional experience. This is a fundamental failure in MI practice.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI means maintaining strategic focus on the target behavior while remaining client-centered. This session has a clear target (reducing alcohol use, preventing DUI recurrence), but the execution is severely flawed.

The psychiatrist does maintain some focus on health behaviors and the DUI issue, which prevents a score of 1. However, the direction is imposed rather than collaborative. The clinician repeatedly pivots away from Derrick's stated priority (managing the DUI and peer pressure) to demand focus on smoking, exercise, and diet—issues Derrick has not identified as problems and that are tangential to his legal crisis.

More problematically, the psychiatrist's approach to direction is rigid and non-strategic. Rather than progressively clarifying Derrick's own goals and building toward change, the clinician uses repetitive, scripted questions that suggest the session is following a predetermined checklist rather than responding to the client's actual needs. The same opening is asked at least 5 times with minimal variation, indicating the psychiatrist is not adjusting direction based on Derrick's responses.

A strategically directed MI session would focus on: *"What would it take to avoid another DUI? What happens when your friends pressure you to drink? What worked during those six months sober, and what made it hard?"* Instead, the psychiatrist demands commitment to support groups and counseling without exploring Derrick's readiness, barriers, or what he actually needs.

**Summary:**

The session has a stated focus but lacks strategic direction. The psychiatrist imposes direction rather than evoking it, repeats the same questions mechanically, and ignores the client's stated priorities in favor of a predetermined agenda.

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners. This session is the antithesis of collaboration—it is entirely clinician-driven and authoritarian.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Adopt the MI Spirit: Partnership Over Authority**
- **Action:** Fundamentally shift from an expert/authority stance to a collaborative partnership. Begin sessions by asking what the client wants to talk about rather than imposing an agenda.
- **Example:** Instead of "Are you smoking cigarettes? Yes or no?" open with: "Thanks for coming in today. I know you're dealing with a lot right now. What brought you in, and what's most important for us to focus on?"
- **Practice:** Before each session, remind yourself: "My job is to help this person explore their own motivations, not to convince them to do what I think they should do." Record yourself and listen for commanding language ("you have to," "you must"). Replace with collaborative language ("What do you think...?" "How do you see...?" "What would work for you?").

---

### 2. **Listen to and Amplify Change Talk**
- **Action:** When the client offers change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment), pause and reflect it back with genuine interest. Ask follow-up questions to deepen and strengthen it.
- **Example:** When Derrick says, "If I need to get my license back, it would be pretty important to stop hanging around with them for a while," respond with: "Getting your license back is really important to you. Tell me more about what that would mean for your life—your work, your independence, your future."
- **Practice:** In supervision or role-play, practice identifying change talk in client statements. For each instance, practice a reflective response that amplifies it. Use the formula: Reflection + Follow-up Question to Deepen.

---

### 3. **Validate and Explore Ambivalence Rather Than Dismiss It**
- **Action:** When clients express concerns about change (e.g., "I felt isolated when I was sober"), treat these as legitimate barriers to explore, not excuses to dismiss. Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge both sides of their ambivalence.
- **Example:** When Derrick says, "I went six months without drinking… I was doing really good, but I felt isolated," respond with: "So you managed to stay sober for half a year, which shows real strength, and at the same time, the loneliness was really hard. Both of those things are real. What do you think about that?"
- **Practice:** In role-play, practice responding to client concerns with curiosity rather than dismissal. Ask: "Tell me more about that" or "What was that like?" rather than "That's just an excuse."

---

### 4. **Ask Open Questions to Evoke Client Ideas and Motivation**
- **Action:** Replace closed yes/no questions with open questions that invite the client to explore their own thinking. Ask about their values, priorities, ideas, and what they think might work.
- **Example:** Instead of "Do you exercise at least three times a week? Yes or no?" ask: "What does health mean to you right now? What would be realistic for you to work on first?"
- **Practice:** Write out 10 open questions related to the client's presenting concern before each session. Practice asking these in a curious, non-judgmental tone. Record yourself and listen for genuine curiosity vs. disguised directives.

---

### 5. **Respect Client Autonomy and Readiness for Change**
- **Action:** Acknowledge that the client has the right to choose, and that change happens on their timeline, not yours. When a client suggests a smaller step, explore it rather than reject it.
- **Example:** When Derrick says, "Maybe if we focus on one thing at a time, I'd have a better shot," respond with: "That makes sense. What do you think would be the most important thing to focus on first? What would give you the best chance of success?"
- **Practice:** In role-play, practice responding to client hesitation with curiosity rather than pressure. When a client says "I'm not ready," ask: "What would need to happen for you to feel ready?" rather than "You have to do this now."

---

### 6. **Use Reflective Listening to Demonstrate Empathy and Understanding**
- **Action:** Regularly reflect back what you hear the client saying, especially their emotions and the meaning behind their words. Use complex reflections that go beyond surface content to capture emotional nuance.
- **Example:** When Derrick talks about his DUI and peer pressure, reflect: "So you're caught between wanting to stay connected to your friends—people who've been important to you since high school—and avoiding another legal disaster. That's a real tension, and it makes sense that you're struggling with it."
- **Practice:** In supervision, practice writing out reflections for client statements. Aim for at least one complex reflection per client turn. Practice delivering reflections with genuine warmth and understanding rather than clinical detachment.

---

### 7. **Focus on the Client's Stated Priorities, Not Your Agenda**
- **Action:** When the client identifies a priority (e.g., "My main thing right now is dealing with that whole DUI mess"), honor that priority rather than insisting on your own agenda. You can address other issues later if appropriate.
- **Example:** Derrick says the DUI is his main concern. Respond with: "Okay, let's focus on that. Tell me what's been most challenging about the DUI situation, and what you think might help you avoid that happening again."
- **Practice:** In each session, explicitly ask: "What's most important for us to focus on today?" and then follow the client's lead. If you have other concerns, ask permission: "I also want to check in about [topic]. Is that okay, or should we stick with what you mentioned?"

---

### 8. **Avoid Confrontation and Argument; Use Strategic Responses to Sustain Talk**
- **Action:** When the client expresses sustain talk (reasons not to change), resist the urge to argue or confront. Instead, use techniques like double-sided reflections, reframing, or strategic agreement to soften resistance.
- **Example:** When Derrick says, "I don't really think I have a problem with drinking, it was an isolated incident," respond with: "You see this as a one-time thing, and at the same time, you've had two DUIs now. I'm curious—what's your take on that?"
- **Practice:** In role-play, practice responding to sustain talk without arguing. Use reflections that acknowledge the client's perspective while gently introducing alternative viewpoints. Never say "You're wrong" or "That's not true."

---

### 9. **Develop Genuine Curiosity About the Client's Experience**
- **Action:** Approach each session with authentic curiosity about the client's life, barriers, values, and ideas. Ask questions you genuinely want to know the answer to, not questions designed to lead the client to your conclusion.
- **Example:** "I'm really curious—when you think about your friends and the pressure to drink, what goes through your head? What makes it hard to say no?"
- **Practice:** Before sessions, write down 3-5 genuine questions you want to explore with the client. During the session, listen carefully to the answers and ask follow-up questions based on what you hear, not on your predetermined script.
