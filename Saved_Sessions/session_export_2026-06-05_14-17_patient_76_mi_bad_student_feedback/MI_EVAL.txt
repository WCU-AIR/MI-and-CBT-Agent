### Acceptance

**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no unconditional positive regard or nonjudgmental stance. Opening statement establishes authoritarian framework ("I'll be giving you some advice on what you need to do to improve"). Uses accusatory language when patient discloses modest wine consumption ("Why didn't you think about this before?" and "Are you sure you're not drinking more than you realize?"). Employs coercive language repeatedly ("It's non-negotiable," "You have to make that commitment"). Dismisses patient's legitimate concerns about feeling overwhelmed, prioritizing clinician's directive over patient's experience. No affirmation of patient's strengths (e.g., proactive avoidance of smoking, willingness to engage). Fails to normalize difficulty of behavior change or acknowledge that ambivalence is natural. Tone throughout is critical and pressuring rather than warm and supportive.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Systematically undermines patient autonomy throughout session. Language is explicitly controlling ("You really need to cut down," "You have to take your asthma seriously," "You must start now," "It's non-negotiable if you want to feel better," "You have to make that commitment; otherwise, your health will only decline"). These statements eliminate perceived choice and communicate that clinician has determined what patient must do and compliance is mandatory. When patient expresses hesitation ("stopping wine completely this week feels really overwhelming"), clinician does not honor autonomy by exploring what feels manageable. Instead, response is pressure ("Why haven't you committed to this already?"). Uses fear-based persuasion to pressure compliance ("Have you thought about what will happen if your asthma gets worse or you end up in the hospital?"). When patient articulates own preference ("Maybe there's a middle ground"), clinician has already stated requirement is "non-negotiable." Only final question ("what do you think would be a manageable first step for you?") represents shift toward autonomy support, but comes after extensive autonomy-undermining interaction.

### Evocation

When patient mentions concern about inhaler side effects ("I just... sometimes I wonder if all these medications are actually helping or if they're causing other problems"), clinician does not explore this at all. When patient discloses wine helps her relax, clinician does not evoke understanding of coping mechanism or explore alternatives. Near end, asks open-ended evocative questions ("Can you tell me more about what makes managing your asthma difficult?") and patient responds with rich material. However, this represents dramatic shift in approach after extensive non-evocative interaction and appears only after directive approach has failed to produce compliance.

When patient says "I get that too much alcohol isn't great," clinician does not build on this but pivots to pressure and confrontation. When patient expresses fear about hospitalization, clinician uses this as fear-based persuasion tool rather than opportunity to evoke and strengthen intrinsic motivation. ## Overall Session Assessment

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.4/5**

**Summary:**

This session demonstrates poor motivational interviewing practice overall. The psychiatrist establishes and maintains an authoritarian, expert-driven stance throughout the majority of the session, using controlling language, pressure, and fear-based persuasion rather than genuine partnership and evocation. While basic counseling skills are present (clinician maintains engagement and does not terminate prematurely), these do not constitute MI practice.

**Key Strengths:**
- Late-session shift toward MI-consistent practice with open-ended questions and balanced reflections
- Maintains session structure and engagement
- Acknowledges complexity of patient's situation near end

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Authoritarian stance with controlling language throughout majority of session
- Systematic dismissal of patient perspective and concerns
- Failure to evoke patient motivation; reliance on advice-giving and directives
- Fear-based persuasion used to pressure compliance
- Clinician-imposed agenda rather than collaborative exploration
- Confrontational handling of sustain talk
- Failure to address patient's stated core concern (inhaler side effects)

**Impact on Patient Engagement:**

The extensive non-MI interaction has likely damaged the therapeutic alliance and reduced likelihood that patient will follow through on stated intentions. While patient demonstrates resilience by articulating own change talk and identifying manageable first steps by session end, this represents patient's own motivation despite clinician's approach, not because of it.

**Recommendation:**

This clinician requires intensive MI training with supervised practice and feedback. Deficiencies are not minor technical gaps but fundamental misunderstandings of MI spirit and principles. Recommend formal MI training program, regular supervision with MI-trained supervisor, audio/video review with feedback, and role-play practice.
