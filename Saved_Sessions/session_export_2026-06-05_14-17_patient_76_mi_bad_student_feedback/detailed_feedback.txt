
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no unconditional positive regard or nonjudgmental stance throughout this session. From the opening statement—"I'll be giving you some advice on what you need to do to improve"—the clinician establishes an authoritarian, judgmental framework that directly contradicts MI spirit. When Linda discloses modest wine consumption (2 glasses weekly), the psychiatrist responds with accusatory language: "Why didn't you think about this before?" and "Are you sure you're not drinking more than you realize? You need to be honest about this." These statements imply dishonesty and poor judgment, communicating disapproval rather than acceptance.

Most critically, the psychiatrist uses coercive language repeatedly: "You really need to cut down," "It's non-negotiable if you want to feel better," "You have to make that commitment," and "Why haven't you committed to this already?" This language conveys conditional regard—acceptance contingent on compliance with the clinician's agenda. When Linda expresses legitimate concerns about feeling overwhelmed and scared about losing coping mechanisms, the psychiatrist dismisses these feelings with "I understand you feel it's difficult, but you really can't afford to keep drinking like this"—a classic invalidation that prioritizes the clinician's directive over the client's experience.

There is no affirmation of Linda's strengths (e.g., her proactive avoidance of smoking due to asthma awareness, her willingness to engage in the session, her honesty about her struggles). The psychiatrist never normalizes the difficulty of behavior change or acknowledges that ambivalence is a natural, healthy part of the contemplation stage. The tone throughout is critical and pressuring rather than warm and supportive.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates poor acceptance, using judgmental language, conditional regard, coercion, and dismissal of the client's concerns. No genuine warmth, affirmation, or unconditional positive regard is evident.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective from their internal frame of reference. This psychiatrist demonstrates minimal empathy throughout the session. When Linda articulates her core concern—"managing the asthma itself is hard enough"—the clinician does not reflect this or explore what makes it difficult. Instead, the psychiatrist pivots immediately to directive advice about alcohol, missing the opportunity to understand Linda's experience.

Most tellingly, when Linda expresses fear ("I'm sort of scared about all the things I have to give up"), the psychiatrist does not reflect or validate this emotion. Instead, the response is "I understand you feel it's difficult, but you really can't afford to keep drinking like this"—a pseudo-empathic statement ("I understand") immediately followed by contradiction and pressure. This is not empathy; it is performative acknowledgment followed by dismissal.

Linda explicitly states: "It feels like another thing added to the list of stuff I have to monitor and control" and "I'm kind of stuck—wanting to take care of myself but also really worried about losing those little things that help me cope." These are rich emotional statements revealing ambivalence, overwhelm, and fear. A skilled MI clinician would reflect these complex feelings with a complex reflection: "So you're caught between wanting to protect your health and feeling like you're already managing so much, and giving up wine feels like losing one of the few things that helps you breathe easier emotionally." The psychiatrist offers no such reflection.

The only moment approaching empathy occurs near the end when the psychiatrist says, "It sounds like managing your asthma involves a lot of challenges, especially with triggers and medications. Your point about the wine helping you relax is important, and I can see why you feel conflicted about it." However, this single adequate reflection cannot compensate for the pervasive lack of empathic engagement throughout the majority of the session.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates poor empathy, with minimal reflective listening, failure to explore Linda's perspective, and dismissal of her emotional experience. One adequate reflection near the end does not offset systematic empathic failure.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI involves maintaining strategic focus on the client's change goals while remaining client-centered. This psychiatrist maintains focus on a target behavior (alcohol reduction and asthma management), but does so in a way that is clinician-driven rather than collaboratively established.

When Linda mentions her core concern—worry about inhaler side effects and medication reliance—the psychiatrist does not pursue this as a potential change target. Linda responds with her own priorities: understanding asthma triggers and gradual alcohol reduction. **Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains focus on target behaviors but imposes direction clinician-driven rather than collaboratively. Strategic depth is lacking, and the clinician fails to explore the client's own goals and priorities until late in the session.

### 5. Autonomy Support
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Autonomy support requires the clinician to respect and actively foster the client's perception of choice, avoiding pressure and emphasizing client ownership. This psychiatrist systematically undermines autonomy throughout the session.

The language is explicitly controlling: "You really need to cut down," "You have to take your asthma seriously," "You must start now," "It's non-negotiable if you want to feel better," "Do you think you can stop drinking wine completely starting this week? When Linda expresses hesitation—"stopping wine completely this week feels really overwhelming"—the psychiatrist does not honor her autonomy by exploring what feels manageable. ## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish Genuine Partnership and Collaborative Agenda-Setting (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Begin every session by explicitly inviting the client to set the agenda. Use language that conveys genuine partnership: "What brings you in today?" "What would be most helpful to focus on?" "What are your priorities for this conversation?" Avoid opening statements that position you as the expert with answers.

**Example:** 
- Instead of: "I'll be giving you some advice on what you need to do to improve."
- Use: "I'd like to understand what's been most challenging for you with your asthma. What would be most helpful to talk about today?"

**Practice:** Before your next session, write out your opening statement. Ensure it invites the client's input and frames the session as collaborative exploration, not clinician-directed advice-giving. Practice this opening with a colleague or supervisor until it feels natural.

---

### 2. **Develop Genuine Empathic Listening and Complex Reflections**

**Action:** When a client expresses concern or ambivalence, pause and reflect back what you hear—especially the emotional content and the complexity. Use reflections that capture both sides of ambivalence, not pseudo-empathic statements followed by contradiction.

**Example:**
- Client: "I'm scared about all the things I have to give up."
- Instead of: "I understand you feel it's difficult, but you really can't afford to keep drinking like this."
- Use: "So you're caught between wanting to protect your health and feeling like you're already managing so much, and giving up wine feels like losing one of the few things that helps you cope with stress. That's a real tension."

**Practice:** Record yourself in role-play sessions. After each client statement expressing emotion or ambivalence, pause the recording and write a complex reflection that captures the emotional nuance and both sides of the ambivalence. Practice delivering these reflections with genuine warmth and without immediately following with directives.

---

### 3. **Eliminate Controlling Language and Replace with Autonomy-Supportive Language**

**Action:** Audit your language for controlling words: "must," "have to," "need to," "should," "non-negotiable." Replace these with autonomy-supportive language that emphasizes choice and client ownership: "What do you think about...?" "It's up to you," "What feels manageable to you?" "What would work for you?"

**Example:**
- Instead of: "You must start now. It's non-negotiable if you want to feel better."
- Use: "I'm wondering what you think about trying to reduce your wine intake. What feels like a manageable change for you?"

**Practice:** In your next three sessions, count how many times you use controlling language. Set a goal to reduce this by 50% in the following three sessions. Have a supervisor listen to recordings and provide feedback on your language patterns.

---

### 4. **Explore and Validate Client Concerns Rather Than Dismissing Them**

**Action:** When a client expresses doubt or concern about your recommendation, treat this as valuable clinical information, not as resistance to be overcome. Ask open-ended questions to understand their reasoning: "Tell me more about that," "What concerns you about...?" "How do you see that playing out?"

**Example:**
- Client: "I don't think my drinking is really causing problems. I haven't noticed any worse symptoms."
- Instead of: "Look, you really need to cut down on the wine. Even a couple of glasses a week can worsen your asthma."
- Use: "That's interesting. You haven't noticed a connection between the wine and your asthma symptoms. Tell me more about what you have noticed. What does your asthma feel like on days when you drink versus days when you don't?"

**Practice:** In your next session, when a client expresses doubt about your recommendation, pause and ask at least two open-ended questions to understand their perspective before offering any information or advice. Document what you learn from their responses.

---

### 5. **Evoke Client Motivation Rather Than Imposing It**

**Action:** Ask questions designed to draw out the client's own reasons for change, their values, and their resources. Use open-ended questions that invite reflection: "What concerns you most about...?" "What would be different if...?" "What matters to you about your health?" "What have you tried before that's worked?"

**Example:**
- Instead of: "You need to cut down on the wine. Alcohol can mess with your asthma and overall health."
- Use: "You mentioned that managing your asthma is really difficult. I'm curious—what do you think might make it feel more manageable? What have you tried that's helped, even a little?"

**Practice:** Develop a list of evocative questions specific to asthma management and alcohol use. Practice asking these questions in role-play until they feel natural. Focus on genuine curiosity about the client's perspective rather than leading questions designed to elicit agreement with your agenda.

---

### 6. **Address the Client's Stated Concerns, Not Just Your Clinical Priorities**

**Action:** When a client mentions a concern (e.g., "I worry about the side effects of inhalers"), explore this thoroughly before pivoting to other topics. Treat the client's stated concerns as the primary agenda unless they explicitly agree to shift focus.

**Example:**
- Client: "I just... sometimes I wonder if all these medications are actually helping or if they're causing other problems."
- Response: "That's an important concern. Tell me more about what worries you about the inhalers. What specifically are you concerned about? Have you noticed any side effects, or is it more about what you've read or heard?"

**Practice:** In your next session, identify the client's stated concerns in the first few minutes. Write these down. Ensure you address each one before introducing new topics. If you need to shift focus, ask permission: "I hear your concern about the inhalers. That's important. I also want to understand your drinking a bit better. Is that okay to talk about?"

---

### 7. **Use Strategic Questioning to Evoke Change Talk Rather Than Confronting Sustain Talk**

**Action:** When a client expresses sustain talk (reasons not to change), do not confront directly. Instead, use strategic questions to evoke change talk: "What concerns you about...?" "What would be different if...?" "How important is your health to you?" "What would help you feel more confident about making this change?"

**Example:**
- Client: "Stopping wine completely this week feels really overwhelming."
- Instead of: "Why haven't you committed to this already?"
- Use: "I hear that stopping completely feels like too much right now. What would feel more manageable to you? What's a change you think you could actually stick with?"

**Practice:** Develop a list of strategic questions designed to evoke change talk in response to common sustain talk statements. Practice these in role-play until they feel natural and genuine.

---

### 8. **Recognize and Affirm Client Strengths and Positive Behaviors**

**Action:** Throughout the session, identify and affirm the client's strengths, positive behaviors, and progress. This builds self-efficacy and supports autonomy.

**Example:**
- "I notice you've never smoked, even though many people with asthma do. That shows real commitment to your health."
- "You're here today, willing to talk about this. That takes courage, especially when you're feeling uncertain."
- "You've thought carefully about what feels manageable for you. That's important self-awareness."

**Practice:** In your next session, identify at least three client strengths or positive behaviors and affirm them explicitly. Notice how this affects the client's engagement and openness.

---

### 9. **Implement Consistent MI Practice Throughout the Session, Not Just at the End**

**Action:** The shift toward MI-consistent practice near the end of this session suggests the psychiatrist recognized the ineffectiveness of the directive approach. However, MI must be consistent throughout. Commit to MI principles from the opening statement through the closing.

**Example:**
- Opening: "I'd like to understand what's been most challenging for you with your asthma and what you'd like to focus on today."
- Throughout: Use open-ended questions, reflections, affirmations, and summaries consistently.
- Closing: "So it sounds like you're thinking about understanding your triggers better and maybe trying to cut back on wine gradually. What feels like the first step for you?"

**Practice:** Review recordings of your sessions and identify where you shift between MI-consistent and non-MI practice. Set a goal to maintain consistency throughout. Have a supervisor provide feedback on your consistency.

---

### 10. **Develop Skill in Handling Ambivalence Without Pressure**

**Action:** Ambivalence is normal and healthy in the contemplation stage. Rather than pressuring the client to resolve ambivalence in your direction, help them explore both sides and develop their own resolution. Use double-sided reflections and normalize ambivalence.

**Example:**
- "So part of you recognizes that alcohol might be affecting your asthma, and part of you isn't convinced it's a big enough problem to give up something that helps you relax. That's a real tension, and it makes sense that you're stuck."
- "It's common to feel this way—wanting to take care of yourself but also not wanting to lose the things that help you cope. What do you think might help you move forward?"

**Practice:** In your next session, when you notice client ambivalence, pause and offer a double-sided reflection. Resist the urge to pressure resolution. Instead, ask open-ended questions that invite the client to explore their own thinking.
