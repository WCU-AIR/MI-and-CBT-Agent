
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 2. Feedback

## Recommendations

### 1. **URGENT: Assess and address medication status**
- **Action:** At the next session, explicitly assess whether the patient is on antipsychotic medication. If not, discuss the role of medication in treating schizophreniform disorder and coordinate with prescribing psychiatrist.
- **Example:** "I want to make sure we're addressing your symptoms comprehensively. Are you currently taking any medications for the voices or headaches? If not, I'd like to discuss this with [prescribing doctor] because medication is often an important part of treatment for what you're experiencing."
- **Practice:** Review the pharmacological treatment of schizophreniform disorder. Understand first-generation vs. second-generation antipsychotics, common side effects, and how to support medication compliance in CBT.

### 2. **Conduct comprehensive safety assessment**
- **Action:** At the next session, systematically assess for suicidal ideation, homicidal ideation, command hallucinations, and risk of acting on delusional beliefs. Develop a safety plan if risk is identified.
- **Example:** "I want to check in about safety. Have you ever had thoughts of hurting yourself? Have the voices ever told you to do something harmful? Do you ever feel like people are trying to hurt you?"
- **Practice:** Learn suicide risk assessment protocols. Develop a template for safety planning that includes crisis contacts, coping strategies, and hospitalization criteria.

### 3. **Implement reality testing for hallucinations**
- **Action:** Use guided discovery to help the patient develop alternative explanations for hallucinations and test their accuracy. This is a core CBT technique for psychosis.
- **Example:** "You mentioned the voices tell you that you're a failure. Have you ever checked whether what they're saying is actually true? For instance, when they say you've let people down, is that always accurate? Can you think of times when you've actually helped someone?"
- **Practice:** Study CBT for psychosis protocols (e.g., Morrison, Renton). Practice reality testing questions that help patients distinguish between voice content and objective reality.

### 4. **Teach coping strategies for managing voices**
- **Action:** Introduce specific techniques for managing hallucinations when they occur, such as grounding, distraction, or talking back to voices.
- **Example:** "When the voices start, we can try some techniques to make them quieter or less bothersome. One is the 5-senses technique—notice 5 things you can see, 4 you can touch, 3 you can hear, 2 you can smell, 1 you can taste. This helps ground you in the present moment. Would you like to try that?"
- **Practice:** Learn and practice grounding techniques, distraction strategies, and voice management skills. Role-play with colleagues.

### 5. **Provide psychoeducation about schizophreniform disorder**
- **Action:** Educate the patient about the nature of his condition, its course, prognosis, and the stress-vulnerability model. This reduces shame and helps the patient understand symptom triggers.
- **Example:** "What you're experiencing—the voices and the guilt feelings—is a condition called schizophreniform disorder. It's a real medical condition, not a character flaw. It happens when the brain's stress response system gets overactive. The good news is that with treatment—medication, therapy, and stress management—most people recover well."
- **Practice:** Develop a psychoeducational handout about schizophreniform disorder. Practice explaining the stress-vulnerability model in simple terms.

### 6. **Assess and address negative symptoms with behavioral activation**
- **Action:** Explicitly assess negative symptoms (anhedonia, avolition, social withdrawal, blunted affect) and implement behavioral activation to address them.
- **Example:** "I've noticed you've been withdrawing from friends and family. That's a common symptom, but it can make things worse. Let's work together to gradually increase activities you used to enjoy or find meaningful. What's one small activity you could do this week?"
- **Practice:** Learn behavioral activation protocols. Create activity schedules with patients. Understand how to pace activities appropriately for someone with negative symptoms.

### 7. **Explore and address ideas of reference**
- **Action:** Gently assess whether the patient believes strangers are talking about him or watching him. Use reality testing to help him evaluate these beliefs.
- **Example:** "You mentioned that strangers have commented on your past behaviors. When that happens, how do you know they're talking about you specifically? Could there be other explanations?"
- **Practice:** Study how to assess and address ideas of reference in CBT for psychosis. Practice gentle reality testing that doesn't invalidate the patient's experience.

### 8. **Develop substance use relapse prevention plan**
- **Action:** Explicitly discuss the patient's history of heavy alcohol use, recent sobriety, and strategies for maintaining abstinence. Identify triggers and coping strategies.
- **Example:** "You've done great work getting sober. That's important because alcohol can make the voices and headaches worse. What situations make you want to drink? What could you do instead?"
- **Practice:** Learn relapse prevention techniques. Develop a written relapse prevention plan with the patient.

### 9. **Assess and address sleep disturbance**
- **Action:** Explore the patient's sleep patterns, nightmares, and their relationship to symptom severity. Implement sleep hygiene strategies.
- **Example:** "Tell me about your sleep. How many hours are you getting? Do you have nightmares? Sleep problems can make the voices worse, so let's work on improving your sleep."
- **Practice:** Learn sleep hygiene interventions. Understand how to address nightmares in trauma-informed ways.

### 10. **Explore the role of religious preoccupation in symptom maintenance**
- **Action:** Gently explore whether the patient's recent intense religiosity is a coping mechanism for psychotic symptoms or a symptom itself. Discuss how religious beliefs interact with hallucinations.
- **Example:** "I noticed you recently joined an evangelical church. That's wonderful if it's meaningful to you. I'm curious—do you think your spiritual beliefs help you understand the voices, or do the voices sometimes conflict with your faith?"
- **Practice:** Develop cultural competence in working with religious patients. Understand how to distinguish between healthy spirituality and religious preoccupation as a symptom.

### 11. **Establish formal homework review process**
- **Action:** At the beginning of each session, systematically review the previous week's homework. Troubleshoot barriers, celebrate successes, and adjust tasks as needed.
- **Example:** "Before we start today, let's review your homework from last week. You were going to talk to your girlfriend and keep a journal. How did that go? What was easy? What was hard?"
- **Practice:** Develop a homework review template. Practice troubleshooting barriers collaboratively.

### 12. **Provide written materials and session summaries**
- **Action:** Give the patient written summaries of each session, including the balanced thought, homework assignments, and key takeaways. This supports memory and compliance.
- **Example:** Provide a one-page summary: "Today we talked about guilt from your past. We developed this balanced thought: 'I made mistakes before, but I'm trying to change and do better now.' Your homework is..."
- **Practice:** Develop session summary templates. Practice writing clear, jargon-free summaries.
