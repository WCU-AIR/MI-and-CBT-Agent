
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made no attempt to establish a collaborative agenda at any point during the session. A proper CBT agenda would have involved: (1) explicitly identifying the session's target problems, (2) prioritizing which issues to address given time constraints, (3) establishing measurable goals for the session, and (4) obtaining patient agreement on the focus. Instead, the therapist opened with casual small talk ("How's your day been so far?") and never transitioned into structured agenda-setting.

When the patient spontaneously introduced critical material—traffic frustration, anger building, difficulty controlling outbursts, sleep disruption, fear of losing his family—the therapist failed to harness this into a coherent session plan. The patient explicitly stated "I want to figure this out" and later "I want to find ways to handle the anger before it gets to that breaking point," yet the therapist never collaboratively prioritized these concerns or established what would be accomplished by session's end.

The session drifted aimlessly through generic advice about positive thinking, water intake, sleep, fresh air, and music—none of which were connected to a coherent treatment strategy for Intermittent Explosive Disorder. By the final exchange, the therapist simply ended the session with encouragement ("You've got this!") without reviewing what was discussed, what the patient learned, or what specific work would occur before the next session.

**Summary:**

No agenda was established. The session lacked structure, prioritization, or explicit goals. This is a fundamental CBT failure that undermines all subsequent interventions.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist elicited minimal feedback and failed to check understanding throughout the session. True feedback elicitation in CBT involves: (1) regularly asking "Does this make sense?", (2) checking whether the patient understands the therapist's conceptualization, (3) soliciting reactions to suggestions, (4) asking what the patient found helpful, and (5) adjusting based on patient responses.

The therapist asked only one genuine feedback question: "Have you been sleeping okay lately?" This was a factual inquiry, not feedback elicitation. The therapist never asked: "Does this approach seem helpful to you?", "Are these suggestions realistic for your situation?", "What do you think about what we've discussed?", or "How are you feeling about our work together?"

More critically, the patient provided explicit feedback that the therapist's suggestions were inadequate, yet the therapist ignored it. When the patient said, "I try to tell myself that, but honestly, sometimes it feels impossible. At session's end, the therapist did not ask: "What was most helpful today?", "What will you take away from this session?", or "How confident are you about trying these strategies?" This represents a complete failure to elicit or respond to patient feedback.

**Summary:**

Minimal feedback elicitation; therapist ignored patient's explicit feedback that interventions were inadequate. No adjustment in approach based on patient responses.

### 3. Understanding
