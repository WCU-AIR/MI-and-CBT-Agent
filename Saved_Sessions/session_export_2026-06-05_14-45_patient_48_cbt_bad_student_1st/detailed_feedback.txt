
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish a Structured CBT Framework with Clear Agenda and Goals**
- **Action:** At the beginning of each session, collaboratively establish a specific agenda with 2-3 target problems prioritized by the patient. Allocate time for each item and follow the agenda.
- **Example:** "Oscar, I'd like to structure our time today. I'm hearing that health anxiety, sleep problems, and avoidance of going outside are all affecting you. Which of these would be most helpful to focus on today? Let's plan to spend about 15 minutes on that, then check in about what we learned."
- **Practice:** Role-play agenda-setting with a supervisor. Practice phrases like "What would be most helpful to work on today?" and "How much time should we spend on this?" Practice following the agenda and redirecting when conversation drifts.

### 2. **Develop a Shared Case Conceptualization Based on the Cognitive-Behavioral Model**
- **Action:** Explicitly teach Oscar how his illness anxiety works using the cognitive-behavioral model. Identify the cycle: triggering event (fatigue, small ache) → automatic thought ("I have Lyme disease") → anxiety/physical sensations → safety behavior (reassurance-seeking, avoidance, body monitoring) → temporary relief → reinforcement of the cycle.
- **Example:** "Oscar, I want to help you understand how your health anxiety works. When you wake up tired, you notice that and think 'My Lyme is coming back.' That thought creates anxiety, which causes physical sensations like tension or fatigue. Then you might read about Lyme symptoms or ask your wife for reassurance, which temporarily calms you down. But then the cycle starts again. Does that match your experience?"
- **Practice:** Draw the cycle on paper with the patient. Have the patient identify his specific thoughts, behaviors, and physical sensations. Use this as the foundation for all subsequent interventions.

### 3. **Eliminate Excessive Self-Disclosure and Maintain Professional Boundaries**
- **Action:** Stop sharing personal anecdotes about your own stress, health concerns, or coping strategies. When tempted to share a personal story, pause and ask yourself: "Does this serve the patient's treatment goals, or does it shift focus to me?" If the latter, don't share it.
- **Example:** Instead of "I totally get that feeling sometimes too. You know, I once had this weird rash and I was convinced it was something terrible," say: "I hear you. Let's focus on understanding what's happening when you notice those sensations. What goes through your mind?"

### 4. **Use Guided Discovery Instead of Reassurance and Advice**
- **Action:** Replace reassurance ("Everyone worries about health stuff") and direct advice ("Just think positive") with Socratic questions that help Oscar examine his own thinking and reach insights independently.
- **Example:**

- **Practice:** Create a list of Oscar's safety behaviors. For each one, explain the mechanism (temporary relief → reinforcement of anxiety). Plan specific, gradual reductions (e.g., "This week, when you feel the urge to read about symptoms, pause for 5 minutes before reading").
