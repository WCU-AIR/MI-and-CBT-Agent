### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance, validating Linda's concerns about side effects and normalizing her ambivalence. Explicit affirmations of her strengths ("Your thoughtful approach and willingness to engage, even with some hesitation, are real strengths") highlight genuine appreciation. However, acceptance could be deepened by more fully exploring and normalizing Linda's fears without pivoting quickly to positive reframing. The clinician maintains warmth and respect but occasionally leans toward gentle redirection rather than sitting fully with Linda's ambivalence about medication safety.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates strong reflective listening and perspective-taking with accurate reflections capturing Linda's core conflict: "It sounds like you're really caught in a tough spot—wanting to keep your asthma under control but feeling hesitant because of those side effects." Reflections accurately validate both poles of her ambivalence. However, reflections remain somewhat surface-level and don't consistently deepen into more complex, exploratory reflections that might reveal underlying values or fears. For example, when Linda mentions throat scratchiness and bone health concerns, the psychiatrist reflects these but doesn't explore what these fears represent about her values regarding bodily safety and control.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains general focus on asthma management but lacks strategic clarity about the specific change goal of consistent medication adherence. The session drifts between exploring concerns, building confidence, and eventually landing on journaling as a strategy—but the overarching direction toward consistent inhaler use becomes less explicit as the session progresses. The focus shifts toward "feeling informed and empowered" rather than maintaining clear focus on the target behavior. The psychiatrist doesn't strategically guide Linda back to exploring her actual experience with inhalers or the evidence-based rationale for their use, instead accepting her framing that inhalers are scary without gently challenging this assumption.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates strong collaborative stance with the opening question—"what's on your mind or what would you most like to focus on?"—immediately establishing partnership. The clinician consistently seeks Linda's input ("What do you think would help you feel more confident?" "What has helped you in the past?") and incorporates her ideas into the plan. Language is inclusive and collaborative throughout. However, moments exist where collaboration becomes slightly unbalanced; the psychiatrist's affirmation of Linda's ideas as "thoughtful" and "insightful" without exploring whether these are truly Linda's own conclusions or reflections of what she thinks the psychiatrist wants to hear could mask subtle expert positioning.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates inconsistent autonomy support. Positively, language emphasizes choice and empowerment: "It puts you in the driver's seat, observing and learning from your own body." However, significant gaps exist. The psychiatrist does not adequately explore or affirm Linda's right to hold concerns about medication without immediately moving toward reassurance or alternative strategies. The session subtly guides Linda toward compliance through journaling rather than genuinely supporting her autonomy to make her own informed decision about treatment. The psychiatrist doesn't explicitly state: "This is ultimately your decision to make" or "You have the right to choose what feels safe for you." The framing subtly positions journaling as a pathway to eventual medication adherence rather than genuinely supporting Linda's autonomy to decide what's right for her body.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates some evocation of Linda's own motivations and resources but inconsistently and with limited depth. The question "What has helped you in the past when you've faced other tough decisions?" successfully evokes Linda's coping strategies and strengths. However, the session lacks strategic evocation of Linda's change talk and deeper motivations. The psychiatrist doesn't explore: "What matters to you about your health?" "What would be possible if your asthma were well-controlled?" or "What do you value about being able to breathe easily?" When Linda expresses openness ("I'm willing to give it a try"), the psychiatrist affirms this but doesn't deepen it by asking: "What makes you willing to try despite your concerns?" The journaling strategy, while potentially useful, is presented as the psychiatrist's idea rather than genuinely evoking from Linda what *she* thinks might help.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates inconsistent partnership. Positively, the clinician uses inclusive language ("our time together," "we can explore") and explicitly invites Linda's agenda from the start. However, significant moments exist where the psychiatrist subtly assumes an expert role. When Linda expresses concerns about inhalers, the psychiatrist doesn't genuinely explore these as potentially valid; instead, the session moves toward reassurance and alternative strategies, implicitly positioning the psychiatrist as the one who knows what's best. The psychiatrist doesn't genuinely share power regarding the treatment decision itself. The session frames the psychiatrist as guiding Linda toward better asthma management rather than truly co-creating what "good asthma management" means for Linda. A more genuine partnership would involve: "What does good asthma management look like to you?"

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal strategic cultivation of change talk—a significant MI deficiency. Linda offers important change talk statements that are not amplified or developed: "I want to keep my asthma under control" (desire), "I'm willing to give it a try" (commitment), and "I'm open to talking about anything that might help me feel less scared and more in control" (openness). When Linda expresses these statements, the psychiatrist validates them but doesn't strategically evoke or strengthen them. The psychiatrist doesn't use targeted questions like: "What would be the benefits of managing your asthma more consistently?" "What strengths do you have that could help you?" or "What would it take to move from a 6 to a 7?" When Linda reaches a readiness score of 6, the psychiatrist doesn't ask follow-up questions to deepen commitment language. The session focuses on validating concerns and building confidence through affirmation rather than strategically evoking and amplifying Linda's own language about change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal skill in softening sustain talk—a significant MI deficiency. Linda expresses substantial sustain talk throughout: "I worry a lot about the inhalers," "I've read some things online about side effects, and it makes me nervous," "I keep wondering if the risks might be worse than the benefits," and "I'm still worried about making the wrong choice." When Linda expresses these concerns, the psychiatrist primarily validates them without strategically redirecting or reframing. The psychiatrist doesn't use double-sided reflections to acknowledge both sides of ambivalence while subtly emphasizing the change side. For example, a more skillful response to Linda's fear about side effects might be: "You're worried about potential side effects AND you want to keep your asthma under control—both of those things matter to you. Tell me more about what keeping your asthma controlled would mean for your life." The psychiatrist also doesn't strategically reframe Linda's concerns or gently explore whether her fears are based on personal experience versus internet-fueled anxiety. The session accepts Linda's framing that inhalers are problematic without exploring the evidence.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Session Score: 3.2/5**

**Strengths:**
- Strong opening and collaborative invitation establishing partnership immediately
- Consistent validation and affirmation creating a supportive environment
- Accurate reflective listening capturing Linda's core conflict and emotional experience
- Practical problem-solving identifying concrete, manageable steps (journaling, reminders, husband's support)
- Avoidance of confrontation maintaining collaborative stance throughout
- Appropriate use of scaling questions to assess readiness and confidence

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- **Minimal cultivation of change talk:** Fails to strategically evoke, amplify, or strengthen Linda's own statements about desire, ability, reasons, or commitment to change
- **Inadequate softening of sustain talk:** Linda's fears about medication are validated but not strategically redirected or reframed; sustain talk persists without MI-consistent intervention
- **Lack of strategic direction:** Session drifts from addressing core ambivalence about medication adherence toward journaling strategy without fully exploring the fundamental concern about medication safety
- **Subtle expert positioning:** Despite collaborative language, the psychiatrist subtly positions themselves as the expert on what's best for Linda's asthma management rather than genuinely sharing power
- **Insufficient autonomy support:** Doesn't explicitly affirm Linda's right to make her own informed decisions about medication or explore what autonomy means in her healthcare
- **Limited evocation of values:** Doesn't strategically explore what matters to Linda about her health, what her life would look like with well-controlled asthma, or her core values regarding treatment

**Impact on Client Engagement:**
The psychiatrist's approach creates a supportive, validating environment that likely increases Linda's comfort and engagement. However, the session is unlikely to significantly strengthen Linda's intrinsic motivation for consistent asthma management or medication adherence. By validating her concerns about inhalers without strategically exploring the evidence or evoking her own reasons for managing her asthma, the psychiatrist may inadvertently reinforce Linda's ambivalence rather than resolve it. Linda ends at 6/10 confidence—slightly improved but substantially uncertain, suggesting the MI intervention, while supportive, was not strategically aimed at evoking and strengthening commitment to change.

**Recommendation:**
The psychiatrist demonstrates basic competence in supportive counseling but falls short of true Motivational Interviewing practice. With focused practice on strategically evoking and amplifying change talk, softening sustain talk through reframing, exploring core ambivalence about medication safety, and genuinely sharing power in defining treatment goals, the psychiatrist can move from supportive counseling toward true MI that effectively evokes client motivation for change. This is a trainable gap with focused feedback and deliberate practice.
