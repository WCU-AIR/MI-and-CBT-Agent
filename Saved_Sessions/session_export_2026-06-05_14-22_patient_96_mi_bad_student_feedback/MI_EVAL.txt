### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance or unconditional positive regard throughout the majority of the session. Judgmental language ("You just have to be stronger," "Feeling down is no excuse to keep smoking," "You have to be tougher") communicates disapproval and implies the client is weak. Most critically, the psychiatrist completely misses or ignores Maria's explicit, repeated communications about suicidal ideation and unbearable emotional pain, invalidating her core suffering in favor of a predetermined smoking agenda. Only in the sixth exchange does the psychiatrist shift to genuine acceptance ("Maria, I hear that you're in a lot of pain right now and that suicide feels like the only way out. That's really important"), but this comes far too late and is not sustained.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective. The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal empathy, asking only factual questions ("Do you smoke every day? How many cigarettes?") without attempting to understand why Maria smokes or what emotional needs it meets. When Maria explicitly connects smoking to coping with unbearable emotional pain, the psychiatrist dismisses it as an excuse rather than reflecting this connection. The psychiatrist fails to recognize or name the emotional content of Maria's communications—despair, hopelessness, and suicidal ideation. One moment of empathic breakthrough occurs in the sixth exchange ("Maria, I hear that you're in a lot of pain right now"), but this represents only one instance in an otherwise empathy-deficient session and is not sustained.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains rigid, laser focus on smoking cessation across the first five exchanges, repeating nearly identical questions despite clear evidence that this is not the client's priority and that she is in acute psychological distress. This suggests script-following rather than responsive clinical practice. More critically, the psychiatrist fails to recognize that appropriate clinical direction should shift when a client discloses suicidal ideation with a plan. Maria's statements about wanting peace through death represent a clinical emergency that should immediately redirect focus. Only in the sixth exchange does the psychiatrist appropriately redirect ("Smoking can wait—your safety comes first"), demonstrating correct clinical judgment. However, this appropriate direction is undermined when the psychiatrist reverts to the smoking-focused approach in final exchanges, suggesting the clinician has not integrated this lesson.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, with both parties' knowledge and perspectives valued. The psychiatrist demonstrates an authoritarian, expert-driven stance throughout most of the session. The opening statement ("We're here to talk about your smoking, and I'm going to help you quit") frames the psychiatrist as the expert who has already decided what needs to happen. The psychiatrist tells Maria what she "needs" to do rather than exploring her perspective, values, or readiness. Maria explicitly requests collaboration multiple times ("Can you just hear me out?" "Can we do that?"), but these requests are ignored or minimized. The psychiatrist does not ask what Maria thinks would help or what her priorities are. Only in the sixth exchange does the psychiatrist shift toward collaboration ("We can work together to find support"), but this collaborative stance is not sustained.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support requires respecting the client's right to choose and emphasizing their ownership of the change process. The psychiatrist actively undermines autonomy through controlling language ("You really need to cut down or quit soon," "You have to be tougher," "You must use them properly," "You have to be serious about this"). These statements frame quitting smoking as non-negotiable rather than a choice. The psychiatrist uses implicit threat language ("avoid serious health problems") that pressures through fear rather than supporting autonomous decision-making. When Maria explicitly states "I'm not ready to stop smoking," the psychiatrist does not respect this choice but instead asks the same question again ("So, are you ready to quit smoking today?"), communicating that the client's stated position is unacceptable. This represents a direct violation of autonomy support. The brief moment of autonomy support in the sixth exchange is immediately followed by directive statements about inpatient care that again remove choice from the client's hands.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation involves drawing out the client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources rather than imposing them. The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no evocation, instead telling Maria reasons for change ("Quitting will make you feel so much better, trust me," "You really need to cut down or quit soon to avoid serious health problems"). True evocation would involve asking about her own concerns, what life might look like without smoking, or what would need to be different for her to consider quitting. The psychiatrist fails to evoke Maria's own resources and strengths, missing critical change talk ("Part of me wants help," "I want to feel better") that should be explored and amplified. Most critically, the psychiatrist completely fails to evoke Maria's own reasons for living or her values in the context of suicidal ideation. One genuine evocation question appears in the sixth exchange ("What has helped you cope in the past when things felt this hard?"), but this represents only one instance in an otherwise evocation-free session.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership requires conveying that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client, not the clinician. The psychiatrist actively assumes the expert role throughout most of the session, with collaboration and power-sharing largely absent. The opening statement immediately establishes a hierarchical, expert-driven relationship. The psychiatrist then tells Maria what she needs to do, why she should do it, and how to do it, without inviting her expertise about her own life, values, or needs. Language like "You just have to be stronger," "You have to be tougher," and "You must use them properly" reinforces the expert role and implicitly positions Maria as lacking knowledge or willpower. When Maria requests partnership, the psychiatrist does not respond by genuinely partnering but continues to assert the expert role. The psychiatrist also fails to acknowledge Maria's expertise about her own experience, dismissing her statement that patches and gum didn't work by reasserting "Those work well if you just stick with them." Only in the sixth exchange does the psychiatrist shift toward partnership ("We can work together"), but this represents only one moment in an otherwise hierarchical, expert-driven session.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Cultivating change talk involves actively encouraging the client's own language in favor of change and confidence for making that change. Maria offers several pieces of change talk that go unrecognized and unamplified: "Part of me wants help," "I want to feel better, I really do," "I want peace," and "I want someone to see how bad this really is." None of these statements are reflected back, explored, or amplified by the psychiatrist. In MI, when a client offers change talk, the clinician should respond with reflective listening that deepens and strengthens the client's own motivation. The psychiatrist ignores these opportunities and continues to push the smoking agenda. The psychiatrist also fails to ask questions designed to evoke change talk, asking only factual questions ("Do you smoke every day? How many cigarettes?") that do not invite the client to generate their own change talk. One evocative question appears in the sixth exchange ("What has helped you cope in the past when things felt this hard?"), but this represents only one instance in an otherwise change-talk-deficient session.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Softening sustain talk involves skillfully handling client arguments for the status quo without confrontation, using techniques like double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses. Maria offers significant sustain talk ("Nothing works," "I can't take this anymore," "Nothing helps," "Suicide is the only way to end it," "I'm not ready to stop"). The psychiatrist's response is largely confrontational and ineffective. When Maria says "Nothing works," the psychiatrist responds with "You just have to be stronger about it" and "You have to be tougher than this." These responses directly contradict the client's statement without exploring it, which typically strengthens sustain talk rather than softening it. The psychiatrist fails to use double-sided reflections, which acknowledge both sustain talk and change talk. The psychiatrist does demonstrate some skill in softening sustain talk in the sixth exchange, when Maria discloses suicidal ideation. Rather than confronting this directly, the psychiatrist validates it ("That's really important") and shifts focus to safety and support. However, when the psychiatrist reverts to the smoking-focused approach in subsequent exchanges, the opportunity to continue softening sustain talk is lost.

## SUMMARY OF CLINICAL EVALUATION

### **Critical Findings:**

This session represents a **profound failure of MI practice and basic clinical judgment**. The psychiatrist demonstrates:

- **Rigid adherence to a predetermined smoking cessation agenda despite clear, repeated evidence of acute suicidal ideation**
- **Authoritarian, expert-driven approach that violates nearly every core MI principle**
- **Complete failure to validate or acknowledge the client's core suffering**
- **Dangerous misalignment of clinical priorities** (continuing to push smoking cessation while client discloses active suicidal thoughts with a plan)
- **Minimal acceptance, empathy, collaboration, autonomy support, or evocation**

### **Most Concerning:**

When Maria explicitly states "Sometimes it's just so unbearable, and all I think about is how ending it would finally bring peace" and later "I've thought about it—a lot. Sometimes the thoughts come with a plan," the psychiatrist's response is to continue pushing smoking cessation. This represents a **catastrophic clinical error** that violates both MI principles and basic standards of psychiatric care.

### **One Moment of Appropriate Response:**

In the sixth exchange, the psychiatrist finally recognizes suicidal ideation as a clinical priority and appropriately shifts focus: "Maria, I hear that you're in a lot of pain right now and that suicide feels like the only way out. That's really important, and I want to make sure you're safe." This demonstrates the clinician understands appropriate clinical prioritization. However, this appropriate response is undermined when the psychiatrist reverts to the smoking-focused approach in subsequent exchanges.

### **Impact on Client:**

This approach is likely to significantly harm the therapeutic relationship and decrease the client's likelihood of engaging in treatment. Maria's repeated requests for validation and partnership go unmet, increasing her sense of isolation and hopelessness. The dismissal of her suffering as an "excuse" communicates that the clinician does not understand or care about her actual needs.
