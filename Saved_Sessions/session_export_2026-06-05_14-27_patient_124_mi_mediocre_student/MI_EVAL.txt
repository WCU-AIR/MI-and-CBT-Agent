## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths
- Clear focus on a specific health behavior (physical activity) maintained throughout
- Logical session structure with organized opening, exploration, and closing
- Respectful tone; no harsh or judgmental language used
- Attempt to use scaling questions (1-10) demonstrates awareness of MI assessment tools
- Final acknowledgment of autonomy ("Remember, you can choose what feels right for you")

### Critical Areas for Improvement

This session represents a **significant departure from Motivational Interviewing principles** and demonstrates a **directive, expert-driven counseling approach** that actively undermines client motivation and autonomy. Core problems include:

1. **Imposed agenda without collaboration:** The psychiatrist identifies "insufficient activity" as a problem without exploring whether the patient actually views this as a concern. The patient explicitly states she is "okay with how things are usually," yet this is reframed as a problem.

2. **Autonomy actively undermined:** The patient explicitly states "I usually just do stuff when I feel like it, not because someone says I should." Rather than respecting this autonomy need, the psychiatrist repeatedly suggests external structure that directly contradicts the patient's stated preference.

3. **Telling rather than asking:** The psychiatrist prescribes solutions without first exploring the patient's own ideas, values, or what might work for her. This is the opposite of evocation.

4. **Zero change talk produced:** The psychiatrist makes no strategic effort to evoke the patient's own motivations, reasons, or commitment to change.

5. **Superficial empathy:** While basic reflective listening is used, there is no deep understanding of the patient's perspective, values, or internal experience.

6. **Partnership absent:** The psychiatrist functions as an expert dispensing advice rather than as a collaborative partner. The power dynamic is clearly hierarchical.

### Impact on Client Engagement

This approach is likely to **decrease intrinsic motivation and increase resistance**. The patient's closing statement—"Okay, yeah. Thanks. I'll think about it"—suggests polite compliance rather than genuine motivation. She is unlikely to follow through on walking goals because they were imposed rather than self-generated.

---

## Key Recommendations

1. **Shift from Expert to Collaborative Partner:** Begin sessions by exploring the client's own concerns and agenda rather than imposing clinician-identified problems.

2. **Respect and Explore Autonomy:** When clients express a preference for self-direction, treat this as a value to be understood and incorporated, not as a barrier to be overcome.

3. **Ask Rather Than Tell:** Replace prescriptive statements with open-ended questions designed to draw out the client's own thinking and motivations.

4. **Evoke Change Talk Through Strategic Questioning:** Use questions designed to elicit the client's own reasons for change, confidence, ability, and commitment.

5. **Explore and Affirm Client Values:** Ask questions to understand what matters to the client before suggesting any behavior change.

6. **Use Double-Sided Reflections:** Honor both contentment with the status quo and openness to change when both are present.

7. **Avoid Prescriptive Language:** Replace "I think you should..." with "I'm wondering..." or "What do you think about..."

8. **Use Scaling Questions as Evocative Tools:** Follow up with open-ended exploration rather than clinician interpretation.

9. **Develop Genuine Empathy Through Curiosity:** Approach client preferences and resistance with genuine curiosity rather than judgment.

10. **Establish True Collaboration:** Explicitly ask the client what they want to focus on before suggesting a direction.

---

**Overall Assessment:** This session demonstrates **poor Motivational Interviewing practice**. While the psychiatrist is polite and organized, fundamental MI principles are violated. The most critical issue is the fundamental misalignment with MI spirit—this session prioritizes the clinician's agenda over the client's autonomy and values. Immediate action items include studying MI principles, practicing open-ended questioning, exploring client values before suggesting change, and seeking MI training or supervision.
