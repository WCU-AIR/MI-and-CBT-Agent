
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated minimal competence in eliciting and responding to patient feedback throughout the session. Feedback elicitation is a core CBT skill—therapists must regularly check for understanding, solicit reactions to interventions, and adjust based on patient responses. This therapist failed to do so systematically.

Specific deficiencies:

1. **No mid-session feedback checks:** The therapist never asked, "Does this make sense?" "How are you feeling about this approach?" or "Is this helpful?" after introducing interventions. For example, after suggesting cognitive reframing ("What if they're not trying to take advantage, but just overwhelmed?"), the therapist did not check whether the patient understood the rationale or found it credible.

2. **Minimal end-of-session feedback:** At session close, the therapist summarized content but did not ask, "How did today feel?" "Was this session helpful?" or "What would you like to focus on next time?" The patient's closing statement—"I appreciate you listening without jumping down my throat"—suggests the patient had concerns about being judged, yet the therapist never explored this.

3. **No response to resistance:** When the patient stated, "I don't buy it very often" and "I'm not sure I can just assume they had a good reason," the therapist acknowledged this ("I see, it sounds like you've had a lot of experiences") but did not explore the patient's skepticism about the intervention itself. A competent therapist would have asked, "What would need to happen for you to consider this approach?" or "What concerns do you have about trying this?"

4. **No collaborative adjustment:** The therapist did not modify interventions based on patient feedback. When the patient expressed doubt about breathing exercises ("when I get irritated like that, it's hard to just stop and breathe"), the therapist minimized this concern rather than collaboratively problem-solving: "Maybe it's about finding little moments where you can pause, even if just for a few seconds." 5. **Session 2 repetition:** In the second session, the therapist again failed to elicit feedback about the homework ("breathing and noticing thoughts"). There was no inquiry about whether the patient attempted it, what happened, or whether it was helpful.

**Summary:**

Feedback elicitation was essentially absent. The therapist did not regularly check understanding, solicit reactions to interventions, or adjust based on patient responses. This represents a critical gap in collaborative CBT practice.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated partial understanding of the patient's presenting concerns but missed critical nuances relevant to personality disorder pathology. Understanding in CBT requires grasping the patient's "internal reality"—their thoughts, feelings, and the logic of their worldview—and communicating this empathically.

When the therapist suggested cognitive reframing, the patient's response—"I don't buy it very often"—reflects not just disagreement but a rigid cognitive style. 4. **Missed the distrust of the therapeutic process:** The patient opened with reluctance to share information, fearing "misunderstanding or misuse of his words." His comment at session end—"I appreciate you listening without jumping down my throat or telling me I'm wrong"—suggests he expected judgment or invalidation. The therapist never explored this transference or demonstrated understanding of the patient's likely wariness of the therapeutic relationship itself.

5. **Missed the secondary gain of hypervigilance:** The patient's constant monitoring and criticism of others serves a defensive function—it maintains his sense of control and superiority ("I could detect manipulation while others 'drank the Kool-Aid'"). The therapist did not understand or reflect this adaptive function of the maladaptive behavior.

**Summary:**

The therapist demonstrated surface-level empathy and basic listening but missed the deeper personality pathology driving the patient's presentation. Understanding was incomplete and did not reflect grasp of the patient's rigid, suspicious worldview or the defensive functions of his behavior.

- **Action:** Explicitly articulate a case conceptualization that identifies the core beliefs and behavioral patterns maintaining the patient's difficulties. Develop a strategic plan for how CBT interventions will address these patterns across multiple sessions.

### 3. Implement Structured Homework with Clear Instructions, Rationale, and Follow-Up (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
- **Action:** Homework is non-negotiable in CBT. Every session must include: (a) review of previous homework, (b) assignment of new homework with clear, specific instructions and written summary, (c) practice of the homework during session, (d) explicit rationale for why the homework will help.

- **Practice:** For each session, prepare: (a) a specific homework assignment with written instructions, (b) a clear rationale for why the homework will help, (c) a plan to practice the homework during session, (d) a reminder to review homework at the start of the next session.

### 4. Use Guided Discovery and Socratic Questioning Instead of Directive Suggestions
- **Action:** Replace directive suggestions with skillful questioning that helps the patient generate insights. Ask questions that help the patient examine evidence, consider alternatives, and reach conclusions independently.
- **Example:** Instead of: "What if they're not trying to take advantage, but just overwhelmed or distracted?"
