# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and effectively affirms Maria's values around autonomy and control. Strong validating statements such as "You've taken the time to learn about your treatment, which shows how much you care about your health" normalize her concerns without judgment. However, there are missed opportunities to explicitly validate her frustration with healthcare system demands and her realistic skepticism about alternatives. Deeper acceptance of her whole experience—not just her asthma concerns—would strengthen this domain.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates solid reflective listening with well-executed complex reflections that capture both practical and emotional concerns. The reflection "It sounds like you're hoping for a straightforward, manageable approach...something that doesn't add complexity to your busy life" accurately captures Maria's dual concerns. Later reflections about her need for control and autonomy show genuine understanding. However, empathy could be deeper by exploring underlying fears of dependency and loss of control more explicitly, and by probing what drives her low confidence (3/10) rather than simply validating the caution.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains some focus on asthma management but lacks strategic clarity about the core change goal. The session explores Maria's perspective thoroughly but never explicitly establishes whether the target is medication adherence, trigger identification, or a hybrid approach. The central ambivalence—worsening asthma versus inhaler resistance—is never directly addressed. While trigger identification emerges as a "first step," its connection to the larger clinical goal remains unclear. No explicit follow-up plan or reassessment timeline is established, limiting strategic direction.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates strong collaborative stance through frequent open-ended questions, invitations for Maria's input, and incorporation of her preferences into the plan. Statements like "What would feel like a good way for us to support you?" and "How does that plan feel to you?" invite genuine partnership. The psychiatrist respects Maria's time constraints and validates her suggestions. However, collaboration could be strengthened by more explicitly co-constructing the clinical direction and collaboratively defining what "success" looks like, rather than presenting trigger identification as a clinical recommendation.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently affirms Maria's right to choose and emphasizes her agency throughout the session. Key autonomy-supporting moments include validating her desire for control ("Wanting to avoid chemicals and maintain control...highlights how important your well-being and autonomy are") and respecting her pace without pressure. The psychiatrist avoids prescriptive language and uses invitational phrasing. However, a critical gap exists: the psychiatrist never explicitly addresses that trigger avoidance alone may be insufficient for worsening asthma, or that inhalers may ultimately be necessary. True autonomy support requires ensuring Maria has full information to make an informed choice.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist makes some effort to evoke Maria's own motivations and resources, particularly through the skillful question about past successes with diet management. However, evocation is inconsistent and surface-level. Critical gaps include: (1) insufficient amplification of Maria's change talk ("I'm willing to try," "I think I could manage that"); (2) limited exploration of values beyond stated preferences (autonomy, simplicity); (3) minimal exploration of Maria's concerns about worsening asthma trajectory; and (4) no systematic evocation of desire, ability, reasons, or commitment. Change talk emerges naturally but remains largely unrecognized and underdeveloped.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains a strong partnership stance by consistently positioning Maria as the expert on her own experience and values. The psychiatrist asks rather than tells, incorporates Maria's preferences, and uses inclusive language ("What might be a small, first step *you'd* feel comfortable taking?"). The psychiatrist acknowledges Maria's expertise and avoids the expert role. However, partnership could be deepened by more explicitly inviting Maria to co-problem-solve around the central clinical tension (worsening asthma + inhaler resistance), positioning the psychiatrist as a true collaborator in solving a shared problem rather than simply supporting her preferred approach.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** This is a significant weakness. While change talk emerges naturally ("I'm willing to try," "I think I could manage that," "It's worth a shot"), the psychiatrist demonstrates minimal strategic cultivation or amplification. Critical failures include: (1) no deepening of Maria's change talk through follow-up questions; (2) no explicit reinforcement or celebration of her emerging motivation; (3) insufficient exploration of confidence scaling ("What would move you from a 3 to a 4?"); (4) no systematic evocation of desire, ability, reasons, or commitment; (5) missed opportunity to connect her successful diet change to current possibility; and (6) no scaling questions to evoke movement. Change talk is present but largely unrecognized and underdeveloped—a major MI gap.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates some skill in gently redirecting away from sustain talk without confrontation, particularly by validating Maria's skepticism and pivoting to exploration of past successes. However, significant gaps exist: (1) no double-sided reflections acknowledging both her concerns about inhalers AND her worsening asthma; (2) no exploration of the cost of maintaining the status quo; (3) no reframing of her concerns about inhaler safety; (4) insufficient direct naming of the central clinical tension; and (5) no explicit exploration of her ambivalence. Sustain talk is softened through validation but not strategically addressed through MI-consistent techniques.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 3.4/5 – Adequate MI Competence with Notable Room for Improvement**

**Strengths:**
- Consistent acceptance and unconditional positive regard throughout the session
- Solid reflective listening with accurate, multi-layered reflections
- Strong collaborative stance with frequent invitations for client input
- Effective autonomy support with affirmation of Maria's right to choose
- Skillful resource-building through exploration of past successes
- Respectful pacing that honors Maria's time constraints and busy life

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- **Insufficient cultivation of change talk** – The most significant gap; change talk emerges but is not strategically amplified or reinforced
- **Lack of strategic direction** – No clear, collaboratively-defined target behavior or explicit connection between trigger identification and larger clinical goal
- **Inadequate exploration of central clinical tension** – The contradiction between worsening asthma and inhaler resistance is never directly named or explored
- **Limited evocation of deeper values and concerns** – Minimal exploration of values beyond stated preferences or concerns about asthma trajectory
- **Insufficient softening of sustain talk** – No double-sided reflections, exploration of status quo costs, or strategic reframing
- **No explicit information-sharing** – The psychiatrist does not invite collaborative exploration of what research shows about inhaler safety

**Impact on Client Engagement and Change:**
Maria leaves the session feeling heard and respected, moving from defensive resistance to cautious openness. However, this represents a *compromise* rather than genuine movement toward improved asthma management. Her underlying ambivalence about inhalers remains unresolved, and the central clinical tension is never addressed. If trigger avoidance proves insufficient and her asthma worsens, she may feel unsupported or return with increased resistance.

**Competency Level:**
The psychiatrist demonstrates strong foundational MI skills but lacks the strategic depth needed for excellent MI practice. The session feels more like "supportive counseling" than strategic motivational interviewing. To reach 4/5 (good MI practice), the psychiatrist must develop greater skill in strategically cultivating change talk, naming and exploring clinical tensions, using double-sided reflections, and evoking deeper values that support intrinsic motivation for change.
