
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance of Oliver as a person. This directly contradicts MI spirit and likely increases client defensiveness and shame rather than supporting intrinsic motivation.

**Summary:**

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

While the psychiatrist maintains a clear focus on health behavior change (alcohol reduction and exercise), the execution is fundamentally misaligned with MI principles. Direction in MI means maintaining strategic focus *while remaining client-centered*—guiding collaboratively toward change goals that the client has identified or co-created. This psychiatrist maintains focus but does so through coercion and prescription rather than collaborative exploration.

The psychiatrist never asks Oliver what his health goals are, what changes he might be willing to consider, or what barriers exist. The direction is one-directional (psychiatrist → client) rather than mutually developed. Additionally, the psychiatrist provides no exploration of *why* these specific targets (three times weekly gym, specific alcohol limits) or whether they align with Oliver's values, schedule, or readiness.

By the end of the session, Oliver has nominally "committed" to the plan, but this commitment appears coerced rather than intrinsically motivated. The psychiatrist never checks whether Oliver actually believes these goals are achievable or important to him—only whether he will comply. This is direction without collaboration, which undermines the likelihood of sustained behavior change.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains clear focus on health behavior change but imposes targets rather than collaboratively developing them. Direction is achieved through coercion rather than strategic client-centered guidance, which reduces the likelihood of genuine commitment to change.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Fundamental Shift in Stance: From Expert-Directive to Collaborative Partnership**
- **Action:** Adopt the MI spirit of partnership from the outset. Begin sessions with genuine curiosity about the client's perspective, values, and goals rather than predetermined prescriptions. Use collaborative language ("we," "let's explore," "what do you think?") and explicitly invite the client's input into treatment planning.
- **Example:** Instead of "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do," open with: "I'm interested in learning about your health and what matters to you. What brings you in today? What are you hoping to work on?"
- **Practice:** Before each session, remind yourself: "My role is to help this client discover their own motivation for change, not to impose my agenda." Practice asking open-ended questions and genuinely listening to answers without planning your next directive.

---

### 2. **Develop Genuine Acceptance and Unconditional Positive Regard**
- **Action:** Eliminate judgmental language entirely. Replace shame-based framing ("you're weak," "quit feeling sorry for yourself") with acceptance of the client's struggle as normal and understandable. Actively affirm the client's strengths, efforts, and self-awareness.
- **Example:** Instead of "quit being weak," say: "I notice you're aware of how hard it is for you to be assertive with yourself. That self-awareness is actually a strength—it's the first step toward change. Many people struggle with this."
- **Practice:** In supervision or role-play, practice reframing every critical statement into an acceptance-based statement. For example: "You're making excuses" becomes "It sounds like there are real barriers to fitting exercise into your schedule. Let's talk about what those are."

---

### 3. **Master Reflective Listening and Complex Reflections**
- **Action:** Implement reflective listening as the primary counselor response. When the client speaks, reflect back what you hear—both content and emotion—to demonstrate understanding. Use complex reflections that capture ambivalence, underlying values, and emotional nuance.
- **Example:** When Oliver says "I care about the person and there's comfort in having someone there, but part of me wants independence," reflect: "So there's a real tension for you—you value connection and loyalty, and at the same time you're recognizing that you need your own space and independence. Both of those matter to you."
- **Practice:** Record yourself in sessions and count your reflections vs. directives. Aim for a 2:1 or 3:1 ratio of reflections to directives. Practice complex reflections that capture multiple dimensions of the client's experience.

---

### 4. **Evoke Rather Than Impose: Ask, Don't Tell**
- **Action:** Replace prescriptive statements with open-ended questions designed to evoke the client's own motivation, values, and ideas. Ask about past successes, barriers, and what the client thinks might work. Build on the client's own change talk.
- **Example:** Instead of "You need to cut down on alcohol and hit the gym three times a week," ask: "What would be different in your life if you were exercising regularly and drinking less? What matters to you about your health? What have you tried before that worked, even a little bit?"
- **Practice:** Develop a repertoire of evocative questions: "What would be the benefits of...?" "How confident are you that...?" "What's one small step you could take...?" "What have you done in the past that you felt proud of?" Practice asking these questions and genuinely listening to the answers.

---

### 5. **Support Autonomy: Emphasize Client Choice and Self-Determination**
- **Action:** Explicitly affirm the client's right to choose. Frame treatment as collaborative exploration rather than compliance with the clinician's agenda. Use language that emphasizes client ownership: "It's your decision," "What do you think would work for you?" "You're the expert on your own life."
- **Example:** Instead of "This isn't optional," say: "You have choices here. Some people find it helpful to start with small changes, others prefer to make bigger shifts all at once. What do you think would work best for you?"
- **Practice:** In every session, explicitly acknowledge client autonomy at least once. For example: "Ultimately, this is your decision. My role is to support you in whatever you decide to do."

---

### 6. **Validate Ambivalence Rather Than Confront It**
- **Action:** When clients express ambivalence or sustain talk, respond with validation and exploration rather than confrontation. Use double-sided reflections that acknowledge both sides of the ambivalence. Explore the function of the current behavior before asking for change.
- **Example:** When Oliver says "I like to unwind with a drink," instead of "You need to cut down on the booze," say: "So drinking helps you relax and unwind after work—that serves a real purpose for you. And you also want to feel healthier and have more energy. How do you see those fitting together? What might help you unwind without the alcohol?"
- **Practice:** When you hear sustain talk, pause and reflect it back with genuine curiosity: "Help me understand what that's about for you." Explore the underlying need or value before suggesting alternatives.

---

### 7. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk Strategically**
- **Action:** Listen carefully for any statements the client makes in favor of change (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment). When you hear change talk, amplify it through reflection, exploration, and affirmation. Ask follow-up questions that deepen and strengthen change talk.
- **Example:** When Oliver says "My health is worth the effort," respond: "That's important to you—your health. Tell me more about that. What does health mean to you? What would be different if you were healthier?"
- **Practice:** In sessions, mark every instance of change talk (even small ones) and respond with a reflection or follow-up question designed to strengthen it. Build a habit of noticing and amplifying change talk.

---

### 8. **Develop Collaborative Goal-Setting and Treatment Planning**
- **Action:** Rather than prescribing specific behavioral targets, collaboratively explore what the client wants to work on and what goals feel achievable and important to them. Co-construct treatment plans that reflect both clinical wisdom and client preferences, values, and readiness.
- **Example:** Instead of "You need to cut down on alcohol and hit the gym three times a week," say: "What would you like to work on first? What feels most important to you? What do you think might be realistic for you to start with?"
- **Practice:** In supervision, practice collaborative goal-setting with role-play clients. Aim to develop goals that the client has genuinely chosen and feels confident about, not goals the client has agreed to under pressure.

---

### 9. **Build Client Self-Efficacy and Confidence**
- **Action:** Actively build the client's confidence in their ability to change. Affirm past successes, explore strengths and resources, and help the client identify small, achievable steps. Avoid shame-based language that undermines self-efficacy.
- **Example:** Instead of "If you slip up, that's just you being weak," say: "Most people find it takes a few tries to build a new habit. If you slip up, that's normal—it's part of the process. What would help you get back on track?"
- **Practice:** In every session, identify and affirm at least one client strength or past success. Help clients see themselves as capable of change, not as weak or broken.

- **Action:** This session demonstrates fundamental misalignment with MI principles. Seek formal MI training (e.g., through MINT—Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers) and regular clinical supervision focused on MI skill development. Record and review your sessions to identify areas for improvement.
- **Example:** Participate in MI training workshops, read MI literature (e.g., "Motivational Interviewing: Helping People Change" by Miller & Rollnick), and engage in regular supervision with an MI-trained clinician.
- **Practice:** Commit to ongoing skill development. MI is a learnable set of skills that improve with deliberate practice and feedback.
