### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates fundamental failure to communicate unconditional positive regard. Repeatedly dismisses client's explicit boundary-setting ("It's not really any of your business") rather than honoring it as valid self-protection. Uses pseudo-empathy ("I understand...but") that invalidates rather than accepts client's perspective. Offers unsolicited advice and prescriptive directives ("You should try," "It would probably help") that signal judgment about what client ought to do rather than acceptance of who they are. No genuine warmth, no acknowledgment of difficulty in talking to stranger, no recognition that defensiveness is reasonable response to intrusion.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1

When client states boundary, psychiatrist responds with dismissal disguised as empathy rather than genuine exploration of what client needs. Minimal client responses ("Mm-hmm," "Okay," "Fine") treated as data points to interpret rather than signals of disengagement warranting curiosity. No attempt to explore what brought client to session, what actual concerns are, or what client values. ### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Maintains predetermined direction (health behavior change) without collaborative agreement. Direction is imposed rather than collaboratively established. Psychiatrist assumes target behavior and guides toward it without asking what client wants to work on. Persists with same agenda despite clear disengagement signals (repeated boundary-setting, minimal responses). Demonstrates inflexible rather than strategic focus. True direction would involve flexibility—if client not ready to discuss behavior change, direction should shift toward understanding what brought them to session or what would make them feel safe enough to engage.

### Collaboration

Unilaterally defines problems without asking client what concerns her. Uses prescriptive advice-giving ("You might want to try," "It would probably help if you") rather than collaborative exploration. Dismisses client input when boundaries are set. No joint problem-solving or genuine invitation for client expertise about own life. Rhetorical questions ("Does that sound okay?") masquerade as collaboration but are asked after psychiatrist has already decided direction. ### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Actively undermines autonomy at every turn. Uses controlling language throughout: "Maybe you should try," "It would probably help if you," "You might want to try," "It might be good to set a goal." These phrases communicate psychiatrist knows what client should do and is directing her toward it. No acknowledgment of client's right to choose or affirmation of agency. When client explicitly asserts autonomy ("It's not really any of your business"), psychiatrist overrides this boundary directly. Fails to offer genuine choice about session itself. No recognition that change must come from client's own motivation.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Contains virtually no evocation—the heart of MI. Psychiatrist engages in pure advice-giving and problem-solving instead. Never asks: "What brings you in?" "What matters most to you?" "What have you thought about changing?" "What would be different if things were better?" Instead, tells client what problems might be (diet, exercise, stress, sleep) and what to do about them (set goals, make schedule, drink water, take walks). This is installation, not evocation. Only evocative moment ("Do you find it hard to stick with those changes?") is followed immediately by prescriptive advice rather than genuine curiosity. No attempt to understand client's own reasons for change, values, strengths, or resources.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.2/5**

### Strengths:
- Warm, non-hostile tone in opening ("Hello! It's nice to meet you today")
- Initial attempt to normalize session purpose
- Uses some reflective language ("I understand," "I get that"), though undermined by immediate contradiction

### Critical Deficiencies:

This session represents a **fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles** and demonstrates significant deficiencies across all MI domains:

1. **Boundary Violations (HIGHEST PRIORITY):** Repeatedly overrides explicit boundary-setting rather than respecting and exploring it. This is critical failure in autonomy support and acceptance.

2. **Pseudo-Empathy:** Uses empathetic language followed immediately by "but," negating empathy and communicating psychiatrist's agenda matters more than client's perspective.

3. **Premature Problem-Solving:** Jumps to advice-giving without understanding client's actual concerns or what she wants to work on.

4. **Failure to Evoke:** Tells client what to do rather than asking questions to draw out own motivations, values, and ideas. This is installation, not evocation.

5. **Ignoring Disengagement Signals:** Client's minimal responses and repeated boundary-setting are clear signals of disengagement. Psychiatrist persists with same agenda rather than pausing to understand.

6. **Controlling Language:** Repeated use of "should," "would probably help," "might want to" communicates psychiatrist knows what client should do, undermining autonomy support.

7. **Unilateral Agenda-Setting:** No collaborative establishment of session goals or direction.

### Impact on Client Engagement:

This session is **likely to deepen resistance and reduce likelihood of engagement or change**. By repeatedly overriding boundaries, psychiatrist communicates client's autonomy and perspective do not matter. This validates initial defensiveness and reinforces distrust of authority figures. Session creates power struggle rather than collaborative relationship. Client never expresses change talk, and psychiatrist makes no effort to evoke it. Session ends with client reasserting boundary in response to yet another prescriptive suggestion.

### Immediate Recommendations:

1. **Seek formal MI training** (e.g., through MINT—Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers)
2. **Obtain regular supervision** with MI-trained clinician
3. **Prioritize respect for client boundaries** as foundation for all future work
4. **Replace advice-giving with evocative questions**
5. **Build rapport before problem-solving**, especially with resistant clients
6. **Develop tolerance for silence and resistance**
7. **Establish collaborative agenda-setting** before discussing specific behaviors

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation for Change: LOW**

Current approach is unlikely to evoke intrinsic motivation and likely to deepen resistance. For change to occur with resistant adolescents, client must feel respected, understood, and in control of own process. This session provides none of these elements.
