
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a near-complete absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. The psychiatrist opens with a directive statement—"Today, I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do"—which immediately establishes a judgmental, expert-driven stance rather than one of respect and partnership. Throughout the session, the psychiatrist repeatedly uses language that conveys disapproval and criticism: "you need to stop making excuses," "you're not pushing yourself hard enough," "no more slip-ups," and "this is not optional." 

When Marco shares his initial success with walking 20 minutes daily and dietary improvements, the psychiatrist dismisses these efforts as insufficient rather than affirming them as meaningful steps. Marco explicitly states, "I felt great and really proud of myself," yet the psychiatrist responds with "only walking 20 minutes isn't enough"—directly contradicting and minimizing his accomplishment. This is particularly damaging because research shows that affirming prior successes strengthens self-efficacy and motivation. The psychiatrist also uses fear-based language ("you're risking serious health problems like diabetes or heart disease") as a motivational tool, which is inconsistent with MI's emphasis on intrinsic motivation and acceptance.

There is no evidence of warmth, genuine support, or recognition of Marco's worth as a person. The repeated use of "you have to," "you must," and "no excuses" conveys conditional regard—acceptance only if Marco complies with the psychiatrist's directives. This violates the fundamental MI principle that clients are worthy of respect regardless of their choices or compliance.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates authoritarian judgment and conditional regard throughout, with zero instances of genuine affirmation or unconditional positive regard. This directly undermines the therapeutic alliance and MI spirit.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening, understanding the client's perspective, and capturing emotional nuance. This psychiatrist demonstrates minimal effort to understand Marco's experience. When Marco shares that he "hit a wall" and "felt discouraging," the psychiatrist does not reflect this emotional experience or explore what that felt like. Instead, the psychiatrist immediately pivots to criticism: "Why didn't you try more than 20 minutes?"

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist dictates specific targets (30 minutes, five days per week; zero orange juice; water only) without exploring Marco's own priorities, constraints, or preferences. This is directive counseling, not MI-informed direction.

The session does maintain topical focus and doesn't wander, which is a minimal positive. However, the direction is maintained through control and authority rather than collaborative exploration. This distinction is critical: MI direction should feel like mutual problem-solving, not compliance with orders.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains clear focus on target behaviors but does so through directive prescription rather than collaborative exploration. Direction is present but executed in a way that violates MI principles.

# Recommendations

### 1. **Adopt a Collaborative, Partnership-Based Stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Begin every session by explicitly positioning yourself as a collaborative partner, not an expert authority. Use inclusive language that emphasizes shared problem-solving.

**Example:** 
- Instead of: "Today, I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do"
- Say: "I'd like to work together with you to figure out what might help you reach your goals. You know your life, your challenges, and what's worked for you better than anyone. What's on your mind today?"

**Practice:** 
- Record yourself in practice sessions and count how many times you use "you have to," "you must," "you need to." Replace these with "What do you think?" "How might we...?" "What would work for you?"
- Practice saying: "I have some ideas, but I'm curious what you think first" before offering suggestions.
- Role-play with a colleague where you deliberately pause and ask for the client's input before providing direction.

---

### 2. **Affirm and Build on Client Strengths and Prior Success**

**Action:** When clients share accomplishments or efforts, reflect and affirm them before addressing gaps. This strengthens self-efficacy and motivation.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "Only walking 20 minutes isn't enough"
- Say: "So you started walking 20 minutes a day and changed your diet to include more fruits and vegetables. You felt great and proud of yourself—that's real progress. You stuck with it for a while, which shows you can do this. What happened that made it harder to keep going?"

**Practice:**
- Before identifying any deficiency, identify and affirm at least one strength or effort.
- Use reflective statements that capture both the accomplishment and the emotion: "You felt proud of yourself—that sense of accomplishment is important."
- Ask open-ended questions about what worked rather than why they failed: "What was it about those first few weeks that felt so good?"

---

### 3. **Develop Genuine Empathic Reflections, Especially Around Ambivalence**

**Action:** When clients express mixed feelings (e.g., "I love that OJ, but I know it's bad for me"), use double-sided reflections that validate both sides of the ambivalence without judgment.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "You have to cut that out immediately"
- Say: "So part of you really enjoys the orange juice—you love the taste and it's part of your routine. And at the same time, you're recognizing that it's loaded with sugar and might be why you're not losing weight. That's a tough spot to be in."

**Practice:**
- When you hear sustain talk or ambivalence, pause and reflect both sides before moving forward.
- Use phrases like: "On one hand... and on the other hand..." or "So you want to... and you also..."
- Practice complex reflections that capture emotion and meaning, not just facts: "You felt discouraged when the weight stopped coming off, even though you were doing everything right."

---

### 4. **Ask Questions Rather Than Provide Directives; Evoke Rather Than Impose**

**Action:** Replace prescriptive statements with genuine exploratory questions that help clients discover their own reasons for change and their own solutions.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "You need to exercise at least 30 minutes a day, five days a week"
- Say: "You mentioned you were walking 20 minutes a day. What do you think would be a realistic increase for you? What would make it sustainable?"

- Instead of: "Why didn't you stick with it longer?"
- Say: "You felt great at first, but then hit a wall. What was happening when that shift occurred?"

**Practice:**
- Before offering any advice or directive, ask at least one open-ended question: "What do you think...?" "How might you...?" "What would help...?"
- Practice the "ask-tell-ask" method: Ask what the client thinks, share your perspective if relevant, then ask what they think about your input.
- Role-play scenarios where you resist the urge to tell and instead ask questions that help the client arrive at their own conclusions.

---

### 5. **Explore Barriers and Constraints with Curiosity, Not Judgment**

**Action:** When clients mention obstacles (time, energy, difficulty), explore these with genuine curiosity rather than dismissing them as excuses.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "No excuses about time or energy—you have to make it a priority"
- Say: "You mentioned sometimes not having the time or energy for more exercise. Tell me more about that. What's going on with your schedule and energy levels? How might we work around that?"

**Practice:**
- When you hear a barrier, pause and ask: "Help me understand that better" or "What does that look like for you?"
- Brainstorm solutions collaboratively: "What are some ways we could fit exercise into your schedule?" rather than "Here's what you need to do."
- Validate the barrier as real: "That's a genuine constraint. Let's figure out what's realistic for you."

---

### 6. **Use Autonomy-Supportive Language; Emphasize Client Choice and Ownership**

**Action:** Replace controlling language with language that emphasizes the client's freedom to choose and their ownership of the change process.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "You have to cut that out immediately" and "This is not optional"
- Say: "Cutting back on the juice seems like it could make a real difference. What do you think about that? How might you approach it?"

- Instead of: "If you don't follow through, you'll be back to square one"
- Say: "You've shown you can make changes when you're ready. What do you think will help you stick with this plan this time?"

**Practice:**
- Eliminate "have to," "must," "need to," "should" from your vocabulary. Replace with "might," "could," "what if," "what do you think about."
- Use phrases like: "It's entirely up to you," "You're the expert on your life," "What feels right to you?"
- End sessions with: "What are you going to do?" rather than "Here's what you have to do."

---

### 7. **Strategically Evoke and Amplify Change Talk**

**Action:** Listen for client statements about desire, ability, reasons, and commitment to change. Reflect and amplify these to strengthen intrinsic motivation.

**Example:**
- **Client says:** "I felt great and really proud of myself"
- **Evoke and amplify:** "That sense of pride and feeling great—that's powerful. What was it about making those changes that made you feel so good?"

- **Client says:** "I want to lose the weight for good"
- **Evoke and amplify:** "So this time you want it to stick. What's different about now? What makes you want to make this work?"

- **Client says:** "I'm willing to try bumping it up"
- **Evoke and amplify:** "You're willing to push yourself—that takes commitment. What do you think will help you follow through?"

**Practice:**
- In every session, identify at least 3-5 instances of change talk and reflect them back.
- Use follow-up questions to deepen change talk: "Tell me more about that," "What else?" "How would that help?"
- Track your own responses: Do you amplify change talk or minimize it?

---

### 8. **Replace Fear-Based Motivation with Intrinsic Motivation**

**Action:** While health consequences are relevant, avoid using fear and threat as primary motivational tools. Instead, help clients connect change to their own values and reasons.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "If you don't follow through, you're risking serious health problems like diabetes or heart disease"
- Say: "You mentioned wanting to feel better and stay healthy. What does health mean to you? What would it be like to have more energy and feel better in your body?"

**Practice:**
- When discussing health risks, do so factually but briefly, then pivot to the client's own values: "Those are real risks. And more importantly, what matters to you about your health?"
- Ask values-clarification questions: "What do you want your life to look like?" "What's important to you?" "How do you want to feel?"
- Connect behavior change to these values: "So if health and feeling good are important to you, how might cutting back on juice support that?"

---

### 9. **Develop Skill in Handling Sustain Talk Without Confrontation**

**Action:** When clients express reasons for not changing, use reflections, reframing, and strategic pivoting rather than direct confrontation.

**Example:**
- **Client says:** "I love that OJ"
- **Instead of confronting:** "You have to cut that out"
- **Soften and explore:** "You really enjoy it—that makes sense. What is it about the juice that you love? The taste? The routine? The feeling?"
- **Then pivot:** "And you also recognize it's loaded with sugar and might be why you're not losing weight. So we have two things to work with: your enjoyment of juice and your goal to lose weight. What might be a way to honor both?"

**Practice:**
- When you hear sustain talk, pause and reflect it with curiosity, not judgment.
- Use double-sided reflections: "You love the juice AND you want to lose weight. Both are true."
- Ask questions that help the client resolve the ambivalence themselves: "How do you think we might handle this?"
- Avoid arguing against sustain talk; instead, let the client's own change talk do the work.

---

### 10. **Fundamentally Reconsider Your Approach: MI vs. Directive Counseling**

**Action:** Recognize that this session represents directive counseling, not Motivational Interviewing. MI is not simply "being nice" while giving advice. It's a fundamentally different approach based on evoking intrinsic motivation rather than imposing external directives.

**Key distinction:**
- **Directive approach:** "Here's what you need to do. Do it or face consequences."
- **MI approach:** "You want to lose weight and feel better. What do you think might help? What have you tried before? What would work for you?"

**Practice:**
- Read Miller & Rollnick's "Motivational Interviewing: Helping People Change" (3rd edition) to deepen your understanding of MI spirit and principles.
- Seek supervision or consultation with an MI-trained clinician who can observe your sessions and provide feedback.
- Record and review your own sessions, scoring yourself on the 9 MI competency domains. Identify patterns in where you struggle.
- Practice MI-consistent responses in role-plays before using them with clients.
