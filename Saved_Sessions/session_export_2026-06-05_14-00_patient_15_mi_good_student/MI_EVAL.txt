### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance throughout. Early validation is evident when acknowledging Andrei's frustration about waiting time and time constraints. However, acceptance could be deepened by more explicitly validating the legitimacy of his doubt about whether small changes will make a difference, and by more warmly reinforcing his boundary-setting around not scheduling check-ins as a strength.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates strong reflective listening and accurate perspective-taking. Complex reflections capture both content and emotional nuance, such as: "It sounds like there's a real tension between wanting to manage your weight and the demands of your work." The clinician accurately identifies core barriers and shows understanding of Andrei's worldview and values. However, empathy could go deeper by more fully exploring the emotional weight of recognizing physical decline (breathlessness, blood pressure concerns) and the stress of being self-employed without ability to "clock out."

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains clear, strategic focus on weight management and health behavior change with logical session progression: acknowledging barriers, exploring past attempts, identifying the walking-during-calls success, developing a concrete action plan (vibration alert), and establishing follow-up preferences. However, direction could be tighter by more explicitly connecting the vibration alert strategy back to Andrei's blood pressure and health concerns, and by exploring what would move his confidence from 4-5 to higher levels before settling on the strategy.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates strong collaborative stance by positioning Andrei as co-director from the opening question. The clinician builds on Andrei's own ideas rather than imposing solutions, particularly evident in the co-construction of the vibration alert strategy. Power-sharing is genuine throughout. However, collaboration could be strengthened by more explicitly inviting Andrei's input on why the session is happening, what health outcomes matter most to him, and what would feel most supportive in terms of follow-up.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently respects and reinforces Andrei's autonomy and right to choose. The clinician avoids prescriptive language, frames exploration as optional, and genuinely respects his preference for flexible follow-up rather than scheduled check-ins. However, autonomy support could be strengthened by more explicitly acknowledging his right to choose *not* to change, which could paradoxically strengthen intrinsic motivation by removing pressure.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Evocation is present through open questions but lacks depth and consistency. The psychiatrist doesn't sufficiently explore Andrei's intrinsic motivations, personal values, health concerns, or past successes beyond the walking-during-calls example. Critical questions are absent: "What does it mean to you that you're getting out of breath?" "What are you worried might happen?" "What does health mean to you?" "What do you want to be able to do?" When Andrei expresses skepticism, the clinician moves to confidence scaling rather than deeply exploring what would convince him. Opportunities to evoke deeper change talk are missed.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates strong partnership by positioning Andrei as the expert on his own life and co-constructing solutions rather than claiming credit or redirecting. The opening question immediately establishes shared power. The vibration alert strategy exemplifies genuine partnership—Andrei generates the idea and the psychiatrist affirms and builds on it. Power-sharing in follow-up negotiation is evident. However, partnership could be deepened by more explicitly inviting Andrei's perspective on session goals and what would make the conversation worthwhile for him.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Change talk is present but underutilized. Andrei generates several instances ("The weight is something I would like to get hold of," "I'd say my confidence is about a 7"), but the psychiatrist reflects these statements without consistently amplifying or deepening them. Follow-up questions like "Tell me more about that" or "What makes you confident?" are largely absent. When Andrei expresses willingness ("I'm half willing to give something a shot"), the clinician doesn't explore what would move him to full willingness. The psychiatrist doesn't ask "What makes you a 7 and not a 5?" to elicit and amplify reasons for confidence.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk is handled with empathy and validation but lacks strategic redirection. Andrei's resistance statements ("I'm not convinced it'd make much difference," "If work gets too hectic, it could slip") are validated without using techniques like double-sided reflection or reframing to soften them. While the clinician avoids reinforcing sustain talk by agreeing or elaborating on barriers, more active softening could strengthen motivation. For example, double-sided reflection acknowledging both skepticism and past success with walking-during-calls could gently redirect toward change while maintaining empathy.

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall Score: 3.7/5**

### Key Strengths:
- ✓ Strong acceptance and genuine warmth throughout
- ✓ Solid empathy with accurate, complex reflections
- ✓ Genuine collaboration and partnership; Andrei positioned as expert
- ✓ Clear direction with logical session progression
- ✓ Concrete, actionable outcome (vibration alert) emerging from client's ideas
- ✓ Consistent respect for autonomy, including boundary-setting around check-ins

### Areas for Development:
- ⚠ Limited evocation of intrinsic motivation and personal values
- ⚠ Underutilization of change talk amplification techniques
- ⚠ Moderate softening of sustain talk; could use more strategic redirection
- ⚠ Limited exploration of ambivalence and right to choose not to change
- ⚠ Vibration alert strategy not explicitly linked to health outcomes
- ⚠ Scaling questions somewhat expert-driven; could use more open-ended exploration

### Clinical Impact:
This session successfully engages a resistant client and moves him toward a practical action plan. Andrei's confidence increased from 4-5 to 7, suggesting growing engagement. However, the session may not have fully tapped into intrinsic motivation for change. While Andrei agrees to try the vibration alert, this appears compliance-based (fitting his schedule) rather than motivation-based (driven by personal values and health concerns). Risk exists that when work gets hectic, he will abandon the strategy without having developed deeper personal investment in his health. A more evocative approach exploring values, health concerns, and intrinsic reasons for change could create more durable motivation.

### Recommendations for Skill Development:
1. Deepen evocation by asking value-based and concern-based questions
2. Amplify change talk through follow-up questions ("Tell me more," "What makes you confident?")
3. Use double-sided reflection to strategically soften sustain talk
4. Explicitly acknowledge client's right to choose not to change
5. Explore past successes and strengths more thoroughly
6. Link behavior change strategies explicitly back to health outcomes and values
7. Balance scaling questions with more open-ended exploration of readiness
