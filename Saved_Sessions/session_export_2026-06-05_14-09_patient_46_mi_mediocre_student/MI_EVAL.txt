### Acceptance

**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal acceptance and fails to establish unconditional positive regard. While opening is warm, the session quickly shifts to a directive, problem-focused stance with subtle judgment. Repeated prescriptive language ("you should," "it would probably help if") communicates disapproval rather than acceptance of Erik's current perspective. No genuine affirmation of Erik's worth, courage, or willingness to consider change. ### Collaboration

Psychiatrist adopts expert-driven, hierarchical stance throughout with psychiatrist as authority and Erik as passive recipient. Prescriptive language dominates (8+ instances of "you should," "it would probably help if," "you might want to"). Unilateral decision-making evident: psychiatrist decides interventions without genuinely exploring what Erik thinks might work or what he has already tried. When Erik expresses core barrier ("It's hard to get motivated by something you can't see or feel yet"), psychiatrist responds with suggestion rather than collaborative exploration. No shared decision-making occurs. Questions are assessment-focused, not collaborative problem-solving. Final summary recaps psychiatrist-created plan, not jointly-developed approach. This is critical deficiency in MI practice.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively undermines autonomy support throughout session. Repeated prescriptive language ("you should," "would probably help if," "you might want to") communicates that psychiatrist knows what's best and Erik should comply. Never affirms Erik's right to choose or his ownership of change process. No statements like "It's entirely up to you," "You get to decide," or "What feels manageable to you?" When Erik expresses hesitation ("I'm not promising anything yet"), psychiatrist responds with more prescriptions rather than affirming his right to move at own pace. Subtle pressure and control evident throughout. Final statement—"Remember, it's up to you to make the changes"—is token acknowledgment that contradicts entire preceding session of prescriptive direction. Fundamentally violates autonomy support principle.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal evocation of Erik's own motivations, values, and resources. Instead of drawing out Erik's reasons for change, psychiatrist tells Erik why change matters ("It's important to take diabetes seriously") and prescribes solutions. Missed opportunities pervasive: when Erik says "I feel fine...so changing stuff just feels like a hassle," evocative response would explore what would make change feel worth it; instead psychiatrist prescribes. When Erik says drinking is how he unwinds, psychiatrist prescribes cutting back rather than exploring what Erik values about ritual or what he fears losing. When Erik articulates "It's hard to get motivated by something you can't see or feel yet," psychiatrist prescribes videos rather than asking "What would motivate you?" Limited strategic amplification of change talk. Some change talk emerges passively ("I could give that a shot," "I'm willing to try") but psychiatrist fails to deepen or reinforce this language through follow-up questions.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no partnership stance. Positions himself as expert with knowledge and solutions; Erik positioned as passive recipient of advice. Classic medical model, not partnership. Psychiatrist gathers information through assessment questions, then prescribes solutions based on that information. Never acknowledges Erik's expertise about his own life ("You know your life better than I do," "What's your instinct?," "You're the expert on what might work for you?"). When Erik expresses uncertainty, psychiatrist responds with information and prescriptions, not exploration of Erik's wisdom. Never asks "What have you already tried?" or "What's worked for you in the past?" Power imbalance maintained throughout: psychiatrist controls agenda, decides focus, determines action plan. Erik's role is to agree or disagree with psychiatrist's suggestions. No recognition that Erik has expertise about his own life, work schedule, values, barriers, and what might be sustainable. Prescribes generic solutions without exploring what Erik thinks might work. Token acknowledgment at end contradicts entire session approach.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal strategic cultivation of change talk. While some change talk emerges naturally from Erik ("I could give that a shot," "I'm willing to try," "Walking a bit more...doesn't seem too much to ask"), psychiatrist fails to strategically evoke, amplify, or reinforce this language. Missed opportunities: when Erik expresses willingness ("I'm willing to try and think about the benefits more"), psychiatrist could ask "What makes you willing?" or "Tell me more about that" to deepen; instead moves to next prescription. When Erik identifies what feels manageable ("Small goals sound better, something I can actually do"), psychiatrist could explore other doable goals; instead prescribes specific ones. When Erik expresses insight about self-monitoring ("Keeping track might help...so I can actually see it happening"), psychiatrist could explore appeal; instead moves on. When Erik rates confidence 5/5, psychiatrist criticizes rather than asking "What's the 5 about?" or "What would move you to a 6?" Some open questions asked but primarily assessment-focused. Sustain talk not addressed skillfully. Strategic amplification of change talk largely absent.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal skill in softening sustain talk. When Erik expresses reasons against changing or maintaining status quo, psychiatrist either prescribes solutions or fails to address underlying concern. Core sustain talk not effectively addressed: "I feel fine" (repeated reason not to change)—psychiatrist responds with prescriptions rather than exploring this barrier or reframing. "It's a hassle"—psychiatrist prescribes rather than exploring what makes it feel like hassle. "I'm used to eating the way I do"—psychiatrist prescribes healthier snacks rather than exploring what Erik values about current eating or what he fears losing. "It's hard to imagine not having that to look forward to"—psychiatrist prescribes alcohol-free days rather than exploring what Erik values about drinking or what he fears losing. "I'm not promising anything yet"—psychiatrist responds with more prescriptions rather than affirming autonomy or exploring reservations. "I'm not all in, but I'm not totally against it"—psychiatrist criticizes confidence level rather than exploring both sides of ambivalence. Prescriptive responses to sustain talk risk triggering defensiveness and entrenching resistance. No double-sided reflections, reframing, or strategic pivoting employed. Respectful tone prevents score of 1, but psychiatrist does not skillfully soften sustain talk.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.9/5**

**Session Strengths:**
- Maintains warm, respectful, non-hostile tone throughout
- Avoids harsh confrontation or overt criticism
- Maintains clear focus on relevant target behaviors without significant topic drift
- Asks some open-ended questions allowing Erik to express perspective
- Acknowledges real barriers Erik faces (work schedule, habit patterns)
- Provides token acknowledgment of autonomy at session end

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Expert-driven stance dominates:** Fundamentally inconsistent with MI's collaborative, partnership-based approach. Psychiatrist prescribes solutions rather than evoking Erik's own motivations and resources.
- **Minimal evocation:** Tells Erik why change matters rather than drawing out Erik's own reasons for change. Prescribes solutions when Erik articulates insights.
- **Sustain talk not skillfully addressed:** Responds to resistance with prescriptions rather than exploring barriers, reframing concerns, or employing double-sided reflections. Risks entrenching resistance.
- **Autonomy undermined:** Repeated prescriptive language subtly pressures compliance rather than affirming Erik's right to choose and his ownership of change process.
- **Empathy limited:** Restates content without capturing emotional nuance or meaning behind Erik's words. Misses fear of deprivation and loss of identity.
- **Change talk not amplified:** Fails to strategically deepen or reinforce Erik's own language about change through follow-up questions.
- **Acceptance undermined:** Prescriptive language and subtle judgment communicate disapproval rather than unconditional positive regard. No affirmation of Erik's courage or willingness.

**Impact on Client Engagement:**
Erik moves from defensive to cautiously compliant, but language suggests compliance rather than genuine commitment. Rates confidence at 5/5 (midpoint), expresses hesitation ("I'm not promising anything yet"), and frames engagement as experiment ("see how it works out") rather than commitment. Externally-motivated change is less sustainable than intrinsically-motivated change. Core ambivalence ("I feel fine, so why change?") remains unresolved. Low confidence and compliance-based engagement predict poor long-term adherence.

**Likelihood of Sustained Change:** Low to moderate. Erik may attempt prescribed changes short-term due to compliance, but without genuine intrinsic motivation, values clarification, or resolution of ambivalence, sustained change is unlikely.

**Recommended Priority Improvements:**
1. Shift from expert-driven to collaborative partnership stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
2. Evoke rather than prescribe—draw out Erik's own motivations
3. Deepen empathy—capture emotional nuance and meaning
4. Strategically amplify change talk—deepen Erik's own language about change
5. Soften sustain talk skillfully—address barriers without prescribing
6. Affirm autonomy consistently—emphasize Erik's right to choose
7. Explore values and meaning—connect changes to what matters to Erik
8. Use confidence scaling skillfully—explore rather than criticize
9. Normalize ambivalence—validate mixed feelings as healthy
10. Collaborative goal-setting—co-create action plan with Erik

**Training Recommendation:** Engage in MI-specific training or supervision focused on collaborative stance, evocation, and autonomy support. Practice eliminating prescriptive language and replacing with curiosity-driven exploration. Record and review sessions to identify patterns of expert-driven advice-giving and practice alternative MI responses.
