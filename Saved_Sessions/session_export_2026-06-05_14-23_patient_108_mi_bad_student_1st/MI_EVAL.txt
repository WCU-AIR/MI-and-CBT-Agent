### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates a near-complete absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. The opening statement ("I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to improve") establishes an explicitly judgmental, authoritarian framework. Throughout the session, the client's perspective is repeatedly invalidated with statements such as "you're not taking your health seriously enough" and "No, you don't get to decide what makes sense here." ### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no genuine empathy or reflective listening. When the client states that "coffee really helps me stay awake and get things done," this genuine benefit is not acknowledged or explored; instead, the psychiatrist immediately counters with warnings about heart health. ### Direction

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** This session is the antithesis of collaboration. The opening statement explicitly rejects partnership and establishes a hierarchical, expert-driven model. The psychiatrist makes unilateral decisions about the treatment plan, dismisses the client's input ("No, you don't get to decide what makes sense here"), refuses to negotiate or explore alternatives ("There's no room for negotiating or delaying"), and frames the client's perspective as an obstacle rather than a resource. There is no joint problem-solving, no "What do you think?" or "Let's figure this out together." The power dynamic is entirely one-directional: psychiatrist as authority, client as non-compliant subject. This is fundamentally anti-MI.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist actively undermines autonomy support at every turn. The explicit statement "No, you don't get to decide what makes sense here" directly denies the client's right to choose. The demand "You must cut down to one cup tomorrow, no questions asked" removes all choice and frames compliance as mandatory. When the client expresses hesitation, it is reframed as moral failure rather than a legitimate exercise of choice. Threat and fear are used as motivators ("If you keep ignoring this, you'll face serious heart problems and sleepless nights"), which are coercive tactics that undermine autonomy. There is no affirmation of the client's freedom to choose or language supporting self-determination. When the client explicitly requests to be "more involved in figuring out" a plan, this request for autonomy support is not honored.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates no genuine evocation. The session is entirely prescriptive and telling, with no effort to draw out the client's own motivations, concerns, or ideas. When the client spontaneously offers change talk ("I've read that coffee might raise blood pressure"), this is not amplified or explored; instead, it is minimized ("But honestly, you haven't noticed anything really serious yourself"). The psychiatrist never asks open questions designed to evoke the client's perspective—only closed, leading questions that assume the desired answer. The client's own resources and strengths (self-awareness, curiosity, willingness to engage, openness to change) are never acknowledged or built upon. This represents a fundamental failure in MI practice.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist explicitly rejects partnership from the opening statement. The assumption is that the psychiatrist holds all expertise and the client does not. All decisions are made unilaterally: the target, the timeline, and the non-negotiability of the plan. When the client suggests alternatives ("Maybe easing off or figuring out what's really causing jitteriness could work better"), these ideas are dismissed as delay tactics rather than engaged as potentially valuable. Most damning is the failure to respond to the client's direct feedback about feeling unheard and having his perspective dismissed. A true partnership would pause, reflect, and adjust; instead, the psychiatrist appears to continue the same approach. The language throughout is hierarchical ("You must," "You have to," "Are you going to do what you're told or not?"), which is the language of authority, not partnership.

**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates no cultivation of change talk and instead suppresses it. Multiple instances of client change talk are dismissed or minimized rather than amplified: recognition of risk ("I've read that coffee might raise blood pressure"), acknowledgment of symptoms ("I get jittery sometimes"), openness to exploration ("I'm curious about what might happen if I did cut back"), and willingness to engage ("I'm open to talking about it"). Rather than deepening these moments, the psychiatrist dismisses them as avoidance or excuse-making. No new change talk is evoked through questions about values, vision of health, past successes, or confidence in ability to change. By the end of the session, Nathaniel's change talk has actually decreased—his final statement is weaker than his earlier expressions of curiosity and openness. ### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist's approach to sustain talk is entirely confrontational rather than skillfully softening it. When the client expresses reasons for the status quo ("Coffee really helps me stay awake," "It's part of my routine," "I'm not convinced it's urgent"), the psychiatrist directly attacks these statements ("You can't just wait around," "The fact that you're hesitant means you're not taking your health seriously," "You can't just make excuses"). This confrontation is the opposite of softening and typically strengthens resistance rather than weakening it. Techniques such as double-sided reflections, reframing, or strategic redirection are not employed. While the psychiatrist does not actively reinforce sustain talk, the confrontational approach is so heavy-handed that it likely has the same effect—provoking defensiveness and entrenchment. The net result is strengthened rather than softened resistance.

## **Overall Session Score: 1.1/5**

### **Summary:**

This session represents a severe departure from Motivational Interviewing principles and practice. The psychiatrist demonstrates authoritarian, confrontational, and controlling behavior across virtually all MI domains. The fundamental therapeutic stance is expert-driven and non-collaborative, explicitly rejecting the client's autonomy, perspective, and right to participate in decision-making.

**Critical Failures:**
- Systematic rejection of MI spirit (judgment instead of acceptance, prescription instead of evocation, control instead of autonomy support)
- Escalating pressure and threat-based motivation rather than collaborative exploration
- Dismissal of client feedback about feeling unheard
- Suppression of emerging change talk and amplification of resistance

**Likely Outcome:**
This approach will almost certainly *decrease* the client's intrinsic motivation for change and damage the therapeutic relationship. Research consistently demonstrates that confrontational, controlling approaches increase resistance and reduce the likelihood of sustained behavior change. The client entered with some openness; he is likely leaving with more entrenchment and defensiveness.

**Urgent Priority:**
This clinician requires fundamental retraining in MI principles, beginning with a shift in underlying beliefs about the therapist's role and the client's capacity for self-determination. The most essential learning is that telling clients what to do, while sometimes necessary in crisis situations, is ineffective for behavior change in non-urgent contexts. Ambivalence is normal and workable—but only if the therapist responds with curiosity, collaboration, and respect rather than pressure and control.
