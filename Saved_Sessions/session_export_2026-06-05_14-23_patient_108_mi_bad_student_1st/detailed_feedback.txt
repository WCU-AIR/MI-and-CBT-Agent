
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a near-complete absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. The psychiatrist opens with an explicitly directive stance ("I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to improve"), immediately establishing a judgmental, authoritarian framework rather than one grounded in respect for the client's autonomy or worth.

Throughout the session, the psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates Nathaniel's perspective and concerns. When Nathaniel expresses hesitation about cutting to one cup daily, the psychiatrist responds with: "No, you can't just make excuses and delay this" and "The fact that you're hesitant means you're not taking your health seriously enough." These statements are explicitly judgmental—they label the client's ambivalence as moral failure ("not taking your health seriously") rather than a normal, understandable part of the change process. The psychiatrist further escalates with: "No, you don't get to decide what makes sense here" and "You must cut down to one cup tomorrow, no questions asked. If you keep ignoring this, you'll face serious heart problems and sleepless nights."

There is no affirmation of Nathaniel's strengths (his self-awareness about jitteriness, his curiosity about change, his willingness to engage in conversation). There is no normalization of ambivalence as a natural part of contemplation. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge that Nathaniel is showing up, engaging, and expressing genuine concerns—all positive indicators. Instead, the psychiatrist frames the client's reasonable hesitation as defiance and irresponsibility.

The tone is consistently cold, controlling, and dismissive. Phrases like "Are you going to do what you're told or not?" reduce the interaction to compliance vs. defiance, stripping away any sense of the client's inherent worth or dignity.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance or unconditional positive regard. The session is characterized by judgment, invalidation, and explicit disrespect for the client's autonomy and perspective. This directly contradicts MI spirit and severely damages the therapeutic alliance.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective from his frame of reference. This session contains almost no evidence of either.

The psychiatrist does not reflect back Nathaniel's stated concerns or feelings. When Nathaniel says, "coffee really helps me stay awake and get things done," the psychiatrist does not acknowledge this genuine benefit or explore what productivity and alertness mean to him. Instead, the psychiatrist immediately counters with warnings about heart health and sleep, dismissing the client's experience as irrelevant.

When Nathaniel expresses ambivalence—"I'm aware it might not be great for me, but I'm not convinced it's urgent to change right now"—the psychiatrist does not reflect this or explore it. A empathic response might be: "So you can see both sides—you recognize the potential risks, and at the same time, coffee is really working for you right now in terms of getting through your day. That's a real tension." Instead, the psychiatrist responds with pressure and threat: "You can't just wait around and hope things don't get worse."

The psychiatrist makes no effort to understand *why* Nathaniel is hesitant. Is it fear of withdrawal? Concern about productivity loss? Attachment to routine? Skepticism about the severity of risk? None of these are explored. The psychiatrist assumes the client is in denial or being irresponsible, rather than curious about his actual experience and reasoning.

Most tellingly, when Nathaniel explicitly states, "when someone comes in telling me what I have to do without hearing my side or letting me express some of my thoughts, it makes me feel like my perspective doesn't matter," the psychiatrist does not pause, reflect, or adjust. This is a direct communication of the client's emotional experience—a gift in terms of feedback—and it is ignored entirely in the transcript's final exchange.

There are zero complex reflections that capture emotional nuance or deepen insight. There are zero attempts to see the world through Nathaniel's eyes.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no genuine empathy or reflective listening. There is no effort to understand the client's perspective, validate his experience, or explore his reasoning. This is a critical failure in MI practice.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI means maintaining strategic focus on a target behavior while remaining client-centered and collaborative. This session has a clear target (reducing coffee consumption), but the approach is so heavy-handed and non-collaborative that it undermines the purpose.

The psychiatrist does maintain focus on coffee consumption throughout—there is no topic drift. However, the direction is imposed rather than collaboratively established. The psychiatrist decides unilaterally that the target is "one cup per day" without exploring what Nathaniel's actual goals might be, what he sees as problematic, or what would feel achievable to him.

A more skillful approach would involve: "So you're noticing the jitteriness and you've read about blood pressure concerns. What's your sense of what might be a healthy amount for you?" or "If you were to make any change with your coffee, what would feel doable?" Instead, the psychiatrist prescribes a specific target and demands compliance.

The direction also lacks strategic progression. In MI, direction should involve gradually clarifying discrepancy between values (health) and behavior (high coffee intake), evoking the client's own reasons for change, and building commitment. This session jumps immediately to prescription and threat, skipping all the collaborative exploration that would actually move the client toward change.

By the end, the client has become more entrenched in resistance ("Not this minute. But I am open to exploring this more..."), suggesting the directiveness has backfired. The psychiatrist's approach has actually moved *away* from the stated goal rather than toward it.

**Summary:**

While the psychiatrist maintains topical focus on coffee consumption, the direction is imposed rather than collaborative, lacks strategic progression, and has provoked client resistance rather than movement toward change.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have valuable knowledge. This session is the antithesis of collaboration.

The opening statement—"I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to improve"—explicitly rejects collaboration and establishes a hierarchical, expert-driven model. The psychiatrist positions himself as the sole authority on what is "needed" and what the client "must" do.

Throughout the session, the psychiatrist:

- Dismisses the client's input ("No, you don't get to decide what makes sense here")

There is no joint problem-solving. There is no "What do you think?" or "What would work for you?" or "Let's figure this out together." The psychiatrist does not seek the client's input on feasibility, preferences, or concerns. When Nathaniel offers his perspective ("cutting down to just one cup tomorrow sounds pretty sudden and tough for me"), the psychiatrist does not incorporate it—he dismisses it as excuse-making.

The power dynamic is entirely one-directional: psychiatrist as authority, client as non-compliant subject. This is the opposite of MI collaboration.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates zero collaboration. The session is entirely expert-driven and hierarchical, with the psychiatrist explicitly rejecting the client's right to participate in decision-making. This is fundamentally anti-MI.

### 5. Autonomy Support
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Autonomy support means respecting the client's right to choose and actively fostering his perception of agency and self-determination. This session actively *undermines* autonomy at every turn.

The psychiatrist explicitly denies the client's autonomy: "No, you don't get to decide what makes sense here." This is a direct statement that the client's choices do not matter. The psychiatrist further states: "You must cut down to one cup tomorrow, no questions asked," which removes all choice and frames compliance as mandatory.

The psychiatrist handles sustain talk through direct confrontation rather than skillful softening. This approach is likely to strengthen rather than weaken the client's resistance. While not actively reinforcing sustain talk, the technique used is anti-MI and counterproductive.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Fundamentally Reframe the Therapeutic Stance**
- **Action**: Shift from an expert-driven, prescriptive model to a collaborative, client-centered partnership. Begin sessions with genuine curiosity about the client's perspective rather than predetermined solutions.
- **Example**: Instead of "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do," open with: "I'd like to understand your experience with coffee. What's been on your mind about it?"
- **Practice**: Before each session, write down three open-ended questions you could ask to understand the client's perspective. Practice asking these without immediately offering advice. Record yourself and listen for how often you ask vs. tell.

### 2. **Develop Reflective Listening Skills**
- **Action**: Learn to reflect back what the client says before offering any perspective. Use both simple reflections (restating content) and complex reflections (capturing emotion and meaning).
- **Example**: When Nathaniel says "Coffee really helps me stay awake and get things done," reflect: "So coffee is really important to your productivity and how you manage your day. That's meaningful to you."
- **Practice**: In supervision or role-play, practice reflecting for every client statement before responding. Aim for a 2:1 ratio of reflections to advice-giving. Record sessions and count reflections vs. directives.

### 3. **Learn to Recognize and Amplify Change Talk**
- **Action**: Identify moments when the client spontaneously expresses desire, ability, reasons, or need for change. Respond by deepening and exploring these moments rather than dismissing them.
- **Example**: When Nathaniel says "I've read that coffee might raise blood pressure," respond with: "You've been thinking about this and doing some reading. What concerns you most about what you've learned?" rather than "But you haven't noticed anything serious yourself."
- **Practice**: After each session, review the transcript and highlight all instances of change talk. For each one, write down what you said in response and what you *could* have said to amplify it. Practice these amplification responses in role-play.

### 4. **Eliminate Confrontational Language and Pressure Tactics**
- **Action**: Remove directive language ("You must," "You have to," "You need to") and threat-based motivation ("If you don't do this, you'll get heart problems"). Replace with collaborative language ("I wonder if," "What do you think about," "It's up to you").
- **Example**: Instead of "You must cut down to one cup a day, no excuses," try: "Some people find that cutting back gradually works better than a sudden change. What do you think might work for you?"
- **Practice**: Review recordings of your sessions and identify every instance of directive language. For each one, write an alternative MI-consistent response. Practice these alternatives until they feel natural.

### 5. **Develop Genuine Curiosity About Client Resistance**
- **Action**: When a client expresses hesitation or resistance, respond with curiosity rather than pressure. Ask questions to understand the barriers, concerns, and values underlying the resistance.
- **Example**: When Nathaniel says "cutting down to just one cup tomorrow sounds pretty sudden and tough for me," respond with: "Tell me more about that. What makes it feel sudden? What concerns you about making that change?" rather than "No, you can't just make excuses and delay this."
- **Practice**: In role-play, practice responding to resistance with open questions. Aim to ask at least three follow-up questions before offering any perspective. Notice how this deepens understanding and often reveals the client's own reasons for change.

### 6. **Support Autonomy Explicitly**
- **Action**: Regularly affirm the client's right to choose and emphasize that change is ultimately their decision. Use language that emphasizes agency: "It's up to you," "You're the expert on your own life," "What feels right to you?"
- **Example**: Instead of "Are you going to do what you're told or not?" try: "This is ultimately your decision. What feels like a realistic first step for you, if any?"
- **Practice**: In every session, include at least three explicit statements affirming the client's autonomy. Record yourself and listen for how often you use autonomy-supporting language vs. controlling language.

### 7. **Learn to Use Double-Sided Reflections to Address Ambivalence**
- **Action**: When clients express ambivalence (both reasons for and against change), reflect both sides to help them explore the tension. This softens sustain talk without confrontation.
- **Example**: "So you can see both sides—coffee really helps you focus and it's part of your routine, and at the same time you're noticing the jitteriness and you've read about potential health risks. That's a real tension to sit with."
- **Practice**: In role-play, practice reflecting ambivalence without taking sides. Aim to capture both the change talk and sustain talk in a single reflection. Notice how this often helps clients move toward change on their own.

### 8. **Respond to Client Feedback About the Process**
- **Action**: When a client explicitly communicates that they feel unheard, judged, or controlled, pause and respond with genuine reflection and adjustment. This is not a sign of failure—it's an opportunity to repair and strengthen the alliance.
- **Example**: When Nathaniel says "when someone comes in telling me what I have to do without hearing my side or letting me express some of my thoughts, it makes me feel like my perspective doesn't matter," respond with: "I hear you. I haven't been listening as well as I should be. I want to understand your perspective. Tell me what I'm missing."
- **Practice**: In supervision, discuss how to respond when clients give you feedback about your approach. Practice pausing, reflecting, and adjusting rather than defending or continuing the same approach.

### 9. **Receive Training in Motivational Interviewing**
- **Action**: Enroll in a formal MI training program (e.g., through MINT—Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers). This session reveals fundamental gaps in MI knowledge and skill that require structured training.
- **Example**: Attend a 2-day MI workshop, followed by ongoing consultation and feedback on recorded sessions.
- **Practice**: After training, seek regular supervision with an MI-trained supervisor. Record sessions and review them together, focusing on fidelity to MI principles.

### 10. **Examine Underlying Beliefs About Your Role**
- **Action**: Reflect on your beliefs about the therapist's role. Do you believe your job is to tell clients what to do, or to help them find their own motivation for change? This fundamental belief shapes everything you do.
- **Example**: Consider: "What would it mean to trust that this client has the wisdom to make his own decisions about his health? What would change in how I approach this session?"
- **Practice**: In supervision, discuss your beliefs about the therapist's role. Explore how these beliefs align or conflict with MI principles. Consider how your approach might change if you genuinely believed the client had the expertise about his own life.
