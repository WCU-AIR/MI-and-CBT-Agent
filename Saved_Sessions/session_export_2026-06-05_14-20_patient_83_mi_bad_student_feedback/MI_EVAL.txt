### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Responds to patient's sustain talk (arguments for status quo) with direct confrontation rather than empathic exploration. Patient offers sustain talk: peer pressure justification ("Everyone there was doing it"), fear of social consequences ("I don't want to lose my friends or feel like I'm missing out"), difficulty of change ("It's hard to stick to a plan when it's new"), resistance to immediate change ("I'm not sure if just quitting immediately is something I can do right now"). This confrontational approach likely strengthens sustain talk through reactance. Does not use double-sided reflections acknowledging both desire to maintain friendships and desire to protect athletic/academic goals. Does not reframe sustain talk as problem-solving. Patient's increasingly defensive responses ("I'm not making excuses," "I'm open to finding a way that works for me, not just being told what to do") suggest psychiatrist's approach is backfiring. Final exchange shows slight improvement with acknowledgment of complexity ("It sounds like you're really trying to manage a tough situation") and reframing of goal ("balancing your friendships with your goals"), but insufficient to overcome earlier confrontational pattern.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.2/5**

**Session Assessment:** This session demonstrates **poor Motivational Interviewing practice** across nearly all domains. The psychiatrist adopts a confrontational, expert-driven approach fundamentally inconsistent with MI principles and spirit. The clinician repeatedly imposes solutions, dismisses patient perspective, uses prescriptive language, fails to evoke or amplify change talk, and argues against sustain talk—all likely to strengthen reactance and undermine intrinsic motivation for change.

**Strengths Identified:**
- Maintains clear focus on target behavior (alcohol cessation), preventing topic drift
- Expresses genuine concern for patient welfare through repeated emphasis on protecting future and health
- Demonstrates awareness by final exchange that different approach may be needed
- Allows patient to express perspective and ambivalence rather than completely shutting down dialogue

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Imposes solutions rather than evoking patient's own motivations and ideas
- Dismisses patient's legitimate concerns about peer relationships as "excuses" and "complications"
- Uses pervasive prescriptive, controlling language ("you have to," "you must," "you need to")
- Fails to build on multiple instances of patient change talk
- Argues against sustain talk, likely strengthening reactance
- Lacks genuine empathic understanding of patient's identity conflict and developmental stage
- Undermines autonomy through pressure toward immediate abstinence

**Impact on Patient Engagement:** Session is **likely to be counterproductive**. Patient's responses become increasingly defensive as session progresses ("I'm not making excuses," "I'm open to finding a way that works for me, not just being told what to do"), indicating psychiatrist's approach triggers resistance rather than collaboration. Failure to evoke and amplify patient's own change talk—emerging awareness of consequences, concern about athletic performance, desire to be responsible—represents missed opportunity to build intrinsic motivation. Lack of genuine partnership and empathic understanding may damage therapeutic relationship, reducing likelihood of future engagement or authentic treatment participation.

**Recommendations for Improvement (Priority Order):**
1. **Adopt genuinely collaborative stance** — Fundamental shift from expert-driven to partnership model
2. **Develop genuine empathic listening skills** — Reflect emotions, values, and conflicts, not just facts
3. **Evoke rather than impose change talk** — Ask open-ended questions to draw out patient's own reasons for change
4. **Amplify and reinforce change talk** — Reflect, explore, and affirm patient's statements about problem recognition and concern
5. **Soften sustain talk through empathy** — Use double-sided reflections and exploration rather than confrontation
6. **Support autonomy and client ownership** — Replace prescriptive language with language emphasizing choice and agency
7. **Acknowledge and normalize adolescent development** — Demonstrate understanding of identity formation and peer relationship challenges
8. **Use open-ended questions to explore values and goals** — Help patient clarify what matters most and connect behavior to values
9. **Build collaborative problem-solving** — Invite patient input and position self as collaborator, not expert
10. **Repair therapeutic relationship** — In future sessions, explicitly acknowledge shift and rebuild trust through consistent collaboration and empathy

**Likelihood of Evoking Intrinsic Motivation for Change:** **Low**. Confrontational approach creates defensiveness and reactance. Patient's final statement—"I'm open to finding a way that works for me, not just being told what to do"—indicates rejection of imposed approach and seeking more collaborative process. For sustained behavior change, particularly with adolescents, intrinsic motivation is essential. This session undermines intrinsic motivation through external pressure, dismissal of patient values and concerns, and failure to support autonomy.
