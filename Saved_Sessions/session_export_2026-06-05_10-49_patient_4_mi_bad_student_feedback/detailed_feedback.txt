
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1.5/5**

**Explanation:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal acceptance throughout most of the session, with only a brief improvement near the end. In the opening exchanges, the clinician immediately dismisses Aisha's stated agenda ("I'm here because I really want to start working out again") and substitutes their own priority without acknowledgment of her perspective. **Summary:**

The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate unconditional positive regard for most of the session, using judgmental language, blame, and dismissal of the client's stated goals. ### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective. This psychiatrist demonstrates almost none of this. When Aisha clearly states, "I really want to start working out again" and explains that quitting smoking "feels overwhelming," the clinician responds by contradicting her rather than reflecting: "smoking is the bigger problem here" and "you can't just put it off until later."

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no empathic listening or perspective-taking. Client emotions are dismissed rather than reflected, and the clinician repeatedly contradicts Aisha's stated experience rather than seeking to understand it.

### 3. Direction

The clinician maintains unwavering focus on smoking cessation as the target behavior, repeatedly redirecting: "smoking is the bigger problem here," "exercise won't fix the damage smoking causes," "you need to focus on quitting cigarettes immediately," and "you must prioritize quitting smoking completely as soon as possible." A client-centered approach would maintain focus on *her* stated target behavior while exploring how smoking might relate to it. Instead, the psychiatrist dismisses her goal as insufficient and insists on substituting their own.

**Summary:**

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, with both contributing knowledge and perspective. This psychiatrist operates almost entirely from an expert, hierarchical stance.

Evidence of non-collaboration is pervasive: "you really have to stop now," "you need to be honest with yourself," "you have to quit smoking now," "you can't wait to feel better before quitting," "you need to put your health first," "you must prioritize quitting smoking completely," and "there's no room for half-measures here." These are directives, not invitations for joint problem-solving. The language is prescriptive and assumes the clinician knows what is best for the client better than the client knows herself.

When Aisha offers her perspective ("I think starting with exercise is a step forward, not a distraction"), the psychiatrist dismisses it: "exercise won't fix the damage smoking causes." When she expresses her readiness ("I'm not at that place yet where I can quit cold turkey today"), the clinician contradicts her assessment of her own readiness: "you need to commit to quitting now."

True collaboration would involve statements like: "You know yourself best. Help me understand what makes exercise feel more doable right now than quitting smoking?" or "I'm hearing that you're worried about being overwhelmed. What would a plan look like that feels manageable to you?" Instead, the psychiatrist asserts expertise and expects compliance.

The brief moments where the clinician appears collaborative ("Let's work together," "I'm here to support you") are undermined by the simultaneous assertion of the clinician's agenda as non-negotiable. ## Recommendations

### 1. **Adopt a Client-Centered Agenda from the Start**
- **Action:** Begin by asking open-ended questions to understand the client's priorities, concerns, and goals. Follow the client's lead rather than imposing your own agenda.
- **Example:** "You mentioned wanting to start working out again. Tell me more about that. What's drawing you toward exercise right now?" or "I see smoking on your chart, and I also hear that exercise is what brought you in today. Help me understand what feels most important to you right now."
- **Practice:** In your next session, spend the first 10 minutes asking open-ended questions and listening without redirecting. Notice the urge to impose your agenda and practice resisting it. Record yourself and listen for how often you redirect vs. follow the client's lead.

---

### 2. **Develop Genuine Empathic Listening Skills**
- **Action:** Practice reflective listening that captures both content and emotion. When a client expresses fear, overwhelm, or vulnerability, reflect it back before moving to problem-solving.
- **Example:** When Aisha says "I'm scared of failing again," respond with: "It sounds like you've experienced disappointment with quitting before, and you're worried that if you try again and fail, it might be even harder. That makes sense." Then explore: "Tell me more about what happened before."
- **Practice:** In your next three sessions, make it a goal to provide at least three complex reflections per session that capture emotional nuance. Record yourself and count how many reflections you provide vs. how many times you give advice or redirect.

---

### 3. **Eliminate Confrontational Language and Pressure**
- **Action:** Remove phrases like "you have to," "you need to," "you must," "you're just making excuses," "you're setting yourself up for failure," and "there's no room for half-measures." Replace with collaborative language.
- **Example:** Instead of "you're just making excuses," try: "I'm hearing that this feels really overwhelming right now. Help me understand what would need to be different for you to feel ready."
- **Practice:** Before your next session, write down 10 confrontational phrases you commonly use. For each one, write a collaborative alternative. Practice saying the alternatives aloud until they feel natural. In session, pause before speaking and ask yourself: "Is this collaborative or confrontational?"

---

### 4. **Support Client Autonomy and Self-Efficacy**
- **Action:** Explicitly affirm the client's right to choose and their expertise about themselves. Avoid demands for commitment; instead, explore readiness and build confidence.
- **Example:** "You know yourself best. What do you think would be most helpful to focus on first?" or "I'm concerned about your health, and I also respect that you're the expert on what feels manageable for you. What are you willing to commit to?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, count how many times you use directive language ("you have to," "you need to") vs. autonomy-supportive language ("what do you think," "what feels manageable to you"). Aim for a ratio of at least 3:1 autonomy-supportive to directive.

---

### 5. **Amplify Existing Change Talk Rather Than Dismissing It**
- **Action:** When the client expresses motivation for a goal (even if it's not your priority), reflect and amplify it. Explore how this goal might support other changes.
- **Example:** When Aisha says "exercise feels like the one thing that could help me feel better," respond with: "It sounds like exercise is something you feel really ready for and that could help you feel better about yourself in multiple ways. Tell me more about what you're imagining." Then later: "I'm wondering if feeling better about yourself through exercise might also give you the strength and confidence to address smoking down the road."
- **Practice:** In your next session, identify the client's stated change talk and practice amplifying it. Ask follow-up questions that deepen their own motivation. Record yourself and listen for how often you amplify vs. dismiss client motivation.

---

### 6. **Explore Sequencing Collaboratively Rather Than Imposing It**
- **Action:** When a client prioritizes one change over another, explore their reasoning rather than dismissing it. They may have valid insights about what will work for them.
- **Example:** "You're thinking that starting with exercise might help you build confidence for addressing smoking later. Help me understand your thinking there. What makes that sequence feel right to you?" Then: "That makes sense. Let's explore how we can support both goals in a way that feels manageable."
- **Practice:** In your next session with a client who has multiple health concerns, practice exploring their prioritization. Ask: "What feels most important to you right now?" and "Help me understand why that feels like the right place to start." Listen for their reasoning and validate it, even if you would prioritize differently.

---

### 7. **Use Strategic Questioning to Evoke Motivation Rather Than Telling**
- **Action:** Replace statements with open-ended questions designed to draw out the client's own reasons for change, concerns about the status quo, and confidence in their ability to change.
- **Example:** Instead of "smoking is the bigger problem," ask: "I'm hearing that exercise feels really important to you right now. I'm also concerned about smoking. Help me understand how you see these two things connecting?" or "What would it take for you to feel ready to address smoking?"
- **Practice:** Before your next session, write down 5-10 open-ended questions designed to evoke the client's own motivation for change. Practice asking these questions and resisting the urge to answer them yourself. In session, ask at least three evocative questions per client concern.

---

### 8. **Soften Resistance Through Reflection and Reframing Rather Than Confrontation**
- **Action:** When the client expresses reasons against change (sustain talk), use double-sided reflections and strategic reframing rather than direct confrontation.
- **Example:** When Aisha says "I can't imagine how you'd expect me to quit smoking and do everything else at once," respond with: "On one hand, you want to improve your health, and on the other hand, you're worried that trying to do too much at once might overwhelm you and lead to failure. Both of those make sense. What do you think would help you feel less overwhelmed?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, when you hear sustain talk, pause and practice a double-sided reflection before responding. Record yourself and listen for how often you use this technique vs. confrontation.

---

### 9. **Build True Partnership by Sharing Power and Seeking Client Input**
- **Action:** Explicitly invite the client's perspective and expertise. Use inclusive language ("we," "let's") and genuinely incorporate their ideas into the plan.
- **Example:** "I have some concerns about smoking, and I also hear that exercise is what feels most important to you right now. How do you think we should approach this? What would a plan look like that addresses both your priorities and my concerns?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, practice using "we" language and explicitly asking for the client's input on the plan. Count how many times you ask for their perspective vs. tell them what to do. Aim for at least 5 genuine requests for their input per session.

- **Action:** Enroll in a formal MI training program (e.g., through MINT—Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers) or seek supervision from an MI-trained clinician. Practice MI skills through role-play and receive feedback.
- **Example:** Attend a 2-day MI workshop, then participate in ongoing consultation groups where you record sessions and receive feedback from MI-trained supervisors.
- **Practice:** Commit to recording your sessions and reviewing them with an MI-trained supervisor. Identify specific moments where you deviated from MI principles and practice alternative responses.
