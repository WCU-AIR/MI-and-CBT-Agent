# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal unconditional positive regard throughout most of the session. Client's stated agenda (exercise) is immediately dismissed in favor of clinician's priority (smoking cessation). Repeated use of judgmental language including "you're just making excuses," "you need to be honest with yourself," and "if you don't quit smoking now, you're just setting yourself up for failure" communicates disapproval and blame. Only brief moments near the end show genuine acceptance ("I appreciate your honesty and your willingness to work on small steps"), insufficient to offset pervasive tone of conditionality and disapproval. Acceptance is contingent on client compliance with clinician's agenda rather than unconditional.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no empathic listening or perspective-taking. Client's emotional expressions—fear ("I'm scared of failing again"), overwhelm ("it feels like climbing an impossible mountain"), and vulnerability ("I already feel pretty crappy about myself")—are dismissed rather than reflected. No complex reflections capture emotional nuance or deepen insight. Clinician responds to expressions of difficulty by intensifying pressure ("you're risking serious health problems, maybe even life-threatening ones") rather than validating experience. Client's stated reasoning about sequencing (building confidence through exercise first) is reframed as procrastination rather than explored with genuine curiosity. Sustained empathic attunement is absent throughout.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains clear, unwavering focus on smoking cessation as the target behavior, repeatedly redirecting: "smoking is the bigger problem here," "exercise won't fix the damage smoking causes," "you need to focus on quitting cigarettes immediately." However, this direction is imposed rather than collaborative and directly contradicts client's explicitly stated priority (exercise). Client-centered direction would maintain focus on her stated goal while exploring how smoking relates to it. No strategic exploration of why exercise feels more achievable, what would increase readiness for smoking cessation, or how success with exercise might build confidence for addressing smoking. Direction is clinician-driven rather than emerging from collaborative clarification of client's own goals.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist operates almost entirely from expert, hierarchical stance. Pervasive use of directives ("you really have to stop now," "you need to be honest with yourself," "you have to quit smoking now," "you can't wait to feel better before quitting," "you must prioritize quitting smoking completely") rather than invitations for joint problem-solving. When client offers perspective ("I think starting with exercise is a step forward, not a distraction"), clinician dismisses it: "exercise won't fix the damage smoking causes." When client assesses her own readiness ("I'm not at that place yet where I can quit cold turkey today"), clinician contradicts her self-assessment: "you need to commit to quitting now." Brief moments of collaborative language ("Let's work together") are immediately undermined by reassertion of clinician's non-negotiable agenda. True partnership cannot exist when one party insists their priority is correct and dismisses the other's.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively undermines client autonomy throughout session. Repeated demands for commitment on specific timeline ("Are you ready to commit to quitting today?" "Can you promise me you'll stop smoking starting today?" "will you commit to quitting smoking entirely within the next week?") are not invitations to choose but demands for compliance. When client declines, clinician intensifies pressure rather than respecting her choice: "you're just setting yourself up for failure" and "if you don't quit smoking now, you're risking serious health problems." Language consistently minimizes genuine choice: "you really have to," "you need to," "you must," "you can't." When client proposes alternative plan, psychiatrist initially appears to accept but then reasserts control: "you must prioritize quitting smoking completely as soon as possible." Autonomy is presented as conditional—acceptable only if client chooses clinician's prescribed behavior.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no evocation; instead tells client what should motivate her. Client provides abundant change talk about exercise ("I really want to start working out again," "exercise feels like the one thing that could help me feel better," "I'm willing to try thinking about quitting cigarettes, maybe even cutting back a bit"), which clinician dismisses as insufficient rather than amplifying. No open-ended questions designed to evoke client's own reasons for change, past successes, or resources. Clinician fails to explore client's articulated theory about sequencing ("If I start moving again, maybe then I'll have the strength to think about quitting smoking"), instead dismissing it as procrastination. No questions like "What would it mean to you to feel comfortable in your body again?" or "What strengths have you used to make changes in the past?" Clinician attempts to install motivation through fear and pressure rather than drawing out client's existing intrinsic motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role and maintains hierarchical stance throughout, even during brief attempts to shift approach. Language positions clinician as arbiter of truth and client as unreliable: "I want to make sure you understand," "you need to realize," "you have to put your health first," "there's no room for half-measures here." When client offers expertise about what she can manage, clinician contradicts her: "exercise won't fix the damage smoking causes." When client assesses her own readiness, clinician dismisses her self-assessment as denial. No genuine power-sharing; client's contributions do not substantially influence session direction or planning. Brief moments of partnership language ("Let's work together," "I'm here to support you") are undermined by simultaneous assertion that clinician's agenda is correct and client's is wrong. True partnership requires mutual respect for each party's expertise; this is absent.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist suppresses client's existing change talk about exercise and fails to evoke meaningful change talk about smoking cessation. Client provides substantial change talk: "I really want to start working out again," "I'm sick of feeling the way I do," "I don't like the way my clothes fit," "exercise feels like the one thing that could help me feel better," "I used to enjoy walking and sometimes doing yoga," "I'm willing to try thinking about quitting cigarettes, maybe even cutting back a bit." Rather than amplifying this motivation, clinician dismisses it: "exercise won't fix the damage smoking causes," "exercise won't help much," "exercise alone won't save you if you keep smoking." No skillful evocation of smoking cessation change talk occurs. Clinician's pressure-based approach typically generates sustain talk or resistance, not change talk. Skillful cultivation would involve reflecting and amplifying existing change talk and evoking confidence through open-ended questions; instead, clinician uses language that undermines confidence: "if you don't quit smoking now, you're risking serious health problems," "you're just setting yourself up for failure."

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist does not employ MI-specific techniques for handling resistance; instead directly confronts client's sustain talk, which typically strengthens it. Client expresses multiple reasons against quitting smoking now: "thinking about quitting smoking right now just feels overwhelming," "it never really stuck," "it's hard," "I can't imagine how you'd expect me to quit smoking and do everything else at once," "I'm still feeling a bit unsure about committing to quitting entirely within the next week." Clinician's response is direct confrontation: "you're just making excuses," "you need to be honest with yourself," "if you keep making excuses, nothing will change," "you're just procrastinating." Research demonstrates confrontation typically strengthens resistance. Skillful softening would involve acknowledging difficulty, exploring barriers, and reframing; none of these techniques are employed. By session end, client reiterates same resistance: "I'm still feeling a bit unsure about committing to quitting entirely within the next week" and "that feels like a huge deadline that could just make me more anxious." Brief acknowledgment near end ("I appreciate your honesty") represents tone shift but not strategic MI technique for handling resistance.

---

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.2/5**

**Session Impact Assessment:** This session is likely to **decrease** client's intrinsic motivation for change and increase resistance. Confrontational, pressure-based approach generates defensiveness rather than openness. Client enters with strong motivation for exercise and some openness to smoking cessation; she exits with resistance to smoking cessation reinforced and potentially reduced confidence in her exercise goal.

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Dismissal of client's stated agenda without exploration or validation
- Pervasive use of pressure, confrontation, and blame
- Failure to evoke or amplify existing change talk
- Suppression of client autonomy and self-assessment
- Absence of empathic listening and perspective-taking
- Expert-driven rather than collaborative stance throughout

**Strengths:**
- Brief moments of tone shift near end of session
- Eventually agrees to consider client's agenda (exercise first)
- Clear communication of health concerns (though delivery undermines effectiveness)

**Primary Recommendation:** This clinician requires formal Motivational Interviewing training and ongoing supervision from MI-trained clinician. Current practice demonstrates that well-intentioned health concerns, without MI skills, can harm therapeutic alliance and reduce likelihood of behavior change. Immediate focus should be on adopting client-centered approach, eliminating confrontational language, developing empathic listening, and supporting client autonomy.
