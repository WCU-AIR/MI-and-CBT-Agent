# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance, affirming Wiremu's courage and maturity throughout the session. Ambivalence is normalized effectively ("That tension between control and connection is powerful"). However, occasional subtle approval-seeking language ("I'm really impressed") may create implicit pressure toward "right" answers. Most significantly, the psychiatrist does not explicitly affirm Wiremu's inherent worth as a person separate from his behaviors—a critical gap given his stated concern about not being a bad person despite past actions.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4/5
**Comments:** Reflective listening is accurate and demonstrates solid perspective-taking. The psychiatrist captures Wiremu's core conflict between independence and family reunification ("You're really wrestling with the balance..."). However, reflections remain somewhat surface-level and lack emotional depth. The psychiatrist does not fully explore the emotional weight of Wiremu's fear of losing his children, underlying shame about violence, or specific anxiety about feeling "watched" or losing control. Missed opportunities to deepen understanding of vulnerability and the difficulty of progress made.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains clear, appropriate focus on Wiremu's stated goal of reuniting with his children and helps identify concrete pathway steps: conversation with whānau → trial living arrangement → continued program engagement → rebuilding trust. Scaling questions effectively track progress and maintain strategic direction. However, the clinician does not explicitly clarify what "getting my kids back" means in concrete terms (custody? visitation? timeline?). Limited exploration of how alcohol reduction and violence prevention interconnect with the housing goal.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3/5
**Comments:** The session begins collaboratively with genuine agenda-setting ("What would you like to focus on?"), but shifts toward expert-guided stance as it progresses. The psychiatrist affirms Wiremu's ideas but does not genuinely co-construct solutions. Solutions (trial period, writing talking points) are suggested by the clinician and agreed to by Wiremu, rather than emerging from collaborative exploration. Language like "Your idea of a trial period" misattributes the psychiatrist's suggestion to Wiremu. No invitation for Wiremu to challenge or reshape conversation direction. True power-sharing is limited.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3/5
**Comments:** While the psychiatrist acknowledges autonomy in principle, the session subtly pressures Wiremu toward predetermined solutions without explicitly affirming his right to choose differently. Frequent affirmations of the "recommended" path ("shows a strong commitment") imply this is the expected direction. The psychiatrist does not explore what would happen if Wiremu chose not to move in with his whānau or what alternatives might exist. No explicit statement affirming Wiremu's freedom to decide differently. Missed opportunities to genuinely support choice by exploring full range of options.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates basic evocation by asking open questions early ("What has helped you keep moving forward?") and Wiremu articulates strong change talk ("My kids are everything to me," "It's better than the alternative—losing my kids for good"). However, the clinician does not strategically amplify or deepen this change talk through follow-up questions. Solutions are suggested rather than evoked from Wiremu. Limited exploration of values, strengths, or past successes in depth. Ambivalence is validated but not deeply explored to help Wiremu work through competing concerns.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3/5
**Comments:** Early genuine curiosity establishes collaborative tone, but the psychiatrist increasingly assumes expert/guide role as session progresses. Language positioning clinician as evaluator ("I'm really impressed," "You've really thought this through," "That shows a lot of maturity") subtly reinforces hierarchy. The psychiatrist dominates speaking time with long reflections and affirmations, leaving less space for Wiremu's own thinking. Solutions are suggested and affirmed rather than co-constructed. No invitation for Wiremu to reshape session direction or challenge clinician's framing. True power-sharing is limited.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3/5
**Comments:** Wiremu articulates powerful change talk early in the session, but the psychiatrist does not strategically amplify or deepen it. When Wiremu states "My kids are everything to me," the clinician reflects but does not ask follow-up questions ("What does being a good dad mean to you?"). Strategic questioning to evoke desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment is largely absent. Scaling questions are used but not strategically followed with "What would help you move from a 5 to a 6?" Sustain talk is validated but not met with questions that evoke competing change talk. Reliance on reflection and affirmation rather than active cultivation.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist effectively avoids reinforcing sustain talk and does not engage in debate. When Wiremu expresses worry about losing freedom, the clinician uses skillful double-sided reflection, acknowledging both independence concerns and the larger goal of reunification. Ambivalence is validated without amplification ("That mix of readiness and hesitation makes a lot of sense"). However, there are missed opportunities for more strategic double-sided reflections and deeper exploration of underlying reasons for ambivalence. The psychiatrist could ask more about what specifically worries Wiremu about losing control or being watched.

### Summary Assessment

**Strengths:**
- ✓ Collaborative opening with genuine curiosity and agenda-setting
- ✓ Accurate reflective listening demonstrating understanding of Wiremu's perspective
- ✓ Effective normalization of ambivalence without judgment
- ✓ Avoidance of confrontation and debate around sustain talk
- ✓ Clear focus maintained on reunification goal
- ✓ Acknowledgment of concrete progress (reduced drinking, violence program enrollment, reconnection with mother)
- ✓ Identification of practical, manageable next steps

**Areas for Improvement:**
- ✗ Over-reliance on suggesting solutions rather than evoking Wiremu's own ideas
- ✗ Frequent affirmation and praise that may subtly pressure toward predetermined answers
- ✗ Insufficient exploration of values, strengths, and past successes
- ✗ Limited strategic amplification of change talk through follow-up questioning
- ✗ Increasing expert positioning rather than maintaining genuine partnership
- ✗ Unequal speaking time with clinician dominating latter half of session
- ✗ Lack of explicit autonomy support and affirmation of right to choose differently
- ✗ Insufficient clarification of concrete goals, timelines, and success criteria

**Clinical Impact:**
This session will likely increase Wiremu's short-term motivation and engagement, but may undermine long-term autonomy and self-efficacy. Solutions presented as externally suggested rather than self-generated may reduce intrinsic motivation and increase relapse risk. Frequent affirmation may create dynamic where Wiremu feels pressure to maintain confidence to please clinician rather than authentically exploring ambivalence.

**Recommendation:** Shift from solution-installation to genuine evocation; reduce affirmation and increase curiosity; strategically amplify change talk; explicitly support autonomy; explore values and strengths more deeply; maintain balanced partnership throughout session.
