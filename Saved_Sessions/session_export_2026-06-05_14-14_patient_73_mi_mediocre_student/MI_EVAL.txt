### Collaboration

When the client proposes her own plan (visit YMCA, one walk per week), the psychiatrist's response is to suggest modifications ("Maybe you should try to walk more than once a week right away"), communicating that the clinician's ideas warrant consideration alongside the client's own plan. ## Summary of Strengths and Areas for Improvement

### **Strengths:**
- Maintains consistent topical focus on exercise and lifestyle change
- Avoids confrontation and argument with the client
- Eventually respects the client's stated boundaries and validates her approach
- Warm, welcoming opening tone
- Acknowledges the client's high motivation (10/10 rating)

### **Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- **Expert-driven stance:** Repeatedly offers suggestions and solutions rather than evoking the client's own ideas
- **Dismissal of client preferences:** Suggests home workouts multiple times despite clear client rejection, communicating subtle judgment
- **Subtle pressure language:** Uses "maybe you should," "you might want to," and "it would probably help if" repeatedly, creating gentle but persistent pressure
- **Failure to evoke change talk:** Abundant client motivation is not strategically deepened; the psychiatrist moves to problem-solving instead
- **Misuse of scaling question:** Uses high motivation (10/10) to justify pushing harder on the clinician's agenda rather than exploring what drives it
- **Surface-level reflections:** Reflective listening lacks emotional depth and complexity
- **Minimal affirmation:** Client's exceptional self-awareness and realistic goal-setting are not explicitly affirmed
- **Predetermined agenda:** Appears to have fixed ideas about what the client should do and gently pushes this throughout rather than collaboratively exploring options

---

## Recommendations for Clinical Improvement

1. **Shift from expert to collaborative stance** – Treat the client's stated preferences and self-knowledge as expert information about their own life, not obstacles to overcome
2. **Eliminate subtle pressure language** – Replace "should," "might want to," and "probably help if" with genuinely open exploration
3. **Strategically evoke and deepen change talk** – When clients express motivation, pause and explore it rather than moving to problem-solving
4. **Respond to high motivation by exploring, not pushing** – Use high confidence ratings as an opportunity to deepen intrinsic motivation, not justify clinician-driven directives
5. **Use complex, emotionally attuned reflections** – Move beyond simple paraphrasing to capture emotional nuance, values, and deeper meaning
6. **Explicitly affirm client strengths and self-knowledge** – Name instances where the client demonstrates self-awareness or realistic goal-setting
7. **Respect stated preferences without repetition** – When a client rejects an idea, accept it and move on; do not suggest the same intervention multiple times
8. **Use double-sided reflections for ambivalence** – Validate both the client's desire for change and their concerns without pushing either direction
9. **Ask permission before offering suggestions** – Respect autonomy by asking "Would you be open to hearing a thought?" before offering advice
10. **Deepen understanding of MI spirit** – Review core MI principles emphasizing partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation; reflect on how current practice aligns with these principles
