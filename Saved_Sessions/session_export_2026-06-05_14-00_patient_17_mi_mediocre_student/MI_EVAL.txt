### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Profound absence of genuine collaboration. Psychiatrist consistently positions self as expert with solutions and client as someone requiring guidance. Prescriptive language dominates: "Maybe you should try," "It would probably help if," "I think that would probably help you a lot." When client generates own ideas (playing basketball with Sarah, video games), these are not built upon; new clinician-generated ideas are introduced instead. Phrase "What do you think about that?" functions as compliance check rather than genuine partnership. No meaningful power-sharing or co-construction of change process occurs.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session actively undermines autonomy support. Repeated use of prescriptive "should" language and directive framing communicates: "I know what you need to do, and you should do it." Client's own autonomy statements ("I'm pretty sure I could stop if I had to," "I'm down to see how it goes") are not reinforced. No explicit acknowledgment of client's right to choose or self-determination. Implicit message is expert-driven solutions rather than client-centered choice. Cumulative effect violates MI's core principle that client is expert on their own life.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Largely absent. Psychiatrist asks factual information-gathering questions but rarely evokes client's own motivations, values, ideas, or resources. When client identifies stress as trigger, psychiatrist does not explore: "What kinds of stress? What matters to you that's causing this?" When client expresses ambivalence ("vaping is fun too, so that makes it kinda tough"), psychiatrist does not ask open-ended questions to explore both sides. Client's own coping ideas are not built upon. Missed opportunities to deepen intrinsic motivation tied to client's values, particularly basketball commitment.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist assumes expert role throughout with no meaningful power-sharing or co-construction. Opening statements position clinician as guide and idea-generator ("I have some ideas we could cover"). No acknowledgment of client's expertise about own life. When client offers ideas, they are not leveraged; clinician's ideas are introduced instead, implicitly communicating that clinician's thinking is more valuable. No statements affirming client's wisdom or capacity. True partnership would involve: "You know yourself best," "What do you think would work for you?" "You're the expert on what will stick."

## Summary Assessment

**Competency Level:** Below-basic MI competence. Session demonstrates significant departures from motivational interviewing principles and spirit.

**Primary Deficiency:** Prescriptive advice-giving replaces evocation. Psychiatrist repeatedly tells client what to do rather than asking and evoking client's own wisdom and motivation.

**Critical Strengths:**
- Maintains respectful, non-hostile tone
- Demonstrates basic clinical engagement
- Maintains adequate focus on target behavior
- Recognizes basketball as important value

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
1. Shift from prescriptive advice-giving to evocation (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
2. Amplify and reinforce change talk when it occurs (HIGH PRIORITY)
3. Use double-sided reflections to explore ambivalence (HIGH PRIORITY)
4. Respect and reinforce autonomy support (HIGH PRIORITY)
5. Build treatment plans around client's own goals and ideas (HIGH PRIORITY)
6. Deepen empathic understanding through complex reflections (MEDIUM PRIORITY)
7. Gently explore minimization and sustain talk without confrontation (MEDIUM PRIORITY)
8. Establish true collaboration and partnership (MEDIUM PRIORITY)
9. Recognize and affirm client strengths and capacity (MEDIUM PRIORITY)
10. Pursue formal MI training and supervised practice (FOUNDATIONAL)

**Impact on Client Motivation:** While client appears to comply with recommendations, compliance is externally driven rather than intrinsically motivated. Research demonstrates that externally imposed motivation is less effective and less sustainable than intrinsic motivation. Psychiatrist's prescriptive approach may inadvertently undermine client's existing intrinsic motivation (commitment to basketball).

**Recommendation:** Psychiatrist should pursue formal MI training and supervised practice before continuing MI-based interventions. Current approach, while well-intentioned, is inconsistent with evidence-based MI practice and may reduce likelihood of sustained behavior change.
