# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and validates Liora's exhaustion and struggle throughout the session. Warm, affirming language communicates genuine respect for her capacity to endure hardship. However, acceptance could be deepened by more fully validating the adaptive functions of self-harm (emotional regulation, grounding, control) before exploring alternatives. The clinician avoids judgment about self-harm itself, which is appropriate, but misses opportunities to explore what makes cutting feel "real" without immediately pivoting toward solutions.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong reflective listening and perspective-taking are demonstrated throughout. Complex reflections accurately capture nuanced functions of self-harm (e.g., "The physical pain almost grounding or shifting you away from the overwhelming mental noise"). The clinician accurately reflects ambivalence: "I'm hearing a strong desire for change alongside a real uncertainty about how to get there." However, empathy could deepen by more fully exploring the somatic-emotional dimensions of Liora's experience, particularly the body-mind connection when she states "the pain in my body kind of takes over." Missed opportunity to reflect the core emotional meaning behind this shift.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains general focus on exploring coping strategies and readiness for change, which is appropriate for early MI work. However, the session shifts toward solution-focused problem-solving relatively early, after the readiness ruler question, before Liora has fully explored her ambivalence or developed intrinsic motivation. The core change target is never explicitly established with the client—is the goal to reduce self-harm frequency, develop alternatives, or address underlying hopelessness? The session lacks clear strategic direction around this critical issue. Additionally, the psychiatrist doesn't maintain focus on Liora's pervasive hopelessness and belief that "nothing will change," which represents powerful sustain talk that should be a strategic focus.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates some collaborative elements through open-ended questions and inviting Liora's input. However, collaboration is undermined by clinician-driven moves: the readiness ruler is introduced without collaborative discussion; specific coping alternatives (ice, rubber bands) are presented without first exploring what Liora thinks might work; and the psychiatrist maintains a subtle expert stance rather than fully sharing power. True collaboration would involve more explicit power-sharing: "What do you think would be most helpful to focus on?" and "How do you want to approach this?" The psychiatrist doesn't invite Liora to co-create the treatment plan or ask what she thinks would be most helpful.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates some autonomy support through open questions and avoiding direct advice. However, autonomy support is compromised by subtle directiveness: the readiness ruler presupposes that Liora *should* be ready to change; the word "but" in responses contradicts her perspective and redirects toward the clinician's agenda ("but trying something simpler could be a way to start shifting that pattern"); and the psychiatrist never explicitly acknowledges Liora's right to continue self-harming if she chooses. True autonomy support would include: "This is entirely your choice. If you decide cutting is what works for you right now, that's your decision to make." The psychiatrist also fails to explore what Liora values, missing an opportunity to connect change to her own intrinsic values rather than the clinician's agenda.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out the client's own motivations, reasons for change, and resources—is significantly underdeveloped. While the psychiatrist asks some exploratory questions early on, there is minimal strategic effort to draw out Liora's own reasons for change, values, or intrinsic motivation. When Liora produces change talk ("I wish I wasn't always so tired"), the psychiatrist reflects it but immediately shifts focus to barriers rather than deepening the change talk. Core evocative questions are absent: "What would be different in your life if you weren't relying on cutting?" "What matters most to you?" "What would you like your life to look like?" "What do you think you'd be capable of if you had other ways to cope?" The session lacks the depth of evocative questioning necessary to build genuine client-centered motivation for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates partial partnership through reflective listening and open questions but maintains a subtle expert stance through clinician-driven assessment and solution introduction. While the clinician uses inclusive language ("It sounds like," "I'm hearing") and validates Liora's expertise about her own experience, the underlying structure—clinician asks questions, clinician reflects, clinician introduces ideas—maintains an implicit expert position. True partnership would involve more explicit power-sharing and co-creation of treatment direction. The psychiatrist doesn't invite Liora to determine what the focus of their work should be or ask what she thinks would be most helpful, instead assuming the direction (exploring coping alternatives, building readiness) without checking alignment with Liora's priorities.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk cultivation is significantly deficient. While Liora produces change talk ("I wish I wasn't always so tired and maybe didn't feel like I had to do this to get some kind of relief" and "I guess I could try"), the psychiatrist fails to strategically amplify, deepen, or reinforce it. When Liora expresses her wish for relief, the psychiatrist reflects it but immediately pivots to exploring barriers and hopelessness, shifting focus away from the change talk. Missed opportunities to ask: "Tell me more about that wish—what would it feel like?" "How important is it to you to feel less tired?" When she says "I guess I could try," the psychiatrist validates but doesn't strengthen her commitment language. The session becomes dominated by discussion of why change is hard rather than why change matters. No questions are asked about values, meaning, or identity that would elicit powerful change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk is inadequately addressed. Liora produces significant sustain talk ("Cutting is the only thing that helps," "There's no other way," "I don't really believe anything else will help," "Nothing's going to change," "Unless these other things feel real, I don't see how they'd do anything"), which dominates the session. The psychiatrist's response is to validate it and then introduce counter-arguments or alternatives without exploring underlying needs. When Liora says alternatives "sound kind of lame," the psychiatrist uses confrontational language ("but trying something simpler could be a way to start shifting that pattern"), which directly contradicts her perspective and likely strengthens resistance. The psychiatrist never explores what "real" means or why authenticity is so important, missing the opportunity to understand the underlying need for genuine relief. Skillful MI would involve strategic reframing of her skepticism as a strength (high standards for authenticity) rather than allowing sustain talk to dominate without softening.

## Summary of Key Areas for Improvement

✗ **Insufficient evocation of intrinsic motivation** – Core evocative questions about values, meaning, and what matters to Liora are absent  
✗ **Inadequate cultivation of change talk** – Change talk is not strategically amplified or deepened; focus shifts to barriers instead  
✗ **Premature problem-solving** – Coping alternatives introduced before ambivalence is explored or intrinsic motivation established  
✗ **Subtle directiveness undermining autonomy** – Readiness ruler, "but" reframes, and implicit pressure toward change communicate pressure rather than genuine choice  
✗ **Inadequate softening of sustain talk** – Powerful sustain talk dominates without skillful exploration or reframing  
✗ **Lack of explicit direction-setting** – Treatment target and focus never collaboratively determined with Liora  
✗ **Limited exploration of somatic-emotional connection** – Body-mind link in her coping not deeply explored  

---

## Clinical Impact

This session demonstrates competent foundational MI skills but falls short of excellent practice. For a client in precontemplation with powerful sustain talk and hopelessness, the failure to strategically evoke intrinsic motivation and instead move toward problem-solving risks reinforcing her hopelessness and skepticism. Subtle directiveness may activate resistance. The session represents a missed opportunity to build genuine client-centered motivation for change.

---

## Primary Recommendations (Priority Order)

**1. Strategically evoke and amplify change talk** – Develop deliberate strategy to draw out Liora's own reasons for change; deepen change talk rather than pivoting to barriers  
**2. Skillfully soften sustain talk without confrontation** – Explore underlying needs; reframe resistance as strength; eliminate "but" reframes  
**3. Delay problem-solving until intrinsic motivation established** – Follow MI sequence: explore → evoke motivation → problem-solve collaboratively  
**4. Explicitly establish autonomy support** – Communicate clearly that change is client's choice; affirm right to decide, including right to continue self-harming  
**5. Collaboratively establish treatment direction** – Ask Liora what she wants to work on; don't assume the direction
