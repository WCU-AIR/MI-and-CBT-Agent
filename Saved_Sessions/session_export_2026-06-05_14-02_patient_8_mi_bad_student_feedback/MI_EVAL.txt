### Critical Areas for Improvement

**Fundamental Paradigm Shift Required:** This session represents a **complete departure from Motivational Interviewing principles**. The psychiatrist operates from an authoritarian expert model rather than a collaborative partnership model. This approach is **directly contraindicated for evoking intrinsic motivation for behavior change**.

**Key Deficiencies:**
- Complete absence of acceptance, empathy, collaboration, autonomy support, evocation, and partnership
- Repeated directive monologue that ignores patient input and boundary-setting
- Zero reflective listening or attempt to understand patient perspective
- Controlling language that undermines autonomy and choice
- No effort to evoke patient's own motivations or change talk
- Dismissive responses to patient resistance ("It's not that hard if you just make up your mind")
- Failure to adjust approach despite clear signs of disengagement
- Unilateral prescription of quit plan without patient input or collaboration

---

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change

**This session is likely to be counterproductive.** Patient's minimal responses ("It's not really any of your business," "Fine," "Mm-hmm") reflect disengagement and resistance. Psychiatrist's failure to adjust approach in response to clear signals of disconnection suggests patient will leave feeling unheard, disrespected, and **less motivated to change**.

Research on MI consistently demonstrates that directive, confrontational approaches **increase client resistance and decrease intrinsic motivation**. The unilateral quit plan, created without patient input or collaboration, is unlikely to be followed because patient has had no voice in creating it and no sense of partnership with clinician.

---

### Recommendations for Improvement

1. **Fundamental Shift to Collaborative Partnership:** Restructure approach from "I will tell you what to do" to "Let's explore this together." Begin with genuine curiosity about client's perspective, not predetermined directives.

2. **Develop Genuine Reflective Listening Skills:** After each client statement, pause and reflect back what you heard—both content and emotion. This demonstrates empathy and helps client feel understood.

3. **Replace Confrontation with Curiosity:** When encountering resistance or boundary-setting, respond with curiosity rather than persistence. Explore ambivalence: "Help me understand both sides."

4. **Eliminate Minimizing and Blaming Language:** Remove statements that minimize struggle ("It's not that hard") or imply blame ("It's about willpower"). Replace with validation of difficulty and acknowledgment of addiction complexity.

5. **Evoke Rather Than Impose Motivation:** Ask open-ended questions designed to draw out client's own reasons for change. Listen for and amplify client's own change talk.

6. **Respect Autonomy and Emphasize Client Choice:** Explicitly affirm client's right to choose. Use language emphasizing ownership: "This is your decision," "You know yourself best," "What feels right to you?"

7. **Develop Skill in Handling Resistance Without Confrontation:** Use double-sided reflections and strategic reframing rather than pushing harder. Validate difficulty and explore both sides of ambivalence.

8. **Collaborate on Goal-Setting and Treatment Planning:** Never prescribe unilaterally. Explore client's goals, preferences, and previous experiences, then co-create plan together.

9. **Adjust Approach Based on Client Response:** Pay attention to signs of disengagement (minimal responses, boundary-setting, silence) and adjust approach. If client is not engaging, problem is likely your approach, not client's resistance.

10. **Receive Formal MI Training:** This session demonstrates fundamental misalignment with MI principles. Seek formal MI training through accredited program (MINT-certified trainer, university-based course, or clinical workshop). Consider obtaining MI certification.
