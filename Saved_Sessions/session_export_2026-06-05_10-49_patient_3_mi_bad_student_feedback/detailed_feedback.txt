
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a complete absence of unconditional positive regard and fundamental disrespect for the client. There is no attempt to understand her perspective as valid, even if different from the clinician's recommendation. Acceptance requires meeting clients where they are; this session meets Aisha with contempt.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates zero acceptance. Judgmental language, dismissal of legitimate barriers, and repeated accusations of dishonesty and lack of seriousness create a hostile, disrespectful environment fundamentally incompatible with MI spirit.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective. This session contains virtually no reflective listening and active rejection of Aisha's stated experience. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand why quitting cold turkey feels overwhelming; instead, the clinician dismisses this as "making excuses" and "avoiding the truth."

Aisha clearly articulates her barriers: "with a full-time job, night classes, and taking care of my little boy, it's tough to just quit cold turkey" and "going cold turkey this week feels like setting myself up for failure, and that stresses me out even more." A clinician demonstrating empathy would reflect this back: *"So you're juggling a lot right now—work, school, and being a single parent—and you're worried that trying to quit everything at once might actually backfire and stress you out more, making it harder to stick with?"* 

Instead, the psychiatrist responds with: "This isn't about what feels manageable; it's about what's necessary." This is a direct rejection of her experience and an invalidation of her emotional reality. There is no attempt to understand her ambivalence, her competing values (health vs. managing stress), or her realistic assessment of her own capacity.

The psychiatrist also fails to capture emotional nuance. Aisha expresses worry ("I do worry about cancer"), love for her son ("Being a mother, I don't want something to happen to me"), and frustration with being pressured. None of these emotional states are reflected or validated. Complex reflections that deepen insight are entirely absent.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no empathy. There is active rejection of the client's perspective, no reflective listening, and repeated invalidation of her emotional experience and legitimate barriers to change.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI means maintaining strategic focus on a target behavior while remaining client-centered. This session has a clear target (smoking cessation), but the approach is so rigid and coercive that it undermines any collaborative direction-setting.

The psychiatrist does maintain focus on smoking throughout—there is no topic drift. However, the direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. The clinician repeatedly demands a specific outcome (cold turkey cessation this week) without exploring Aisha's own goals, values, or readiness. The psychiatrist states: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you have to do to quit" and "No, that's not good enough. You have to quit completely right now"—language that is prescriptive and authoritarian, not collaborative.

A clinician with appropriate direction would acknowledge the target behavior but explore it with the client: *"So smoking is something you're thinking about. Help me understand—what would quitting look like for you? What feels possible right now?"* Instead, the psychiatrist imposes a single acceptable outcome and rejects all alternatives.

The session does maintain some focus, preventing complete aimlessness, but the rigid, non-collaborative approach means the direction is counterproductive. Aisha becomes increasingly defensive and entrenched in her position (cutting back) rather than exploring deeper motivation for change. Strategic guidance requires flexibility and responsiveness to client readiness; this session has neither.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains focus on smoking but in a rigid, imposed manner that is not client-centered. Direction is prescriptive rather than collaborative, undermining the potential for strategic guidance toward change.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, with both parties' knowledge valued. This session is entirely devoid of collaboration. ## Recommendations

### 1. **Fundamental Shift from Expert to Collaborative Stance** (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
- **Action:** Abandon the "I will tell you what to do" approach entirely. Adopt a genuine collaborative stance where the client's perspective, values, and ideas shape the session direction.
- **Example:** Instead of "I'm going to tell you exactly what you have to do to quit," say: *"I'm glad you came in today. Smoking is something you've been thinking about. I'd like to understand your perspective better. What's your thinking about your smoking right now? What concerns you about it, and what makes it hard to change?"*
- **Practice:** Before your next patient interaction, write out 5 open-ended questions that invite the client to share their perspective without judgment. Practice asking these questions with genuine curiosity, not as a setup for your advice.

---

### 2. **Develop Genuine Empathic Listening and Reflective Responses**
- **Action:** Learn to reflect back what you hear—the client's perspective, emotions, and barriers—without judgment or immediate problem-solving. Use complex reflections that capture both emotion and meaning.
- **Example:** When Aisha says "with a full-time job, night classes, and taking care of my little boy, it's tough to just quit cold turkey," reflect: *"So you're juggling a lot right now—work, school, and being a single parent—and you're worried that trying to quit everything at once might actually backfire and stress you out more, making it harder to stick with. That makes sense."*
- **Practice:** In your next 3 patient interactions, practice making at least 2 reflective statements per patient without immediately following with advice. Record yourself and listen for: (1) Did you accurately capture their perspective? (2) Did you add judgment or interpretation? (3) Did the reflection deepen their thinking or close it down?

---

### 3. **Stop Demanding Binary Answers; Explore Ambivalence Instead**
- **Action:** Replace "Are you going to quit or not? Yes or no?" with open-ended exploration of the client's ambivalence. Acknowledge both sides of their conflict without pressure.
- **Example:** Instead of demanding a yes/no answer, say: *"So part of you is worried about your health and wants to be there for your son. And another part of you feels like quitting cold turkey is too much right now, especially with everything else going on. Both of those things are true for you. Tell me more about each side."*
- **Practice:** In your next patient interaction, when you feel the urge to demand a specific answer, pause and ask an open-ended question instead. Notice what the client shares when given space to explore their own thinking.

---

### 4. **Amplify and Reinforce Client Change Talk; Don't Dismiss It**
- **Action:** When the client offers change talk (even small steps like "I could cut back in the evenings"), treat it as valuable and explore it further. Never dismiss it as "not good enough."
- **Example:** When Aisha says "I could maybe try to cut back in the evenings from six to three," respond: *"That's interesting—you're thinking about cutting back in the evenings. Tell me more about that. What makes you think you could do that? What would help you stick with it?"* Then listen and reflect back what you hear.
- **Practice:** In your next 3 patient interactions, identify at least one piece of change talk per patient and spend 2-3 minutes exploring and amplifying it. Ask follow-up questions like: "What else?" "Tell me more about that." "How would that help?" Notice how the client's motivation deepens when their own ideas are valued.

---

### 5. **Respect Client Autonomy; Acknowledge Their Right to Choose**
- **Action:** Explicitly communicate that the client has the right to make their own decisions about their pace and approach to change. Avoid language that frames their choices as moral failures.
- **Example:** Instead of "You have to quit cold turkey," say: *"You're the expert on your own life. You know what feels doable for you right now. Cutting back in the evenings might be a realistic first step. That's your call to make. What would help you move forward with that?"*
- **Practice:** In your next patient interaction, use the phrase "It's your choice" or "You get to decide" at least once. Notice how the client responds when their autonomy is explicitly respected.

---

### 6. **Ask About Client Values and Connect Them to Change**
- **Action:** Explore what matters most to the client (family, health, independence, etc.) and help them see the connection to the behavior you're discussing. This evokes intrinsic motivation.
- **Example:** *"You mentioned being a mother is really important to you. Tell me about that—what does being a good mother mean to you? How does your health fit into that? What would it mean to be healthier for your son?"*
- **Practice:** In your next 3 patient interactions, ask at least one question about what the client values most. Listen carefully and reflect back the connection you hear between their values and the behavior change you're discussing.

### 7. **Use Double-Sided Reflections to Handle Resistance**
- **Action:** When the client expresses sustain talk or resistance, acknowledge both their concern about change AND their concern about the status quo. This softens resistance without confrontation.
- **Example:** When Aisha says "Going cold turkey feels overwhelming," respond: *"So on one hand, you're worried that quitting all at once might feel overwhelming and set you up for failure. And on the other hand, you do worry about your health and want to be there for your son. Both of those things matter to you. What do you think about that?"*
- **Practice:** In your next patient interaction, when you hear resistance, pause and construct a double-sided reflection before responding. Notice how the client's defensiveness decreases.

### 8. **Eliminate Accusatory Language; Replace with Curiosity**
- **Action:** Stop using language that implies moral judgment ("making excuses," "not being serious," "avoiding the truth"). Replace with genuine curiosity about the client's perspective.
- **Example:** Instead of "Why haven't you stopped already?" (implying you should have), ask: *"What's been in the way for you? What would need to be different for quitting to feel possible?"*
- **Practice:** Review your last 3 patient interactions. Identify any language that could be perceived as judgmental. Rewrite those statements using curious, non-judgmental language. Practice saying the new versions aloud.
