### Collaboration

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is virtually absent. Psychiatrist consistently undermines client's perception of choice and ownership through directive language, subtle pressure, and failure to acknowledge Jamal's right to choose. Prescriptive language masquerades as suggestion: "it would probably help if you tried to cut back," "maybe you should think about," "it would probably help if you had a plan" are soft directives, not genuine choice-offering. True autonomy support would be: "What do you think might help?" or "What feels doable to you?" Clinician ignores explicit autonomy assertion: when Jamal states "I'm not gonna stop just 'cause someone says so," this is direct assertion of autonomy—clinician does not reflect or explore this fundamental need. Framing compliance as only option: repeated focus on "getting it done" reinforces external locus of control rather than building intrinsic motivation. No exploration of ambivalence or choice: Jamal shows implicit recognition of risk ("I don't wanna end up with more crap on my record") but clinician never explores this or invites Jamal to own decision-making. Endorsement of avoidance without exploring alternatives: clinician says "That sounds like a smart approach" without helping Jamal explore whether this is truly what HE wants or whether other options exist.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation—the heart of MI—is essentially absent. Psychiatrist relies heavily on advice-giving and problem-solving rather than drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources. Advice-giving instead of asking: repeatedly tells Jamal what to do rather than asking what Jamal thinks. Examples: "Maybe if we figure out what's stopping you, it would probably help," "Maybe you should think about some ways to handle situations," "It would probably help if you had a plan for that." None are genuine questions designed to evoke Jamal's thinking—all are disguised directives. Missed opportunities to evoke change talk: Jamal provides multiple openings—"I don't wanna end up with more crap on my record," "I'm not about taking risks that'll get me caught again," "I know how to stay low and not mess up"—but clinician moves past without exploration. Skilled evocation would amplify: "Tell me more about why you don't want more on your record. What would that mean for your future?" No exploration of values: clinician never asks about Jamal's values, goals, or what matters beyond legal compliance. What does Jamal want for future? What are his strengths? What does he care about? Never asked. No exploration of ambivalence: Jamal shows implicit ambivalence (wants to use but doesn't want consequences) but clinician never evokes or helps explore this. Closed questions dominate: most questions are closed ("Have you tried to cut back?" "Do you think maybe it would probably help?" "What number would you say?"). Open questions like "What's important to you about staying out of trouble?" or "Tell me what you like about using marijuana" are absent. No curiosity about client's world: clinician never asks about Jamal's life, relationships, school, future plans, or what drives his choices. No genuine curiosity about who Jamal is as person.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership is absent. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role and maintains power imbalance throughout. Expert-driven stance: clinician opens stating what "I thought we could talk about" and what "would probably help"—positioning self as expert with knowledge about what Jamal needs. True partnership would begin with curiosity: "What brought you in today? What's on your mind?" Clinician as problem-solver: throughout session offers solutions ("maybe you should try to cut back," "maybe you should think about some ways," "it would probably help if you had a plan"), positioning psychiatrist as one with answers and Jamal as one needing fixing. Dismissal of client expertise: when Jamal demonstrates knowledge ("I know how to stay low and not mess up," "I kinda just keep my mouth shut and don't hang around with people who get into trouble"), clinician does not build on this or position Jamal as expert on own life—moves to offering more advice instead. Unequal power dynamic: psychiatrist asks questions, Jamal answers; psychiatrist interprets, Jamal receives; psychiatrist summarizes, Jamal agrees. No genuine back-and-forth where Jamal's contributions substantially shape session direction. Clinician controls agenda: sets agenda ("I thought we could talk about your health habits"), redirects when Jamal tries to set own ("I'm more worried about getting this whole stuff with the weed behind me"), maintains control throughout. True partnership would have Jamal's priorities drive session. No co-construction: final summary ("to sum up, you're focused on staying out of trouble mainly by keeping quiet and avoiding certain people") is clinician's interpretation, not collaborative co-construction. True partnership would involve: "So here's what I'm hearing... Is that right? What would you add?"

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Cultivating change talk is essentially absent. Psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no skill in strategically evoking and reinforcing client's own language about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change. Multiple instances of implicit change talk provided by Jamal are not amplified: (1) "I don't wanna end up with more crap on my record" (need/reason for change)—missed opportunity to reflect and deepen; clinician moves to offering advice about cutting back instead. (2) "I'm not about taking risks that'll get me caught again" (commitment/ability)—missed opportunity to explore what Jamal learned; clinician asks about strategies without amplifying. (3) "I know how to stay low and not mess up" (ability/self-efficacy)—missed opportunity to explore how Jamal developed this skill; clinician moves past without exploration. (4) "I'm just trying to do what I gotta do and keep outta trouble" (commitment to compliance)—missed opportunity to explore what "keeping outta trouble" looks like; clinician summarizes without deepening. Instead of cultivating change talk, clinician: asks closed questions eliciting minimal responses, offers advice positioning psychiatrist as source of solutions, fails to use open questions to evoke Jamal's own reasons for change, does not reinforce or amplify change talk when it appears, does not ask about confidence, commitment, or ability in ways that strengthen change talk. Clinician also fails to distinguish between change talk (statements favoring change) and sustain talk (statements favoring status quo). When Jamal says "It's not a lot though" and "It's not like I was gonna sell it," these are sustain talk that should be gently redirected, not reinforced—clinician does not address these at all.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Softening sustain talk is underdeveloped. Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal skill in skillfully handling client arguments for status quo without confrontation. Jamal's sustain talk includes: "It's not a lot though," "It's not like I was gonna sell it or anything," "Yeah, whatever, it's California, no one cares," "I don't really see the point" (of cutting back), "I don't think I need to change." Clinician's responses are generally inadequate. Ignoring sustain talk: when Jamal says "It's not a lot though" and "It's not like I was gonna sell it," clinician does not address these minimizations. Skilled response would use double-sided reflection: "So you're thinking about it in terms of quantity and intent—that it wasn't a large amount and you weren't dealing. I hear that. At the same time, it did result in you being here. What do you make of that?" Missed opportunity with "California, no one cares": this is rationalization/normalization of use—clinician does not address. Skilled response: "It sounds like you're noticing that marijuana use is pretty common where you live, and a lot of people don't see it as a big deal. And at the same time, the legal system does care—which is why you're here. How do you make sense of that?" Reinforcing avoidance as solution: clinician says "That sounds like a smart approach to keep your head down and avoid risky situations," which may inadvertently reinforce sustain talk by validating avoidance as primary solution without exploring whether this addresses underlying issues. Lack of strategic redirection: clinician does not use techniques like amplified reflection (exaggerating sustain talk to create cognitive dissonance), reframing (presenting information in new light), or strategic questioning to gently shift perspective. One partial success: when Jamal says "I don't think I need to change," clinician asks "Have you thought about what might happen if you keep using it the same way?"—attempt to introduce discrepancy, though executed in somewhat confrontational way ("it would probably help to think about that") rather than through genuine curiosity and reflection.

## Summary Assessment

### Strengths
- Basic courtesy and professionalism maintained throughout
- Respectful tone; does not become hostile or judgmental in demeanor
- Acknowledgment of external pressure (court-mandated status)
- One attempt at introducing discrepancy regarding consequences
- Session completion with basic structure provided

### Critical Deficiencies
- **Fundamental MI spirit violation:** Session does not embody MI spirit; expert-driven, directive, focused on clinician's agenda rather than evoking client motivation
- **Repeated prescriptive language:** "It would probably help" appears 8+ times—red flag for non-MI practice
- **Failure to establish collaboration:** Clinician controls agenda, offers solutions, positions Jamal as passive recipient rather than active collaborator
- **Missed change talk opportunities:** Multiple instances of implicit change talk not amplified or explored
- **Inadequate resistance handling:** When Jamal expresses resistance, clinician offers more advice rather than skillfully rolling with resistance
- **Confused direction:** Lacks clear, collaboratively-established target; conflates marijuana use with legal compliance; shifts focus inconsistently
- **Lack of empathic depth:** Reflections are surface-level and factual rather than emotional and exploratory
- **Autonomy undermined:** Repeated prescriptive language and lack of genuine choice-offering reinforce external locus of control

### Impact on Client Engagement
**This session is unlikely to increase intrinsic motivation for change.** Jamal enters already disengaged ("I'm here 'cause I gotta be") and psychiatrist's directive approach does nothing to build genuine engagement or partnership. Session reinforces external locus of control and may increase reactance. Jamal leaves with compliance strategy ("keep my head down") but not genuine motivation for behavioral change. Reliance on external pressure (legal consequences) rather than building internal motivation is less effective for sustained change, particularly with adolescents developmentally prone to reactance.

### Recommendation
**Clinician requires significant MI training and supervision.** Recommend: (1) formal MI training through MINT, (2) regular clinical supervision with MI-trained supervisor, (3) session recording and feedback using MITI scale, (4) elimination of prescriptive language, (5) development of evocation and complex reflection skills, (6) genuine collaboration and autonomy support practices.
