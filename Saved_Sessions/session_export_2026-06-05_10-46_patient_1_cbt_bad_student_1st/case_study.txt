--- DISORDER ---
Acute Stress Disorder

--- CASE STUDY ---

Dylan was a 15-year-old high school student referred to a psychiatrist for stress following a serious automobile accident 2 weeks earlier.
On the day of the accident, Dylan was the front passenger when their car was struck on the driver’s side by an oncoming SUV running a yellow light. The car rolled once before coming to rest. The driver was briefly unconscious and bleeding, and Dylan feared for the lives of his friends. Dylan’s door was jammed, and he feared the car might catch fire. All occupants eventually exited safely and were transported to the ER, then released to their parents.

Since the accident, Dylan had trouble sleeping, often waking at night with heart racing and visualizations of oncoming headlights. He had difficulty concentrating and completing homework. He became anxious when leaving driveways or crossing intersections, refused to practice driving, and displayed irritability toward family and friends. He avoided loud environments, such as leaving a movie theater early, and resisted discussing his stress. A teacher encouraged him to seek psychiatric evaluation after he performed poorly on an exam.

Dylan reported feeling jumpy around loud noises, recurrent distressing images of his injured friend, anger toward the SUV driver, and embarrassment over his reluctance to drive. Past traumatic experiences, including witnessing a younger sister nearly drown and the recent anniversary of his grandfather’s death, contributed to his stress.

Discussion: Dylan meets DSM-5 criteria for Acute Stress Disorder (ASD), with symptoms causing clinically significant distress, impairing social and school functioning, and disrupting developmental tasks such as learning to drive. Prompt intervention can help adolescents manage reminders, cope with distress, and regain academic functioning.
