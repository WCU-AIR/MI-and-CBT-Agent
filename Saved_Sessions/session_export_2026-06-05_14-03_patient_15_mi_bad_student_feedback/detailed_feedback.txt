
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. The psychiatrist opens with a directive, prescriptive stance: *"I want to talk with you about some changes you really need to make for your health. We're going to focus on what you should do to get better."* This immediately frames the interaction as expert-driven rather than collaborative, signaling judgment about what Andrei "needs" to do.

The psychiatrist's repeated opening question—asked verbatim multiple times across the transcript—*"First, do you smoke cigarettes? And if so, why haven't you quit yet? You really need to stop smoking immediately"*—is deeply judgmental. The phrasing "why haven't you quit yet?" contains implicit criticism and assumes failure. There is no acknowledgment of Andrei's frustration about the 45-minute wait, no validation of his time pressures, and no recognition of his legitimate irritation. When Andrei expresses frustration ("I'm kept in your waiting room—for 45 minutes. It's not acceptable"), the psychiatrist completely ignores this concern and proceeds with the agenda unchanged.

You need to commit to a proper exercise routine and a strict diet change."* This is overtly rejecting and devaluing—the opposite of acceptance. The psychiatrist is communicating that Andrei's efforts are inadequate and that his perspective is wrong.

There is no affirmation of Andrei's self-awareness, his acknowledgment of health risks, or his willingness to try anything. No normalization of the struggle between work demands and health priorities. The tone throughout is disapproving and controlling.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance. The session is characterized by judgment, dismissal of client concerns, rejection of client-generated ideas, and a controlling, expert-driven stance that communicates conditional regard based on compliance with the psychiatrist's agenda.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

It's not optional."* This is not empathy—it's dismissal. The psychiatrist is not attempting to understand *why* time is genuinely scarce or what Andrei's actual constraints are. Instead, the psychiatrist is implying that Andrei's time pressure is an excuse or rationalization.

When Andrei articulates his practical reality—*"Food is just fuel... grazing, food on the run"*—the psychiatrist does not reflect this back or explore what this means about his work pace and stress. Instead, the psychiatrist prescribes: *"cut out junk food completely."* There is no curiosity about Andrei's lived experience.

This one adequate reflection does not compensate for the pervasive lack of empathic engagement.

The psychiatrist never explores Andrei's feelings about his health, his fears about heart disease or stroke, his internal conflict between work and health, or what change would actually mean for him. There is no attempt to understand his perspective from the inside.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal empathy. While one basic reflection appears late in the session, the overwhelming pattern is dismissal of Andrei's perspective, failure to explore his experience, and argumentative responses that communicate lack of understanding or interest in his actual situation.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Fundamentally Shift from Expert to Collaborative Stance**
- **Action:** Begin sessions by explicitly inviting the client's perspective and acknowledging their expertise about their own life. Use language that communicates partnership: "I'd like to understand your perspective," "What's your sense of what might help?" "Let's think through this together."
- **Example:** Instead of "I want to talk with you about some changes you really need to make," say: "I appreciate you coming in today. I know you're busy, and I want to make sure we use this time in a way that's actually helpful for you. What brought you in, and what would be most useful to focus on?"
- **Practice:** Record yourself and count how many times you use prescriptive language ("you need," "you must," "you should") vs. collaborative language ("we could explore," "what do you think," "let's consider"). Aim for a 1:3 ratio of prescriptive to collaborative language.

---

### 2. **Validate Client Frustration & Barriers Before Addressing Health Concerns**
- **Action:** When a client expresses frustration (like the 45-minute wait), acknowledge it genuinely and apologize. This demonstrates respect and sets a collaborative tone. Only then move to the health discussion.
- **Example:** "I'm really sorry you had to wait 45 minutes—that's frustrating, and I understand you're pressed for time. I want to make sure we use this time well. What's most important for you to talk about today?"
- **Practice:** In your next three sessions, begin by acknowledging any frustration or barrier the client mentions before introducing your agenda. Notice how this affects the client's openness.

---

### 3. **Actively Listen for and Amplify Change Talk**
- **Action:** Train yourself to recognize change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment, taking steps). When you hear it, pause and explore it with reflections and follow-up questions rather than continuing with your agenda.
- **Example:** When Andrei says "The weight is something I would like to get hold of," respond with: "So you do want to address your weight. Tell me more about what that would mean for you—what would be different if you were able to get it under control?" Then listen and reflect back what you hear.
- **Practice:** In your next session, identify every instance of change talk and write it down. For each one, ask yourself: "Did I amplify this, ignore it, or argue against it?" Aim to amplify at least 80% of change talk you hear.

---

### 4. **Use Double-Sided Reflections to Soften Sustain Talk**
- **Action:** When you hear sustain talk (barriers, reasons not to change), acknowledge both the barrier AND the change talk the client has expressed. This validates without arguing.
- **Example:** When Andrei says "I don't have time to exercise," instead of arguing ("you have to make time"), reflect: "So your work really does demand a lot of your time and attention—that's real. And I also heard you say earlier that you'd like to get your weight under control. How do you see those two things fitting together?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, when you hear sustain talk, pause and construct a double-sided reflection before responding. Practice this with a colleague or supervisor.

---

### 5. **Invite Client-Generated Solutions Rather Than Prescribing**
- **Action:** Ask open questions that invite the client to generate their own ideas for change. When they offer ideas, affirm them and explore them further rather than dismissing them.
- **Example:** Instead of "You need to exercise 30 minutes daily," ask: "What do you think might be realistic for you, given your schedule? What small change could you see yourself trying?" When Andrei suggests swapping snacks, respond: "That's a great idea—you know your schedule better than anyone. How would that work for you?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, count how many times you prescribe vs. how many times you ask open questions. Aim for a 1:5 ratio of prescriptions to open questions.

---

### 6. **Explore Values & Connect Change to What Matters Most**
- **Action:** Ask about what Andrei cares about most (his business, his family, his sense of control). Then explore how weight loss or health improvement might connect to those values.
- **Example:** "It sounds like your work and your business are really important to you—you're committed to doing well and providing. I'm curious: how do you think your health and energy level affect your ability to do that work well? What would be different if you had more energy or felt better physically?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, ask at least one question about what the client cares about most. Listen for values (autonomy, family, achievement, health, security). Then explicitly connect potential changes to those values.

---

### 7. **Support Autonomy by Emphasizing Choice & Client Control**
- **Action:** Use language that emphasizes the client's right to choose and their control over the process. Avoid "must," "have to," "need to." Instead use "could," "might," "you could choose to," "it's up to you."
- **Example:** Instead of "You must start exercising tomorrow," say: "You could try some small movement during your workday if that feels doable. It's completely up to you what you want to try and when."
- **Practice:** In your next session, eliminate prescriptive language. When you catch yourself saying "you need to," pause and rephrase: "You could consider..." or "One option might be..." Notice how the client responds differently.

---

### 8. **Use Affirmations to Recognize Client Strengths & Efforts**
- **Action:** Actively look for and affirm client strengths, efforts, and positive qualities. This builds self-efficacy and demonstrates acceptance.
- **Example:** "I really respect that you're willing to think about this even though you're incredibly busy. That shows you care about your health. And I appreciate your honesty about what's realistic for you—that kind of self-awareness is important."
- **Practice:** In your next session, offer at least three genuine affirmations. Make them specific and tied to observable behavior or qualities.

---

### 9. **Develop Genuine Curiosity About the Client's Perspective**
- **Action:** Approach each session with authentic curiosity about how the client sees their situation, what barriers they face, what they've tried before, and what they think might work. Ask questions you genuinely want to know the answer to.
- **Example:** "I'm curious—you mentioned that food is just fuel for you. What does that look like in a typical day? Walk me through what you're eating and when."
- **Practice:** Before your next session, write down three genuine questions you want to ask the client. During the session, ask these questions and truly listen to the answers rather than planning your response.

---

### 10. **Seek Supervision & Training in Motivational Interviewing**
- **Action:** This session demonstrates significant departures from MI principles. Seek formal MI training (e.g., MINT-certified trainer, MI workshops) and regular clinical supervision focused on MI skill development.
- **Example:** Record your sessions (with consent) and review them with a supervisor, focusing on: (1) How often do you use prescriptive vs. collaborative language? (2) How do you respond to change talk? (3) How do you handle sustain talk? (4) How much do you talk vs. listen?
- **Practice:** Commit to at least 20 hours of formal MI training and monthly supervision focused on MI skill development.
