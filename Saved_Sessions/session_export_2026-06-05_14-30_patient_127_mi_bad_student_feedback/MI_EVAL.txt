## OVERALL CLINICAL SUMMARY

### **Global MI Competency Score: 1.3/5**

**Summary of Findings:**

This session demonstrates a fundamental lack of Motivational Interviewing competence in the majority of the interaction. The psychiatrist relies heavily on directive advice-giving, confrontation of resistance, prescriptive language, and pressure tactics—all of which directly violate MI principles and are actively counterproductive to evoking client motivation for change.

**Critical Observations:**

1. **Non-MI Approach Dominates:** Approximately 75% of the session consists of prescriptive, directive language that violates core MI principles. Statements such as "You really need to stop smoking as soon as possible," "You should just pick one and stick with it," and "No excuses, okay?" are fundamentally non-MI.

2. **Patient Explicitly Reports Harm:** Patient directly states that the psychiatrist's approach causes disengagement: "this kind of pressure just makes me shut down a bit." This is critical feedback indicating the non-MI stance is directly undermining the therapeutic goal.

3. **Brief MI Moments Generate Engagement:** Approximately 25% of the session (fifth intervention onward) demonstrates genuine MI principles—collaborative exploration, evocative questioning, autonomy support, and reflective listening. When these principles are applied, the patient becomes genuinely engaged, articulates his own motivations ("I do care about being there for my family"), and expresses willingness to try alternatives ("I could try a quick walk or some deep breaths").

4. **Inconsistent Application Undermines Effectiveness:** The session alternates between MI and non-MI approaches, creating confusion and undermining the collaborative relationship. The repeated reversions to the directive opening monologue signal to the patient that the psychiatrist does not genuinely value his perspective or autonomy, despite the brief acknowledgment of this in the fifth intervention.

5. **Patient Has Intrinsic Motivation:** The patient demonstrates clear intrinsic motivation for change (family, health, energy) that can be evoked through MI principles but is suppressed by directive, confrontational approaches. This suggests the patient would respond well to genuine MI practice.

### **Strengths Demonstrated:**

- Recognition of error and course correction in fifth intervention
- Brief moments of genuine empathic reflection and collaborative exploration
- Ability to shift toward evocative questioning when consciously attempting MI
- Patient engagement when MI principles are applied

### **Critical Areas for Improvement:**

- **Eliminate prescriptive, directive language** and replace with collaborative, evocative approaches
- **Develop consistent application of MI principles** throughout sessions rather than alternating between MI and non-MI approaches
- **Build skill in evoking patient's own motivations** rather than imposing external reasons for change
- **Replace confrontation of resistance** with double-sided reflections and strategic responses that soften sustain talk
- **Develop genuine acceptance and unconditional positive regard** for patient as person, even while addressing concerning behaviors
- **Seek formal MI training and ongoing supervision** to develop competence in this evidence-based approach

### **Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation for Change:**

**Current Session:** Unlikely to evoke sustained client motivation for change. The directive, pressuring approach suppresses intrinsic motivation and amplifies resistance.

**With MI Training:** High likelihood of effectiveness. The patient's positive response to collaborative, evocative approach in fifth intervention and beyond demonstrates that MI-consistent practice would be effective with this client. Patient has clear intrinsic motivations that can be evoked through proper MI technique.

### **Recommendation:**

This psychiatrist requires formal Motivational Interviewing training (e.g., MINT-certified training) and ongoing clinical supervision with an MI-trained supervisor. While significant gaps in MI competence are evident, the brief moments of skillful MI practice and the patient's positive response to these moments suggest this clinician has potential to develop genuine MI competence with structured training and feedback.
