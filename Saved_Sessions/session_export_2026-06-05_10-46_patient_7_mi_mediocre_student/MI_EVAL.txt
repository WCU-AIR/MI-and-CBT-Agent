## **OVERALL SESSION ASSESSMENT**

### **Overall Score: 2.3/5**

**Session Summary:**

This session demonstrates significant deficiencies in Motivational Interviewing practice. While the psychiatrist maintains topical focus on alcohol use and client's goals, and the client remains engaged, the session is fundamentally characterized by a directive, expert-driven approach that violates core MI principles. The psychiatrist confronts the client, prescribes solutions, minimizes genuine empathy, and fails to evoke the client's own intrinsic motivation for change.

**Strengths Identified:**
- Maintains general focus on target behavior and relationship to client's goals
- Acknowledges client's stated aspirations (college soccer, scholarships)
- Asks some relevant questions about peer pressure and barriers to change
- Client remains cooperative and engaged despite directive approach

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Confrontational and judgmental stance violates MI principles and creates defensiveness
- Excessive advice-giving and prescription positions psychiatrist as expert, client as passive recipient
- Minimal genuine empathy; reflections are basic and factual, missing emotional nuance
- Weak evocation; psychiatrist tells rather than asks; spontaneous change talk not amplified
- Lack of genuine collaboration and partnership; no joint problem-solving
- Insufficient autonomy support; implicit pressure to comply with psychiatrist's agenda
- Sustain talk addressed through confrontation rather than validation

**Impact on Client Engagement and Change:**

This session is unlikely to evoke sustainable, intrinsic motivation for change. While the client generates some change talk and agrees to suggestions, he is doing so because the psychiatrist told him to, not because he has discovered his own compelling reasons for change. Research demonstrates that extrinsic motivation (external pressure) is less sustainable than intrinsic motivation (client's own values). The psychiatrist's prescriptive approach may set the client up for failure and undermine his confidence in his own decision-making capacity.

**Does This Session Embody MI Spirit?**

**No.** While the psychiatrist demonstrates some basic MI knowledge, the overall approach is directive, expert-driven, and inconsistent with MI principles of partnership, evocation, and autonomy support. The implicit message is "I know what you should do" rather than "I'm here to help you discover your own motivation for change."

**Primary Recommendations for Improvement:**

1. **Eliminate confrontation** – Replace challenging language with genuine curiosity
2. **Shift from advice-giving to evocation** – Ask rather than tell; make evocation the default mode
3. **Develop complex, emotionally attuned reflections** – Capture emotional nuance and underlying meaning
4. **Amplify spontaneous change talk** – Pause and explore when client generates motivation language
5. **Establish genuine partnership** – Invite client into problem-solving; co-create solutions
6. **Support autonomy explicitly** – Affirm client's right to choose; explore what feels realistic to him
7. **Use validation and double-sided reflections** – Address sustain talk gently rather than confrontationally
8. **Offer genuine affirmations** – Build confidence by recognizing strengths and positive qualities

**Recommendation for Training:**

This psychiatrist would benefit from intensive MI training, including: (1) comprehensive MI workshop attendance, (2) regular supervision with MI expert, (3) session recording and review to identify directive language, (4) deliberate practice with open-ended questions and complex reflections, and (5) study of MI case examples to deepen understanding of MI spirit and technique.
