# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal acceptance and unconditional positive regard demonstrated. While basic warmth is present in opening ("It's nice to meet you today"), the clinician quickly adopts a directive, problem-focused stance. Repeated use of prescriptive language ("you should," "it would probably help if") appears 6+ times throughout the session, communicating subtle judgment rather than acceptance of Richard's autonomy. Affirmations of effort are delayed until final exchanges and immediately paired with additional directives. Richard's concerns about medication side effects are dismissed rather than validated. Overall, courtesy is present but genuine acceptance of Richard's perspective and autonomy is significantly undermined.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Empathy is superficial and inconsistently applied. Reflective listening—a core MI skill—occurs in only 1-2 instances. When Richard expresses uncertainty about medication efficacy ("will the medicine and all this really make a difference long-term?"), the clinician does not reflect this concern but instead pivots to goal-setting. When Richard rates importance at 7 but minimizes urgency ("it doesn't worry me as much as it probably should"), the clinician does not explore this ambivalence. Missed opportunities to explore emotional experience include: Richard's nervousness about starting medication, his deeper fears about whether change is worth the effort, and what "being active with my wife" means emotionally. Complex, emotionally attuned reflections are absent. Most responses are questions, directives, or reassurance rather than reflections.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The clinician maintains clear, consistent focus on cholesterol management and medication/diet adherence throughout the session, demonstrating adequate direction. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. The clinician introduces "small goals" without first exploring what Richard thinks might help sustain motivation. When Richard mentions his retirement goal, the clinician does not strategically deepen this exploration to build intrinsic motivation. The session lacks strategic sequencing: the clinician moves quickly to planning without fully resolving ambivalence or evoking Richard's own reasons for change. Direction is present and consistent but lacks the strategic, client-centered quality that characterizes strong MI direction.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Collaboration is minimal and superficial. While the clinician asks Richard's opinion in a few instances ("Do you think it's more about not having enough time, or maybe motivation?"), these questions are narrow and directive, guiding Richard toward predetermined conclusions rather than genuinely exploring his perspective. True collaboration is absent: the clinician does not ask "What do you think might help you stick with changes?" before suggesting solutions. When Richard expresses concern about side effects, the clinician provides reassurance and redirects to compliance rather than exploring collaboratively. The clinician decides the diet change (cutting back on fried foods) without asking what change Richard thinks would be most feasible. Language patterns ("you should," "you might want to," "maybe you should try") position the clinician as advisor, not partner. Richard remains in a passive, compliant role throughout.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Autonomy support is significantly undermined through repeated prescriptive language and pressure toward compliance. The phrase "it would probably help if" appears 6+ times, creating subtle but persistent pressure that contradicts autonomy support. The clinician prescribes solutions (small goals, medication, diet change) rather than exploring options and supporting Richard's choice. When Richard expresses uncertainty about medication ("I'm still a bit unsure"), the clinician responds with reassurance and direction rather than acknowledging his right to decide. The clinician does not explicitly affirm that "this is ultimately your decision" or "you get to decide what feels right for you." While the session ends with "We're here to support you," this gesture is rare and overwhelmed by directive language. Richard's freedom to choose and ownership of the change process are not emphasized.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out Richard's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources—is minimal. Initial open questions ("Have you tried changing your diet or exercising before?") create space for Richard to share, but the clinician does not strategically deepen exploration. Critical missed opportunities include: not evoking deeper exploration of Richard's retirement vision and what "being active together" means; not exploring his past success with cutting back on fried foods ("What helped you make that change?"); not evoking his own ideas about sustaining motivation; not exploring his concerns about medication in depth. When Richard mentions his wife and retirement, the clinician does not ask "Tell me more about what that means to you?" The clinician relies on telling and advising rather than asking and exploring. Approximately 1-2 questions are designed to elicit Richard's own thinking; most clinician turns are statements, directives, or reassurance.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Partnership—the understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client—is largely absent. The clinician actively assumes the expert role and positions himself as the authority on what Richard should do. While inclusive language appears in a few moments ("We're here to support you," "Let's plan to touch base again"), these are superficial and do not reflect genuine collaborative stance. The clinician makes decisions for Richard (small goals, medication, diet change, tracking feelings) rather than facilitating Richard's own problem-solving. Richard's ideas, concerns, and preferences do not substantially influence the session direction or plan. When Richard expresses worry about medication efficacy and side effects, these concerns are acknowledged but not allowed to shape the plan. The clinician does not communicate that Richard has wisdom about his own life, barriers, and solutions. Power-sharing is minimal; the clinician maintains expert authority throughout.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** While Richard produces several instances of change talk naturally ("I do want to be around and active with my wife when we retire," "Yeah, I'll take the medicine," "If it can help me stay active and healthy with my wife in retirement, then I'm more open to it"), the clinician does not strategically evoke or amplify it. When Richard mentions his retirement goal, the clinician does not deepen exploration with "Tell me more about what being active together means to you?" When Richard says "Yeah, I'll take the medicine," the clinician does not amplify with "What makes you willing to try it?" The clinician does not ask questions specifically designed to elicit desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment. When Richard expresses mixed feelings ("it doesn't worry me as much as it probably should"), the clinician does not use this as an opportunity to evoke change talk. Approximately 1-2 questions are designed to elicit change talk; most clinician turns are statements or directives. Critical opportunities to deepen Richard's commitment and confidence are missed.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk is adequately managed without confrontation or reinforcement. When Richard expresses minimization ("it doesn't worry me as much as it probably should"), the clinician does not argue or confront, instead accepting the 7 rating and moving forward. When Richard expresses uncertainty about medication efficacy, the clinician provides reassurance rather than arguing. However, the approach lacks sophistication. The clinician does not use double-sided reflections to validate both perspectives of Richard's ambivalence. When Richard says "I just kinda lose steam after a while," the clinician does not reframe this as valuable information about his learning. The clinician does not strategically pivot sustain talk toward change talk through techniques like "What would need to happen for you to feel more concerned about this?" The approach is passive rather than strategic. Sustain talk is handled without creating defensiveness, but opportunities to skillfully transform it into change talk are missed.

### **Overall Session Score: 2.2/5**

## Summary of Key Findings

**Strengths:**
- Clear, consistent focus on cholesterol management
- Avoids confrontation and defensiveness
- Establishes basic warmth and respectful tone
- Provides concrete structure and plan
- Acknowledges client willingness to change

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Prescriptive language dominates** (6+ instances of "you should," "it would probably help if")
- **Minimal reflective listening** (1-2 instances across entire session)
- **Superficial evocation** of Richard's values and motivations
- **Change talk not cultivated or amplified** despite Richard producing it naturally
- **Minimal genuine collaboration** in problem-solving and planning
- **Ambivalence not explored** as valuable information
- **Client expertise not valued** or elicited
- **Autonomy support significantly undermined** through persistent directive language

**Fundamental Issue:**
The clinician adopts an expert, directive stance rather than a collaborative, evocative one. This undermines MI spirit and misses the critical opportunity to help Richard discover and strengthen his own intrinsic motivation for change (being active with his wife in retirement). While Richard expresses compliance, this is based on clinician direction rather than intrinsic motivation, which research shows results in less durable behavior change.

**Recommendation:**
The clinician must fundamentally shift from directive advice-giving to true motivational interviewing by: (1) replacing prescriptive language with open questions, (2) increasing reflective listening to 50%+ of responses, (3) strategically evoking and amplifying change talk, (4) exploring ambivalence deeply, (5) engaging in genuine collaboration, and (6) deeply exploring Richard's core values and intrinsic motivations.
