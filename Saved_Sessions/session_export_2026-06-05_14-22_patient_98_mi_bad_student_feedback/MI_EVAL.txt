### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session actively undermines autonomy at nearly every turn. Repeated use of controlling language: "You have to quit now," "You must be stronger about this," "You have to stop making excuses and just commit," "You have to take control," "You can't wait for everything to be perfect," "You have to commit to cutting down tomorrow. No more delays." Language communicates that patient has no choice—change is mandatory rather than chosen. No acknowledgment of patient's right to choose or affirmation of capacity for self-determination.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1

Psychiatrist provides external reasons for change ("Smoking is really harmful," "It's a serious risk to your health") rather than evoking patient's intrinsic motivations. When patient offers insight ("Maybe if I had something to replace it with?"), psychiatrist does not explore further or ask evocative follow-up questions. Only one brief moment approaching evocation near end ("What have you tried before that you felt helped even a little? What do you think could be a manageable first step for you now?") represents isolated shift from predominant pattern of telling rather than asking.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Patient offers multiple instances of change talk throughout session ("Yeah, it'd be good to cut down," "Maybe if I had something to replace it with? Like, if I had something else to do when I felt like smoking, that could help"), but psychiatrist fails to evoke, amplify, or reinforce any of these. When patient expresses desire for change, psychiatrist immediately shifts to pressure and criticism rather than reflecting, affirming, or building on change talk. Patient's problem-solving language not developed through evocative exploration. External reasons for change provided by psychiatrist rather than patient's own reasons being drawn out. Confidence and ability language not cultivated; instead, psychiatrist implies patient lacks necessary strength ("You need to be stronger about this"). Research shows client-generated change talk far more predictive of behavior change than therapist-provided reasons.

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.3/5**

**Clinical Assessment:** This session represents fundamental departure from motivational interviewing principles and spirit. Psychiatrist assumes expert, authoritarian role relying almost exclusively on directive advice, pressure, and confrontation. Patient's autonomy undermined, concerns dismissed as "excuses," and change talk not evoked or amplified. While psychiatrist maintains focus on target behavior (smoking) and demonstrates one brief moment of collaborative exploration, these minimal strengths vastly outweighed by pervasive pattern of non-MI practice.

**Key Strengths:**
- Maintains consistent focus on smoking as target behavior
- One moment of collaborative exploration near session end
- Patient remains engaged despite non-MI approach

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Fundamental violation of MI spirit through expert-driven, authoritarian stance
- Absence of empathy and acceptance; patient's concerns treated as "excuses"
- Failure to evoke change talk; external reasons imposed rather than patient's intrinsic motivation drawn out
- Confrontational response to sustain talk likely strengthens defensiveness and resistance
- Repeated pressure and coercion undermines autonomy and triggers reactance

**Likelihood of Evoking Intrinsic Motivation:** **Low.** Directive, confrontational approach likely to trigger psychological resistance and strengthen sustain talk. Patient's final statement ("I'm not sure I'm ready to do that just yet. Feels like there's stuff in my life I'd have to fix first") indicates deepened ambivalence rather than movement toward change. Superficial compliance possible but sustained behavior change unlikely without significant changes to clinician approach.

**Immediate Recommendations:**
1. Seek structured MI training with supervised practice and feedback
2. Record and review sessions with trained MI supervisor
3. Shift from expert to collaborative partner role (highest priority)
4. Develop genuine empathic reflections
5. Replace directive language with evocative questions
6. Evoke and amplify patient's own change talk
7. Handle sustain talk skillfully without confrontation
8. Explicitly affirm patient autonomy and right to choose
