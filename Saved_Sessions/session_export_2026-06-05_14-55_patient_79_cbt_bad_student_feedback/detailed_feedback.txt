
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish Structured CBT Framework (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action**: Begin every session with explicit agenda-setting, establish time boundaries, prioritize topics collaboratively, and end with summary and homework assignment
- **Example**: "Emma, I want to make sure we use our time well today. Based on what you've shared, I'm thinking we should focus on three things: (1) understanding the pattern of your mood swings, (2) developing a plan to manage the anxiety without cutting, and (3) identifying what thoughts come up during these episodes. We have 50 minutes. Does that sound good? Is there anything else you'd like to add?"
- **Practice**: Use a session structure template for every session. Write the agenda on a whiteboard or paper. Review it at the end. This becomes automatic with practice.

### 2. **Conduct Immediate Safety Assessment and Planning Around Self-Injury (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action**: Systematically assess the self-injury (frequency, severity, methods, triggers, consequences, intent), establish a safety plan with alternatives, and monitor at each session
- **Example**: "Emma, I want to understand the cutting better so I can help. When you cut, what's happening right before? What are you feeling? What are you thinking? What does the cutting do for you—how do you feel after? Have you ever thought about hurting yourself more seriously? Let's develop a list of things you can do instead of cutting when you feel that urge."
- **Practice**: Use a standardized safety assessment tool. Develop a written safety plan collaboratively. Review it at each session. Assess for escalation.

### 3. **Implement Prospective Daily Symptom Tracking (HIGH PRIORITY)**
- **Action**: Assign systematic tracking of mood, anxiety, irritability, energy, concentration, and urges to self-injure across the menstrual cycle to confirm PMDD diagnosis and establish baseline
- **Example**: "I'd like you to track your symptoms every day for the next two months. Here's a form—rate your mood, anxiety, irritability, energy, and concentration on a 0-10 scale each evening. Also note the date of your period. This will help us see the exact pattern and confirm what's happening."
- **Practice**: Provide a simple tracking form or recommend an app (e.g., Clue, Flo). Review the data at each session. Use it to build the case formulation.

### 4. **Develop a Functional Analysis of Self-Injury (HIGH PRIORITY)**
- **Action**: Systematically explore the antecedents, behaviors, and consequences of self-injury to understand what maintains it and develop targeted alternatives
- **Example**: "Let's map out what happens when you cut. What's the situation? What thoughts go through your head? What feelings are you having? What does the cutting do—does it help you feel better, release pressure, punish yourself, feel something? What happens after? Understanding this will help us find other ways to handle those moments."
- **Practice**: Use a functional analysis worksheet. Explore multiple instances of self-injury. Identify patterns. Use this to guide intervention.

### 5. **Eliminate Excessive Self-Disclosure and Establish Professional Boundaries (HIGH PRIORITY)**
- **Action**: Significantly reduce personal sharing; maintain professional authority; use self-disclosure only when clinically indicated and brief
- **Example**: Instead of "When I was stressed before exams, I'd just try to think positive," say "Many people find it helpful to track what they're thinking during difficult moments. That's something we can work on together."
- **Practice**: Before each session, review your planned responses. Notice when you're about to share a personal story and ask: "Is this clinically necessary? Does it serve Emma's treatment or does it shift focus to me?" If it's not clinically necessary, don't share it.

### 6. **Implement Guided Discovery and Socratic Questioning (HIGH PRIORITY)**
- **Action**: Replace direct advice with skillful questioning that helps Emma reach her own insights and develop problem-solving capacity
- **Example**: Instead of "You should try thinking positive," ask "When you're in the middle of a mood swing and trying to think positive, what happens? What thoughts come up? Can you give me an example of a thought that feels really strong in those moments?"
- **Practice**: For every suggestion you want to make, first ask a question. Practice Socratic questioning in supervision. Record sessions and review your questioning patterns.

### 7. **Develop a Coherent Case Formulation and Treatment Strategy (HIGH PRIORITY)**
- **Action**: Create a written case formulation that explains how Emma's thoughts, feelings, and behaviors interact during the premenstrual phase; use this to guide all interventions
- **Example**: "Based on what you've described, here's what I think is happening: During the week before your period, your brain chemistry shifts, which makes you more sensitive to negative thoughts. You start thinking things like [specific thoughts Emma identifies], which makes you feel anxious and irritable. To manage the anxiety, you withdraw from people and sometimes cut yourself. The cutting temporarily relieves the anxiety, but then you feel worse about yourself, which makes the mood worse. Does that match your experience? If so, our plan is to: (1) identify these thoughts early, (2) develop ways to manage anxiety without cutting, (3) stay connected to people even when you want to withdraw, and (4) track how this helps."
- **Practice**: Write out your case formulation. Share it with Emma. Revise based on her feedback. Use it to guide every intervention.

### 8. **Assign Specific, Monitored Homework at the End of Each Session (HIGH PRIORITY)**
- **Action**: Assign between-session tasks with clear rationale, specific instructions, and monitoring at the next session
