
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Adopt a Collaborative, Partnership-Based Stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Fundamentally shift from expert-driven advice-giving to genuine collaboration. Position yourself as a partner exploring the patient's perspective, not as an expert telling the patient what to do.

**Example:** Instead of:
- "It would probably help if you started with small steps like walking more often or cutting down on salt. Do you think that could work for you?"

Use:
- "You mentioned being busy with work and that your blood pressure is high. I'm curious—what are your thoughts about what might help? What ideas do you have?"

Or when the patient expresses doubt:
- **Patient:** "I don't know if it'd make much difference."
- **Instead of:** "I understand it might seem like a small change won't help much, but even little steps can add up."
- **Use:** "It sounds like you're wondering whether making changes would actually be worth the effort. Tell me more about that. What makes you unsure?"

**Practice:** Before your next MI session, write down 5-10 questions that invite the patient's perspective rather than leading them toward your conclusion. Practice asking these in role-play. Record yourself and listen for how often you're telling vs. asking.

---

### 2. **Develop Genuine Empathy Through Complex Reflections**

**Action:** Move beyond basic factual reflections to complex reflections that capture emotional nuance, ambivalence, and underlying concerns. Reflect not just what the patient said, but what it might mean.

**Example:** When Andrei says "I don't know if it'd make much difference," a basic reflection is:
- "You're not sure if changes would help."

A complex, empathic reflection is:
- "Part of you recognizes your blood pressure is high and that's concerning. At the same time, you're wondering whether the effort of making changes would actually pay off—like maybe it won't make enough difference to be worth it."

Or when he says "I'm just busy with work mostly":
- **Basic:** "Work keeps you busy."
- **Complex:** "It sounds like work is really demanding right now, and fitting in new health habits feels like it might be one more thing on an already full plate."

**Practice:** After each patient statement, pause and ask yourself: "What is the emotion or concern underneath what they just said?" Then reflect that. Practice with a colleague or supervisor using role-play.

---

### 3. **Proactively Explore Alcohol Use with Curiosity (CRITICAL CLINICAL GAP)**

**Action:** Systematically assess alcohol use early in the session, before prescribing solutions. Use open-ended questions to understand the patient's drinking patterns, attitudes, and perceived connection to health.

**Example:** Instead of waiting for the patient to raise it, ask:
- "Tell me about your typical week. How often do you have a drink?"
- "What role does alcohol play in your life? What do you enjoy about it?"
- "Have you noticed any connection between drinking and how you feel—like your energy, sleep, or blood pressure?"
- "What are your thoughts about your drinking? Any concerns?"

Then explore ambivalence:
- "It sounds like you enjoy having a drink to unwind after work, and at the same time, you're wondering if it might be affecting your blood pressure. Tell me more about that."

**Practice:** Review the patient profile before sessions. Identify key health behaviors (alcohol, smoking, diet, exercise) and develop 3-4 open-ended questions for each. Practice asking these naturally in conversation.

---

### 4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk, Especially Past Successes**

**Action:** Ask questions designed to draw out the patient's own motivations, values, and evidence of past capability. When change talk emerges, reinforce it.

**Example:** Andrei mentioned quitting smoking years ago. This is critical change talk that should be explored:
- **Psychiatrist:** "You quit smoking—that's a significant change. Tell me about that. What helped you make that decision?"
- **Patient:** "I just decided it was time. My wife wanted me to."
- **Psychiatrist:** "So your wife's support mattered, and you decided it was time. What was important about that? What made you ready?"

This evokes change talk about values (family), self-efficacy (capability to change), and motivation (internal readiness).

When Andrei mentions his blood pressure concern:
- **Instead of:** "On a scale from 1 to 10, how confident do you feel about making changes?"
- **Use:** "You mentioned your blood pressure is high. What concerns you about that? What would it mean for you if it stayed high?"

This evokes reasons for change and values.

**Practice:** Before each session, identify 2-3 areas where you can ask evocative questions. Write them down. After each patient statement that hints at change talk, pause and ask: "How can I reinforce this? What follow-up question would deepen this?"

---

### 5. **Address Sustain Talk with Curiosity and Double-Sided Reflections, Not Reassurance**

**Action:** When the patient expresses doubt, barriers, or reasons against change, explore these with genuine curiosity rather than contradicting them with reassurance. Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge both sides of ambivalence.

**Example:** When Andrei says "I don't know if it'd make much difference":
- **Avoid:** "I understand it might seem like a small change won't help much, but even little steps can add up." (This contradicts and dismisses.)
- **Use:** "Part of you recognizes your blood pressure is high and that matters. Another part of you isn't convinced that making changes would actually help enough to be worth the effort. That's a real tension. Help me understand both sides. What makes you think changes might not help?"

When he says "I'm just busy with work mostly":
- **Avoid:** Immediately pivoting to advice.
- **Use:** "Work is really demanding right now. When you think about adding exercise or changing your diet, what feels most difficult? What would need to happen to make it feel more doable?"

**Practice:** In role-play, have a colleague express doubt or a barrier. Practice responding with curiosity and double-sided reflections instead of reassurance. Record yourself and listen for how often you're contradicting vs. exploring.

---

### 6. **Support Autonomy by Emphasizing Choice and Client Ownership**

**Action:** Consistently communicate that change is the patient's choice and that you respect his right to decide. Use language that emphasizes his agency and control.

**Example:** Instead of:
- "It would probably help if you tried to walk a bit more or watch your salt intake. Maybe you could start with one of those next week?"

Use:
- "There are lots of things that could help with blood pressure—exercise, diet, reducing alcohol, managing stress. What, if anything, feels like it might be worth trying? What would make sense for you?"

Or:
- "It's completely up to you what you want to focus on. What are your thoughts? What feels most important or doable for you right now?"

When summarizing:
- **Avoid:** "You're thinking about trying to walk more or eat less salt."
- **Use:** "So you're considering trying to walk more if you can find the time. Is that right? What else are you thinking about?"

**Practice:** Before each session, identify 3-5 places where you can emphasize choice and autonomy. Use phrases like: "It's up to you," "What do you think?" "What feels right to you?" "You're the expert on your own life."

---

### 7. **Use Open-Ended Questions to Maintain Curiosity and Avoid Premature Problem-Solving**

**Action:** Replace closed questions and prescriptive statements with open-ended questions that invite exploration. Resist the urge to solve the problem immediately.

**Example:** Instead of:
- "Do you find it hard to keep a regular exercise routine?" (closed)
- "Maybe things like time or motivation get in the way?" (leading)

Use:
- "Tell me about your current routine. What does a typical day look like for you?"
- "What gets in the way of exercising more, from your perspective?"
- "What would need to be different for you to feel like exercise was doable?"

**Practice:** Before each session, write down your planned questions. Count how many are open-ended vs. closed. Aim for at least 70% open-ended. Practice asking these without immediately following up with advice.

---

### 8. **Develop a Systematic Assessment Process Before Prescribing Solutions**

**Action:** Before suggesting any interventions, systematically explore the patient's perspective on the problem, his readiness for change, his ideas about solutions, and his barriers.

**Example:** For blood pressure management:
1. **Explore the problem:** "What concerns, if any, do you have about your blood pressure?"
2. **Assess readiness:** "On a scale from 1 to 10, how important is it to you to address this? What would make it more important?"
3. **Evoke ideas:** "What ideas do you have about what might help?"
4. **Explore barriers:** "What would get in the way of making changes?"
5. **Assess confidence:** "How confident do you feel about making changes?"
6. **Collaborate on solutions:** "Given what you've told me, what feels like it might be worth trying?"

Only after this exploration should you offer suggestions, and frame them as options: "Some people find that... What do you think about that?"

**Practice:** Use this framework in your next 3-5 sessions. Notice how much more information you gather and how much more engaged the patient becomes.

---

### 9. **Avoid Prescriptive Language; Use Collaborative Language Instead**

**Action:** Eliminate phrases like "You should," "It would help if," "You need to," and replace them with collaborative language that invites partnership.

**Example:**

| **Prescriptive (Avoid)** | **Collaborative (Use)** |
|---|---|
| "You should walk more." | "What are your thoughts about adding more movement to your day?" |
| "It would help if you cut down on salt." | "Some people find that reducing salt helps their blood pressure. Does that seem relevant to you?" |
| "You need to make changes." | "What changes, if any, feel important to you?" |
| "Try setting small goals." | "What would feel like a realistic first step for you?" |
| "Keep at it, and we'll check in next time." | "What would be helpful to focus on before we meet again? What feels doable?" |

**Practice:** Record yourself in a session. Listen for prescriptive language. Count how many times you use "should," "need to," "would help if," etc. Set a goal to reduce this by 50% in your next session.

### 10. **Receive Supervision and Training in MI-Specific Techniques**

**Action:** Seek formal training in Motivational Interviewing, ideally through an accredited MI training program. Request regular supervision from an MI-trained clinician who can provide feedback on your sessions.

**Example:** 
- Enroll in a 2-day MI training workshop.
- Record your sessions (with consent) and review them with a supervisor.
- Practice MI techniques in role-play with colleagues.
- Join an MI consultation group where clinicians review each other's work.

**Practice:** Commit to 10 hours of MI-specific training and 5 hours of supervised practice in the next 3 months. This is the most effective way to develop genuine MI competence.
