### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration is essentially absent. The psychiatrist adopts a clear expert role and makes unilateral decisions about what the patient should do. Prescriptive advice is disguised as questions ("Do you think that could work for you?" following directive suggestions). No joint problem-solving; the psychiatrist generates all solutions. Unilateral goal-setting occurs without exploring feasibility or alignment with patient values. When the patient expresses doubt, the psychiatrist dismisses rather than explores collaboratively. The final summary attributes intention to the patient that he never explicitly expressed, imposing a collaborative narrative rather than co-constructing one. Power imbalance is evident throughout.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is fundamentally undermined. Prescriptive language dominates ("It would probably help if you started..."; "Maybe you could start with one of those next week?"). No acknowledgment that ambivalence is valid or that the patient has the right to choose. Implicit pressure communicates that the psychiatrist expects change, subtly pressuring compliance rather than autonomous choice. Barriers (being "busy with work") are dismissed rather than explored as legitimate concerns. The psychiatrist does not emphasize that change is the patient's choice. True autonomy support would communicate: "It's completely up to you. What do *you* think would be helpful?" Instead, the message is: "Here's what you should do."

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation—the core of MI—is almost entirely absent. The psychiatrist rarely asks questions designed to draw out the patient's own motivations, values, or resources. No exploration of what matters most to the patient or how health fits into his priorities. Missed critical opportunity: the patient quit smoking years ago (significant change demonstrating capability), yet this is never explored for past motivation or self-efficacy. The question "Why do you think making some changes could be important for you?" is leading rather than genuinely evocative. No exploration of ambivalence when the patient expresses doubt. The approach is entirely imposition-based rather than evocation-based; the psychiatrist decides what matters and tells the patient.

The agenda is expert-driven; the psychiatrist decides what to discuss without asking what the patient wants to focus on. Problem definition and solutions are unilateral. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of advice, not as a partner in exploration. Andrei's responses are brief and compliant ("Sure," "Maybe," "Okay") because he has no genuine role in shaping the session. No power-sharing; the psychiatrist asks all questions and provides all answers. The patient never asks questions back or challenges the psychiatrist's framing, indicating passive engagement.

After the patient mentions blood pressure concern, the psychiatrist pivots to a confidence scale rather than exploring the concern further. ### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk is not skillfully addressed. The patient expresses multiple concerns: doubt about efficacy ("I don't know if it'd make much difference"), barriers ("I'm just busy with work mostly"), status quo preference ("I just keep things pretty much the same"), and low commitment ("Sure, maybe. If I can find the time"). The psychiatrist's response to doubt is reassurance and contradiction ("even little steps can add up"), which may entrench resistance rather than soften it. When the patient mentions work demands, the psychiatrist acknowledges but does not explore this barrier. Low confidence is met with prescriptive advice rather than curious exploration. Skillful MI would use double-sided reflections and exploration; instead, the psychiatrist uses avoidance through reassurance, which is ineffective in addressing ambivalence.

### Strengths
- Warm, welcoming opening tone establishes basic rapport
- Initial open-ended question ("Is there a particular health or lifestyle issue...?") is appropriate for MI
- Acknowledgment of work as a barrier shows some contextual awareness
- Patient remains engaged and compliant throughout, suggesting no overt defensiveness

### Critical Areas for Improvement

**Fundamental MI Stance:** The psychiatrist operates as an expert dispensing advice rather than as a collaborative partner evoking intrinsic motivation. This is the core deficiency undermining all other MI elements.

**Minimal Empathy and Reflection:** Basic factual reflections are present, but complex reflections capturing emotional nuance, ambivalence, and underlying concerns are absent.

**Failed Evocation:** The psychiatrist rarely asks questions designed to draw out the patient's values, motivations, past successes, or resources. The approach is entirely imposition-based.

**Prescriptive Advice Without Exploration:** Specific behaviors (walk more, cut salt) are suggested without exploring feasibility, meaningfulness, or alignment with patient priorities.

**Missed Critical Clinical Content:** Alcohol use—a key concern for hypertension—is never proactively explored until the patient raises it at the end, representing a significant clinical gap.

**Sustain Talk Not Addressed Skillfully:** The patient's doubts and barriers are met with reassurance and contradiction rather than curious exploration and double-sided reflection.

**Autonomy Undermined:** Prescriptive language and subtle pressure toward compliance undermine the patient's sense of choice and agency.

**No Amplification of Change Talk:** The patient's past success (smoking cessation) and emerging concern (blood pressure) are not explored or reinforced.

---

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change

This session is unlikely to increase Andrei's intrinsic motivation for change. While he remains compliant, his responses are passive and conditional ("Sure, maybe. If I can find the time"). His low confidence (4/10) and expressed doubt ("I don't know if it'd make much difference") remain unresolved. The prescriptive approach may actually decrease intrinsic motivation by positioning change as something the doctor wants rather than something Andrei wants for himself. Without genuine evocation and partnership, Andrei is likely to leave with compliance-based intentions that will not survive competing work demands. True behavior change requires intrinsic motivation, which this session has not cultivated.

---

### Key Recommendations

1. **Adopt a collaborative, partnership-based stance** (HIGHEST PRIORITY): Shift from expert-driven advice-giving to genuine collaboration. Ask "What are your thoughts?" rather than telling the patient what to do.

2. **Develop genuine empathy through complex reflections:** Move beyond factual reflections to capture emotional nuance and ambivalence. Reflect what the patient's statement might mean, not just what was said.

3. **Proactively explore alcohol use with curiosity** (CRITICAL CLINICAL GAP): Systematically assess drinking patterns, attitudes, and perceived connection to health early in the session using open-ended questions.

4. **Evoke and amplify change talk, especially past successes:** Ask about the patient's past smoking cessation to explore capability and past motivation. Reinforce emerging concerns about blood pressure.

5. **Address sustain talk with curiosity and double-sided reflections:** When the patient expresses doubt or barriers, explore with genuine curiosity rather than contradicting with reassurance.

6. **Support autonomy by emphasizing choice:** Consistently communicate that change is the patient's choice. Use language like "It's up to you," "What do you think?" and "What feels right to you?"

7. **Use open-ended questions to maintain curiosity:** Replace closed questions and prescriptive statements with open-ended questions that invite exploration.

8. **Develop a systematic assessment process before prescribing solutions:** Explore the patient's perspective, readiness, ideas, and barriers before suggesting interventions.

9. **Avoid prescriptive language; use collaborative language:** Eliminate "should," "need to," "would help if" and replace with collaborative phrasing that invites partnership.

10. **Receive formal MI training and supervision:** Enroll in an accredited MI training program and request regular supervision from an MI-trained clinician to develop genuine competence.

---

### Conclusion

This session represents **significant deficiency in Motivational Interviewing practice**. The psychiatrist operates as a traditional expert dispensing advice rather than as a collaborative partner evoking the patient's own motivation for change. While warm and maintaining patient compliance, the session lacks core MI elements: genuine partnership, empathy, evocation, autonomy support, and skillful handling of ambivalence. The psychiatrist fails to explore the patient's perspective, values, past successes, and reasons for change—the very factors that generate sustainable motivation. Without fundamental shifts in stance and technique, particularly toward genuine collaboration and evocation, the psychiatrist's sessions are unlikely to evoke sustainable behavior change.
