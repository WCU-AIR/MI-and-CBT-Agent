
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist opened with a vague, non-specific invitation: "I thought maybe we could talk a bit about what's been on your mind lately and see if we can figure out some ways to feel a bit better. Does that sound okay?" This is not agenda-setting in the CBT sense. The therapist never did this.

The therapist never helped prioritize these or established which would be the focus. By the end of the first session, no explicit agenda had been set for subsequent sessions either. The second session opening repeats this same pattern verbatim, suggesting no learning or structure was established.

This lack of agenda-setting is particularly problematic for a 65-year-old hospitalized patient with adjustment disorder who is experiencing multiple concurrent stressors. Clear prioritization would help her feel less overwhelmed and provide direction. Without it, the session feels reactive rather than strategic.

**Summary:**

The therapist failed to establish a collaborative, prioritized agenda in either session. Multiple presenting problems were identified but never organized or focused, leaving the session direction unclear and potentially overwhelming for the patient.

## Recommendations

### 1. Develop an Explicit Case Conceptualization and Treatment Strategy
- **Action**: Before the next session, write out a one-page case formulation that includes: (a) the stressor (serious illness, medical uncertainty), (b) the patient's core thoughts (catastrophic predictions, identity threat), (c) her emotional responses (anxiety, sadness, guilt), (d) her behavioral patterns (avoidance, reassurance-seeking, rumination), and (e) how these maintain the adjustment disorder. Then develop a 4-6 session treatment plan with specific goals and sequenced interventions.
- **Example**: "Gabriela's catastrophic thoughts about cancer progression trigger anxiety, which leads her to avoid activities and seek reassurance. This avoidance maintains her depression and prevents her from reconnecting with valued activities. Additionally, her identity as 'the rock' is threatened by her illness, creating guilt and shame. Treatment will focus on: (1) behavioral activation to improve mood and test avoidance, (2) cognitive restructuring to address catastrophic thinking, (3) values clarification to address identity concerns, and (4) problem-solving for medical uncertainty."
- **Practice**: Use the CBT case formulation template (cognitive model diagram) to organize your thinking. Practice articulating your conceptualization to a supervisor or peer before each session.

### 2. Establish a Collaborative, Prioritized Agenda at the Start of Each Session
- **Action**: Begin each session by explicitly setting a collaborative agenda. Ask the patient what she wants to focus on, identify 2-3 specific topics, prioritize them together, and establish time allocation. Write the agenda down and reference it throughout the session.
- **Example**: "Today I'd like to check in on how the walks went last week, and then focus on one of two things: either working more on those scary thoughts about the cancer, or talking about what it means to be strong right now. Which feels more important to you today? We have about 45 minutes, so we can go deep on one or two things."
- **Practice**: Role-play agenda-setting with a colleague. Practice phrases like "What would be most helpful to focus on today?" and "I want to make sure we use our time well. Does this plan work for you?"

### 3. Implement Systematic Homework Review at the Start of Each Session
- **Action**: Make homework review the first item on every session agenda. Ask: "Did you attempt the homework?" "What happened?" "What did you notice?" "What got in the way?" "How confident are you that you can do it again?" Use this information to adjust interventions.
- **Example**: "Last time we talked about taking short walks. I'd like to start today by hearing how that went. Did you try any walks? What did you notice about your mood or worry when you were outside? If you didn't try, what got in the way? Let's problem-solve together."
- **Practice**: Create a homework review checklist to use at the start of each session. Practice asking open-ended questions about homework rather than yes/no questions.

### 4. Use Guided Discovery (Socratic Questioning) Instead of Direct Suggestion
- **Action**: When the patient identifies a problem or thought, use Socratic questions to help her discover insights rather than providing solutions. Ask about evidence, alternatives, consequences, and implications.
- **Example**: Instead of "Try telling yourself something more positive," ask: "When you have the thought 'I won't see my son's wedding,' what evidence do you have that this will happen? What evidence do you have that you might see it? What would be a more balanced way to think about this? What would you tell a friend who had this worry?"
- **Practice**: Practice Socratic questioning with a supervisor. Record yourself and listen for how often you ask questions vs. make statements. Aim for at least 70% questions.

### 5. Implement Structured Thought Records for Cognitive Work
- **Action**: Instead of suggesting positive self-talk, teach the patient to use a thought record. This involves: (1) identifying the situation, (2) identifying the automatic thought, (3) identifying the emotion, (4) examining evidence for and against the thought, (5) developing a balanced thought, and (6) re-rating the emotion.
- **Example**: Create a simple thought record form with columns for Situation, Automatic Thought, Emotion (0-10), Evidence For, Evidence Against, Balanced Thought, and New Emotion (0-10). Walk the patient through one example in session, then assign it as homework.
- **Practice**: Complete thought records yourself for your own anxious thoughts. Practice teaching the technique to a colleague or supervisor.

### 6. Implement Structured Behavioral Activation with an Activity Schedule
- **Action**: Instead of suggesting "try walks," create a written activity schedule. Identify valued activities, grade them by difficulty (0-10), start with low-difficulty activities, and schedule them at specific times. Track mood before and after each activity.
- **Example**: "Let's make a list of activities you used to enjoy or that you think might help you feel better. We'll pick the easiest ones to start with—maybe 5-10 minutes. Let's schedule them for specific times this week. I'm giving you this sheet to track your mood before and after. This will help us see what actually helps."
- **Practice**: Create activity schedules for yourself or a colleague. Practice grading activities by difficulty and starting with achievable goals.

### 7. Assess and Address Barriers to Homework Before Assigning It
- **Action**: Before assigning any homework, ask: "What might get in the way of doing this?" "How confident are you that you can do this (0-10)?" "What would help you succeed?" Problem-solve barriers collaboratively.
- **Example**: "I'm suggesting you try a 5-minute walk three times this week. What might make that hard for you? You mentioned feeling tired—what time of day do you have the most energy? Would it help to do it right after breakfast? What if you don't feel up to it one day—what's your backup plan?"
- **Practice**: Before assigning homework, pause and ask barrier-assessment questions. If confidence is below 7/10, problem-solve or adjust the assignment.

### 8. Provide Clear Rationale for All Interventions
- **Action**: Explain the CBT model and how each intervention addresses the patient's specific problems. Connect interventions to her case conceptualization.
- **Example**: "The reason we're focusing on getting you back to activities is that when we're depressed, we tend to avoid things, which actually makes depression worse. When you do activities, even small ones, it helps your brain produce chemicals that improve mood. Also, it gives you evidence that you can still do things, which helps with that feeling of 'not being strong enough.'"
- **Practice**: Before each session, write out the rationale for each intervention you plan to use. Practice explaining it in simple, non-jargon language.

### 9. Use Behavioral Experiments to Test Catastrophic Predictions
- **Action**: Instead of reassuring the patient that her fears won't come true, design behavioral experiments where she tests her predictions. This is more powerful than reassurance.
- **Example**: "You're worried that if you go for a walk, you'll feel worse or get too tired. Let's test that. This week, try a 5-minute walk and notice what actually happens. Write down your prediction before ('I'll feel exhausted'), then write down what actually happened. We'll look at the data together."
