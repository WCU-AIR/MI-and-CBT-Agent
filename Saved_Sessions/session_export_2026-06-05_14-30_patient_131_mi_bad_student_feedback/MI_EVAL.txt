Uses prescriptive language ("You really should think about quitting," "You need to stop") and shame-inducing statements ("You're only making excuses"). Misses critical emotional content when Viktor expresses ambivalence ("sometimes it causes fights at home") and concern about readiness ("I'm just not there yet"). ### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Direction is present but poorly executed. Psychiatrist maintains clear focus on smoking cessation as target behavior throughout session. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. Repeatedly attempts to move Viktor toward immediate action (setting quit date, committing to reduction) without first exploring Viktor's own goals, values, or readiness. Rigid and confrontational approach ("You have to act now. Quitting smoking is the only way to avoid those serious health issues") does not account for Viktor's stage of change. Later question "What would need to happen for you to consider changing your smoking habits in the future?" represents better direction by allowing Viktor to define conditions for change. Overall pattern shows direction without collaboration.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Collaboration almost entirely absent in early and middle portions of session. Psychiatrist adopts expert-driven, authoritarian stance from outset. Hierarchical language ("You really should," "You have to," "You must," "You need to") establishes superior-subordinate relationship. When Viktor expresses hesitation, clinician responds with confrontation rather than curiosity ("You're only making excuses"). Fails to seek Viktor's input on treatment process itself—no discussion of shared goals, no exploration of what Viktor wants from session, no invitation to co-create agenda. Later exchanges show improvement ("I want to hear from you," "What would need to happen?"), but these represent small fraction of session. Question about enjoying/disliking smoking is genuinely collaborative, but damage to alliance is substantial.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Autonomy support virtually absent. Psychiatrist consistently minimizes Viktor's right to choose and attempts to control behavior through pressure and directive language. Key violations include: "You really should think about quitting," "You have to understand," "You have to commit to quitting now," "You need to stop making excuses," "You have to have the willpower," "You need to make a commitment right now." These statements communicate that Viktor's autonomy is irrelevant and clinician's agenda supersedes his decision-making process. When Viktor states "I don't think I can commit to cutting down tomorrow," clinician responds with further pressure rather than respecting choice. Only late-session question "What would need to happen for you to consider changing?" respects Viktor's timeline and agency. Single question cannot compensate for pervasive autonomy-undermining stance throughout session.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Evocation largely absent. Psychiatrist tells Viktor why he should change ("Smoking is dangerous," "you're putting yourself at risk for heart disease, cancer, and lung problems") rather than asking questions that invite Viktor to generate his own reasons for change. Multiple instances of change talk are missed: "Sometimes it causes fights at home," "Maybe if things got worse at home... I might think about it," "She worries about my health," "It sometimes causes fights with my wife. That's the worst part." Clinician fails to deepen these openings with curiosity. Late-session question "What are some things you enjoy about smoking, and what are some things you don't like about it?" is genuinely evocative and produces authentic change talk ("What I don't like though is when it causes fights with my wife. That's the worst part"), but session ends without further exploration or amplification. Overall pattern shows installation of motivation rather than evocation of intrinsic motivation.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.6/5 – POOR**

### Key Strengths:
- **Late-session pivoting:** Psychiatrist demonstrates awareness that early approach is not working and shifts toward genuine curiosity and evocation in final exchanges
- **Clear focus:** Maintains consistent focus on smoking as target behavior throughout session
- **Accurate value identification:** In final exchanges, accurately reflects Viktor's values ("Your relationship and home life are really important to you")
- **Capacity for adjustment:** Demonstrates flexibility by shifting from directive advice to open-ended questions, suggesting capacity for growth

### Critical Deficiencies:

**1. Pervasive Confrontation and Pressure**
Early and middle portions dominated by directive language ("you have to," "you must," "you need to"), shame-inducing statements ("you're only making excuses"), and pressure tactics. Directly contradicts MI spirit and damages therapeutic alliance. Viktor explicitly states: "It feels like you're not really hearing me," indicating confrontational approach has failed.

**2. Absence of Autonomy Support**
Consistently undermines Viktor's right to choose and attempts to control behavior. No explicit affirmation of autonomy or respect for his timeline. Fundamental MI principle violated throughout most of session.

**3. Failure to Evoke Change Talk**
Multiple opportunities to draw out Viktor's own motivations missed. When Viktor mentions fights at home, concerns about marriage, and feeling pressured, clinician does not explore these openings but uses them as ammunition for further pressure.

**4. Expert-Driven Stance**
Positions himself as authority who knows what's best for Viktor rather than partnering to explore Viktor's perspective. Hierarchical relationship undermines collaboration and partnership.

**5. Installation vs. Evocation**
Tells Viktor why he should change (health risks, family consequences) rather than asking Viktor to generate his own reasons. Opposite of MI's core principle of evocation.

**6. Missed Discrepancy Development**
Viktor expresses ambivalence (acknowledging fights at home while minimizing problem), but psychiatrist does not skillfully develop this discrepancy. Does not explore gap between Viktor's stated values (marriage, balance) and his behaviors (smoking despite marital conflict).

### Impact on Client Engagement:

This session **significantly undermines** likelihood of Viktor engaging in change. Confrontational, pressuring approach triggers defensive resistance, evidenced by increasingly entrenched sustain talk ("I'm just not there yet," "I can't promise to smoke fewer cigarettes tomorrow"). Research demonstrates confrontation and pressure typically strengthen resistance and reduce intrinsic motivation. Viktor enters with external motivation (wife's pressure) and limited insight; psychiatrist's approach does not help develop intrinsic motivation but reinforces defensive position and likely increases resistance to future interventions.

### Recommendations for Improvement:

1. **Eliminate confrontation and pressure—replace with genuine curiosity**
2. **Develop genuine partnership—explicitly invite Viktor's expertise about himself**
3. **Evoke rather than install motivation—ask about Viktor's own reasons for change**
4. **Amplify and reinforce change talk—strategically deepen Viktor's own language about downsides**
5. **Respect autonomy explicitly—affirm Viktor's right to choose, even if he chooses not to change**
6. **Develop discrepancy skillfully—help Viktor explore gap between values and behaviors**
7. **Soften sustain talk through strategic redirection—not confrontation**
8. **Assess stage of change and tailor approach accordingly**
9. **Build therapeutic alliance first—establish safety and trust before addressing change**
10. **Recognize and repair ruptures—when Viktor says "You're not hearing me," stop and listen**

### Training Needs:

- Intensive MI training with focus on spirit and core principles
- Supervised practice with feedback on confrontation and directive language
- Role-play practice developing evocative questions and reflective listening
- Recording review to identify patterns of confrontation and missed opportunities
- Ongoing supervision with MI-trained supervisor

**Positive Note:** Late-session shift demonstrates capacity for change. With focused training and supervision, this clinician could develop solid MI competence.
