
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Feedback: Therapist Evaluation

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist failed to establish a structured, collaborative agenda at any point in this session. The opening statement—"I thought we could talk a bit about anything that's been bothering you lately or any stress you've been feeling. Does that sound okay? We can just kind of take it from there"—is vague, non-specific, and lacks any prioritization or time allocation. This is generic supportive therapy language, not CBT agenda-setting.

A proper CBT agenda would have included: (1) explicit identification of target problems (illness anxiety disorder and panic disorder), (2) prioritization of which symptoms to address first, (3) time allocation for the session, (4) collaborative agreement on specific goals, and (5) connection to the treatment plan. For example, the therapist should have said: "I'd like to focus our time today on three things: understanding the cycle of health worry that's been happening, identifying specific situations that trigger the most anxiety, and developing a concrete plan to interrupt that cycle. We have 50 minutes. Does that work for you? What feels most urgent to address today?"

The therapist also failed to review the previous session's homework (music listening and thought monitoring) at the beginning of the second session, which is a critical CBT component. There is no mention of checking whether the patient attempted the assignments or what happened when he did. This represents a fundamental break in CBT continuity and accountability.

**Summary:**

The therapist did not establish a structured, collaborative agenda with specific target problems, time allocation, or prioritization. No review of prior homework occurred in the second session, violating core CBT protocol.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist elicited minimal feedback throughout both sessions and never systematically checked for understanding, agreement, or the patient's reaction to interventions. In the first session, after suggesting music and thought monitoring, the therapist asked only: "Does that sound like something you might want to try?"—a single yes/no question that does not constitute genuine feedback elicitation.

True feedback elicitation in CBT requires: (1) asking the patient's reaction to the therapist's conceptualization, (2) checking whether explanations made sense, (3) soliciting concerns about proposed interventions, (4) asking what the patient found most/least helpful, and (5) explicitly asking for critical feedback about the session itself.

The therapist never asked: "Does this explanation of how worry cycles work make sense to you?" or "What concerns do you have about trying music as a coping strategy?" or "Is there anything I said today that didn't sit right with you?" or "What was most helpful about today's session?" or "What could I do differently to be more helpful?"

In the second session, the therapist again failed to ask for feedback on the homework or the patient's experience since the last session. The patient volunteered that "the worry just doesn't go away" and that "it's exhausting," but the therapist did not explore whether the music strategy was attempted, what barriers existed, or whether the patient felt the approach was helpful or misguided.

**Summary:**

The therapist did not systematically elicit feedback about understanding, agreement with interventions, or satisfaction with the session. No explicit feedback solicitation occurred at any point.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated partial understanding of the patient's presenting concerns but missed critical clinical nuances that are essential for effective IAD treatment. The therapist accurately identified that the patient experiences: (1) tiredness and fogginess, (2) worry about Lyme disease recurrence, (3) difficulty accepting reassurance despite negative tests, and (4) a cycle of worry that is difficult to interrupt.

However, the therapist missed or minimized several critical aspects of Oscar's internal experience: (1) the *strength* of his conviction that he has Lyme disease despite repeated negative testing—this is not simple doubt but a deeply held belief resistant to disconfirmation; (2) the *safety-seeking behavior* of staying home to avoid symptom exacerbation, which maintains the disorder; (3) the *reassurance-seeking pattern* (requesting more tests despite normal results), which is a core maintaining factor in IAD; (4) the *avoidance of responsibility* and reliance on his wife, which reinforces illness identity; and (5) the *catastrophic interpretation* of minor lab abnormalities (low hemoglobin), indicating hypervigilance to medical information.

The therapist also did not acknowledge or explore the patient's statement: "I stay inside because I'm afraid I'll get tired or feel bad while I'm out." This is classic avoidance behavior that maintains both illness anxiety and panic disorder, yet it was treated as a logistical obstacle rather than a therapeutic target.

**Summary:**

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
### **Score: 4/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated adequate warmth, empathy, and professionalism without significant interpersonal problems. The therapist also did not establish appropriate boundaries around reassurance-seeking. When the patient expressed doubt about alternative explanations, the therapist offered reassurance ("it's okay to be cautious") rather than redirecting the patient toward self-discovery or behavioral experiments that would test the validity of his concerns.
