
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist failed to establish a structured, collaborative agenda in either session. In Session 1, the opening ("I thought we could just chat a bit about anything that's been on your mind") is vague and non-directive. No explicit priorities were established, no time allocation discussed, and no clear session goals were negotiated. In Session 2, the identical opening occurs ("I was thinking maybe we could just talk a bit about whatever's been on your mind"), demonstrating no learning or progression. Again, no agenda is set. For a patient with chronic suicidality, dissociative amnesia, PTSD, and compulsive behaviors, this is clinically dangerous. CBT requires explicit agenda-setting that prioritizes safety concerns, identifies target symptoms, and allocates time strategically. **Summary:**

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist never elicited feedback from the patient about the session, her understanding of interventions, or her reactions to the work. This is a complete absence of a core CBT competency. Feedback elicitation serves multiple functions: (1) ensuring the patient understands the rationale for interventions, (2) identifying obstacles to engagement, (3) checking whether interventions are landing, and (4) maintaining collaborative partnership.

In Session 1, after introducing thought-replacement and behavioral activation, the therapist asks "Do you think trying to replace those thoughts with something more positive might help you feel a bit better?" This is not feedback elicitation—it's a leading question designed to gain agreement, not to assess the patient's genuine reaction or understanding. The patient responds with doubt ("I don't know... it feels like trying to tell myself 'I'm doing my best' is just not true most days"), which is a critical signal that the intervention isn't resonating. The therapist does not explore this resistance. Instead, in the next turn, the therapist pivots to acceptance-based language ("maybe it would help to notice when those sad thoughts come up and just kind of observe them") without checking whether the patient understood the previous intervention or why it didn't fit.

In Session 2, identical patterns repeat. After assigning thought-monitoring homework, the therapist never asks: "What are your thoughts about this assignment?" "Do you see how this might help?" "What concerns do you have?" "What would make this easier to do?" The patient's statement—"I don't know if I really believe those positive thoughts"—is a direct challenge to the intervention's credibility, yet the therapist does not explore it collaboratively.

At the end of both sessions, the therapist does not summarize key points, ask what the patient found most helpful, or solicit feedback on the session itself. A 6/6 rating would include explicit feedback checks: "Before we wrap up, I want to make sure this made sense to you. What's one thing you're taking away from today?" or "How did today feel? Was there anything that didn't land for you?"

**Summary:**

Zero instances of genuine feedback elicitation. The therapist never checked patient understanding, explored resistance, or solicited reactions to interventions. This undermines collaboration and prevents course-correction.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrates partial understanding of the patient's explicit presenting concerns but misses critical clinical nuances and the deeper architecture of her symptoms. On the surface level, the therapist accurately reflects that the patient experiences "fatigue and sadness," "automatic thoughts," and "exhaustion." A trauma-informed understanding would recognize that compulsive behaviors (counting, singing, checking, arranging) are likely trauma responses and anxiety regulation strategies, not merely symptoms to be "observed."
