# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates near-complete absence of unconditional positive regard. Psychiatrist uses judgmental language ("you just have to stop, no excuses," "you're putting yourself and others at risk") and explicitly invalidates Elena's coping mechanisms and lived experience. No affirmations of her strengths (e.g., efforts to avoid smoking around child, willingness to attend). Tone is authoritarian and punitive, creating defensiveness rather than openness. Fundamental disrespect for Elena's autonomy and judgment undermines therapeutic relationship.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal evidence of reflective listening or genuine effort to understand Elena's perspective. Single superficial acknowledgment ("I understand you're feeling stressed") is immediately negated by dismissal. Psychiatrist never reflects Elena's actual concerns: overwhelming workload, child's health problems, role as single parent managing multiple stressors. No complex reflections capturing emotional nuance. Critical moment missed when Elena states smoking "keeps me going when things get rough"—receives no empathic exploration. Elena's change talk ("I've thought about quitting") not amplified. Elena left feeling unheard and misunderstood.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains clear, consistent focus on smoking cessation as target behavior and does not allow session to wander—this accounts for score of 2 rather than 1. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. Psychiatrist repeatedly directs Elena toward specific behavioral commitments (patches tomorrow, cutting down by half this week, quit date within two weeks) without understanding her readiness, values, or own reasons for change. Direction is prescriptive and controlling rather than strategically guiding toward change goals while remaining client-centered. Never clarifies what Elena's actual change goal is or explores whether complete cessation aligns with her values.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates opposite of collaboration—clear hierarchical, expert-driven stance. Opening statement ("I'm going to give you clear advice on what you need to do") establishes authority position. Repeated use of "you have to," "you need to," "you must" language. Elena's input consistently dismissed as invalid; her perspective reframed as obstacles to overcome or excuses to dismiss. No joint problem-solving; psychiatrist does not ask Elena what might help, what barriers she anticipates, or what resources she has. No collaborative language ("what do you think," "let's explore," "how might we"). This is prescription, not partnership.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session actively undermines autonomy at every turn. Repeated controlling language: "you have to quit," "you just have to stop, no excuses," "you need to be more responsible." When Elena states "I need to do it when I feel ready, not because I'm being pushed," psychiatrist responds by pressuring further and reframing her need for self-determination as irresponsible delay. Guilt and fear used as motivators ("your child's health is at stake," "you're risking serious diseases")—external pressure that undermines intrinsic motivation. No affirmation of Elena's freedom to choose or acknowledgment that change is ultimately her decision. Psychiatrist attempts to impose timeline and specific behavioral commitments—opposite of autonomy support.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates almost no evocation; tell-based approach dominates. Psychiatrist provides external reasons for change ("terrible for your lungs," "risking serious health problems") rather than evoking Elena's own reasons. Elena offers change talk ("I've thought about quitting")—critical moment for evocation—but psychiatrist does not explore this; instead immediately follows with pressure and directive statements, shutting down further exploration of her own thinking. No attempt to explore what smoking means to Elena beyond surface level. Fails to recognize or reflect Elena's values (child's health, work, stress management). No attempt to elicit Elena's resources, strengths, or past successes with change. No curiosity about what might help her.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates opposite of partnership. Opening statement frames psychiatrist as expert with answers and Elena as someone who must be told what to do. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role and dismisses client's contributions. Elena's perspective (stress management, efforts to protect child, realistic assessment of capacity) consistently reframed as obstacles rather than incorporated into discussion. No genuine power-sharing; psychiatrist makes unilateral decisions about what Elena should do and expects compliance. When Elena declines, she is pressured further rather than engaged as partner in problem-solving. Language throughout is hierarchical and controlling, not collaborative and equal. This is directive expert relationship, not partnership.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates virtually no cultivation of change talk and actively suppresses it. Elena offers change talk early ("I've thought about quitting")—critical moment for exploration and amplification—but psychiatrist immediately follows with pressure and directive statements, shutting down further development of her own motivation. No evocation of other change talk forms (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment). Psychiatrist could ask "What would be benefits of quitting?" "What have you done when making changes?" "What are most important reasons?" but does not. Repeated use of guilt and fear may temporarily increase stated commitment but does not cultivate intrinsic change talk. Research shows externally motivated change is less sustainable than intrinsically motivated change. By session end, Elena more entrenched in sustain talk than at beginning.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist directly confronts sustain talk rather than skillfully softening it. Elena's legitimate concerns and barriers ("I don't smoke around him," "It helps me deal with stress," "I'm already juggling enough," "I need to do it when I feel ready") met with direct confrontation: "that's not an excuse," "you have to stop using stress as an excuse," "you're making excuses." Research shows direct confrontation increases resistance and sustain talk rather than decreasing it. No double-sided reflections acknowledging both change talk and sustain talk. No reframing of concerns. No strategic responses to minimize sustain talk while maintaining collaboration. By session end, Elena more defensive and entrenched in position ("I need to do it when I feel ready, not because I'm being pushed") than at beginning. Intervention has backfired.

## Overall Session Score: **1.1/5**

### Summary Assessment

**Strengths:**
- Clear, consistent focus on target behavior (smoking cessation)
- Explicit communication of health risks associated with smoking

**Critical Deficiencies:**
This session represents fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles across virtually all domains. Psychiatrist employs directive, expert-driven, controlling approach directly contraindicated for behavior change work. Rather than evoking Elena's intrinsic motivation, psychiatrist applies external pressure through repeated directives, guilt, and fear-based messaging. This predictably increases psychological reactance and resistance.

**Impact on Client Engagement:**
Elena enters with ambivalence ("I've thought about quitting") and acknowledgment of harm. By session end, she is more entrenched in her position, explicitly refusing any behavioral commitment and stating need to "do it when I feel ready, not because I'm being pushed." Psychiatrist's repeated pressure and dismissal have activated Elena's psychological reactance. Session has decreased rather than increased her motivation to change. Elena likely to leave feeling judged, misunderstood, and more resistant to future interventions.

**Clinical Outcomes Likely:**
- Decreased likelihood of sustained behavior change
- Damaged therapeutic relationship; decreased likelihood of follow-up
- Intensified sustain talk and defensiveness
- Reduced receptiveness to future health recommendations from this provider

**MI Competency Level:** Minimal competency demonstrated. Comprehensive training in MI principles, skill-building practice, and supervised application with feedback essential.

**Most Critical Improvements Needed:**
1. Shift from directive to collaborative, partnership-based stance
2. Develop genuine empathy through reflective listening
3. Replace telling with asking; evoke Elena's own reasons for change
4. Respect autonomy; support Elena's right to choose and perception of agency
