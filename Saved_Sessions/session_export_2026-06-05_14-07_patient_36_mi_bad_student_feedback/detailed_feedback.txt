
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

There are no affirmations of Elena's strengths (e.g., her effort to avoid smoking around her child, her willingness to attend the appointment, her acknowledgment that smoking is harmful). The psychiatrist never normalizes the difficulty of change or validates the genuine complexity of her situation. The tone throughout is authoritarian and punitive, not warm or supportive. This approach directly violates the MI principle of acceptance and creates defensiveness rather than openness.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance of Elena as a person. Judgmental language, dismissal of her reasoning, and absence of affirmation create a fundamentally disrespectful stance that undermines the therapeutic relationship and violates core MI principles.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

The psychiatrist makes one superficial acknowledgment ("I understand you're feeling stressed") but immediately negates it by dismissing stress as an invalid reason for smoking ("but you have to stop using stress as an excuse").

The psychiatrist also fails to recognize or reflect Elena's change talk ("I've thought about quitting") in a way that amplifies it. Instead, change talk is immediately followed by pressure and directive statements that shut down further exploration of her own motivation.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal empathy. There is no accurate reflective listening, no genuine effort to understand Elena's perspective, and no complex reflections that deepen insight. ### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

While the psychiatrist maintains a clear focus on smoking cessation as the target behavior, the approach to direction is fundamentally misaligned with MI principles. The psychiatrist does maintain consistent focus on the smoking issue and does not allow the session to wander—this accounts for the score of 2 rather than 1.

The psychiatrist also fails to use direction strategically to explore ambivalence. Rather than helping Elena examine her own reasons for and against change, the psychiatrist simply insists on the "correct" direction, which predictably increases resistance.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains focus on smoking cessation but does so in an imposed, prescriptive manner rather than through collaborative exploration. Direction is not strategically used to evoke client motivation or clarify goals; instead, it functions as a tool for control.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have valuable knowledge. This session demonstrates the opposite: a clear hierarchical, expert-driven stance where the psychiatrist positions themselves as the authority who knows what is best and the client as someone who needs to comply.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no genuine collaboration. The stance is authoritarian and expert-driven, with the psychiatrist positioning themselves as the authority and Elena as someone who must comply. There is no joint problem-solving or shared decision-making.

### 5. Autonomy Support
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Autonomy support requires respecting the client's right to choose and actively fostering their perception of choice. This session actively undermines autonomy at every turn. The psychiatrist repeatedly uses language that minimizes choice: "you have to quit," "you just have to stop, no excuses," "you need to," "you have to take this seriously," "you need to be more responsible," "you have to stop making excuses."

This directly contradicts autonomy support.

The psychiatrist also uses guilt and fear as motivators ("you're putting yourself and others at risk," "your child's health is at stake," "you're risking serious diseases"), which are forms of external pressure that undermine intrinsic motivation and autonomy. Research shows that autonomy-supportive approaches are more effective for behavior change than controlling approaches.

There is no affirmation of Elena's freedom to choose, no acknowledgment that change is ultimately her decision, and no exploration of what would help her feel more in control of the process. Instead, the psychiatrist attempts to impose a timeline and specific behavioral commitments, which is the opposite of autonomy support.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist actively undermines autonomy through controlling language, external pressure, and dismissal of Elena's need for self-determination. There is no respect for her right to choose or support for her perception of agency in the change process.

### 6. Evocation
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Evocation requires drawing out the client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources through skillful questioning and reflection. This session demonstrates almost no evocation. Instead, the psychiatrist uses a tell-based approach, providing reasons for change without exploring Elena's own reasons.

The psychiatrist tells Elena that smoking is "terrible for your lungs and overall health," "risking serious health problems," "dangerous," and "putting yourself and others at risk." These are external reasons for change, not Elena's reasons. Effective MI would ask questions like: "What concerns you most about your smoking?" "How do you see smoking affecting your life?" "What would be different if you quit?" "What matters most to you about your health?" These questions would evoke Elena's own motivations.

Elena does offer change talk ("I've thought about quitting"), which is a critical moment for evocation. However, the psychiatrist does not explore this. Instead, the psychiatrist immediately follows with pressure and directive statements ("you really need to quit smoking as soon as possible"), which shuts down further exploration of her own thinking about change.

The psychiatrist also fails to explore Elena's values. What does she care about? Her child's health is clearly important to her (she mentions trying not to smoke around him multiple times). Her work is important. Her ability to manage stress is important. A skillful MI practitioner would explore these values and help Elena connect them to change, asking questions like: "You mentioned your son's health is important to you—how do you see smoking fitting with that?" Instead, the psychiatrist simply asserts the connection without exploration.

There is no attempt to elicit Elena's own resources, strengths, or past successes with change. No curiosity about what has worked for her in the past or what she thinks might help.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no evocation. The approach is tell-based rather than ask-based, providing external reasons for change rather than drawing out Elena's own motivations, values, and resources. Critical moments for evocation (e.g., when Elena mentions thinking about quitting) are missed.

### 7. Partnership
### **Score: 1/5**

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Softening sustain talk requires skillfully handling client arguments for the status quo without confrontation, using techniques like double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk while maintaining the collaborative relationship.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist directly confronts sustain talk rather than skillfully softening it. This increases resistance and defensiveness rather than decreasing it. No double-sided reflections, reframing, or strategic responses are used to minimize sustain talk while maintaining collaboration.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Adopt a Collaborative, Partnership-Based Stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Fundamentally shift from an expert-driven, directive approach to a collaborative partnership where Elena is recognized as the expert on her own life and the psychiatrist serves as a facilitator of her own change process.

**Example:** 
- **Instead of:** "You have to quit smoking as soon as possible. It's terrible for your lungs and overall health. You just have to stop, no excuses."
- **Use:** "I can see that smoking is something you're dealing with, and I'm also hearing that you've thought about quitting. I'm wondering—what's your sense of where you stand with smoking right now? What matters most to you about your health?"

**Practice:** 
- Before each session, remind yourself: "My job is not to convince this person to change. My job is to help them explore their own reasons for change."
- Practice using collaborative language: "What do you think?" "How do you see this?" "What would work for you?" "Let's think about this together."
- Record yourself in practice sessions and count how many times you use "you have to/need to/must" vs. collaborative language. Aim for zero directive statements per session.

### 2. **Develop Genuine Empathy Through Reflective Listening**
