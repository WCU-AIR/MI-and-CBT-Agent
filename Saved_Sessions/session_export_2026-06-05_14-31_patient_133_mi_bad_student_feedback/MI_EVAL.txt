### Acceptance

**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates profound lack of acceptance throughout session. Uses judgmental, prescriptive language ("I think you really need to make some changes," "You must quit any unhealthy habits," "Stop making excuses"). Dismisses patient's core concern about burnout from strict discipline as something to "push through." Conveys conditional regard based on adherence to clinician's strict discipline model rather than unconditional positive regard. Single moment of improvement near end ("It's really important to hear how your past experiences...") insufficient to offset systematic lack of acceptance. Patient's values, concerns, and lived experience are minimized rather than affirmed throughout majority of session.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Minimal empathic reflection demonstrated. When patient states desire for sustainability and fear of crash-and-burn, psychiatrist responds with dismissal ("Forget those crash-and-burn excuses") rather than empathic reflection. Patient's core conflict ("caught between wanting results and not losing myself") met with insistence that discomfort is "just part of the process"—no attempt to understand internal experience. Patient's perspective consistently invalidated rather than understood from her frame of reference.

### Direction

However, direction is entirely clinician-imposed rather than collaboratively established with patient input. Patient's stated goals ("feel good mentally and physically," "find something sustainable") never explored or validated. Clinician redirects toward strict discipline without clarifying patient's actual goals. Minimal strategic progression—same questions repeated ("Why haven't you started?" "What's stopping you?") without building on responses or deepening exploration of patient's own motivations. Later question about "what has worked for you in the past" shows some collaborative direction-setting, but occurs only after significant therapeutic damage and represents dramatic shift rather than consistent MI practice.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Authoritarian, expert-driven stance throughout majority of session. Opening statement establishes clinician as expert who will direct conversation. Commanding language throughout ("You really should," "You must quit," "You have to commit," "You must stop," "You can't expect") leaves no room for collaborative problem-solving. When patient expresses perspective, psychiatrist counters with "you really need to understand," positioning clinician's understanding as correct and patient's as misguided. No joint problem-solving or genuine exploration of what might work for patient. Patient's legitimate concerns about past failures dismissed as "excuses" rather than explored as valuable data. Single collaborative statement near end ("Let's explore together") insufficient to offset pervasive expert-driven, hierarchical stance dominating session.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Systematically undermines patient autonomy throughout session. Repeated use of "must," "should," "have to" frames requirements as non-negotiable rather than choices. Dismisses patient's preference for listening to body during exercise ("if you don't have a solid plan, you're just wasting time"). Overrides patient's autonomy regarding food tracking by insisting she must "push through" stress and obsession. False choice presented ("Are you ready to take this seriously and follow a strict plan starting now?") pressures compliance rather than supporting freedom to choose. Implies not following psychiatrist's plan means "not taking it seriously"—coercive framing. No language affirming patient's right to make decisions about own health or recognition that she is expert on her own body and experience. Fundamental stance is controlling rather than autonomy-supportive.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Virtually no evocation demonstrated. Rather than drawing out patient's own motivations through skillful questioning, psychiatrist tells patient what reasons should matter ("You must quit any unhealthy habits if you want to feel better"). Patient's spontaneous change talk ("wouldn't wanna crash and burn," "finding foods I like makes sense," "want something sustainable") not amplified or explored—instead dismissed as "excuses." Patient's clearly articulated values ("strive to be physically and mentally healthy," "want something sustainable") not evoked or built upon; instead contradicted by clinician ("can't expect to feel kind to yourself if you don't see progress"). Questions asked are not evocative—closed questions inviting justification ("Why haven't you started?" "What's stopping you?") rather than exploration of patient's own wisdom. Only late question ("Can you tell me more about what has worked...") represents evocative approach, but occurs after clinician has spent majority of session imposing rather than evoking.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Actively assumes expert role throughout majority of session, conveying that clinician knowledge is superior to patient self-knowledge. Statement "you really need to understand that without strict discipline, you won't get results" positions clinician's understanding as correct and patient's as incorrect. When patient shares lived experience that strict discipline leads to burnout, psychiatrist dismisses this as something to "push through" rather than valuing as expertise about what works for her. Language frames patient as deficient ("Stop making excuses," "You must stop being so lenient," "You can't expect to feel kind to yourself") rather than as someone with legitimate knowledge about own experience and needs. No genuine curiosity about patient's expertise regarding her own body, psychology, or what has/hasn't worked. Prescribes solutions as if patient has no relevant knowledge. Minor movement toward partnership near end ("Let's explore together") insufficient to offset pervasive expert-driven stance dominating session.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Patient spontaneously offers multiple instances of change talk throughout session: "wouldn't wanna crash and burn," "finding foods and activities I like makes sense," "want something sustainable," "want to feel good mentally and physically," "like when I can do things that feel fun or energizing," "when I focus on enjoyment, easier to keep going." Psychiatrist fails to amplify, reinforce, or build upon any of this. Instead: dismisses change talk as "excuses," contradicts it with clinician's agenda, and redirects toward strict discipline. When patient expresses desire for sustainability, psychiatrist insists on strict discipline instead. When patient articulates that enjoyment helps her stick with habits, clinician does not explore or reinforce this insight. Language actively undermines confidence ("can't expect to feel kind if you don't see progress," "without strict discipline won't get results"). Effective change talk cultivation would involve reflections amplifying and deepening patient's statements; instead, clinician contradicts and dismisses emerging motivation for sustainable change.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Patient expresses ambivalence rather than traditional sustain talk ("still would want to lose weight… maybe that changes or maybe it doesn't"), representing legitimate ambivalence about whether non-diet approach will satisfy weight loss goals. Psychiatrist does not skillfully soften this ambivalence; instead ignores it and pushes toward strict discipline, which reinforces patient's resistance. When patient expresses concern that strict approaches have failed ("tried so many diets, never stick"), psychiatrist frames as excuse rather than exploring as valuable information about barriers. No double-sided reflections acknowledging both sides of ambivalence (e.g., "part of you wants weight loss results, part of you concerned strict approach might harm mental health"). One moment where psychiatrist asks "What's stopping you from sticking to strict routine?" could explore barriers, but framing assumes barrier is patient's weakness rather than genuinely exploring legitimate difficulties. Later shift toward exploring "what has worked for you" represents better approach to handling ambivalence, but occurs too late and does not demonstrate skillful softening throughout session.

## **OVERALL SESSION SCORE: 1.1/5**

### **Summary Assessment:**

**MI Competency Level:** Poor – Fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles

**Key Findings:**
- Psychiatrist operates from authoritarian, expert-driven stance directly contradicting MI spirit of partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation
- Systematic dismissal of patient's lived experience, values, and expertise about herself
- Failure to recognize, amplify, or build upon patient's spontaneous change talk
- Use of imperatives, pressure tactics, and false choices undermining autonomy
- Patient's stated priority of mental and physical health contradicted rather than validated

**Clinical Impact:**
This session is **unlikely to evoke intrinsic motivation for change** and likely to **increase patient defensiveness and resistance**. Patient entered with genuine change talk and openness to exploring alternatives; psychiatrist's approach systematically undermined this emerging motivation rather than building upon it.

**Recommendation:**
**Significant intervention and retraining required.** Psychiatrist must complete formal MI training (MINT-certified), engage in ongoing clinical supervision with MI expert, and fundamentally shift from expert/authoritarian role to collaborative, client-centered partnership approach. Current practice does not meet basic MI standards and may be counterproductive to patient's change process.
