# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal unconditional positive regard demonstrated. While opening is warm, psychiatrist quickly pivots to problem-focused questioning without establishing acceptance foundation. Repeated use of directive language ("it would probably help if," "maybe you should") subtly conveys judgment about what patient *ought* to do rather than accepting current state and autonomy. Fails to affirm patient's struggle or normalize difficulty faced. No genuine warmth acknowledging that staying connected and managing stress are legitimate needs.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Empathy is superficial and inconsistently applied. Basic reflective listening present but simple and factual rather than complex or emotionally attuned. Fails to capture emotional nuance and deeper meaning of patient's experience. When patient expresses genuine ambivalence and fear of isolation, psychiatrist does not deepen reflection to explore emotional conflict underneath. Misses opportunities to recognize and reflect patient's growing openness. Complex, emotionally attuned reflections absent throughout session.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** General focus on social media use maintained appropriately. However, direction becomes increasingly prescriptive and solution-focused rather than exploratory. Early problem-focused questions appropriate for establishing discrepancy, but follow-up immediately suggests specific interventions, steering patient toward predetermined conclusions. By third exchange, shifted almost entirely to action planning despite patient rating importance as 5/10 and confidence as 4/10—classic contemplation indicators. Pacing too rapid for early contemplation stage. Clinician-driven rather than client-centered direction.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Collaboration minimal; psychiatrist assumes expert role and drives agenda unilaterally. Frames phone use as "causing problems" without first exploring patient's own problem definition. Prescriptive suggestions offered without co-construction or invitation for patient to generate ideas first. Rhetorical questions masking directives ("It would probably help if...") leave little room for genuine collaboration. When patient expresses doubt about confidence level, psychiatrist does not explore collaboratively but instead imposes solution. Patient positioned as passive recipient of expert advice rather than equal partner.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Autonomy support significantly compromised. Directive language disguised as suggestions ("It would probably help if," "Maybe you should") creates subtle pressure despite technically offering options. Prescriptive action planning presents specific plan for patient confirmation rather than choice. Fails to explicitly state patient is in control or can modify plan as needed. Emphasis on client choice appears only at end as afterthought. No explicit permission to not change offered. Pressure through framing when patient expresses low confidence, implying psychiatrist has solution to stated doubt.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Evocation largely absent; psychiatrist relies on telling and suggesting rather than asking and exploring. Multiple missed opportunities to deepen spontaneous change talk ("My grades slipped," "I'm tired in class"). Leading questions guide toward predetermined answers rather than evoking patient's own perspective. Fails to explore values and discrepancy between patient's values and behavior. Prescriptive suggestions offered before evoking patient's own ideas. No exploration of strengths, resources, or past successes. Minimal amplification of soft change talk statements. Open questions designed to elicit change talk absent.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Partnership fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role throughout, positioning self as authority on what patient should do. All specific interventions generated by psychiatrist without inviting patient contribution. Dismisses patient's stated low confidence rather than honoring self-knowledge. Plan essentially decided by psychiatrist with patient asked to confirm rather than choose. Implicit message throughout: clinician's expertise superior to patient's self-knowledge. Lack of genuine curiosity about what patient already knows about themselves. Overall structure sends message that clinician is expert and patient is passive recipient.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk present but rarely evoked, amplified, or reinforced. Multiple spontaneous change talk instances ("grades slipped," "tired in class," "willing to try," "smaller changes could work") acknowledged but not deepened. No strategic questioning designed to elicit change talk. Commitment language ("I'm willing to give this a try") not reinforced or explored. Rapid pivot to action planning misses opportunity to deepen intrinsic motivation. No exploration of values-based change talk. Minimal use of open questions—primary tool for evoking change talk. Psychiatrist appears to be installing motivation rather than evoking it from patient's own perspective.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk handled with moderate skill; psychiatrist neither reinforces nor dismisses patient's reasons for maintaining phone use. Acknowledges sustain talk in reflections appropriately. Double-sided reflection used to acknowledge both sides of ambivalence ("helps you relax and stay connected, but also affecting sleep and focus"). Reframing through small steps reduces perceived threat and makes change feel manageable. However, missed opportunities for deeper softening techniques. Does not explicitly validate legitimate needs underlying sustain talk (connection, stress relief, entertainment). Could have used more sophisticated reframing and exploration of ambivalence.

---

### Overall Clinical Summary

**Session Score: 2.3/5 – Below Average MI Competence**

**Strengths:**
- Basic warmth and non-threatening environment established
- Acknowledgment of patient's ambivalence present
- Appropriate suggestion of small, incremental changes rather than major behavior change
- Adequate handling of sustain talk without argumentation
- Maintenance of focus on target behavior throughout

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Premature action planning:** Specific behavioral interventions suggested before adequate exploration of ambivalence, values, and intrinsic motivation. Patient rates importance 5/10 and confidence 4/10—classic contemplation indicators—yet psychiatrist already planning specific goals.
- **Clinician-driven agenda:** All solutions generated by psychiatrist; patient positioned as passive recipient rather than active partner.
- **Minimal evocation:** Reliance on telling and suggesting rather than asking and exploring. Spontaneous change talk acknowledged but not deepened.
- **Shallow empathy:** Factual, surface-level reflections; emotional nuance and deeper meaning missed.
- **Weak autonomy support:** Directive language and prescriptive planning subtly pressure patient toward predetermined solutions.

**Impact on Patient Engagement:**
Patient agreement to try suggested plan appears to be compliance with psychiatrist's recommendations rather than genuine commitment rooted in own values and reasons for change. Tentative commitment ("I'm not promising I'll stick to it forever") and low importance/confidence ratings indicate high risk of relapse. Psychiatrist has not helped patient discover own reasons for change or develop intrinsic motivation.

**Recommendation:**
Psychiatrist must fundamentally shift from expert, problem-solving role to evocative, partnership-based stance. Requires: asking more/telling less; exploring ambivalence before action planning; amplifying change talk; using complex, emotionally attuned reflections; explicitly supporting autonomy; and helping patient discover own values, reasons for change, and solutions rather than generating these clinician-directed.
