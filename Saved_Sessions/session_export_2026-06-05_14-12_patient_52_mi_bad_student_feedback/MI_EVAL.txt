### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2

Rather than exploring patient's understanding, concerns, and readiness collaboratively, clinician uses repeated yes/no questions to force compliance ("Are you going to stop it today? Yes or no?"). ### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates stark power imbalance where psychiatrist assumes complete authority and dismisses patient input. Key failures: "I'm going to tell you what you need to do" (unilateral declaration); "You should not worry about anything else right now" (dismissing patient's agenda); "You have to stop one of them immediately" (directive without exploration); "Just do it" (fear-based pressure). Patient attempts collaboration by explaining need to consult her doctor, but psychiatrist treats this as resistance to overcome rather than valid perspective. True collaboration would involve: "Your doctor knows your full medical history. How could we work together to get you connected with them quickly?" Only final statement acknowledges: "I should listen to your concerns and work with you," representing departure from entire preceding session.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist establishes hierarchical, expert-driven dynamic from opening: "I'm going to tell you what you need to do." While clinician possesses legitimate medical expertise about serotonin syndrome, this is used to justify complete control over decision-making rather than facilitate informed choice. Evidence of failed partnership: dismissing patient's concern ("You should not worry about anything else right now"); unilateral decision-making ("You have to stop one of them immediately"); rejecting patient's input about consulting doctor ("Hold on, you can't just delay this"); escalating pressure ("You have to stop citalopram immediately to avoid life-threatening effects"); framing as non-negotiable ("This isn't something to take lightly"). ### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist fails to evoke or amplify patient's change talk; instead imposes directives. Patient's genuine change talk statements include: "I just have a quick question about citalopram" (demonstrates safety concern); "Can you quickly tell me what the risk is?" (seeking information for informed decision); "I understand it's serious" (acknowledging importance); "I want to be safe too" (expressing safety motivation); "I'm willing to take it seriously" (demonstrating openness to change); "I appreciate your concern" (showing respect for clinician input). None of these are reflected, amplified, or developed. Skilled response to "I want to be safe too" would be: "Safety is really important to you. What would feel like the safest way to handle this?" Instead, psychiatrist responds with continued pressure and yes/no questions, which suppresses further change talk. Psychiatrist's own language about change is imposed ("You have to," "You must," "You need to") rather than evoking patient's reasons and commitment. Final statement acknowledges this error too late to demonstrate actual skill.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist's response to patient's sustain talk is confrontational rather than skillful. Patient's sustain talk is minimal but present: "I can't just stop without talking to my doctor first" and "I'm not ready to decide on my own right now." Rather than using double-sided reflection ("On one hand, you want to address this serious interaction. On the other hand, you want your doctor involved in any changes"), psychiatrist argues directly: "Hold on, you can't just delay this. You have to stop citalopram immediately to avoid life-threatening effects." This direct confrontation typically increases defensiveness and entrenchment. Patient's response demonstrates this—she becomes more firm: "I need to be sure and have my doctor's advice before stopping anything. I'm not comfortable making that decision on my own right now." Skillful approach would reframe patient's desire to consult doctor as aligned with safety goal: "It sounds like you want medication changes done safely with professional guidance. That's smart. How can we help you get in touch with your doctor quickly?" This would soften sustain talk by reframing as part of solution rather than resistance. Psychiatrist eventually recognizes error in final statement, but session demonstrates confrontational rather than skillful handling.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.2/5**

### Key Findings:

This session represents a **fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles** and demonstrates how well-intentioned clinical concern can be systematically undermined by authoritarian, pressure-based communication. The psychiatrist correctly identifies a serious medication interaction (citalopram-phenelzine serotonin syndrome risk) but pursues resolution through coercion rather than collaboration, resulting in **decreased rather than increased likelihood of patient follow-through**.

### Critical Pattern Observed:

The session demonstrates a counterproductive escalation cycle:
- Patient enters with genuine safety motivation and willingness to engage
- Psychiatrist applies increasing pressure and control
- Patient becomes more entrenched in her position with each escalation
- By session end, patient is less likely to follow through with either psychiatrist or primary care physician

This pattern illustrates how pressure tactics **backfire** and increase defensiveness rather than motivation.

### Strengths Identified:

- ✓ Correctly identifies legitimate clinical concern (serious drug interaction)
- ✓ Maintains focus on safety as clinical target
- ✓ Demonstrates self-awareness in final statement acknowledging approach error
- ✓ Underlying motivation to protect patient from harm is evident

### Critical Deficiencies:

- ✗ Systematic violation of MI spirit across all nine competencies
- ✗ Authoritarian stance with dismissal of patient input
- ✗ Pressure and coercion tactics ("no excuses," "just do it," repeated yes/no questions)
- ✗ Failure to evoke patient's own change talk or amplify motivation statements
- ✗ Dismissal of legitimate concerns (consulting doctor framed as "dangerous delay" rather than good judgment)
- ✗ Missed opportunity for collaboration on urgent timeline
- ✗ Confrontational handling of sustain talk increases defensiveness

### Clinical Outcome Impact:

Patient enters with change talk ("I want to be safe too," "I'm willing to take it seriously") but exits more entrenched in resistance. Therapeutic alliance is damaged. Trust is compromised. Likelihood of patient contacting either psychiatrist or primary care physician is reduced. **The irony: an MI-consistent approach would likely achieve the same clinical outcome (medication safety) while preserving alliance and increasing sustained engagement.**

### Immediate Recommendations:

1. **Urgent formal MI training and supervision required** before resuming independent clinical practice
2. **Shift from expert-driven to collaborative stance** in all patient interactions
3. **Develop reflective listening skills** to demonstrate genuine empathy
4. **Replace yes/no questions with open-ended evocative questions**
5. **Recognize and amplify patient change talk** rather than continuing to pressure
6. **Respect autonomy and reframe concerns as part of solution** rather than resistance
7. **Use double-sided reflections** to soften sustain talk without confrontation
8. **Establish urgency through collaboration** rather than pressure
9. **Develop genuine acceptance and unconditional positive regard** regardless of compliance
10. **Seek peer consultation and recorded session review** with MI-trained supervisor

### Conclusion:

This clinician demonstrates good clinical judgment in identifying the medication interaction but poor communication skills in addressing it. The stakes are high: patient health outcomes and willingness to engage with healthcare depend on clinicians' ability to communicate with respect, collaboration, and genuine partnership. **Systematic skill development through formal training and supervision is essential.**
