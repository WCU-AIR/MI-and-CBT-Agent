--- DISORDER ---
Schizophreniform Disorder (Provisional)

--- CASE STUDY ---

Hakim Coleman was a 25-year-old Black male U.S. Army veteran and current community college student who presented to the emergency room with his girlfriend and sister. He reported several days of “migraines” and “hallucinations of a spiritual nature” persisting for 3 months.

Mr. Coleman described sharp, bilateral headaches and a “ringing” sensation along the midline of his brain, which worsened when thinking about personal vices, including alcohol, cigarettes, disrespecting his parents, and relationships with women. He denied depressive, manic, or violent symptoms. Three months prior, he began hearing voices most days that attempted to make him feel guilty. He became socially withdrawn, and strangers reportedly commented on his past behaviors.

He had a history of heavy alcohol use (3–4 beers most days) until quitting 4 months earlier upon joining an evangelical church; occasional drinking now occurs (1–2 beers biweekly). Past cannabis use was sporadic (up to twice monthly) and ceased entirely after church initiation. No recent illicit drug use except one episode of cocaine 3 years earlier. Sleep was limited with occasional nightmares.

Physical exam, neurological screen, routine labs, blood alcohol, urine toxicology, and noncontrast CT head were all normal.

Collateral information from his girlfriend and sister indicated recent social isolation and religiosity, with no prior unusual religious interest. Church members described his guilt-related hallucinations as atypical.

Discussion: Differential diagnosis included PTSD, mood disorder with psychotic features, substance-induced psychosis, medical condition, cultural syndrome, or primary psychotic disorder. Psychotic symptoms preceded religious involvement, persisted despite minimal alcohol/cannabis use, and were accompanied by negative symptoms (blunted affect, social withdrawal). No evidence of mania or depression. Duration of 4–5 months indicates provisional schizophreniform disorder.
