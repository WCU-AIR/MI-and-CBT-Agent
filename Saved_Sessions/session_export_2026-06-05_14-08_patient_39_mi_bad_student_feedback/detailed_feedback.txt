
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1.5/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a severe and consistent failure to communicate unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly employs judgmental language, dismisses Elena's legitimate concerns, and frames her ambivalence as weakness or avoidance rather than a normal part of the change process.

In the opening segment, the psychiatrist states: "You really need to start taking better care of yourself" and "Don't let feelings like stress or fear stop you—that's just an excuse." This language is explicitly judgmental and invalidating. Elena's fear about contraceptive side effects and her need for time to process are reframed as character flaws ("You need to be strong and take control") rather than understandable human concerns. The psychiatrist also introduces smoking cessation—a topic Elena never mentioned—and demands immediate action: "Just stop the bad habits now; it's that simple." This demonstrates a fundamental lack of acceptance for where Elena actually is in her readiness for change.

When Elena appropriately expresses her ambivalence ("I'm still not sure what that would look like for me"), the psychiatrist's response in the third exchange reverts to the same authoritarian stance: "I'm going to give you a plan to follow exactly: quit smoking, eat better, exercise daily. No exceptions." This is coercive language that communicates conditional regard—Elena is only valued if she complies immediately.

The psychiatrist does demonstrate moments of acceptance in the second and fourth exchanges ("It's completely understandable to feel nervous" and "I want to acknowledge that the pregnancy scare you experienced must have been very frightening"). However, these brief moments are overwhelmed by the dominant pattern of judgment and dismissal. The ratio of accepting to rejecting statements is approximately 2:8, making acceptance fundamentally absent from this session.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate unconditional positive regard, repeatedly invalidating Elena's concerns and framing her ambivalence as weakness. Brief moments of acceptance are overwhelmed by judgmental, coercive language that communicates conditional regard based on immediate compliance.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective. This session shows minimal empathic attunement and several instances of failed perspective-taking.

In the opening exchange, the psychiatrist demonstrates no attempt to understand Elena's actual concerns. Instead of exploring her experience of the pregnancy scare or her ambivalence about contraception, the psychiatrist pivots to smoking cessation—a topic Elena never raised. This represents a fundamental failure to understand her perspective. The psychiatrist asks "Why haven't you quit yet?" without any prior discussion of smoking, demonstrating that the psychiatrist is imposing an agenda rather than understanding Elena's world.

The psychiatrist does offer one accurate reflection in the second exchange: "It sounds like you've been through a lot with the pregnancy scare, and that's understandably made you think a lot about your health." This is a basic, factual reflection that captures surface-level content but lacks emotional depth or nuance. A more empathic response would have explored *why* the scare was terrifying, what specifically frightened her about pregnancy, or what her relief tells us about her values.

In the third exchange, when Elena explicitly states "it feels like you're just telling me what to do without really hearing me," the psychiatrist responds by reverting to the same authoritarian stance rather than genuinely hearing this feedback. This is a critical empathic failure—Elena is directly communicating that she feels unheard, and the psychiatrist's response demonstrates that the feedback was not actually received or understood.

The fourth exchange shows improved empathy: "I want to acknowledge that the pregnancy scare you experienced must have been very frightening. I'm here to support you and understand your feelings." However, this comes after multiple failures and represents a single moment rather than a consistent pattern. The psychiatrist asks open-ended questions ("Could you share what your thoughts and concerns are?") that invite Elena's perspective, but this occurs only once in the entire session.

Elena's core concern—fear of contraceptive side effects and feeling overwhelmed by pressure—is never deeply explored or reflected back with emotional accuracy. The psychiatrist never captures the nuance of her ambivalence: that she simultaneously wants to prevent pregnancy AND fears the methods available to her.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal empathic attunement, failing to understand Elena's actual concerns and imposing an external agenda. One accurate but superficial reflection and one empathic opening statement are insufficient to overcome the dominant pattern of misunderstanding and dismissal.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Eliminate Agenda Imposition and Start Where the Client Is**
- **Action:** Begin every session by asking the client what brought them in and what they want to discuss. Do not introduce topics the client has not mentioned. If multiple health concerns exist, ask the client which they want to address first.
- **Example:** Instead of "I want to talk about some important health habits, and I'll be giving you some advice," say: "I'm glad you're here. What brought you in today? What would be most helpful to talk about?"
- **Practice:** In your next 5 sessions, spend the first 5 minutes asking open-ended questions about what the client wants to discuss. Do not introduce any topics until you fully understand the client's agenda. Record yourself and listen for how often you impose topics vs. follow the client's lead.

---

### 2. **Replace Confrontation with Collaborative Exploration of Ambivalence**
- **Action:** When a client expresses sustain talk or ambivalence, use double-sided reflections that validate both sides rather than confronting one side. This softens resistance without confrontation.
- **Example:** Instead of "Don't let feelings like stress or fear stop you—that's just an excuse," say: "So you're really motivated to prevent another pregnancy scare, and at the same time, you're worried about side effects and want to take time to decide. Both of those make sense. Tell me more about what concerns you most."
- **Practice:** In your next 5 sessions, when you hear sustain talk, pause and write down a double-sided reflection before responding. Practice using phrases like "On one hand... and on the other hand..." or "So you want to... and you're also concerned about..." Record yourself and count how many times you use collaborative exploration vs. confrontation.

---

### 3. **Validate and Explore Client Concerns Rather Than Dismissing Them**
- **Action:** When a client expresses a concern (like fear of side effects), treat it as legitimate and worthy of exploration rather than an obstacle to overcome. Ask questions to understand the concern more deeply.
- **Example:** Instead of "Don't let feelings like stress or fear stop you—that's just an excuse," say: "I hear that you're concerned about side effects. That's a really important concern. Tell me more about what you've heard or what worries you most. Let's talk through what's realistic and what might be misunderstanding."
- **Practice:** In your next 5 sessions, when a client expresses a concern, ask at least 3 follow-up questions to understand it fully before offering any information or reassurance. Practice phrases like "Tell me more about that," "What specifically concerns you?" and "Where did you hear that?"

---

### 4. **Use Evocative Questions to Draw Out Client's Own Motivations Rather Than Providing Arguments**
- **Action:** Replace statements like "You'll feel much better if you do" with questions like "What would it mean to you to prevent another pregnancy scare?" or "What would help you feel more confident about trying a contraceptive method?" This allows clients to discover their own reasons for change.
- **Example:** Instead of "Continuing these habits will only make things worse," ask: "What do you think might happen if things stay the same? What concerns you most about that?"
- **Practice:** In your next 5 sessions, for every argument you want to make for change, instead ask an evocative question. Record yourself and count the ratio of statements vs. questions. Aim for at least 70% questions and 30% statements.

---

### 5. **Support Autonomy and Client-Determined Pace Rather Than Demanding Immediate Compliance**
- **Action:** Replace language like "You have to," "You must," and "start immediately" with language that respects client choice and readiness. Offer options and ask what feels manageable.
- **Example:** Instead of "I'm going to give you a plan to follow exactly: quit smoking, eat better, exercise daily. No exceptions. Do you understand? Good. Now go ahead and start immediately," say: "There are several things we could focus on. What feels most important to you right now? What would feel manageable as a first step?"
- **Practice:** In your next 5 sessions, eliminate the words "have to," "must," "need to," and "should" from your vocabulary. Replace them with "might," "could," "what do you think about," and "what would feel right for you?" Record yourself and listen for these patterns.

---

### 6. **Cultivate and Amplify Client Change Talk**
- **Action:** When a client expresses change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment to change), respond in ways that amplify and develop it. Ask follow-up questions, reflect it back, and explore it more deeply.
- **Example:** When Elena says "I don't want that to happen again," respond with: "That pregnancy scare really affected you. Tell me more about what you don't want to happen. What would be different if you found a contraceptive method that felt right for you?"
- **Practice:** In your next 5 sessions, identify every instance of change talk. For each instance, ask at least one follow-up question that deepens or amplifies it. Record yourself and count how many times you amplify vs. ignore client change talk.

---

### 7. **Establish True Partnership by Sharing Power and Incorporating Client Input**
- **Action:** Use language that communicates partnership: "Let's figure this out together," "What do you think?" "How would you like to approach this?" Ask for the client's input on session direction and incorporate their ideas into the plan.
- **Example:** Instead of "I'm going to give you a plan to follow exactly," say: "I have some ideas about what might help, and I'm sure you have ideas too. What do you think would be most helpful? How would you like to approach this?"
- **Practice:** In your next 5 sessions, ask the client at least 5 times what they think should happen or how they want to approach something. Incorporate at least 3 of their ideas into the session plan. Record yourself and listen for how often you ask for client input vs. impose your own ideas.

---

### 8. **Develop Accurate Empathy Through Reflective Listening**
- **Action:** Use reflective listening to demonstrate that you understand the client's perspective, including the emotional nuance. Move beyond factual reflections to complex reflections that capture meaning and emotion.
- **Example:** Instead of "It sounds like you've been through a lot," say: "So the pregnancy scare really shook you up. You felt relieved when you found out you weren't pregnant, but it also made you realize how vulnerable you felt. And now you're trying to figure out how to prevent that from happening again, but you're also worried about whether the methods available will work for you or make you feel worse. That's a lot to navigate."
- **Practice:** In your next 5 sessions, practice offering at least 2 complex reflections per session that capture both content and emotion. Record yourself and listen for whether your reflections demonstrate genuine understanding of the client's perspective.

---

### 9. **Recognize and Respond to Feedback About Your Approach**
- **Action:** When a client explicitly tells you that your approach isn't working (like Elena did: "it feels like you're just telling me what to do without really hearing me"), pause and genuinely hear this feedback. Acknowledge it, apologize if appropriate, and shift your approach.
- **Example:** When Elena says "it feels like you're just telling me what to do without really hearing me," respond with: "You're right, and I appreciate you telling me that. I haven't been listening as well as I should. Let me start over. Tell me what's most important to you about this situation."
- **Practice:** In your next 5 sessions, if a client gives you feedback about your approach, pause and genuinely listen. Acknowledge the feedback, apologize, and ask what would be more helpful. Record yourself and listen for how you respond to client feedback.

---

### 10. **Complete MI Training and Supervision**
- **Action:** This session demonstrates fundamental gaps in MI competence that require formal training and supervision. Enroll in a comprehensive MI training program (at least 2 days) and engage in ongoing clinical supervision with an MI expert.
- **Example:** Seek out MI training through organizations like MINT (Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers) or similar programs in your region. Participate in role-plays, receive feedback on recorded sessions, and practice with supervision.
- **Practice:** Complete at least 40 hours of MI training and 10 hours of clinical supervision focused on MI. Record your sessions and review them with a supervisor who can provide specific feedback on your MI competence.
