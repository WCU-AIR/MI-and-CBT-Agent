# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates surface-level politeness but lacks genuine, sustained unconditional positive regard. While the opening is warm, acceptance is undermined by early pivoting to advice-giving and problem-solving. The psychiatrist fails to normalize Adrian's ambivalence, affirm his autonomy, or validate his concerns beyond superficial acknowledgment. No explicit affirmation of Adrian's worth or courage in attending the session is present. The tone becomes increasingly directive and prescriptive, communicating judgment rather than acceptance of Adrian as a person struggling with mixed feelings about change.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist offers basic, surface-level reflections that capture content but miss emotional depth and nuance. Critical moments of shame, ambivalence, and internal conflict go unacknowledged. When Adrian expresses embarrassment about disclosure ("I don't really want them knowing what I'm up to. Feels kinda embarrassing"), no empathic reflection follows. The psychiatrist fails to capture Adrian's all-or-nothing thinking or the complexity of his mixed feelings about change. Reflections remain shallow (e.g., "It sounds like the infection is uncomfortable but you're not sure if it's enough to stop using") rather than exploring the underlying emotional experience and conflict Adrian is experiencing.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The session maintains general focus on Adrian's methamphetamine use and the infection as a health concern, with appropriate early questions about readiness to change. However, direction becomes increasingly prescriptive rather than exploratory. By the third exchange, the psychiatrist imposes direction through suggestions (finding other activities, cutting down, avoiding times/places, talking to friends) without first exploring Adrian's own ideas and self-identified barriers in depth. Direction is clinician-driven rather than collaboratively discovered. A more MI-consistent approach would maintain focus while allowing Adrian to discover his own direction through guided exploration.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist assumes an expert, directive role throughout rather than behaving as an equal partner. All solutions are generated by the psychiatrist (finding activities, cutting down, avoiding triggers, setting goals), not by Adrian. When Adrian expresses hesitation or resistance, the psychiatrist does not pause to genuinely collaborate on understanding his perspective. Adrian's ideas and concerns are treated as obstacles to overcome rather than valuable information to explore together. Power remains with the clinician. Adrian's role is to accept or gently resist suggestions, not to co-create the session direction. By the final exchange, Adrian has become compliant rather than engaged.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no autonomy support and employs subtle pressure throughout. Prescriptive language dominates: "It would probably help if," "You might want to," "It's important to," "You should try," "Maybe you should." These minimize Adrian's agency and choice. The psychiatrist never explicitly affirms Adrian's right to choose, including the right to continue using. When Adrian expresses ambivalence or resistance, the psychiatrist does not validate his autonomy. The final statement—"Remember, it's your choice, but taking small steps like this could make a difference"—uses "but" to subtly undermine the autonomy statement. Adrian's response reflects compliance rather than intrinsic motivation.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist asks some evocative questions but fails to follow up to deepen change talk. When Adrian expresses worry about drug purity ("I guess the whole 'not knowing what's mixed in' with the stuff I get is kind of worrying sometimes"), the psychiatrist does not explore this concern further. When Adrian mentions soccer, the psychiatrist does not explore what he enjoyed about it or whether he'd want to return to it. The overall pattern is directive advice-giving rather than systematic evocation of Adrian's own motivations, values, and ideas. The psychiatrist never explores Adrian's deeper values, identity, or life goals that might motivate change. Critical opportunities to draw out intrinsic motivation are consistently missed.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no partnership and actively assumes the expert role. The psychiatrist generates all solutions; Adrian does not. When Adrian expresses his own ideas (soccer, outdoor activities), the psychiatrist transforms them into directives rather than exploring Adrian's thinking. The psychiatrist never explicitly acknowledges Adrian's expertise about his own life. Statements affirming Adrian's knowledge ("You know your situation better than I do," "What do you think would actually work for you?") are absent. The power dynamic is consistently unequal. Adrian's contributions do not substantially influence the session direction. This is expert-driven, not partnership-based.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Adrian offers multiple instances of change talk that are not explored or deepened: worry about drug purity, discomfort with infection, ideas about soccer and outdoor activities, and tentative commitment ("I guess giving it a shot once or twice wouldn't hurt"). In each instance, the psychiatrist fails to amplify this change talk. After Adrian expresses worry about drug purity, the psychiatrist immediately shifts to exploring sustain talk rather than deepening the concern. When Adrian mentions soccer, the psychiatrist does not explore this as a potential source of motivation. The psychiatrist never systematically evokes change talk across the four domains (desire, ability, reasons, need). Focus remains narrowly on the infection as a reason for change.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist acknowledges Adrian's sustain talk but does not skillfully soften it. Adrian offers substantial arguments for continuing use (feeling in control, boredom relief, social connection, all-or-nothing thinking, fear of missing out), yet the psychiatrist either ignores these or indirectly confronts them through advice-giving. Double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic exploration of discrepancy are absent. When Adrian says "If I'm just gonna half-ass it, what's the point? Feels like it's all or nothing," the psychiatrist does not explore this important insight about his cognitive pattern. The psychiatrist does not help Adrian develop discrepancy between his values and his behavior, nor does he use curiosity to deepen understanding of Adrian's barriers to change.

---

### **Overall Session Score: 1.9/5**

---

## Summary Assessment

**Strengths:**
- Appropriate warm opening that invites Adrian to share
- Maintains focus on methamphetamine use and health consequences
- Asks some evocative questions designed to explore motivations and barriers
- Acknowledges Adrian's ambivalence without aggressive confrontation
- Attempts to establish concrete next steps

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Pervasive directiveness:** Prescriptive language dominates; the psychiatrist tells Adrian what to do rather than evoking his own ideas
- **Failure to amplify change talk:** Multiple instances of Adrian's own motivation are not explored or strengthened
- **Inadequate handling of sustain talk:** Arguments for continuing use are not skillfully addressed; barriers remain unexplored
- **Minimal empathy and acceptance:** Critical moments of shame and ambivalence go unacknowledged
- **Expert-driven rather than collaborative:** The psychiatrist assumes the role of expert with solutions; Adrian's ideas do not substantially influence direction
- **Weak autonomy support:** No explicit affirmation of Adrian's right to choose; subtle pressure undermines agency
- **Insufficient exploration of values and strengths:** Intrinsic motivation is not accessed

**Impact on Client Engagement:**
This session is unlikely to increase Adrian's intrinsic motivation for change. Adrian's compliance with the suggested activity reflects acquiescence to authority rather than genuine motivation. Adrian's ambivalence remains unresolved, his shame is unaddressed, and his sustain talk has not been worked through. Without genuine intrinsic motivation, sustained behavior change is unlikely.

**Recommendation:**
The psychiatrist must fundamentally shift from an expert, directive stance to a collaborative, evocative stance. The goal is to help Adrian discover his own reasons for change, not to convince him. This requires systematic use of open-ended questions, reflective listening, amplification of change talk, skillful handling of sustain talk, and explicit support for autonomy.
