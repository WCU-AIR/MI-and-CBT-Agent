### Acceptance

### Collaboration

Repeatedly makes unilateral decisions without soliciting client input: "you need to start now," "you must take this seriously," "you have to make a firm decision," "you must quit now." Expert authority maintained throughout.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist minimizes choice and maximizes pressure throughout session. Language consistently controlling: "you really should," "you need to," "you must," "you have to," "it's the only way." These phrases communicate that client has no genuine choice—compliance is mandatory. When client expresses hesitation about complete abstinence, psychiatrist responds with escalating pressure rather than affirming right to choose: "If you don't quit completely, you're risking your health and future. You must quit now." Imposed behavioral goal with demand for commitment backed by threat: "If you slip up, it's not an option." This frames agreement as non-negotiable rather than autonomous choice. Even when client agrees to try week, psychiatrist responds with further pressure and threat rather than affirming autonomy. Only moment of autonomy support appears in final exchange but is isolated and contradicts entire preceding pattern.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist tells client why he should change rather than asking client to explore own reasons. Provides clinician-imposed reasons for change without exploring client's perspective: "drinking can mess up your health," "you're risking your health and future." Fails to amplify or explore client's spontaneous change talk: client states "I didn't wanna mess stuff up or get in more trouble" (implicit desire to avoid consequences) and psychiatrist ignores this; client states "I wanna do well in school too" (stated value) and psychiatrist never explores connection to drinking. Uses closed yes/no questions rather than open-ended evocative questions. No curiosity about client's reasons for drinking, values, strengths, or own ideas about change. Final exchange shows one evocative question but insufficient to overcome pervasive absence of evocation throughout session.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively assumes and maintains expert role throughout, communicating that clinician possesses knowledge and client must comply. Opening statement positions psychiatrist as expert with solutions: "I have some ideas I think you should follow." Stance reinforced repeatedly: "you really should think about cutting back," "you need to start now," "you must take this seriously," "you must quit now," "it's the only way to be safe." Never invites client to be expert on own experience, values, or potential solutions. Makes unilateral decisions about what client should do without exploring alignment with client's thinking or resources. No genuine power-sharing; psychiatrist retains authority and client positioned as passive recipient of expert advice. Final exchange represents slight shift but does not constitute genuine partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist fails to recognize, explore, or amplify client's spontaneous change talk. Multiple instances of change talk go unrecognized: (1) Desire for change: "I didn't wanna mess stuff up or get in more trouble"—ignored; (2) Recognition of problem: "I wanna say I have a drinking problem"—never explored; (3) Value alignment: "I wanna do well in school too"—never connected to drinking; (4) Commitment language: "I know I have to figure something out"—met with prescription; (5) Ability: "I tried to be safe. I tried to control how much I drank"—dismissed as insufficient; (6) Willingness: "I wanna prove to myself that I can handle it"—met with threat; (7) Problem recognition: "I know I've been drinking a lot, and sometimes it gets me into trouble"—never explored. Instead of evoking and amplifying change talk, psychiatrist provides reasons for change and demands compliance. Never asks open-ended questions designed to elicit change talk: "What concerns you most about your drinking?" "What would be different if you cut back?" "How might reducing your drinking help you achieve your goals?"

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist handles sustain talk poorly, often through confrontation and pressure rather than skillful redirection. Client offers sustain talk throughout: "It's pretty normal," "part of the whole college experience," "tough to stop when all my friends are doing it," "big part of my social life," "makes things more fun and helps me relax." Psychiatrist's response is consistently confrontational: "you really have to stop making excuses," "you can't keep making excuses about your friends," "Don't let peer pressure ruin your life." These direct challenges may increase defensiveness rather than softening sustain talk. Never uses double-sided reflections acknowledging both appeal of drinking and reasons to change. When client expresses concern about social consequences ("I don't want to feel left out"), psychiatrist dismisses rather than exploring with curiosity—missed opportunity to soften sustain talk through understanding. Approach of pressure and threat may temporarily suppress sustain talk but represents suppression through coercion, which typically leads to increased sustain talk or dropout rather than genuine softening.

## Overall Assessment Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.2/5**

**MI Competency Level:** Minimal to Non-Existent

**Session Embodies MI Spirit:** No

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Intrinsic Motivation:** Low

---

### Critical Findings:

This session represents a **fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles and spirit**. The psychiatrist operates from an expert-driven, coercive stance that violates nearly every core MI competency. The clinician communicates judgment rather than acceptance, dismisses perspective rather than demonstrating empathy, imposes solutions rather than evoking motivation, demands compliance rather than supporting autonomy, maintains expert authority rather than fostering partnership, and fails to recognize or amplify spontaneous change talk.

**Approach Utilized:** Traditional directive counseling—telling the client what to do and threatening consequences for non-compliance. While this may produce short-term behavioral compliance (client agrees to week-long trial), it undermines intrinsic motivation and is likely to result in relapse, dropout, or continued ambivalence.

**Impact on Therapeutic Alliance and Change Likelihood:**

- **Increased Defensiveness:** Client's responses show growing hesitation ("I guess," "I'm still kinda worried," "I'm not making any promises beyond that"), suggesting pressure triggers resistance rather than evoking motivation.
- **Undermined Intrinsic Motivation:** Client's agreement appears compliance-based rather than internally motivated. Research indicates compliance-based change is less durable than intrinsically motivated change.
- **Damaged Therapeutic Alliance:** Judgmental stance and dismissal of concerns communicate lack of respect and understanding, eroding trust and reducing likelihood of honest disclosure.
- **Missed Opportunities:** Client's stated values (education, avoiding trouble, proving to himself he can handle it) never explored or connected to drinking behavior—powerful levers for change remain untapped.
- **Entrenched Ambivalence:** Pressure to commit to complete abstinence when client expresses preference for moderation may entrench ambivalence rather than resolve it.

---

### Most Critical Improvements Needed:

1. **Shift from expert to collaborative stance** — Foundational change required; all other improvements flow from this
2. **Develop reflective listening and empathy** — Understand and validate client's perspective before offering guidance
3. **Evoke rather than impose** — Ask open-ended questions to draw out client's own motivations and values
4. **Support autonomy** — Communicate that change is client's choice and emphasize their control
5. **Recognize and amplify change talk** — Listen for statements favoring change and explore them further

---

### Recommendation:

**Psychiatrist should engage in formal MI training and clinical supervision before continuing MI-informed sessions.** This session demonstrates that without proper training, well-intentioned efforts to address substance use can inadvertently undermine the therapeutic process and reduce likelihood of positive outcomes.
