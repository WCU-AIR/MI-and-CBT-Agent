### PART II. CONCEPTUALIZATION, STRATEGY, AND TECHNIQUE

| Item | Expectation (CTRS Descriptor) | Criterion (CTRS 4/6) | Rating (0-6) | Comments |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 7. Guided Discovery | Uses questioning to enable patient self-discovery (Socratic Method). | Generally used questioning to enable the patient to discover new perspectives on dysfunctional thinking and beliefs. Adeptly helped the patient challenge core beliefs/assumptions. | **1** | Guided discovery was essentially absent. Therapist relied entirely on direct advice and suggestion rather than Socratic questioning. Examples of therapist's approach: "You could try thinking about it differently, like 'this might just be a temporary thing'..." "Maybe it would help to do something distracting, like going for a walk..." "Maybe it would help to try and catch those thoughts when they pop up..." All direct suggestions, not guided discovery. Effective Socratic approach would involve: "When you think 'What if this is something serious,' what evidence supports that thought? What evidence goes against it?" "You mentioned doctors did extensive testing. What would it mean if they truly found nothing dangerous?" "When you avoid walking because worried it makes legs worse, what actually happens—does pain increase or does worry increase?" Patient was never invited to examine beliefs through evidence or generate alternative explanations. Patient was not guided to become scientist testing her own thoughts; instead told what to think. When patient expressed doubt ("it feels… kind of fake"), therapist did not use as opportunity for guided discovery ("What makes it feel fake? What would need to be true for it to feel real?"). Instead offered reassurance and moved on. Guided discovery is particularly critical for SSD; patients must learn to evaluate own catastrophic thoughts and test safety behaviors. Without this skill, patient remains dependent on therapist's reassurance—which reinforces disorder. |
| 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors | Identifies and targets the most central dysfunctional thoughts or core beliefs. | Usually focused on appropriate target cognitions or behaviors that were clearly central to the patient's distress. | **2** | Therapist identified some relevant cognitions but failed to focus on most central ones maintaining SSD. Correctly identified catastrophic thoughts ("What if this is the start of something they missed?") but did not systematically explore core SSD cognitions: (1) Health threat beliefs ("My symptoms mean something serious is wrong"), (2) Interoceptive misinterpretation ("Normal bodily sensations are signs of disease"), (3) Safety behavior beliefs ("I must monitor symptoms constantly to catch illness early"), (4) Reassurance-seeking beliefs ("If doctors say nothing is wrong, I need more tests to be sure"). Therapist touched on catastrophizing but did not explore patient's actual illness beliefs or function of symptom monitoring and online research—explicitly mentioned in case formulation as key SSD maintenance factors. Regarding behaviors, therapist identified some (avoidance of walking, reduced knitting) but did not focus on most critical SSD behaviors: Symptom monitoring ("spent significant time documenting symptoms, linking them to vital signs")—never addressed. Health-related research ("significant time researching symptoms online")—never explored. Reassurance-seeking ("frequent reassurance-seeking and medical visits")—not addressed. Avoidance ("normal physical activity")—only briefly mentioned. Therapist's focus on "thinking differently" and "distraction" addresses neither core beliefs nor key behaviors. For SSD, most central targets are: (1) reducing symptom-focused attention and monitoring, (2) reducing reassurance-seeking and health-related research, (3) increasing activity despite symptoms, (4) testing belief that symptoms indicate serious illness. Focus was scattered rather than strategic. |
| 9. Strategy for Change | Establishes and executes a clear, coherent, and individualized cognitive-behavioral plan. | Pursued a generally coherent and appropriate cognitive-behavioral strategy for change. Strategy seemed "custom tailored" to the patient's key cognitions. | **1** | No coherent, evidence-based strategy for change evident. Interventions offered (cognitive restructuring, distraction, thought-catching) are not primary evidence-based approaches for SSD and do not target disorder's maintenance mechanisms. Evidence-based SSD strategy involves: (1) Psychoeducation about mind-body connection and how attention/anxiety amplify symptoms, (2) Behavioral activation and graded exposure to feared activities despite symptoms, (3) Attention retraining to reduce symptom-focused monitoring, (4) Reduction of reassurance-seeking and health-related research, (5) Cognitive work focused on testing health threat beliefs through behavioral experiments, (6) Acceptance of symptoms as non-threatening. Therapist's strategy was: Suggest cognitive restructuring ("think more positively"), Suggest distraction ("do something distracting"), Suggest thought-catching ("notice your thoughts"). This is generic anxiety approach, not SSD-specific. Cognitive restructuring alone is ineffective for SSD because symptoms are real and distressing—telling patient "it's probably nothing" does not address core problem: excessive attention to and catastrophic interpretation of normal bodily sensations. Critically, therapist offered reassurance multiple times ("doctors are trained," "if they didn't find anything, it's probably nothing dangerous"), which is contraindicated in SSD. Reassurance reinforces health anxiety by validating patient's need for certainty about health—a need that cannot be permanently satisfied and thus perpetuates disorder. No clear progression toward goals. Therapist did not establish what success looks like (e.g., "reduced symptom monitoring," "increased activity," "reduced reassurance-seeking") or how interventions connect to those goals. Strategy was not individualized to SSD; appeared generic. |
| 10. Application of Cognitive-Behavioral Techniques | Selects and executes techniques with precision and appropriate rationale. | Selected and applied appropriate cognitive and/or behavioral techniques for the target problem. Techniques were skillfully executed with appropriate timing and rationale. | **2** | Therapist attempted cognitive restructuring but applied it poorly and without systematic structure required for effective CBT. **Cognitive Restructuring Attempt:** Identified catastrophic thoughts ("What if this is the start of something they missed?"), Suggested alternative thoughts ("This might just be a temporary thing"), Encouraged practice. **Problems with Application:** (1) No thought record or systematic structure—informal advice-giving, not systematic cognitive restructuring. (2) No evidence examination—did not ask patient to examine evidence for/against catastrophic thoughts. Effective cognitive restructuring involves: identifying thought, examining evidence, considering alternatives, evaluating which thought most realistic. None occurred. (3) Premature alternative thought—therapist jumped to alternative without patient generating it. Effective CBT involves guided discovery where patient generates alternatives. Therapist imposed alternatives. (4) Ignoring patient resistance—when patient said alternative thought "feels… kind of fake," therapist did not address this. In effective cognitive restructuring, if alternative doesn't feel believable, therapist explores why and adjusts approach. Therapist simply repeated same suggestion. (5) No behavioral experiment—did not suggest testing catastrophic thought through behavioral experimentation (e.g., "Let's see what happens when you do short walk—does leg pain increase or does worry increase?"). Critical component of cognitive work in CBT. **Behavioral Interventions Attempted:** Distraction ("do something distracting, like going for a walk"), Activity scheduling (suggested knitting and short walks). **Problems with Application:** (1) No graded exposure plan—suggested activities without systematic plan. For patient avoiding walking due to health anxiety, effective behavioral work involves: identifying feared outcome, creating graded hierarchy, planning exposure with clear rationale, predicting vs. observing outcomes. None occurred. (2) No rationale for behavioral change—did not explain why activity important for SSD (e.g., "Activity helps us test whether symptoms are as dangerous as you fear, and reduces focus on symptoms"). Without rationale, patients less likely to comply. (3) Distraction as primary strategy—problematic for SSD. While distraction provides temporary relief, it reinforces idea that symptoms are dangerous (hence need to distract from them). For SSD, goal is to reduce symptom-focused attention through acceptance and behavioral activation, not distraction. Application was superficial and inconsistent with evidence-based CBT standards. |
| 11. Homework | Reviews previous homework and assigns new, relevant, and "custom tailored" assignments. | Reviewed previous homework and assigned "standard" cognitive therapy homework. Homework was explained in sufficient detail. | **1** | Homework was assigned but with critical deficiencies in specificity, rationale, and follow-up. **First Session Homework:** (1) "Notice your thoughts when symptoms come up and try to catch yourself before panic gets too bad," (2) "Keep doing small things you enjoy, like knitting or short walks," (3) "Remind yourself that thoughts are just thoughts, not facts." **Problems:** (1) Lack of specificity—"Notice your thoughts" is vague. Effective homework specifies: When exactly? How will you record them? What format? Therapist did not provide thought record, worksheet, or specific instructions. (2) No clear rationale—patient not told why homework important or how connects to core problem. Therapist said "We can check in next time" but did not explain purpose. (3) Insufficient detail on behavioral component—"Keep doing small things you enjoy" is vague. Effective homework specifies: Which activities? How often? For how long? What should you notice while doing them? What if symptoms increase—what should you do? (4) No written materials—therapist did not provide thought record, activity log, or written homework sheet. Effective CBT provides structured worksheets. (5) No commitment check—therapist did not explicitly ask: "Do you think you can do this? What might get in the way? How will you remember to do it?" Essential for homework compliance. **Second Session:** Therapist did not review previous week's homework. Critical CBT failure. Therapist should have asked: "How did thought-catching go last week?" "Were you able to do any walks or knitting?" "What did you notice?" "What was easy? What was hard?" "What did you learn?" Instead, therapist opened with generic agenda-setting question, completely bypassing homework review. This signals to patient that homework is not important and undermines collaborative, structured nature of CBT. **Homework Assignment Quality:** For SSD, effective homework would target: (1) Reducing symptom monitoring ("Limit checking symptoms to twice per day at set times. Notice what happens to anxiety"), (2) Reducing reassurance-seeking ("Resist urge to research symptoms online or call doctor for reassurance. Notice what happens to anxiety"), (3) Behavioral activation ("Do 10-minute walk Monday, Wednesday, Friday. Before each, predict what will happen. After, record what actually happened"), (4) Attention retraining ("When you notice yourself focusing on symptoms, redirect attention to surroundings. Notice 5 things you see, 4 you hear, 3 you touch"). None of these were assigned. Homework was generic and not tailored to SSD maintenance mechanisms. |
