
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made minimal effort to establish a structured, collaborative agenda. In the opening ("I was thinking we could just talk a bit about what's been on your mind lately"), there is vague language without specific target problems identified or prioritization. The therapist never explicitly asked the patient which symptoms or concerns should be the focus, nor did they establish time boundaries or session structure. A competent CBT therapist would have said something like: "Norma, I know you're dealing with several physical symptoms and worry about them. The patient's extensive symptom list and health anxiety require deliberate prioritization to prevent session drift and to target the core SSD maintenance mechanisms (excessive health-related thoughts, reassurance-seeking, symptom monitoring). Without agenda-setting, the session becomes reactive rather than strategic.

**Summary:**

No explicit agenda was established in either session opening. The therapist used vague, open-ended language without collaboratively identifying target problems, priorities, or session structure—a fundamental CBT requirement.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish SSD-Specific Conceptualization and Treatment Plan**
- **Action**: Develop a clear, written case formulation that identifies the patient's core SSD maintenance mechanisms: (a) catastrophic health beliefs, (b) excessive symptom-focused attention and monitoring, (c) reassurance-seeking and health-related research, (d) avoidance of normal activities, (e) interoceptive misinterpretation. Create a treatment plan that explicitly targets these mechanisms.
- **Example**: "Norma's symptoms are maintained by her belief that normal bodily sensations indicate serious illness, combined with her constant monitoring of symptoms and reassurance-seeking through medical visits and online research. This attention amplifies her awareness of symptoms and anxiety. Treatment will focus on: (1) reducing symptom-focused attention through attention retraining, (2) reducing reassurance-seeking and health-related research, (3) behavioral activation despite symptoms, and (4) testing health threat beliefs through behavioral experiments."
- **Practice**: Review SSD treatment manuals (e.g., Barsky & Ahern's "Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Hypochondriasis"; Glombiewski et al.'s SSD treatment protocols). Attend training on SSD-specific CBT. Consult with a supervisor experienced in SSD treatment.

### 2. **Eliminate Reassurance-Giving; Shift to Collaborative Exploration**
- **Action**: Stop providing reassurance about the patient's health ("the doctors are trained," "if they didn't find anything, it's probably nothing dangerous"). Instead, use collaborative exploration to help the patient test her own beliefs through behavioral experiments.
- **Example**: Instead of "The doctors are trained and careful, so if they didn't find anything, it's probably nothing dangerous," say: "I notice you're worried the doctors might have missed something. That's a common concern. What if we designed an experiment to test whether your symptoms actually get worse when you do normal activities, or whether your worry gets worse? That way, you can gather your own evidence rather than relying on reassurance."
- **Practice**: Role-play with a supervisor. Practice saying "I understand you're worried about this. Rather than me reassuring you, let's work together to test whether your worry is accurate" in response to reassurance-seeking. Recognize that the urge to reassure is often the therapist's own anxiety—supervision can help address this.

### 3. **Implement Systematic Agenda-Setting at the Start of Each Session**
- **Action**: Begin each session with explicit, collaborative agenda-setting that includes: (a) review of previous week's homework, (b) identification of target problems, (c) prioritization among multiple concerns, (d) time allocation, (e) explicit agreement on session focus.
- **Example**: "Norma, I know you're dealing with several physical symptoms and worry about them. Last week, we talked about noticing your thoughts and doing some activities. Before we dive in, I'd like to check: How did that go? What was most helpful? What was challenging? And today, I'd like us to focus on understanding why you spend so much time monitoring your symptoms and researching online—I think that's a key piece we need to address. Does that sound like a good use of our time, or is there something else you'd like to prioritize?"
- **Practice**: Write out an agenda-setting script and practice it with each patient. Use a session structure template (e.g., opening, agenda, review homework, work on target, assign homework, feedback, closing). Ensure you follow it consistently.

### 4. **Implement Systematic Cognitive Restructuring with Evidence Examination and Behavioral Experiments**
- **Action**: When working with cognitions, use a structured approach: (a) identify the automatic thought, (b) examine evidence for and against it, (c) generate alternative thoughts, (d) design a behavioral experiment to test the thought, (e) review the experiment results.
- **Example**: 
  - Therapist: "You mentioned the thought 'What if this is the start of something serious they missed?' Let's examine this thought together. What evidence do you have that supports this thought?"
  - Patient: "Well, my symptoms are real and they keep coming back."
  - Therapist: "Okay, so the symptoms are real. But does that mean they're serious? What evidence do you have that they're serious?"
  - Patient: "The doctors haven't found anything, but that doesn't mean it's not serious."
  - Therapist: "Right, so you're worried they missed something. What evidence do you have that they missed something? Have they ever missed something before?"
  - Patient: "No, not that I know of."
  - Therapist: "Okay. So what if we designed an experiment to test this? You mentioned you've been avoiding walks because you're worried they'll make your legs worse. What if we did a short walk and you predicted beforehand what would happen—would your leg pain increase, or would your worry increase? Then we'd see what actually happens. That way, you'd have your own evidence rather than just worrying."
- **Practice**: Use a thought record worksheet with every patient. Practice evidence examination with a supervisor. Learn to ask "What evidence do you have?" rather than providing reassurance.

### 5. **Implement Systematic Behavioral Activation and Graded Exposure**
- **Action**: Create a graded hierarchy of avoided activities (e.g., walking, physical exertion, activities that trigger symptom awareness). Systematically work through the hierarchy with clear rationale, prediction-testing, and outcome review.
- **Example**: 
  - Therapist: "I notice you've been avoiding walks because you're worried they'll make your legs worse. In SSD, avoidance actually maintains the problem because it reinforces the belief that activities are dangerous. What if we worked together to gradually increase your activity? We'd start small, predict what will happen, do the activity, and then review what actually happened. This helps you test whether your worry is accurate."
  - Create a hierarchy: (1) 5-minute walk around the house, (2) 10-minute walk around the block, (3) 15-minute walk in the park, (4) 20-minute walk with mild exertion, etc.
  - For each step: (a) predict symptom change and anxiety change, (b) do the activity, (c) record actual symptom change and anxiety change, (d) review the results.
- **Practice**: Learn graded exposure protocols. Practice creating hierarchies with a supervisor. Use an exposure worksheet with each patient.

### 6. **Implement Attention Retraining to Reduce Symptom-Focused Monitoring**
- **Action**: Teach the patient to redirect attention away from symptoms and toward external environment. This is a core intervention for SSD because excessive symptom-focused attention amplifies symptom awareness and anxiety.
- **Example**: 
  - Therapist: "One thing that maintains SSD is that we pay a lot of attention to our symptoms, which makes us more aware of them and more anxious. It's like if I told you 'Don't think about your left foot,' you'd suddenly become very aware of your left foot. What if we practiced redirecting your attention? When you notice yourself focusing on your symptoms, you could redirect to your surroundings—notice 5 things you can see, 4 you can hear, 3 you can touch. This helps break the cycle of symptom-focused attention."
  - Homework: "This week, practice attention redirection 3 times per day. When you notice yourself focusing on symptoms, redirect to your surroundings using the 5-4-3 technique. Record what you notice."
- **Practice**: Learn attention retraining techniques. Practice with a supervisor. Teach the technique to patients and review their experience.

### 7. **Implement Systematic Reduction of Reassurance-Seeking and Health-Related Research**
- **Action**: Identify the patient's reassurance-seeking behaviors (medical visits, online research, asking family members, checking vital signs) and systematically reduce them through behavioral experiments and acceptance work.
- **Example**: 
  - Therapist: "I notice you spend a lot of time researching your symptoms online and visiting doctors. This is understandable—you want to know what's going on. But in SSD, reassurance-seeking actually maintains the problem because it temporarily reduces anxiety but then the anxiety comes back, so you seek more reassurance. It's a cycle. What if we experimented with reducing your online research? For example, this week, you could limit yourself to researching symptoms only once per day for 15 minutes, rather than throughout the day. Then we'd see what happens to your anxiety. Does it actually increase, or does it eventually decrease?"
  - Homework: "This week, limit symptom-related online research to once per day for 15 minutes. Record your anxiety before, during, and after. Notice what happens."
- **Practice**: Learn acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) techniques for managing the urge to seek reassurance. Practice with a supervisor. Help patients understand the reassurance-seeking cycle.

### 8. **Implement Systematic Feedback Elicitation Throughout Sessions**
- **Action**: Regularly check for understanding, explore patient reactions to interventions, and solicit feedback about session helpfulness. Do this at multiple points: during the session when introducing interventions, at the end of the session, and at the start of the next session.
- **Example**: 
  - During session: "I've suggested we try reducing your online research. How does that land for you? Does it feel realistic, or does something about it not quite fit?"
  - End of session: "What was most helpful today? Is there anything that didn't make sense? How are you feeling about the plan we discussed?"
  - Start of next session: "How did last week go? What was helpful? What was challenging? What did you learn?"
- **Practice**: Write out feedback-elicitation questions and practice them with each patient. Make feedback-checking a routine part of your session structure.

### 9. **Implement Structured Homework with Written Materials and Clear Rationale**
- **Action**: Assign specific, structured homework with written worksheets, clear rationale, and explicit commitment checks. Always review homework at the start of the next session.
- **Example**: 
  - Homework assignment: "This week, I'd like you to do three things:
    1. **Thought Record**: When you notice a catastrophic thought about your symptoms (e.g., 'This is serious'), write it down on this thought record. Then examine the evidence for and against it. This helps you test whether your worry is accurate. Do this 3 times this week.
    2. **Behavioral Experiment**: Do a 10-minute walk on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. Before each walk, predict what will happen (Will your leg pain increase? Will your anxiety increase?). After the walk, record what actually happened. This helps you test whether activities are as dangerous as you fear.
    3. **Attention Redirection**: When you notice yourself focusing on symptoms, practice the 5-4-3 technique (5 things you see, 4 you hear, 3 you touch). Do this 3 times this week. Record what you notice."
  - Provide written worksheets for each task.
  - Commitment check: "Do you think you can do this? What might get in the way? How will you remember to do it?"
  - Next session: "How did the homework go? What did you learn from the thought record? What did you discover from the walks? What was easy? What was hard?"
- **Practice**: Create homework worksheets for each intervention. Practice assigning homework with clear rationale. Always review homework at the start of the next session.

### 10. **Develop Competence in SSD-Specific CBT Through Training and Supervision**
- **Action**: Recognize that SSD requires specialized knowledge and skills. Seek training, supervision, and consultation from experts in SSD treatment.
- **Example**: 
  - Attend workshops or trainings on SSD and health anxiety treatment
  - Read treatment manuals: Barsky & Ahern (2004), Glombiewski et al. (2014), Tyrer et al. (2011)
  - Seek regular supervision from a therapist experienced in SSD treatment
  - Join consultation groups focused on SSD and medically unexplained symptoms
  - Review session recordings with a supervisor to identify areas for improvement
- **Practice**: Make a commitment to ongoing learning. Schedule regular supervision. Review your SSD cases with a supervisor monthly. Track your competence using the CTRS or similar measures.

### 11. **Implement Systematic Session Structure and Tracking**
- **Action**: Use a consistent session structure for every session: (1) opening/agenda, (2) homework review, (3) work on target problem, (4) assign homework, (5) feedback/closing. Track what you do in each session to ensure consistency and identify patterns.
- **Example**: 
  - Session structure template:
    - Opening (2 min): "Hi Norma, good to see you. How have you been?"
    - Agenda (3 min): "Last week we talked about [X]. Today I'd like to focus on [Y]. Does that work for you?"
    - Homework review (5 min): "How did the homework go? What did you learn?"
    - Work on target (35 min): [Specific intervention]
    - Homework assignment (5 min): "This week, I'd like you to [specific task]. Here's a worksheet to help. Do you think you can do this?"
    - Feedback (3 min): "What was most helpful today? Is there anything that didn't make sense?"
    - Closing (2 min): "Great work. I'll see you next week."
  - After each session, note: What was the agenda? What homework was assigned? What did the patient learn? What will you focus on next session?
- **Practice**: Use a session template for every session. Review your notes to ensure you're following the structure. Adjust as needed based on supervision feedback.

The interventions offered (cognitive restructuring, distraction, reassurance) do not target the mechanisms maintaining SSD and may inadvertently reinforce health anxiety. The patient is unlikely to experience meaningful symptom improvement and may become frustrated or lose confidence in psychotherapy. The therapist's repeated, nearly identical suggestions despite the patient's clear resistance suggest poor tracking of the session and lack of flexibility.

**Most Critical Changes Needed Immediately:**

1. **Eliminate reassurance-giving** and shift to collaborative exploration and behavioral experimentation
2. **Implement systematic agenda-setting** at the start of each session with explicit prioritization and time allocation
3. **Develop SSD-specific conceptualization** that targets the core maintenance mechanisms: symptom-focused attention, catastrophic health beliefs, safety behaviors, and avoidance
4. **Implement systematic behavioral activation and graded exposure** rather than generic distraction
5. **Use guided discovery** (Socratic questioning) rather than direct advice
6. **Review homework at the start of each session** and assign structured, specific homework with written materials

**Likelihood of Producing Therapeutic Change:**

This session does not qualify as evidence-based CBT for SSD. The probability of producing meaningful, sustained therapeutic change is low. Without significant changes to the therapist's approach—particularly the elimination of reassurance-giving, implementation of SSD-specific interventions, and systematic use of behavioral activation and exposure—the patient is likely to continue experiencing symptoms and may develop increased frustration with treatment. Urgent supervision and training in SSD-specific CBT are recommended.
