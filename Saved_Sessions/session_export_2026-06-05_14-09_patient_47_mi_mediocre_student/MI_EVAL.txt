# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal unconditional positive regard demonstrated. Psychiatrist fails to normalize Ethan's ambivalence or validate his struggle with peer pressure. No explicit affirmation of Ethan's worth or positive qualities. Subtle "you need to change" message conveyed through prescriptive language rather than genuine respect for Ethan as a person navigating real developmental challenges. Affirmation and normalization largely absent.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Limited and primarily factual rather than emotional. Basic reflective listening present in isolated moments but surface-level and immediately followed by directive advice. Multiple missed opportunities for complex, emotionally attuned reflections. When Ethan expresses fear of being "the odd one out," psychiatrist intellectualizes rather than empathizes. Ambivalence treated as a problem to solve rather than as a window into internal struggle. Emotional nuance not captured.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Adequate but somewhat unfocused direction maintained. Target behavior (reducing alcohol use) identified early and generally maintained throughout. However, direction pursued through directive means rather than collaborative exploration. Psychiatrist fails to clarify why change matters to Ethan or explore his deeper values. Discrepancy between Ethan's stated values and current behavior acknowledged superficially but never deeply examined. Direction driven by psychiatrist's agenda rather than Ethan's intrinsic motivation.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration nearly absent. Session exemplifies expert-driven, hierarchical approach rather than true partnership. Psychiatrist consistently makes unilateral decisions about what should happen next. All solutions proposed by psychiatrist, not collaboratively developed. Ethan's contributions minimal and reactive. Power dynamic entirely asymmetrical with psychiatrist holding all expertise and Ethan passively complying. No genuine co-construction of plan—only psychiatrist's plan with Ethan's passive acceptance.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy fundamentally undermined throughout session. Repeated "probably" statements frame suggestions as imperatives, subtly pressuring Ethan toward psychiatrist's preferred outcome. Confidence scaling used to motivate compliance rather than explore barriers. No exploration of Ethan's actual preferences or ideas. Closing statement contradictory—claims choice while pressuring compliance with psychiatrist's plan. Genuine respect for Ethan's agency absent. True autonomy support would explore Ethan's own ideas first and affirm his right to choose differently.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal and poorly executed. Psychiatrist primarily tells Ethan why he should change rather than drawing out his own motivations and values. Few genuinely evocative questions asked. When Ethan offers change talk ("I don't like feeling sick or messing stuff up"), psychiatrist doesn't explore further. Never asks about Ethan's values, goals, or what he wants his life to look like—foundation of intrinsic motivation. Discrepancy between values and behavior pointed out by psychiatrist rather than discovered by Ethan. Client self-discovery minimal.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership entirely absent. Psychiatrist assumes expert role throughout with no genuine power-sharing or co-creation of therapeutic process. Psychiatrist diagnoses problem, prescribes solutions, controls agenda, and summarizes unilaterally. Inclusive language absent—uses "you" and "your plan" rather than "we" or "our plan." No sense of shared ownership. True partnership would involve asking Ethan what he thinks should happen and treating his perspective as equally valid. Client expertise and perspective not genuinely valued or incorporated.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk present in Ethan's speech but minimally evoked or reinforced by psychiatrist. Ethan spontaneously offers multiple instances of change talk (willing to try, could drink non-alcoholic beverages, hang out with different friends). However, psychiatrist acknowledges change talk briefly and moves on rather than deepening it. Fails to strategically ask questions designed to evoke change talk. When Ethan expresses willingness, psychiatrist responds with "sounds like a plan" rather than exploring what makes him willing or what he hopes will happen. Opportunities to deepen motivation missed.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk present throughout but handled poorly—sometimes reinforcing rather than skillfully redirecting. Early minimization ("not a big deal") not addressed. Peer pressure sustain talk acknowledged but not reframed; practical advice offered instead of exploring fear. Doubt about change not addressed; psychiatrist moves to confidence scaling which can feel dismissive. No double-sided reflections used to acknowledge both sides of ambivalence while gently emphasizing change side. Approach more "overcome the resistance" than "understand and gently redirect it," inconsistent with MI principles.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Current MI Competency Level:** Minimal MI competence demonstrated

**Session Score: 1.6/5**

**Key Strengths:**
- Basic warmth and welcoming tone in opening
- Topic maintenance and focus on alcohol use
- Acknowledgment of change talk with brief affirmations
- Attempt at goal-setting showing forward momentum

**Critical Deficiencies:**
1. **Directive, expert-driven approach** fundamentally violates MI spirit and undermines client autonomy
2. **Minimal evocation** of client's own reasons, values, and motivations—core of MI largely absent
3. **Weak empathy** with superficial reflective listening and missed opportunities for emotional attunement
4. **Insufficient exploration of ambivalence** regarding peer belonging versus health concerns
5. **Prescriptive language** ("probably," "would help if") creates subtle pressure and undermines autonomy
6. **Inadequate handling of sustain talk** without skillful redirection or reframing
7. **No values exploration** essential for understanding intrinsic motivation
8. **Unilateral planning** with no genuine client input shaping the intervention

**Impact on Client Engagement and Change:**
Session likely to produce **short-term compliance without sustained motivation**. Ethan's 5/10 confidence rating and tentative commitment ("I don't know if it'll be perfect, but at least it's something") suggest weak intrinsic motivation. Without genuine exploration of values and reasons for change, Ethan likely to abandon plan when peer pressure intensifies. Psychiatrist has positioned change as something psychiatrist wants rather than something Ethan wants for himself—opposite of effective MI.

**Highest Priority Recommendations:**
1. Shift from expert to collaborative partner role
2. Deepen empathic understanding of Ethan's ambivalence
3. Replace prescriptive statements with genuine evocative questions
4. Eliminate implicit pressure through language choices
5. Skillfully handle sustain talk with curiosity rather than confrontation

---

**MI Spirit Assessment:** This session does **NOT embody the spirit of MI**. While not confrontational or overtly judgmental, session lacks core elements of partnership, evocation, and autonomy support that define motivational interviewing. Approach more consistent with traditional directive counseling than MI.
