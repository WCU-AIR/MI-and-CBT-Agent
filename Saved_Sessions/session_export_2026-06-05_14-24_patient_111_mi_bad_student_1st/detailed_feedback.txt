
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and contains multiple instances of explicit judgment and disapproval. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses Richard's concerns, fears, and legitimate hesitations with phrases like "No, you don't get to say you want help figuring this out," "No, you don't get to negotiate here," and "No, you don't get to ask for understanding or to work together." These statements are fundamentally rejecting of the client as a person and communicate contempt for his autonomy and emotional experience.

This labeling is judgmental and dismissive. There is no affirmation of Richard's strengths—notably, he has already quit smoking, he expresses genuine motivation tied to his wife and retirement, and he demonstrates willingness to engage with medication. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses exclusively on what Richard is "failing" to do and what he "must" do. The tone is punitive and threatening ("If you don't take this seriously, you're risking your life and your wife's future" is repeated multiple times as a fear-based ultimatum rather than collaborative exploration).

There is no warmth, no genuine support, and no communication that Richard is valued as a person worthy of respect. The psychiatrist's final statement—"I expect you to follow through"—frames the relationship as one of authority and compliance rather than partnership.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental, rejecting stance that violates the core MI principle of unconditional positive regard. This approach communicates disapproval of the client and his concerns, fundamentally undermining the therapeutic alliance.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective. This session contains virtually no reflective listening and demonstrates minimal effort to understand Richard's experience. The psychiatrist does not once reflect back Richard's concerns, fears, values, or emotional state. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly talks *at* Richard rather than *with* him.

A therapist demonstrating empathy would reflect this value back, explore it deeply, and use it as a foundation for collaborative planning. Instead, the psychiatrist uses this motivation only as a threat: "you're risking your wife's future" rather than "I hear that staying active with your wife in retirement is really important to you—let's figure out how to make that happen."

There are no complex reflections, no emotional attunement, and no demonstration that the psychiatrist understands Richard's internal experience. The psychiatrist never acknowledges the legitimate difficulty of behavior change, the reality of habit formation, or the validity of Richard's concerns about side effects and sustainability.

**Summary:**

The session is devoid of empathic reflection and demonstrates no genuine effort to understand Richard's perspective, fears, or values. This represents a fundamental failure of MI empathy.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

While the psychiatrist maintains a clear focus on cholesterol management and behavioral change (diet, exercise, medication), the *manner* in which direction is maintained is antithetical to MI principles. Direction in MI should involve strategic guidance that maintains focus while remaining collaborative and client-centered. This psychiatrist maintains focus through authoritarian prescription and threat rather than collaborative exploration.

The psychiatrist does establish and repeatedly reinforce specific behavioral targets: eliminate fried foods and red meat completely, exercise 30 minutes daily without exception, take medication exactly as prescribed. This is clear direction. However, the approach is rigid, non-negotiable, and delivered as ultimatum rather than as collaborative goal-setting. The psychiatrist explicitly rejects any discussion of gradual implementation, realistic planning, or individualized approaches—all of which are consistent with MI direction.

The repeated phrase "no middle ground" and "no exceptions" reflects a directive stance that is more consistent with confrontational or authoritarian counseling than with MI. In MI, direction should guide the conversation toward change while respecting the client's autonomy and capacity for self-direction. Here, direction is imposed through fear and authority.

Additionally, the psychiatrist does not explore Richard's own ideas about how to implement change, does not ask about barriers or facilitators, and does not invite Richard's input on realistic goal-setting. This represents direction without collaboration—a significant MI deficiency.

**Summary:**

While the psychiatrist maintains clear focus on target behaviors, the authoritarian, non-negotiable manner of establishing direction violates MI principles of collaboration and client-centeredness.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Fundamental Reorientation to MI Spirit and Principles**
- **Action:** Complete retraining in Motivational Interviewing principles, with particular focus on the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation) and the distinction between MI and confrontational approaches.
- **Example:** Rather than "No, you don't get to negotiate here," the psychiatrist should say: "I hear that you're concerned about making all these changes at once. That's actually a sign you're thinking realistically. Let's talk about what a realistic first step might look like for you."
- **Practice:** Attend a formal MI training program (e.g., MINT-certified training), practice with role-play feedback, and review recordings of sessions to identify moments where confrontation replaced collaboration.

---

### 2. **Develop Genuine Empathic Reflection Skills**
- **Action:** Learn and practice reflective listening as the primary tool for understanding and responding to clients. Reflections should capture both content and emotion, and should be used to deepen understanding rather than to impose direction.
- **Example:** When Richard says "I'm scared that if I don't ease into it a little, I might give up completely," reflect: "You're worried that if you try to do everything at once, you might get discouraged and give up entirely. That's actually a realistic concern about what it takes to make lasting change."
- **Practice:** Record sessions and count the number of reflections vs. questions vs. directives. Aim for a ratio of at least 2 reflections for every 1 directive. Practice complex reflections that capture emotional nuance and meaning.

---

### 3. **Restore Autonomy Support and Eliminate Coercive Language**
- **Action:** Consciously eliminate language that removes client choice ("you have to," "you must," "no exceptions," "this is not optional"). Replace with language that preserves autonomy while maintaining clear recommendations.
- **Example:** Instead of "You have to exercise 30 minutes every day, no exceptions," say: "The evidence shows that 30 minutes of daily exercise is most effective for cholesterol management. What do you think about that as a goal? What would be realistic for you to start with?"
- **Practice:** Before sessions, review your planned recommendations and reframe them to preserve client choice. During sessions, pause when you notice yourself using coercive language and rephrase.

---

### 4. **Evoke and Amplify Client Change Talk and Values**
- **Action:** Actively listen for and strategically amplify client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change. Use open-ended questions to evoke deeper exploration of client values and motivations.
- **Example:** When Richard says "I want to stay active with my wife when we retire," respond with: "Tell me more about what that looks like for you. What activities do you imagine doing together? What would it mean to you to be able to do those things?" Then reflect back: "So staying active and independent with your wife in retirement is really important to you, and that's connected to managing your health now."
- **Practice:** In each session, identify at least 3 instances of client change talk and reflect them back to strengthen them. Ask at least 5 open-ended questions designed to evoke client thinking about change.

---

### 5. **Establish True Collaboration and Partnership in Treatment Planning**
- **Action:** Invite client input on treatment planning, acknowledge client expertise about their own life and habits, and co-construct realistic goals rather than imposing them.
- **Example:** "I have some recommendations based on the evidence about what helps with cholesterol management. You know your life and habits better than I do. Let's put our heads together and figure out what might actually work for you. What do you think would be a realistic first step?"
- **Practice:** In each session, explicitly ask for client input at least 3 times: "What are your thoughts on this?" "What would work for you?" "How do you see this fitting into your life?" Incorporate client suggestions into the treatment plan.

---

### 6. **Use Client Strengths and Past Successes as Foundation for Confidence**
- **Action:** Identify and explicitly acknowledge client strengths and past behavior change successes. Use these as evidence of capacity for change and as foundation for building confidence about managing cholesterol.
- **Example:** "I notice you've already quit smoking—that's a significant accomplishment that shows you can make important changes when you decide to. That same capacity is going to help you manage your cholesterol. Tell me about how you were able to quit smoking. What helped you succeed?"
- **Practice:** In each session, identify at least one client strength or past success and explicitly connect it to current change goals.

---

### 7. **Explore and Address Ambivalence Through Reflection Rather Than Confrontation**
- **Action:** When clients express ambivalence or concern (e.g., about side effects, sustainability, difficulty of change), respond with curious exploration and double-sided reflection rather than dismissal or confrontation.
- **Example:** When Richard expresses concern about side effects, respond with: "You're interested in taking the medication to manage your cholesterol AND you're concerned about potential side effects. Both of those make sense. Tell me more about your concerns. What have you heard about side effects? What would help you feel more confident about trying the medication?"
- **Practice:** When you notice yourself wanting to confront or dismiss a client concern, pause and instead ask a curious question: "Tell me more about that" or "What's your biggest concern about that?"

---

### 8. **Develop Realistic, Graduated Goal-Setting Rather Than All-or-Nothing Demands**
- **Action:** Work collaboratively with clients to establish realistic, graduated goals that can be sustained long-term, rather than demanding immediate, perfect adherence to all recommendations.
- **Example:** Rather than "You must exercise 30 minutes every day with no exceptions," explore: "What would be realistic for you to start with? Could you commit to 15 minutes of walking 3 days a week to start, and then build from there? What would help you succeed with that?"
- **Practice:** For each client, develop a tiered goal-setting approach: identify the ideal outcome, then work backward to identify realistic first steps that preserve client autonomy and increase likelihood of success.

---

### 9. **Eliminate Fear-Based and Guilt-Based Motivation in Favor of Values-Based Motivation**
- **Action:** Stop using threat and guilt ("you're risking your wife's future," "you could have a heart attack") as primary motivators. Instead, connect health recommendations to client values and meaningful life goals.
- **Example:** Rather than "If you don't take this seriously, you're risking your wife's future," say: "I hear that being active and healthy with your wife in retirement is really important to you. Managing your cholesterol now is one of the most important things you can do to make that happen. Let's figure out how to make that work for you."
- **Practice:** In each session, identify the client's core values and meaningful life goals, and explicitly connect health recommendations to those values.

- **Action:** Engage in regular clinical supervision with an MI-trained supervisor, record and review sessions, and seek feedback on MI consistency and skillfulness.
- **Example:** Record this session and review it with a supervisor, identifying specific moments where MI principles were violated and practicing alternative responses.
- **Practice:** Commit to monthly supervision focused on MI skill development, with recorded session review and role-play practice of challenging scenarios.
