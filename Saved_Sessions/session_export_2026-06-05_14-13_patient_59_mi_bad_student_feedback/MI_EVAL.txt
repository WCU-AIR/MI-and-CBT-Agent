# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates fundamentally judgmental and disapproving stance throughout most of the session. Uses language conveying conditional regard tied to behavioral compliance ("stop feeling sorry for yourself," "Why didn't you try setting an alarm already?"). Invalidates Jamal's legitimate struggles rather than affirming his worth as a person. Only single moment of slight acceptance near end ("I appreciate your honesty") cannot offset pervasive judgmental tone. Fails to communicate unconditional positive regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Demonstrates minimal empathic reflection or genuine effort to understand Jamal's perspective from his internal frame of reference. Consistently dismisses emotional experiences rather than exploring them ("I get that it's tough, but you have to push through that feeling"). Negates Jamal's core struggle about intrinsic vs. external motivation ("It's not about whether it matters to you; it's about doing what's necessary"). Shows no curiosity about what "stuck," "tired," or "feeling pushed" means to Jamal. Only final exchange shows slight empathic language, insufficient to offset dismissive pattern.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Maintains clear focus on target behavior (getting to school on time) throughout session, preventing topic drift. However, pursues direction through prescription and authority rather than collaboration. Prescribes specific solution ("put your alarm across the room") and demands commitment without exploring alternatives. Uses threat-based motivation ("If you don't change now, it's going to mess up your future"). Only final exchange demonstrates collaborative direction-setting ("What are some small steps you think you could try?"), which is insufficient to offset authoritarian approach throughout majority of session.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Adopts expert-driven, authoritarian stance fundamentally antithetical to collaboration. Uses prescriptive language ("You really should try," "You have to fix this quickly," "That's a simple fix. You don't need to overthink it"). Repeatedly overrides Jamal's attempts to assert perspective and autonomy rather than incorporating them into session direction. Positions self as authority figure ("I'm counting on you to make this work starting tomorrow. No backing out"). Only final exchange ("Let's explore together") represents genuine power-sharing, but single instance cannot offset pervasive authoritarian approach.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Systematically suppresses autonomy through directive language, obligation-framing, and pressure tactics. Uses repeated "you have to" statements, negates client's right to struggle, and frames change as mandatory and non-negotiable. Explicitly denies autonomy over motivation ("It's not about whether it matters to you; it's about doing what's necessary"). Ignores and overrides Jamal's explicit need for autonomy ("I just don't like feeling pushed all the time"). Never affirms client's right to choose or says "This is your choice." Increases pressure and control when Jamal articulates need for autonomy, directly contradicting MI's core principle.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Predominantly tells rather than asks, prescribes rather than evokes, and imposes solutions rather than facilitating self-discovery. Asks primarily diagnostic or compliance-checking questions rather than evocative questions designed to draw out Jamal's own motivation and resources. Misses abundant opportunities to explore what "for me" means, what "stuck" or "tired" feels like, or what would help Jamal feel less pressured. Only final question ("What are some small steps you think you could try?") demonstrates genuine evocation, with Jamal responding with own ideas about sleep and phone use. Single instance insufficient to offset prescriptive pattern throughout session.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Actively assumes expert role and minimizes client contributions throughout most of session. Positions self as authority on discipline, solutions, and consequences. Prescribes specific interventions without exploring client's ideas first. Never conveys that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within client. When Jamal offers perspective ("I just need to find a way that works for me"), psychiatrist overrides it. Only in final exchange does psychiatrist genuinely incorporate client's ideas and foster power-sharing. Never uses language positioning Jamal as expert on his own change ("You know yourself best," "Your ideas matter here").

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Fails to evoke, amplify, or reinforce Jamal's change talk despite multiple opportunities. Jamal offers substantial change talk ("I'm willing to try," "I want to do this for me," "I'm willing to commit," "I did get up easier than before," "I'm trying to cut back on it"). Psychiatrist responds with prescription, criticism, or negation rather than amplification. Responds to progress report with criticism ("Why didn't you just get up right away?") rather than exploring and building on success. Only minimal affirmation ("Hey, that's a good start") followed immediately by criticism. Never uses reflective listening to amplify change talk or asks follow-up questions to deepen commitment language.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3/5
**Comments:** Handles sustain talk primarily through confrontation and override rather than skillful softening. When Jamal expresses resistance to external pressure, psychiatrist responds confrontationally ("stop feeling sorry for yourself"). Avoids some MI errors: no explicit agreement with sustain talk, no amplification of barriers, no reinforcement of reasons against change. However, lacks collaborative reframing and strategic redirection characteristic of effective sustain talk management. Final exchange shows slight improvement: when Jamal says "I'm not going to suddenly be perfect overnight," psychiatrist responds with acceptance and collaborative exploration rather than confrontation. This represents only partial softening of sustain talk approach.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.4/5**

**Session Strengths:**
- Maintains consistent focus on target behavior (getting to school on time)
- Identifies concrete, addressable barrier (snoozing)
- Proposes practical solution (alarm across room)
- Acknowledges partial progress in follow-up ("Hey, that's a good start")
- Final exchange demonstrates capability for collaborative, evocative MI practice

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- Pervasive judgmental stance communicating disapproval and conditional regard
- Authoritarian expert role with consistent prescription and demand for compliance
- Systematic suppression of autonomy through controlling language and pressure tactics
- Failure to evoke: predominantly tells rather than asks, prescribes rather than explores
- Dismissal of legitimate ambivalence rather than exploring client's core struggle
- Confrontational responses to sustain talk rather than skillful softening
- Failure to amplify change talk despite multiple opportunities
- Undermines intrinsic motivation through external pressure and control

**Impact on Client Engagement:**
Jamal enters with some readiness for change and explicitly articulates desire for autonomy and intrinsic motivation. Psychiatrist's controlling, judgmental stance directly contradicts this emerging motivation. By session end, Jamal's language reveals motivation remains primarily external ("For now, I'm doing it so people leave me alone"). Partial behavioral progress appears driven by external compliance rather than genuine commitment. Defensive responses in follow-up suggest psychiatrist's controlling approach is generating reactance.

**Recommendation:**
Immediate, intensive MI training and supervision required. Psychiatrist must fundamentally shift stance from expert/authoritarian to collaborative/partner, develop genuine acceptance and unconditional positive regard, support autonomy explicitly, and commit to evoking rather than prescribing. Final exchange demonstrates change is possible with focused effort and commitment to MI principles.
