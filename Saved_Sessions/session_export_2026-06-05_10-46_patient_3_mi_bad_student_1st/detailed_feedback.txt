
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a complete absence of unconditional positive regard and fundamental acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly uses judgmental language, dismissive framing, and confrontational challenges that directly violate MI spirit. Examples include: "No, you're not serious enough about this," "you're just making excuses again," "you have to stop making excuses and start taking responsibility," and "you're not listening." These statements communicate disapproval, blame, and judgment rather than respect for Aisha as a person of worth.

The psychiatrist never affirms Aisha's strengths—her concern for her son, her willingness to engage in the conversation, her honesty about her capacity, or her openness to reducing evening cigarettes. Instead, the clinician repeatedly invalidates her perspective ("No, no, you don't get it"), dismisses her lived experience ("Stress is not an excuse"), and frames her ambivalence as moral failure rather than normal human ambivalence. The repeated demand for immediate commitment ("set a quit date today, no excuses") combined with threats about her son's future ("you're risking your life and your son's future") creates a coercive, punitive dynamic that is antithetical to acceptance.

Most critically, the psychiatrist shows no warmth, support, or genuine interest in understanding Aisha's world. The opening statement—"I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do"—establishes an authoritarian stance from the outset. There is zero normalization of struggle, no acknowledgment that behavior change is difficult, and no recognition that Aisha's hesitation is understandable given her circumstances (full-time work, night classes, single parenting).

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates pervasive judgment, blame, and dismissal throughout the session. Acceptance is entirely absent, replaced by confrontation and coercion that communicates conditional regard based on compliance with the clinician's demands.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective. This session contains virtually no reflective listening and demonstrates active resistance to understanding Aisha's experience. The psychiatrist never reflects back what Aisha has said, never explores her feelings, and never demonstrates curiosity about her internal world.

When Aisha explains her barriers ("I've been under a lot of stress with work and night classes, and smoking helps me calm down"), the psychiatrist responds with dismissal: "Stress is not an excuse." This is the opposite of empathy—it invalidates her experience rather than seeking to understand it. When she articulates her readiness level and preference for gradual change, the psychiatrist responds with "No, you're not serious enough about this" rather than exploring what "serious" means to her or what would help her feel more ready.

There are no complex reflections that capture emotional nuance. No statements like: "It sounds like you're caught between wanting to be healthier for your son and feeling overwhelmed by everything on your plate right now" or "You care deeply about your health and your son, and you're also worried that quitting completely might feel impossible given your stress level." **Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no genuine empathic understanding, uses no reflective listening, and actively invalidates Aisha's perspective. This represents a fundamental failure in MI empathy.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI means maintaining strategic focus on a change goal while remaining client-centered. This psychiatrist maintains focus on smoking cessation (the target behavior), but does so in a way that is entirely clinician-driven rather than collaboratively established or strategically guided.

The psychiatrist has a single, rigid direction: "You must set a quit date today and quit completely." This direction is repeated verbatim at least 8 times throughout the session with no variation, no strategic adjustment based on client response, and no exploration of what direction Aisha herself might prefer. True direction involves progressive clarification—understanding where the client is, where they want to go, and how to bridge that gap. This psychiatrist shows no such progression; instead, there is repetitive insistence on the same demand despite clear evidence it is not working.

The psychiatrist does not explore Aisha's own goals (e.g., "What would feel like a meaningful change for you right now?"), does not assess her readiness level systematically, and does not help her clarify her own direction. The session lacks strategic guidance toward change; it is simply directive prescription.

One minor positive: the psychiatrist does maintain focus on smoking rather than allowing the conversation to drift. However, this narrow focus without flexibility or client input is not skillful direction—it is rigidity.

**Summary:**

While the psychiatrist maintains focus on smoking cessation, the approach is rigid, repetitive, and entirely clinician-imposed rather than strategically guided or collaboratively developed. This represents directive control rather than MI direction.

### 5. Autonomy Support

## Recommendations

### 1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance from the Outset**
- **Action:** Begin the session by explicitly positioning yourself as a partner rather than an expert. Use language that reflects shared decision-making and genuine curiosity about the client's perspective.
- **Example:** "I'd like to talk with you about your smoking. I know you've been thinking about this, and I'm curious to hear what's on your mind. What brings you in today, and what are your thoughts about your smoking?"
- **Practice:** Before each session, remind yourself: "My role is to understand this person's perspective and help them explore their own motivations for change, not to convince them to do what I think they should do." Practice opening statements that communicate partnership rather than expertise.

---

### 2. **Use Reflective Listening to Demonstrate Genuine Understanding**
- **Action:** After the client speaks, reflect back what you heard—both the content and the emotion—to demonstrate that you understand their perspective. Use complex reflections that capture ambivalence and nuance.
- **Example:** When Aisha says "I've been under a lot of stress with work and night classes, and smoking helps me calm down," respond with: "So smoking is one of the ways you manage the stress of balancing work, classes, and parenting. It helps you unwind when things feel overwhelming."
- **Practice:** Record yourself in practice sessions and count how many reflective listening statements you use. Aim for at least 3-4 reflections for every piece of advice or information you provide. Practice complex reflections that capture both sides of ambivalence: "On one hand, you see smoking as a way to manage stress, and on the other hand, you're worried about cancer and want to be there for your son."

---

### 3. **Evoke Rather Than Tell: Ask Questions to Draw Out Motivation**
- **Action:** Replace lectures about health consequences with open-ended questions designed to help the client explore their own reasons for change. Ask about values, concerns, and what matters most to them.
- **Example:** Instead of "Don't you want to avoid cancer and heart disease?" ask: "You mentioned being worried about cancer and wanting to be there for your son. Tell me more about what that means to you" or "What would it look like if you were able to stay healthy for your son?"
- **Practice:** For every statement you make about why the client should change, replace it with a question that helps them explore their own reasons. Practice questions like: "What concerns you most about your smoking?" "What would be different if you made a change?" "What strengths have helped you make changes in other areas of your life?"

---

### 4. **Recognize and Amplify Change Talk**
- **Action:** When the client expresses desire, ability, reasons, or commitment to change, respond by reflecting it back, asking follow-up questions, and reinforcing it. This strengthens intrinsic motivation.
- **Example:** When Aisha says "I could try to cut back a bit, like smoking three cigarettes in the evening instead of six. That feels like something I could actually do," respond with: "So you've identified a specific change that feels manageable—cutting back to three in the evening. What makes you think you could actually do that?"
- **Practice:** In each session, identify at least 3 instances of change talk and respond to each one by reflecting it, asking a follow-up question, or reinforcing it. Never dismiss change talk as "not enough" or "just excuses."

---

### 5. **Support Autonomy: Respect the Client's Right to Choose**
- **Action:** Explicitly affirm the client's right to make their own choices, even if those choices differ from your recommendation. Avoid "have to," "must," and "need to" language. Instead, use language that emphasizes choice and ownership.
- **Example:** Instead of "You have to set a quit date today," say: "I know you're thinking about making a change. What feels like the right next step for you? Some people find it helpful to set a specific date, while others prefer to start by cutting back first. What do you think would work best for you?"
- **Practice:** Remove "have to," "must," "need to," and "should" from your clinical vocabulary. Replace these with: "You might consider," "Some people find it helpful to," "What do you think about," "What would feel doable for you?" Practice affirming client autonomy: "This is your decision, and I respect whatever you choose. My role is to support you in whatever direction you decide to go."

---

### 6. **Soften Sustain Talk Through Double-Sided Reflection and Reframing**
- **Action:** When the client expresses reasons not to change, acknowledge both sides of their ambivalence rather than confronting or dismissing. Use reframing to gently shift perspective.
- **Example:** When Aisha says "I don't smoke or drink that much compared to people I work with," respond with: "So you compare yourself to others and feel like you're doing okay relative to them. At the same time, you mentioned being worried about cancer and wanting to be there for your son. How do those two things fit together for you?"
- **Practice:** Practice double-sided reflections: "On one hand... on the other hand..." Practice reframing barriers as opportunities: "It sounds like stress is a real challenge for you. What if we explored other ways to manage that stress while also reducing smoking?"

---

### 7. **Abandon the Confrontational Approach: Replace "No" with Curiosity**
- **Action:** Stop using confrontational language ("No, you're making excuses," "No, you don't get it"). Instead, respond to disagreement with curiosity and genuine interest in understanding the client's perspective.
- **Example:** Instead of "No, cutting back won't save you from cancer," say: "I hear that you're thinking about cutting back as a first step. Help me understand—what makes you think that starting with cutting back might work better for you than quitting all at once?"
- **Practice:** In your next 5 sessions, count how many times you use the word "No" in response to client statements. Your goal is zero. When you feel the urge to say "No," pause and ask a curious question instead.

---

### 8. **Assess Readiness and Meet the Client Where They Are**
- **Action:** Rather than insisting on a specific change (complete cessation, immediate quit date), assess the client's actual readiness level and collaborate on a plan that matches their current stage of change.
- **Example:** "On a scale of 0-10, where 0 is not ready at all and 10 is completely ready to quit smoking completely, where would you say you are right now? And what would need to happen for you to move up even one point on that scale?"
- **Practice:** Use readiness rulers and stage-of-change assessment in every session. For clients in contemplation or early preparation, focus on exploring ambivalence and building motivation rather than demanding immediate action. Recognize that small steps (like cutting back) are valid progress and can build momentum toward larger changes.

---

### 9. **Use Affirmations to Build Self-Efficacy and Strengthen the Therapeutic Relationship**
- **Action:** Regularly affirm the client's strengths, efforts, and positive qualities. This builds self-efficacy and communicates genuine respect and support.
- **Example:** "I really respect that you're willing to have this conversation about smoking. It shows that you care about your health and your son. The fact that you're already thinking about ways to cut back shows that you're taking this seriously."
- **Practice:** In each session, provide at least 3-4 genuine affirmations. Focus on effort, willingness, strengths, and values rather than compliance with your recommendations.

---

### 10. **Receive Training in Motivational Interviewing**
- **Action:** This session demonstrates fundamental misunderstandings of MI principles. Seek formal training in MI through accredited programs (e.g., MINT-certified trainers, university-based MI courses).
- **Example:** Attend a 2-day MI workshop, complete online MI training modules, or seek supervision from an MI-trained clinician.
- **Practice:** After training, practice MI skills in role-plays with colleagues or supervisors. Record your sessions and review them with an MI trainer to identify areas for improvement. Aim for ongoing skill development rather than one-time training.
