# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates profound lack of acceptance throughout the session. Opening statement establishes judgmental, directive stance with conditional regard based on compliance. Use of "must," "should," and "why haven't you" conveys implicit judgment that patient is failing. No affirmation of current yoga practice or validation of genuine constraints. When patient appropriately sets boundaries, psychiatrist escalates judgment and dismisses patient's autonomy. Repeated pressure after patient declines multiple times demonstrates contempt for patient's worth as decision-maker. No warmth, genuine curiosity, or unconditional support evident.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal reflective listening and no genuine effort to understand patient's perspective. When patient explains yoga feels like self-care and schedule is packed, psychiatrist responds with dismissal rather than reflection. When patient articulates sophisticated insight about intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation ("when it gets pushed as something I have to do, it makes me a bit resistant"), psychiatrist uses pseudo-reflection immediately followed by contradiction. No exploration of patient's past failed attempts with guilt-driven burnout mechanism. No complex reflections capturing emotional nuance. Clinician listens to rebut, not to understand.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains consistent focus on exercise behavior (three sessions per week minimum), demonstrating some directional consistency. However, direction is imposed rather than collaborative and becomes increasingly rigid and counterproductive. When patient proposes alternative direction (one session weekly), psychiatrist initially appears to accept but immediately undermines it. Direction never becomes responsive to patient input or adjusted based on feedback. Never explores patient's stated values as foundation for direction-setting. MI direction should be progressive and responsive; here it becomes increasingly inflexible and pressuring, reducing effectiveness.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates authoritarian, expert-driven stance throughout. Opening statement explicitly positions clinician as authority: "I'll be telling you what you should do." When patient proposes gradual approach, psychiatrist uses superficial collaborative language initially but abandons it when patient doesn't comply, returning to directives. When patient explicitly requests "middle ground," psychiatrist dismisses proposal: "You can't just say you're open to trying more activity." No genuine power-sharing evident. Patient's ideas do not shape session direction. Psychiatrist's ideas presented as non-negotiable facts. Expert-driven practice dominates; true partnership absent.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively undermines autonomy throughout session. Controlling language dominates: "You should," "You must," "You need to commit," "You have to," "You can't," "no excuses." When patient declines commitment to three sessions weekly, psychiatrist escalates pressure with yes/no framing. Uses fear-based coercive language: "If you don't commit to three sessions a week immediately, you risk your health deteriorating further." No affirmation of patient's right to choose own path. No language supporting autonomy ("It's ultimately your decision," "You know yourself best"). Patient forced to defend autonomy against clinician's pressure, demonstrating healthy boundary-setting in response to autonomy violation.

When patient proposes one session weekly (self-generated motivation), psychiatrist undermines it. No open-ended questions designed to evoke reasons for change, values, past successes, or resources. ### Partnership

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates no skill in cultivating change talk and actively suppresses it. Patient provides multiple instances: "I really love how it helps me feel grounded," "I do want to be healthier overall," "I'm willing to give that a go," "I want to do it because I want to, not because I feel pressured." Psychiatrist does not amplify, explore, or reinforce any change talk. When patient expresses willingness to try one session weekly, psychiatrist responds: "You really should push yourself to do more." Pseudo-affirmation immediately followed by negation. By session end, patient's change talk shifted to defensive statements about autonomy. Psychiatrist provokes sustain talk through pressure rather than cultivating change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal skill in softening sustain talk and actually amplifies it through confrontation. When patient expresses concern about fitting in more activity, psychiatrist responds confrontationally: "You should be doing both at least three times a week. Why haven't you been consistent?" Argues against sustain talk rather than skillfully softening it. When patient articulates psychological principle that external pressure creates resistance, psychiatrist dismisses it with pressure rather than acknowledging validity. No double-sided reflections acknowledging both patient's desire for health and legitimate concerns about feasibility. No strategic reframing of sustain talk. By session end, patient's sustain talk intensified: "I can't promise," "I'm not ready," "I need to do it my way." Confrontational approach amplified rather than softened resistance.

## Summary Assessment

**Clinical Competency Level:** Minimal MI competence demonstrated across all domains.

**Primary Clinical Deficiency:** Fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles. Psychiatrist operates from directive, expert-driven, authoritarian model rather than collaborative, client-centered approach. Repeated use of controlling language ("should," "must," "need," "have to") establishes power-over dynamic antithetical to MI.

**Impact on Therapeutic Outcomes:** Session likely to reduce intrinsic motivation and damage therapeutic alliance. Patient appropriately defended autonomy and proposed sustainable approach. However, patient had to do this *against* clinician rather than *with* clinician. Psychiatrist positioned as adversary rather than partner. Research demonstrates client-centered approaches more effective than directive approaches for evoking sustained behavior change.

**Critical Concerns:**
- Suppression of patient change talk and amplification of sustain talk through pressure
- Failure to connect recommendations to patient values
- Coercive pressure and autonomy violation
- Dismissal of valid patient self-knowledge
- Use of fear-based pressure and threat language
- No genuine exploration of patient expertise about own life

**Immediate Recommendations:**
1. Fundamental shift from expert-driven to client-centered stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
2. Eliminate controlling language; replace with autonomy-supportive language (CRITICAL)
3. Develop skill in evoking and amplifying change talk (CRITICAL)
4. Stop pressuring; start exploring when patient declines suggestions (CRITICAL)
5. Connect recommendations to patient values (IMPORTANT)
6. Develop skill in softening sustain talk without confrontation (IMPORTANT)
7. Use reflective listening to demonstrate genuine understanding (IMPORTANT)
8. Eliminate fear-based pressure and threat language (CRITICAL)
9. Develop genuine curiosity about patient expertise (IMPORTANT)
10. Seek formal MI training and supervision with MINT-certified trainer (FOUNDATIONAL)

**Recommendation:** Clinician should not continue MI practice without formal training and supervision. Current approach likely to harm therapeutic outcomes and violates core principles of client-centered care. Immediate enrollment in MI training strongly recommended.
