# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal unconditional positive regard and frequently adopts a judgmental, prescriptive stance. Repeatedly tells patient what "should" be done ("It's important to keep those up," "it would probably help if you tried cutting back") rather than accepting current perspective. Fails to normalize ambivalence or affirm autonomy. Final response contradicts session direction and appears patronizing, communicating permission rather than genuine support.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal effort to understand patient's perspective. Relies on factual questions rather than reflective listening. When patient states "I like beer...it's part of how we connect," clinician does not reflect emotional meaning but pivots to health risks. One superficial reflection offered ("So you drink about five or six beers on weekends") but misses underlying ambivalence and identity concerns. No complex reflections demonstrating deep understanding. Patient unlikely to feel genuinely heard.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains clear focus on reducing alcohol consumption throughout session, demonstrating some directional consistency. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively established. Multiple topics introduced early (water, eating, sleep, mood, energy, health risks) without allowing patient to identify priorities. Direction becomes clearer when clinician repeatedly returns to drinking reduction, but this feels like pressure rather than strategic guidance emerging from patient's own values.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5
**Comments:** Collaboration is nearly absent. Psychiatrist consistently adopts expert-authority stance, telling rather than inviting joint problem-solving. Uses prescriptive language throughout: "It's important to keep those up," "It would probably help if you tried cutting back," "Maybe you could start by having fewer beers." No genuine invitation for patient to contribute ideas or co-construct solutions. When patient expresses doubt, clinician dismisses concern rather than exploring collaboratively.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is virtually absent and actively undermined. Psychiatrist pressures patient toward predetermined outcome (reduced drinking) while minimizing right to choose. Uses directive language throughout ("it would probably help if," "it's important to think about"). When patient states "I'm not ready to make any changes," clinician does not affirm right to choose but subtly pressures continued consideration. No explicit affirmation of patient's autonomy or right to decide differently.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out patient's own motivations and reasons for change—is largely absent. Psychiatrist tells patient why he should change rather than asking questions that explore his perspective. Leading questions suggest answers rather than genuine exploration ("Do you think that might be causing any problems...?"). When patient offers emerging change talk ("I guess there could be some risks"), clinician does not explore or amplify but pivots to prescriptive advice. Never asks open-ended questions designed to evoke patient's own reasons for change.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership is fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist operates from clear expert-authority model with no genuine power-sharing or recognition that patient possesses wisdom about own life. Unilaterally decides topics to discuss, tells patient what is "important," offers solutions without inviting input, dismisses patient's concerns. No language of genuine collaboration ("we," "let's explore together"). Final response appears patronizing and suggests clinician has abandoned collaborative process rather than genuinely partnering.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5
**Comments:** Change talk is rarely evoked and never strategically amplified. Patient offers two instances of emerging change talk ("I guess it's something I could think about"; "Maybe if I got sick...I'd want to change"), but clinician fails to explore or deepen either. No strategic questioning to evoke change language. Instead, clinician provides reasons for change without exploring whether patient sees these as important. Few instances of change talk treated as minor acknowledgments rather than opportunities to strengthen motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist makes minimal effort to skillfully handle patient's sustain talk (arguments for status quo). Responds with direct contradiction, dismissal, or pressure rather than sophisticated techniques. When patient expresses sustain talk ("I don't see a problem," "Cutting back might make things less enjoyable"), clinician responds confrontationally ("But maybe there are some reasons," "You might find it's not as bad as you think"). No double-sided reflections, reframing, or strategic redirection. Responses likely to entrench defensiveness rather than soften resistance.

## Overall Summary

### **Overall Session Score: 1.6/5**

### Strengths
- Clear focus on target behavior (alcohol consumption) maintained throughout session
- Willingness to continue engagement despite patient resistance
- One instance of acknowledging patient's low confidence regarding change

### Critical Areas for Improvement

**Fundamental Violations of MI Principles:**

1. **Expert-Authority Model:** Psychiatrist prioritizes telling over asking, prescribing over exploring, clinician agenda over patient autonomy
2. **Absent Genuine Partnership:** Unilateral decision-making about session content and direction; no collaborative problem-solving
3. **Minimal Empathy:** Questions asked but not deeply listened to; patient unlikely to feel genuinely understood
4. **Failure to Evoke Change Talk:** Tells patient why to change rather than drawing out own motivations; emerging change talk not amplified
5. **Pressure and Directiveness:** Filled with advice-giving and subtle pressure; undermines autonomy and triggers reactance
6. **Poor Sustain Talk Management:** Confrontational responses likely to entrench defensiveness
7. **Inconsistent Messaging:** Final response contradicts entire session direction

### Impact on Client Engagement

This session is unlikely to increase patient's intrinsic motivation for change. Directive, expert-authority approach likely to trigger psychological reactance and entrench resistance. Patient's final statement ("I'm not making any promises") reflects polite compliance rather than genuine motivation. Session may harm therapeutic relationship and reduce willingness to engage in future health conversations.

### Competency Assessment

**Current Level:** Pre-MI competence. While basic counseling skills present, core MI principles (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation) largely absent. Clinician views session as opportunity to educate and persuade toward predetermined behaviors rather than collaborative exploration of patient's own motivations and values.

### Critical Recommendations

1. **Shift from expert-authority to genuine partnership** — foundational issue underlying all deficiencies
2. **Develop reflective listening and empathy** — essential for building rapport and trust
3. **Learn to evoke rather than tell** — replace prescriptive advice with open-ended questions
4. **Explicitly affirm patient autonomy** — communicate respect for patient's right to choose
5. **Amplify emerging change talk** — deepen and strengthen when it emerges
6. **Use double-sided reflections** — address ambivalence without confrontation
7. **Establish collaborative agenda** — ask patient priorities rather than imposing clinician's
8. **Develop comfort with ambivalence** — recognize change happens at patient's pace
9. **Receive formal MI training and supervision** — current approach requires structured learning

### Final Assessment

**Recommendation:** Psychiatrist requires formal Motivational Interviewing training and supervised practice before continuing MI-focused sessions. Current approach, while well-intentioned, is likely to harm therapeutic relationship and reduce patient motivation for change. Without significant modifications to clinical approach, future sessions unlikely to be more effective.
