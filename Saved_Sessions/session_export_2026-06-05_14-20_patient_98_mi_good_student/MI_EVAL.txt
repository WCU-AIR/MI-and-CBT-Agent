### Empathy

Double-sided reflections are particularly sophisticated—e.g., "caught between wanting to grow and the comfort or gravity of what's known" and "weighing hope against worry." Demonstrates deep understanding of internal conflict. No instances of misattunement or superficial reflections. Reflective listening allows client space to feel genuinely understood.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Clear, strategic focus on target behavior (reducing recidivism, building stability/family goals) maintained throughout with flexible, client-centered approach. Logical session progression: vision → ambivalence/obstacles → past successes → resources → first step. Collaborative direction-setting established early. Minor opportunity: could explore specific barriers (e.g., "old people I used to hang with") more strategically before moving to broader confidence questions. Resource introduction could be more strategically timed after deeper exploration of client's own ideas.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong collaborative stance demonstrated through opening question that establishes partnership and builds from client's stated goals. Uses collaborative language consistently: "Would it be okay if I shared...?" and "What would you want to know more about?" Positions client as expert on own experience. One minor opportunity: when introducing resources, explanation is somewhat didactic. More collaborative approach would be to first ask "What kinds of support do you think would help?" before introducing specific programs.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Exemplary autonomy support throughout. Consistently respects and actively fosters client's perception of choice and self-determination. Explicitly seeks permission before offering information ("Would it be okay if I shared...?"). Emphasizes low-stakes exploration ("just seeing what it's like, no pressure"). Uses choice-oriented language throughout: "feels doable to you," "what you're thinking," "you could try." No instances of pressure, prescription, or minimization of client's right to choose. Positions client as expert on own needs.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong evocative practice drawing out client's own motivations, values, and resources. Effectively asks "What do you imagine 'not messing this up' would look like?" and "What kind of things have you done in the past...?" to elicit client's own understanding. Strategically evokes past successes as evidence of capability. Three minor opportunities: (1) could evoke client's own solution ideas earlier before introducing resources; (2) limited exploration of deeper values—could ask "What does having a family mean to you?" to strengthen emotional connection; (3) vague obstacles ("stuff happens") could be explored more specifically to deepen understanding.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintains genuinely collaborative, power-sharing stance throughout most of session. Opening establishes partnership: "I want to make sure this time is as helpful for you as possible." Uses inclusive language and positions client as expert on own experience. Builds explicitly on client's contributions rather than imposing interpretation. Demonstrates partnership through permission-seeking and inviting client input on how information "fits with what you're thinking." One minor opportunity: when introducing resources, could more fully center client's ideas first by asking "What kinds of support do you think would help?" before offering clinician expertise.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Skillful cultivation of change talk with consistent reinforcement of desire, ability, and commitment language. Effectively reflects and reinforces desire ("You have this real desire to change"). Strategically evokes ability talk through past successes and affirms these as "significant strength." Cultivates commitment by asking about first steps and affirming job coaching as "thoughtful and manageable." Four minor opportunities: (1) limited amplification of need talk—could ask "So having that solid proof is really important to you?" when client expresses need for evidence; (2) insufficient exploration of reasons talk beyond initial vision; (3) confidence scaling (4-5) not fully leveraged—could ask "What would move you to a 6 or 7?"; (4) commitment talk could be strengthened at end of session through explicit readiness exploration.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Skillful handling of sustain talk without confrontation or reinforcement. Effectively uses double-sided reflections to acknowledge doubt while pivoting toward possibility—e.g., "weighing hope against worry." Normalizes fear about job coaching as manageable. Does not dwell on vague obstacles, which is appropriate. Four minor opportunities: (1) "old people I used to hang with" could be explored more strategically with "What would you need to do differently to stay on track even around those people?"; (2) vague "stuff happens" could be transformed into specific, addressable barriers through gentle exploration; (3) past failures could be more explicitly reframed as learning opportunities; (4) self-doubt ("not sure it's gonna stick") could be explored with "What would need to be different for you to believe it could stick?"

## Overall Session Score: **4.2/5**

## Summary Assessment

**Strengths:**
- Exemplary empathy with sophisticated, accurate reflections capturing complex ambivalence
- Exceptional autonomy support with explicit permission-seeking and emphasis on client choice
- Strong partnership stance with genuine power-sharing and collaborative language
- Skillful use of double-sided reflections to handle ambivalence without confrontation
- Effective affirmation of past strengths and reframing as evidence of capability
- Clear, logical session progression with appropriate pacing
- Warm, supportive, nonjudgmental stance throughout

**Areas for Development:**
- Earlier evocation of client's own solutions before introducing resources
- Deeper exploration of values and emotional meaning underlying goals
- More specific exploration of vague barriers ("stuff happens," "old people")
- Systematic amplification of need talk and reasons for change
- Leverage confidence scaling to evoke what would increase confidence
- Explicit reframing of past failures as learning opportunities
- Stronger commitment talk exploration at session end

**Clinical Impression:**

This is **good, competent MI practice** with solid fundamentals and several exemplary elements. The psychiatrist demonstrates genuine MI spirit, particularly in empathy, autonomy support, and partnership. The session creates a safe, collaborative environment where the client feels heard and respected. The identification of a concrete first step provides hope and a manageable entry point. However, the session could have generated **stronger intrinsic motivation** through deeper values exploration, more systematic evocation of reasons for change, and strategic amplification of change talk. The introduction of resources, while appropriate, somewhat shifts toward an informational stance rather than maintaining pure evocation. The client leaves with a plan and hope, but without deeper emotional connection to reasons for change or explicit problem-solving around specific barriers, follow-through may be uncertain. With focused practice on maintaining evocation depth, this clinician can move from good to excellent MI practice.
