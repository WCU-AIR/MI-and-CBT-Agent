### Acceptance

**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance. Strong early validation of Andrei's frustration and time pressures ("Thank you for sharing how busy and overwhelmed you feel"). ### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Solid reflective listening and perspective-taking demonstrated throughout. Accurate reflections capture both content and emotional undertones (e.g., "right in the middle—recognizing the importance and possibility of change, but also feeling the weight of your busy schedule"). However, empathy lacks depth in critical moments. Misses emotional nuance when Andrei reveals loss about his younger, healthier self ("I used to jog... but that was years ago"). Reflections remain surface-level; deeper, emotionally resonant reflections exploring meaning and feeling behind statements would strengthen empathic connection.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains appropriate focus on weight management and lifestyle change throughout. Opening question establishes collaborative direction-setting. Session progressively narrows toward concrete, actionable steps (standing during calls, healthier snacks, reminders). However, direction could be sharper by explicitly exploring the connection between work stress, eating patterns, and weight—the underlying mechanism driving behavior. Does not explore whether health concerns (blood pressure medication, breathlessness) could serve as additional motivators or challenge unhelpful beliefs about food as mere "fuel."

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong collaborative stance demonstrated through opening question ("What's on your mind or what would you like to focus on?") and consistent use of collaborative language ("What do you think?" "What kinds of small changes do you think might fit?"). Honors Andrei's preference for efficiency and practical solutions. Treats his ideas as valid contributions. However, collaboration could be deepened by more consistently checking in before offering suggestions. When Andrei expresses doubt, psychiatrist moves to confidence scaling without first exploring his own thinking about efficacy. Occasionally leans toward clinician-guided problem-solving rather than true co-construction.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist consistently respects Andrei's right to choose and emphasizes his agency. Opening frames session as Andrei's choice. Avoids prescriptive language; asks "What do you think might fit?" rather than "You should try." Affirms his identified choices without implying obligation. However, autonomy support could be stronger by more explicitly emphasizing his right to modify or abandon plans without judgment. Subtle tone of approval ("It's great to hear you're identifying options") could feel like conditional support. Could more clearly state: "You get to decide what support helps—and you can change your mind anytime."

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Significant gap in this critical MI skill. Psychiatrist asks some evocative questions ("What do you think would be most helpful?") but rarely deepens exploration of Andrei's own motivations, values, and resources. When Andrei expresses change talk ("The weight is something I would like to get hold of"), psychiatrist acknowledges but doesn't amplify: "It sounds like you're motivated" rather than "Tell me more about that—what would that look like?" Misses opportunities to explore past successes (jogging years ago) or deeper values. Never asks "What matters most to you?" or "What would change if your health improved?" Session prioritizes practical problem-solving over drawing out intrinsic motivation for change.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates some partnership elements through collaborative language and invitation of Andrei's input. However, frequently shifts into subtle expert or guide role. When Andrei expresses doubt, psychiatrist responds with confidence scaling and reassurance rather than exploring his own thinking. Suggests solutions (reminders, visible notes, standing during calls) before fully exploring Andrei's own problem-solving capacity. Does not fully honor his expressed preference by asking "What support structure would actually work for you?" True partnership would consistently position Andrei as expert on his own life, with clinician as facilitator rather than problem-solver.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Significant deficiency. Andrei produces multiple instances of change talk (desire: "The weight is something I would like to get hold of"; reasons: breathlessness; ability: "I could try"; commitment: "I'd say about a 5"), but psychiatrist rarely amplifies or reinforces these statements. Reflections are minimal: "It sounds like you're motivated" rather than deepening with "Tell me more—what would that mean for you?" No strategic questions to evoke change talk (e.g., "What are the reasons you'd want to make a change?" "What would be the benefits?"). Session ends with Andrei at modest confidence level (6/10). Critical opportunities to strengthen his own language in favor of change are consistently missed throughout.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Significant gap. Andrei produces substantial sustain talk ("I haven't got time," "Food is just fuel," "I can't see much room for anything new," "I'm not convinced there's much more I can realistically do without dropping the ball on work"), but psychiatrist primarily reflects and pivots to problem-solving without strategically addressing underlying ambivalence. Does not use double-sided reflection ("On one hand... and on the other hand..."), reframing ("Has that approach worked well for you?"), or discrepancy amplification ("You'd like to get hold of your weight, and you feel completely squeezed by work—how do those sit together?"). Sustain talk is largely accepted and moved past rather than skillfully addressed, limiting effectiveness in resolving ambivalence.

## Overall Summary

**Overall Session Score: 3.4/5 – Adequate MI Competence with Notable Room for Improvement**

### Strengths
- ✓ Strong opening and collaborative stance; invites Andrei's input into agenda
- ✓ Consistent validation and acceptance; maintains genuine warmth and respect
- ✓ Accurate reflective listening; captures content and some emotional undertones
- ✓ Practical problem-solving; identifies concrete, feasible steps respecting constraints
- ✓ Autonomy support; avoids prescriptive language; frames change as Andrei's choice
- ✓ Appropriate pacing and structure; respects time pressure and maintains focus

### Critical Areas for Improvement
- ✗ **Insufficient evocation of intrinsic motivation** – Prioritizes problem-solving over exploring reasons for change, values, and past successes
- ✗ **Minimal cultivation of change talk** – Reflects change talk but doesn't amplify or deepen; misses opportunities to strengthen commitment
- ✗ **Inadequate softening of sustain talk** – Accepts barriers without strategically addressing underlying ambivalence through double-sided reflection or reframing
- ✗ **Limited exploration of deeper drivers** – Doesn't explore connection between work stress, eating patterns, and weight; doesn't investigate meaning of food or health beyond efficiency
- ✗ **Surface-level reflections** – Misses emotional nuance and opportunities for complex, resonant reflections
- ✗ **Subtle expert role** – Occasionally assumes guide role before fully exploring Andrei's own problem-solving capacity

### Impact on Client Engagement
While Andrei leaves with concrete steps and modest confidence (6/10), the session likely underestimates his potential for change. By not deeply exploring intrinsic motivation and values, critical opportunities to strengthen commitment are missed. Without stronger evocation and resolution of ambivalence, likelihood of sustained change is moderate; Andrei may abandon steps when work demands intensify.

### Recommendations for Development (Priority Order)
1. **Deepen evocation of intrinsic motivation** – Ask open questions that help Andrei articulate his own reasons for change and what change would mean
2. **Strategically cultivate change talk** – Amplify and reinforce client statements about desire, ability, reasons, and commitment; ask follow-up questions
3. **Soften sustain talk through strategic responses** – Use double-sided reflection, reframing, and discrepancy amplification rather than accepting and moving past
4. **Explore deeper drivers and mechanisms** – Investigate connections between work stress, eating patterns, health, and identity
5. **Use complex, emotionally resonant reflections** – Capture emotional nuance and deeper meaning, not just content
6. **Strengthen partnership** – Invite Andrei's problem-solving before offering suggestions; position him as expert on his own life
7. **Explore confidence more deeply** – Use confidence scaling as evocation tool; ask what would increase confidence and what barriers exist
8. **Address health concerns as motivators** – Explore what blood pressure medication and breathlessness mean to Andrei; use as potential motivators
9. **Balance problem-solving with evocation** – Spend 60-70% of session on evocation before moving to practical steps
10. **Develop efficient evocation for time-pressured clients** – Ask powerful, open questions that access motivation quickly without lengthy exploration

---

**Recommendation:** With focused development in evocation and change talk cultivation, this psychiatrist would move from adequate (3/5) to good (4/5) MI practice, significantly increasing likelihood of evoking sustained client motivation for change.
