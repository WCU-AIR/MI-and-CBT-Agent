
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

**Summary:**

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish Explicit, Collaborative Agenda-Setting**
- **Action:** At the start of each session, collaboratively establish a prioritized agenda with time allocation. Use language like: "Today we have 50 minutes. I'd like to focus on [problem 1] and [problem 2]. What feels most important to you right now? How should we divide our time?"
- **Example:** "Wendy, I know you're dealing with the clutter, the anxiety, and feeling down. Today, I'm thinking we could focus on understanding what makes it hard to discard items and start planning how to address that. Does that sound right, or is there something else you'd rather focus on?"
- **Practice:** Role-play agenda-setting with a supervisor. Practice prioritizing multiple problems and allocating time explicitly. Record yourself and review for vague language ("see where things go").

### 2. **Implement Systematic Feedback Elicitation Throughout Sessions**
- **Action:** After each intervention or suggestion, explicitly ask for feedback. Use language like: "Does this make sense to you?" "How are you feeling about this approach?" "What concerns do you have?" "Is there anything I've missed?"
- **Example:** After proposing cognitive restructuring, ask: "I'm suggesting we examine whether the thought 'I might need this later' is always true. Does that feel like a helpful approach, or would you rather try something different?"
- **Practice:** Set a goal to elicit feedback at least 5 times per session. Use a checklist during sessions to track this. Record sessions and count feedback-seeking questions.

### 3. **Develop Coherent Case Formulation and Treatment Strategy**
- **Action:** Explicitly articulate a case formulation that explains: (1) Why does the patient hoard? (2) What maintains the behavior? (3) What are the barriers to change? (4) How do depression and anxiety interact with hoarding? (5) What is the treatment sequence?
- **Example:** "Wendy, here's how I understand what's happening: You worry that you might need items later, so you keep them to feel safe and prepared. When you try to discard items, the anxiety increases, so you avoid discarding—which reinforces the belief that discarding is dangerous. Your depression makes it harder to motivate yourself to tackle the clutter. We're going to work on this in stages: first, we'll address your mood to build motivation; then, we'll practice discarding small items to test whether your fears actually happen; and we'll work on reducing how many new items you bring in."
- **Practice:** Write out a detailed case formulation for this patient before each session. Discuss it with a supervisor. Practice explaining it to the patient in clear, collaborative language.

### 4. **Align Interventions with Evidence-Based Hoarding Disorder Treatment**
- **Action:** Prioritize exposure-based interventions (practicing discarding with anxiety tolerance) and behavioral experiments (testing feared outcomes) over cognitive restructuring alone. Use cognitive work to support behavioral change, not as the primary intervention.
- **Example:** Instead of "Try to think that you can get things again," say: "We're going to practice discarding a few items and see what actually happens. You predict you'll regret it, but let's test that prediction. This helps your brain learn that discarding is safe."
- **Practice:** Review evidence-based hoarding disorder treatment protocols (e.g., Steketee & Frost's cognitive-behavioral model). Attend training on exposure-based interventions. Practice framing behavioral experiments as hypothesis tests.

### 5. **Use Guided Discovery Instead of Direct Instruction**
- **Action:** Replace "You should think..." or "Try doing..." with Socratic questions that help the patient reach insights independently. Ask: "What do you predict will happen if...?" "What evidence do you have that...?" "What would it mean if...?" "How could we test that?"
- **Example:** Instead of "Remind yourself you can get things again," ask: "If you did discard something and later realized you needed it, what would you do? How would you handle that? Has that ever happened before?"
- **Practice:** Record sessions and count directive statements vs. questions. Aim for at least 60% questions. Practice Socratic questioning with a supervisor using role-plays.

### 6. **Explore Deeper Cognitions and Emotional Functions of Hoarding**
- **Action:** Move beyond surface-level worry thoughts ("What if I need this later?") to explore identity, security, emotional regulation, and loss. Ask: "What do these items mean to you?" "How do they make you feel?" "What are you protecting yourself from?" "What would it feel like to let them go?"
- **Example:** When the patient says, "It feels like something's missing," ask: "Tell me more about that. What's missing? How do these items help you feel complete?"
- **Practice:** Develop a list of deeper cognitions relevant to hoarding (identity, security, control, loss, responsibility). Practice asking exploratory questions about each. Discuss with a supervisor.

### 7. **Address Depressive Symptoms and Behavioral Activation**
- **Action:** Assess depression severity and functional impairment. Consider behavioral activation (scheduling valued activities) as a first-line intervention to improve mood and motivation for hoarding-related work.
- **Example:** "Wendy, I notice you're feeling down and isolated. Before we focus on clearing the clutter, let's work on getting you more active and connected. What activities used to bring you joy? What would you like to do more of? Let's schedule some of those this week."
- **Practice:** Learn behavioral activation protocols for depression. Practice assessing mood and motivation. Discuss sequencing of interventions with a supervisor.

### 8. **Design Specific, Detailed Homework with Clear Rationale and Monitoring**
- **Action:** Provide explicit instructions, clear rationale, discussion of obstacles, and systematic monitoring. Use thought records or behavioral logs to track thoughts, feelings, and outcomes.
- **Example:** "This week, I'd like you to pick one small area—maybe one shelf—and spend 10 minutes sorting items. Before you start, rate your anxiety 0-10. ### 12. **Avoid Repetition Without Learning; Ensure Session-to-Session Progress**
