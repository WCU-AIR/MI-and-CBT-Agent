### Partnership

## Summary Assessment

### Strengths
- Maintains consistent focus on a specific health concern (smoking)
- Gathers information about patient's smoking behavior
- Expresses genuine concern for patient's health and baby's wellbeing (though execution is poor)

### Critical Areas for Improvement
- **Complete absence of acceptance and empathy:** No reflective listening, no validation, no unconditional positive regard
- **Authoritarian stance:** Repeated controlling language, explicit denial of patient autonomy
- **Dismissal of patient's stated priorities:** Patient clearly indicates exhaustion and infant care are primary concerns; clinician imposes smoking as focus
- **Failure to evoke change talk:** Patient expresses genuine motivation; clinician ignores these statements
- **Confrontational approach to sustain talk:** Uses shame and direct contradiction rather than skillful MI techniques
- **No collaboration or partnership:** Patient makes multiple attempts to co-construct solutions; all are rejected
- **Repeated ineffective strategy:** Same demand repeated seven times without variation or response to patient feedback
- **Coercive language:** Uses guilt and shame to pressure compliance rather than supporting intrinsic motivation

### Impact on Client Engagement
This session is likely counterproductive. Research shows confrontational, coercive approaches trigger psychological resistance and lead to worse outcomes than collaborative approaches. Patient's change talk is ignored rather than amplified. Attempts at collaboration are rejected, communicating that input is not valued. Shame and guilt induction may produce temporary compliance but not sustained behavior change. Most concerning is erosion of therapeutic relationship—vulnerable patient seeking support receives judgment and demands instead.

### Recommendation
**Clinician must fundamentally shift from expert-driven, controlling stance to collaborative, evocative, empathic stance.** This requires developing reflective listening skills, abandoning controlling language, evoking patient's own motivation, and treating patient as partner with valuable expertise about her own life. These are learnable skills requiring focused practice and commitment to MI principles.
