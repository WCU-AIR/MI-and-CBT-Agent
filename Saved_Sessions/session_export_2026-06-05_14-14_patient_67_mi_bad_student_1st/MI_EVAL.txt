### Acceptance

**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates profound absence of unconditional positive regard. Repeated use of judgmental language ("your diet is probably terrible," "you're digging your own grave") and explicit rejection of client autonomy ("This isn't about what you want—it's about what you have to do") characterizes entire interaction. ### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no reflective listening demonstrated. Psychiatrist fails to understand or validate patient's perspective. When patient states smoking "helps me chill out, handle stress," psychiatrist does not acknowledge this. Dismisses emotional reality with "This isn't about feelings; it's about facts." No complex reflections, no attempts to capture emotional nuance, no genuine curiosity about patient's internal world. Operates from assumed knowledge rather than collaborative exploration.

### Direction

**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains consistent focus on smoking cessation, preventing topic drift. However, execution is fundamentally misaligned with MI principles. Uses coercive, repetitive demands ("Will you quit smoking today?" asked at least six times) with no variation in approach, no exploration of barriers, and no attempt to understand readiness. Introduces multiple competing targets (smoking, exercise, diet) without prioritization. Repetitive, escalating demand structure suggests attempt to force compliance rather than guide toward intrinsic motivation. ### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates zero collaboration. Psychiatrist explicitly positions himself as sole authority and patient as passive recipient of directives. Opening statement establishes unilateral authority: "I'm going to tell you what you need to do." Explicitly rejects client input: "This isn't about what you want—it's about what you have to do." Never asks for patient's ideas about change approach. When patient suggests "cut down gradually first," psychiatrist rejects outright rather than explores. By session end, psychiatrist restarts conversation with identical language, suggesting patient's input has zero impact. This is unilateral imposition, not collaboration.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session characterized by systematic undermining of autonomy and explicit pressure to comply. Repeated use of mandatory language: "You have to quit," "You must start exercising," "no ifs, ands, or buts." Frames change as mandatory rather than as choice patient can make. Explicitly denies client autonomy: "You need to stop saying you're not ready." Rejects patient's autonomy to define own process: "No, you can't just say 'maybe' or 'on my terms.'" Patient explicitly states need for autonomy ("I just need to find a way that works for me, not because someone's telling me to"), and psychiatrist responds by intensifying pressure. Violates fundamental MI principle of respecting client self-determination.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Demonstrates almost no evocation. Psychiatrist tells patient what he needs to do without exploring patient's own reasons for change or internal resources. Never asks open-ended questions designed to elicit motivation: "What matters most to you about your health?" or "Tell me about being there for your family?" Provides external reasons for change without exploring whether these resonate with patient. Ignores patient's powerful change-oriented statements: "I want to live longer and be there for my family" and "I don't want to hurt my family or myself" receive no follow-up exploration. Fails to acknowledge or build upon patient's self-awareness, honesty about ambivalence, and concern for family. Imposes external reasons rather than evoking internal motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Demonstrates opposite of partnership. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role throughout and explicitly rejects notion that patient has relevant wisdom about own change process. Opening frames psychiatrist as expert who will tell patient what to do. Dismisses patient's proposed approach without exploration. Rejects patient's self-knowledge: "You need to stop saying you're not ready." Frames patient's perspective as irrelevant: "This isn't about what you want—it's about what you have to do." Contradicts patient's understanding of own experience: "Smoking isn't a relaxation tool; it's a poison." Never acknowledges that patient knows his own life, stress patterns, family situation, and capacity for change better than psychiatrist. No genuine power-sharing or co-creation of change process.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session contains minimal change talk from patient, and when it occurs, psychiatrist fails to amplify or reinforce it. Patient's change talk includes: "Sure, I can quit," "I want to live longer and be there for my family," "I don't want to hurt my family or myself," and "I'm open to the idea." These valuable statements should have been explored and amplified. Instead, when patient says "Sure, I can quit," psychiatrist responds with "No, you can't just say 'maybe' or 'on my terms.'" When patient expresses family concern, this is ignored entirely. Psychiatrist uses confrontation and pressure, which strengthens sustain talk rather than change talk. By session end, patient's change talk has diminished and sustain talk has intensified: "I'm not going to quit right now."

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Demonstrates opposite of softening sustain talk. Psychiatrist directly confronts patient's arguments for status quo, which typically strengthens resistance. Patient's sustain talk ("I like smoking," "It helps me chill out," "I'm not ready," "Quitting cold turkey might be tough") is met with direct confrontation: "Smoking isn't a relaxation tool; it's a poison," "You need to stop saying you're not ready," "You have to quit now, no ifs or buts." Skilled MI practitioner would use double-sided reflections acknowledging both appeal of smoking and reasons to quit. Instead, psychiatrist argues directly against sustain talk, triggering defensive responses and strengthening resistance. By session end, patient's sustain talk has intensified: "I like smoking; it helps me relax" and "I'm not going to quit right now." Confrontational approach has backfired, making patient more entrenched in resistance.

## Overall Session Score: **1.1/5**

### Summary Assessment

**Critical Findings:**

This session represents a **fundamental failure to practice Motivational Interviewing** and demonstrates profound misunderstanding of evidence-based behavior change practice. The psychiatrist operates from an authoritarian, expert-driven model that violates nearly every core MI principle and is likely to **harm rather than help** the patient's motivation for change.

**Key Deficiencies:**
- Complete absence of acceptance and empathy
- Systematic undermining of autonomy through mandatory language and pressure
- Coercive rather than collaborative approach
- Failure to evoke patient's own motivation
- Confrontational approach to resistance that strengthens rather than softens it
- Repetitive, escalating pressure with no variation in strategy

**Impact on Patient Engagement:**

By session end, patient has moved from **ambivalence to more entrenched resistance**. Change talk has diminished; sustain talk has intensified. Patient explicitly stated psychiatrist's approach is counterproductive: "I don't think yelling at me is helping either." Patient clearly communicated need for autonomy: "I just need to find a way that works for me, not because someone's telling me to." Psychiatrist responded by intensifying pressure, directly contradicting patient's stated needs.

**Clinical Recommendation:**

**This psychiatrist requires comprehensive MI training and reorientation.** Current approach is harmful and should not continue without significant intervention and supervision. Fundamental philosophical shift required from expert/directive stance to collaborative/evocative stance. This requires not just new techniques but genuine commitment to respecting client autonomy, understanding client perspective, and evoking intrinsic motivation rather than imposing external pressure.
