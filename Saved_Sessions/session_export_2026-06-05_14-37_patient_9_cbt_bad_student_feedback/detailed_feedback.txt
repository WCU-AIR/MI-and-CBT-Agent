
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## CRITICAL PRELIMINARY NOTE

**This session is fundamentally inappropriate for the patient and diagnosis.** Ike Crocker presents with Antisocial Personality Disorder (APD)—a condition characterized by pervasive disregard for others' rights, deceitfulness, lack of remorse, and poor insight. Standard CBT is contraindicated as a primary intervention for APD without significant modifications and explicit focus on behavioral accountability and harm reduction. The therapist demonstrates no awareness of this diagnostic reality and applies generic, empathy-based interventions that are ineffective and potentially harmful with this population.

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made two separate attempts to establish an agenda, appearing near the beginning and again mid-session (suggesting disorganization). The first agenda-setting attempt ("How have you been feeling lately? Just take it easy and let's chat about whatever's on your mind") is vague, unstructured, and lacks specificity. There are no identified target problems, no prioritization, no time allocation, and no collaborative problem definition. The therapist essentially invites open-ended discussion without clinical direction.

The second agenda attempt ("Before we jump in, I thought maybe we could have a quick chat about what you'd like to focus on") is marginally better—the therapist does ask what the patient wants to focus on and the patient identifies work stress. However, the therapist never translates this into concrete, measurable treatment goals. There is no discussion of what "dealing with all the crap" means operationally, no identification of specific behavioral targets, and no establishment of how progress will be measured. The agenda remains superficial: "talk about what's happening at work, how it's affecting you, and what you'd like to see change."

Critically, **the therapist never addresses the actual presenting problem from the referral source**: workplace safety violations, absenteeism, poor workmanship, dangerous errors, and interpersonal conflict. Instead, the therapist allows the patient to reframe the problem as "everyone else is incompetent"—which is precisely the externalized blame pattern that characterizes APD and perpetuates the dysfunction. A competent therapist would have clarified that the referral was for *his* behavior and performance, not for managing others' incompetence.

The session lacks structure, prioritization, and clinical direction. The therapist does not establish a coherent treatment plan or identify specific behavioral targets for intervention.

**Summary:**
Agenda-setting was attempted but remained vague, unstructured, and failed to identify concrete treatment goals or address the actual presenting problem. The therapist allowed the patient to externalize responsibility rather than establishing accountability-focused objectives.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

"What do you think about what we just discussed?" or "Is this helpful?" That's a joke," "Like relaxing is some magic fix," "Don't expect it to make all this mess just disappear"), the therapist did not pause to understand why these interventions were unhelpful or ask for feedback on what *would* be useful. This represents a fundamental failure in collaborative practice. With a patient presenting with poor insight and externalized blame patterns (as in APD), feedback elicitation is *essential* to identify whether the patient recognizes the connection between his behavior and consequences. The absence of this skill means the therapist has no mechanism to assess whether the patient is engaging authentically or simply complying superficially.

**Summary:**
No meaningful feedback was elicited at any point in the session. The therapist did not check understanding, solicit reactions, or adjust approach based on patient response—a critical failure with this population.
