### Acceptance

**Comments:** Minimal acceptance demonstrated. While initial warmth is present ("It's nice to meet you today"), the psychiatrist repeatedly minimizes Maria's suffering by prescribing action without validating her emotional reality. The psychiatrist fails to affirm Maria's worth as a person or normalize her struggle. ### Empathy

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration is virtually absent. The psychiatrist maintains an expert-driven, hierarchical stance throughout, using prescriptive language ("You might want to," "It would probably help if," "Maybe you should try") that positions the psychiatrist as the authority and Maria as a passive recipient. Maria is never asked what she thinks might help or what she is willing to try. The psychiatrist makes unilateral decisions about treatment without inviting Maria's input or acknowledging her expertise about her own experience.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is absent. The psychiatrist consistently pressures Maria toward predetermined solutions with language that undermines her sense of choice and agency. When Maria expresses doubt or resistance, the psychiatrist reframes it as something to overcome ("maybe you should try to push through anyway") rather than respecting her autonomous choice. The psychiatrist never acknowledges Maria's right to make her own decisions about treatment or validates her reservations. This controlling approach contradicts MI principles.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation is virtually absent. The psychiatrist tells rather than asks, prescribes rather than explores. Maria offers multiple opportunities for evocation ("a tiny part of me wants to believe maybe things could get better," "I don't want to keep feeling like this forever," "Maybe it's worth a shot"), but the psychiatrist responds with superficial affirmation or prescription rather than deepening exploration. The psychiatrist never asks about Maria's values, what matters to her, or what she would want her life to look like. Critical opportunities to draw out her own motivation are missed.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Softening sustain talk is minimally effective. The psychiatrist relies on confrontation, reassurance, and prescription rather than empathic exploration and strategic reframing. When Maria expresses hopelessness ("Nothing works"), the psychiatrist responds with "maybe you should try to push through anyway"—confrontational and dismissive. When Maria expresses fear ("I'm scared it'll just be the same thing all over again"), the psychiatrist reassures without exploring the underlying concern. ## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.4/5 – Poor Motivational Interviewing Practice**

### Key Strengths:
- Initial safety assessment appropriately addresses suicidal ideation and plans
- Psychiatrist remains engaged throughout despite client hopelessness
- Basic effort at reflective listening, though lacking depth
- Attempt to provide encouragement and hope

### Critical Areas for Improvement:
- **Fundamental MI stance:** Psychiatrist operates from expert-driven, prescriptive model rather than evocative, collaborative approach—this is the core deficiency
- **Acceptance and empathy:** Minimization of suffering rather than validation; superficial reflections lacking emotional attunement
- **Autonomy and collaboration:** Pressure toward predetermined solutions rather than respect for client autonomy or co-creation of treatment
- **Evocation:** Tells rather than asks; prescribes rather than explores; misses opportunities to amplify client's own motivation
- **Change talk cultivation:** Emerging change talk not deepened or reinforced; critical moments of ambivalence not explored
- **Sustain talk management:** Met with confrontation and reassurance rather than empathic exploration

### Impact on Client Engagement:
This session is likely to **decrease** Maria's engagement and motivation for change. By repeatedly prescribing solutions without exploring her perspective, the psychiatrist communicates that her experience doesn't matter. Maria's final statement—"I'll try to do those things... but I guess I owe it to the tiny part of me that still wants to feel better"—reflects compliance, not genuine motivation. For a client with active suicidal ideation, this represents a critical failure to establish the collaborative partnership necessary for sustained engagement and safety.

### Primary Recommendation:
The psychiatrist must fundamentally shift from a prescriptive, expert-driven stance to an evocative, collaborative one. This requires replacing "you should" with "what do you think?", validating Maria's suffering before problem-solving, asking open questions that draw out her own motivation, and genuinely respecting her autonomy and expertise about her own experience.
