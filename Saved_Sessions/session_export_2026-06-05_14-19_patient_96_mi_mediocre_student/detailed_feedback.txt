
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal acceptance and unconditional positive regard throughout this session. While the opening ("It's nice to meet you today") and occasional reflections ("I hear you saying it feels really exhausting") show surface-level warmth, the overall stance is fundamentally judgmental and dismissive of Maria's experience.

Most critically, the psychiatrist repeatedly minimizes Maria's suffering by prescribing action without first validating her emotional reality. When Maria states "Nothing works" and "I'm just so tired of pretending I'm okay," the psychiatrist responds with "maybe you should try to push through anyway" and "Sometimes you just have to keep trying different things until something works." These responses communicate: *Your pain isn't real enough to warrant understanding; just try harder.* This is the opposite of acceptance. The psychiatrist also uses conditional language ("It's good that you still have a little hope") that subtly judges Maria's current emotional state rather than accepting where she is.

Additionally, the psychiatrist never affirms Maria's worth as a person or normalizes her struggle. There is no statement like "What you're experiencing is real and understandable given what you've been through" or "Many people in your situation feel this way." Instead, the focus remains on what Maria *should* do, not on who she *is*. The repeated directive framing ("You might want to," "It would probably help if") creates an expert-driven, prescriptive tone that contradicts acceptance of her autonomy and current state.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist shows minimal acceptance. Surface warmth is undermined by repeated minimization of Maria's pain, prescriptive language, and failure to affirm her worth or normalize her experience. This stance communicates judgment rather than unconditional positive regard.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening that captures both content and emotional nuance. The psychiatrist demonstrates only basic, surface-level reflections that miss the depth of Maria's experience.

Examples of inadequate reflections:
- Maria: "Nothing works. I'm just so tired of pretending I'm okay." 
- Psychiatrist: "It sounds like you've been through a lot with therapy and meds without much relief, and you're feeling pretty stuck."

This reflection captures the *facts* but misses the *emotional core*—the exhaustion, the inauthenticity, the despair. A more empathic reflection would be: "It sounds like you're not just tired of the pain itself, but tired of the effort of trying to manage it while feeling like nothing changes. And there's something about having to pretend everything's okay that adds another layer of loneliness to this."

Similarly, when Maria says "Maybe no one really gets how deep this pain goes," the psychiatrist does not explore this profound statement about feeling misunderstood. Instead, the psychiatrist pivots to advice: "Maybe you should try to find someone you feel even a little comfortable with to share this with." This is problem-solving, not empathy.

The psychiatrist also fails to reflect Maria's ambivalence—her simultaneous hopelessness and faint desire for relief. Maria explicitly states "a tiny part of me wants to believe maybe things could get better" and "there's a tiny part of me that hopes it might help," but the psychiatrist never deepens this by reflecting the *conflict* she's experiencing: "So part of you is exhausted and convinced nothing will work, and another part—even if it's small—still wonders if maybe this time could be different. That's a really difficult place to be."

**Summary:**

Empathy is superficial. Reflections capture facts but miss emotional nuance, ambivalence, and the depth of Maria's suffering. The psychiatrist does not demonstrate understanding of her internal conflict or validate the legitimacy of her pain.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI means maintaining strategic focus on the target behavior (in this case, reducing suicidal ideation and increasing engagement with treatment) while remaining client-centered. The psychiatrist does maintain a general focus on treatment engagement and safety, but the approach is inconsistent and poorly executed.

The psychiatrist does ask relevant safety questions early ("Are you thinking about hurting yourself right now, or have you had plans or thoughts about how you might do that?"), which is appropriate and maintains focus on the presenting concern. However, after this initial assessment, the direction becomes muddled.

The psychiatrist repeatedly pivots to prescriptive advice ("You might want to set up an appointment soon," "You might want to consider setting up that appointment anyway," "You might want to try to reach out to someone") without strategically exploring Maria's own reasons for change or her barriers. This is directive in the wrong way—it's prescriptive rather than evocative. True direction in MI would involve: "What would need to happen for you to feel like trying again might be worth it?" or "What's one small thing that, if it changed, might make you feel a bit less hopeless?"

The psychiatrist also fails to maintain focus on Maria's stated ambivalence. When Maria mentions "a tiny part of me wants to believe maybe things could get better," this is a critical moment to explore and amplify. Instead, the psychiatrist says "It's good that you still have a little hope" and moves on. This represents a missed opportunity to strategically deepen the client's own motivation.

**Summary:**

Direction is present but inconsistently applied. The psychiatrist maintains general focus on treatment but relies on prescription rather than strategic evocation. Critical moments of ambivalence are not explored or amplified.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, with both bringing valuable knowledge. This session demonstrates almost no collaboration—it is entirely expert-driven and hierarchical.

The psychiatrist consistently uses language that positions the psychiatrist as the expert with solutions and Maria as the passive recipient: "You might want to," "It would probably help if," "Maybe you should try," "It would probably be a good step to take." This is not collaborative language; it is prescriptive language that assumes the psychiatrist knows what Maria needs better than Maria does.

True collaboration would involve:
- "What do you think might help, even if you're skeptical?"
- "If you were to try something different, what would that look like from your perspective?"
- "What would need to be true for you to feel like this was worth trying?"

Instead, the psychiatrist makes unilateral decisions about what Maria should do: make an appointment, tell someone, write down feelings, try new meds or therapy. Maria is never asked what *she* thinks might help or what *she* is willing to try. When Maria expresses doubt ("I'm scared it'll just be the same thing all over again"), the psychiatrist does not explore this fear collaboratively; instead, the psychiatrist reassures and redirects: "It's good that you still have a little hope... Maybe you should try to hold on to that."

The psychiatrist also never asks Maria about her previous treatment experiences in a way that honors her expertise about what has and hasn't worked for her. The statement "It would probably help if you talked to a doctor about adjusting your meds or trying a new therapy" assumes Maria hasn't already considered this, when in fact she has explicitly stated she's tried therapy and meds without relief.

**Summary:**

Collaboration is virtually absent. The psychiatrist maintains an expert-driven, hierarchical stance throughout, prescribing solutions rather than co-creating them with Maria. There is no genuine power-sharing or joint problem-solving.

### 5. Autonomy Support
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Autonomy support means actively fostering the client's perception of choice and emphasizing their ownership of the change process. This session demonstrates the opposite—consistent pressure and prescription that undermines autonomy.

The psychiatrist repeatedly uses language that pressures Maria toward predetermined solutions: "Maybe you should try to push through anyway," "It would probably help if you talked to a doctor," "You might want to set up an appointment soon," "You should try to reach out to someone," "It would probably be a good step to take soon."

Critically, when Maria expresses doubt or resistance—which is her autonomous choice—the psychiatrist does not respect it. Instead, the psychiatrist reframes it as something to overcome: "I get that it feels pretty low right now, but maybe you should try to push through anyway." This communicates: *Your choice to be cautious is wrong; you should do what I'm suggesting regardless of your reservations.*

True autonomy support would sound like:
- "It's completely up to you whether you want to try again. What would need to be different for you to feel like it was worth trying?"
- "You're the expert on what has and hasn't worked for you. Given that, what do you think might be worth exploring?"
- "I can't make this decision for you, and I wouldn't want to. What feels right to you?"

Instead, the psychiatrist subtly pressures Maria by suggesting that her current choice (not to try) is wrong and that the psychiatrist's suggestion (to try) is right. This is controlling, not autonomy-supportive.

Additionally, the psychiatrist never acknowledges Maria's right to make her own decisions about treatment, including the right to decline. In a high-risk suicidality case, this is particularly important—Maria needs to feel that her autonomy is respected while also feeling that the psychiatrist cares about her safety. The psychiatrist achieves neither.

**Summary:**

Autonomy support is absent. The psychiatrist consistently pressures Maria toward predetermined solutions and fails to respect her reservations or acknowledge her right to choose. This undermines rather than supports her sense of agency.

### 6. Evocation
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Evocation is the core of MI—drawing out the client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources rather than imposing them. This session demonstrates almost no evocation. Instead, the psychiatrist tells Maria what she should do.

Maria offers several opportunities for evocation:
1. "a tiny part of me wants to believe maybe things could get better" — This is change talk that should be explored and amplified.
2. "I don't want to keep feeling like this forever" — This is a statement of desire for change that should be deepened.
3. "Maybe I could think about telling someone" — This is a tentative step toward change that should be encouraged and explored.
4. "Maybe it's worth a shot" — Another moment of emerging motivation.

In response to these moments, the psychiatrist does not evoke further. Instead, the psychiatrist either:
- Affirms superficially and moves on ("It's good that you still have a little hope")
- Prescribes action ("You might want to try to reach out to someone")
- Reassures without exploring ("Remember, you're not alone in this")

A skilled evocative response to "a tiny part of me wants to believe maybe things could get better" would be: "Tell me more about that part. What is it that makes you think maybe things could be different? What would that look like?" This draws out Maria's own reasons for change.

Instead, the psychiatrist says "It's good that you still have a little hope, even if it feels small. Maybe you should try to hold on to that." This is prescriptive ("should try to hold on"), not evocative.

The psychiatrist also never asks Maria about her values, what matters to her, or what she would want her life to look like if the pain were manageable. - **Action:** Fundamentally reframe your role from "expert with solutions" to "collaborative partner exploring possibilities." Replace prescriptive language ("You should," "You might want to") with evocative questions ("What do you think...?" "Tell me more about...").
