
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist failed to establish a structured, collaborative agenda appropriate for a patient with moderate alcohol use disorder and alcohol-induced depressive disorder. In the opening, the therapist offered only vague language: "I was thinking we could just chat a bit about what's been on your mind lately and maybe talk through some things that have been bothering you." This is not agenda-setting; it is an invitation to free-form discussion without prioritization, time allocation, or explicit session goals.

A proper CBT agenda for this patient should have included: (1) explicit identification of target problems (depressive symptoms, alcohol cravings, relapse prevention, family conflict), (2) prioritization of which problems to address today, (3) time allocation for each topic, and (4) collaborative agreement on focus. For example: "Matthew, I know you're dealing with depression, worry about staying sober, and family stress. Today we have 50 minutes. What feels most urgent to tackle first? There is no evidence of session structure, prioritization, or collaborative goal-setting across either session.

**Summary:**

Agenda-setting was essentially absent. The therapist used open-ended invitations rather than structured, collaborative goal-setting. No explicit session priorities, time allocation, or measurable targets were established.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated basic comprehension of the patient's explicit statements but missed critical nuances and failed to explore the patient's internal experience with sufficient depth. This is below-average performance for a diagnostic/early treatment session with a complex patient.

**What was understood:**
The therapist accurately reflected that the patient feels "down," experiences "heaviness," worries about relapse, and feels pressure from family. The therapist also recognized that negative thoughts ("I'm not cut out for this," "I'll probably end up drinking again") are present and distressing.

**What was missed or inadequately explored:**

1. **The functional relationship between depression and drinking:** The patient stated, "It feels easier to just give in and have a drink to quiet all those thoughts for a little while." This is a critical insight—alcohol is functioning as a negative reinforcement mechanism (escape from depressive/anxious thoughts). The therapist never explored this. A deeper understanding would have led to questions like: "When you drink to quiet those thoughts, how long does that relief last? What happens after?" This would establish the maintenance cycle and inform treatment strategy.

2. **Distinguishing primary vs. secondary depression:** The case formulation indicates alcohol-induced depressive disorder, meaning depressive symptoms are expected to improve with abstinence. However, the therapist never assessed whether the patient understands this distinction or whether he believes his depression is permanent/treatment-resistant. This affects motivation and hopelessness.

3. **The patient's ambivalence about change:** The patient says, "It's hard to quiet that voice in my head telling me I'm going to fail again" and "It's just been hard to get into any new habits lately." These statements suggest ambivalence and low self-efficacy. The therapist offered encouragement but never explored the roots of this ambivalence or the patient's past attempts at change (he had two 30-day abstinence periods). Understanding what worked before and what derailed him would be essential.

4. **Family dynamics and social support:** The patient mentions family pressure multiple times ("My family's been on my case," "I'm letting everyone down — my wife, my daughter"). The therapist never explored whether family support is a resource or a stressor, whether the patient has discussed his recovery plan with them, or how to leverage family involvement constructively.

5. **Substance use triggers and high-risk situations:** The patient never explicitly identified what triggers drinking or what situations are highest-risk. The therapist assumed thoughts are the primary trigger but never asked about situational, emotional, or social triggers.

The therapist's empathy is adequate ("I hear you," "That's completely understandable") but remains surface-level. True understanding in CBT requires grasping the patient's cognitive and behavioral patterns, not just validating emotions.

**Summary:**

The therapist grasped explicit content but missed critical functional relationships, ambivalence, and contextual factors essential for case conceptualization. Understanding was adequate for basic rapport but insufficient for effective CBT case formulation.

### 1. **Establish a Structured, Collaborative Agenda and Case Formulation (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

### 2. **Conduct Systematic Assessment of Substance Use Triggers and Functional Analysis (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
