# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal acceptance and unconditional positive regard demonstrated. While opening is warm, clinician quickly shifts to directive problem-solving mode. Prescriptive statements ("Maybe you should try to avoid those triggers") undermine genuine acceptance. When patient appropriately identifies barriers, clinician does not validate concerns or affirm patient's realistic assessment. Lacks authentic affirmation of patient's strengths, motivation, and self-awareness. Final praise ("Keep up the good work!") feels perfunctory rather than genuinely affirming.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal empathy and emotional attunement. Clinician relies on surface-level factual reflections rather than capturing emotional nuance. Misses patient's frustration about repeated failure, fear of failure, and weight of struggle. When patient describes social pressure ("it's hard to say no in those moments"), clinician responds with advice rather than empathic reflection. Does not explore what smoking means to patient, how it functions as stress relief, or what patient fears about quitting. Complex reflections that deepen insight are entirely absent. Fails to validate patient's realistic concerns about timing and social commitments.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Maintains strategic focus on smoking cessation throughout session; conversation does not wander from primary goal. Asks clarifying questions about history, previous attempts, and timeline. Consistently returns to quit date and medication options. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. Prescribes specific strategies (avoiding triggers, nicotine replacement therapy, cutting down gradually, two-week quit date) without exploring patient's own ideas or preferences. Asks some open questions ("Why do you think quitting smoking is important for you right now?"), which is positive, but these are interspersed with closed questions and directives, creating uneven balance.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal collaboration and joint problem-solving. Clinician adopts expert-driven, hierarchical stance throughout. Language is consistently directive: repeated use of "should" and "maybe you should" positions clinician as expert prescribing solutions rather than partner exploring options. When patient identifies realistic barrier ("Avoiding those triggers isn't always possible with my social life and job"), clinician does not engage collaboratively; instead moves forward with medication discussion. Fails to seek patient's input on key decisions. Final summary presents clinician's interpretation rather than collaborative co-construction. True collaboration would involve "Let's think together" and genuine invitation for patient to shape plan.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Significantly undermines autonomy throughout session. Prescriptive statements position clinician as authority determining what patient should do rather than supporting patient's own decision-making. When patient expresses realistic hesitation about timing ("I just don't want to pick a date too soon and then fail"), clinician does not affirm patient's autonomy; instead prescribes: "It would probably help if you set a quit date soon." Fails to emphasize patient's ownership of change process. Never explicitly affirms patient's right to choose differently, move at own pace, or reject suggestions. Patient's compliance appears driven by clinician pressure rather than genuine autonomous choice.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal evocation of patient's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources. Patient spontaneously offers significant change talk ("I really need some medication... to get me off smoking," "I'm serious about quitting this time"), but clinician does not strategically evoke or amplify this language. When patient provides rich material about reasons for change (health concerns, wife's support, desire to avoid serious health problems), clinician does not deepen exploration. Fails to ask "What have you thought about trying?" or "What do you think might help?" Instead, clinician tells patient what to do. Does not explore patient's ambivalence about cutting down or evoke patient's own problem-solving. Advice-giving predominates over asking.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Actively assumes expert role and minimizes partnership. Positions clinician as authority with answers rather than treating interview as occurring between equal partners. Repeated use of "I think" and "you should" establishes hierarchical dynamic. When patient identifies barriers, clinician does not acknowledge patient's expertise about own life. Language is hierarchical rather than inclusive; uses "I" statements about what patient should do rather than "we" language reflecting collaboration. Clinician says "I'm here to support you," but this is undermined by preceding prescriptive statements. Final summary presents clinician's interpretation rather than inviting patient to co-construct plan. Client expertise about own life is not genuinely valued or incorporated.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill in evoking, amplifying, or reinforcing patient's change talk. Patient offers multiple powerful statements of desire, need, and commitment ("I'm serious about quitting this time," "I'm done with this smoking habit," "I really need some medication... to get me off smoking"), but clinician does not strategically respond to amplify or reinforce. After patient expresses serious commitment, clinician responds with closed question about confidence rather than deepening change talk with follow-up questions. When patient expresses willingness, clinician does not reinforce or explore what patient believes he can do. Misses opportunity to evoke change talk about ability and commitment when patient rates confidence at 6/10. Does not follow up on excellent change talk about health and wife's support with deeper amplifying questions. Generic final statement does not specifically reinforce patient's change talk or confidence.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Inconsistent skill in handling patient's arguments for status quo. Patient expresses sustain talk about stress barriers, social triggers, difficulty cutting down, and fear of failure. Clinician does not skillfully soften this sustain talk through double-sided reflections or reframing. When patient says "Avoiding those triggers isn't always possible with my social life and job," clinician does not use double-sided reflection; instead moves forward with medication discussion. However, clinician avoids directly confronting or arguing with sustain talk, which is positive. Attempts to redirect toward solutions through prescriptive advice rather than evocative exploration. More skillful approach would involve acknowledging validity of concerns, exploring sustain talk more deeply, using double-sided reflections, and evoking patient's own problem-solving. Does not reinforce sustain talk, which is positive.

## Overall Session Assessment

### **Overall Session Score: 2.3/5**

---

### Summary of Key Findings

**Strengths:**
- Maintains focus on smoking cessation throughout; conversation does not wander from primary goal
- Avoids confrontation when patient expresses sustain talk; does not argue or dismiss concerns
- Asks some open questions that allow patient to express motivations
- Demonstrates basic politeness and warmth in opening
- Acknowledges patient's willingness to try medication and strategies
- Offers concrete support through medication discussion and follow-up scheduling

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- **Excessive prescription and advice-giving** dominate session; clinician tells patient what to do rather than evoking patient's own ideas and solutions
- **Minimal empathy and emotional attunement**; captures facts but misses emotional nuance, fear of failure, and weight of struggle
- **Failure to amplify change talk**; patient offers multiple powerful statements of commitment, but clinician does not strategically reinforce or deepen this language
- **Undermines autonomy** by prescribing timelines, strategies, and decisions rather than supporting patient's own choice-making
- **Hierarchical, expert-driven stance** rather than genuine partnership; power is held by clinician, not shared with patient
- **Minimal collaboration** in developing change plan; patient's input is not genuinely sought or incorporated
- **Missed opportunities** for deeper exploration of patient's motivations, barriers, confidence, and resources
- **Lack of complex reflections** that would deepen patient's insight into ambivalence and strengths

---

### Clinical Impact

**Likelihood of Sustained Change: Moderate to Low**

While patient remains engaged and compliant, engagement appears to be **compliance rather than genuine intrinsic motivation**. Patient's moderate confidence level (6/10) and hesitation about timing suggest underlying ambivalence that has not been explored or addressed. Prescriptive approach may increase patient's anxiety about failure rather than building confidence and self-efficacy. Realistic identification of barriers (stress and social triggers) has not been collaboratively problem-solved. Plan is clinician's plan, not patient's plan. Research suggests that when clients feel plan is imposed rather than collaboratively developed, adherence and success rates decline.

---

### Recommendations for Skill Development

1. **Shift from advice-giving to evocation** – Replace prescriptive statements with open questions that draw out patient's own ideas
2. **Develop complex reflections** – Move beyond factual parroting to capture emotional content and deeper meaning
3. **Strategically amplify change talk** – Deepen patient's own language in favor of change through follow-up questions
4. **Respect and support autonomy** – Explicitly affirm patient's right to choose and move at own pace
5. **Validate and explore sustain talk** – Use double-sided reflections and deeper exploration rather than prescribing over barriers
6. **Develop genuine partnership** – Actively invite patient input; use "we" language; share power explicitly
7. **Ask about confidence and ability, then explore** – Follow up confidence questions with exploration of what would increase confidence
8. **Use open questions strategically throughout** – Aim for 60% open questions; replace closed questions with exploratory ones
9. **Develop deeper empathy through curiosity** – Ask questions exploring what smoking means, what patient fears, what values are important
10. **Integrate medication discussion with evocation** – Discuss medication collaboratively as one tool among many, not as primary solution

---

### Training Recommendations

**Current MI Competency Level:** Below-average to adequate (2-3/5)

Clinician demonstrates basic supportive counseling skills but lacks strategic, skillful application of MI techniques necessary for effective motivational interviewing. Recommended training pathway:

- Formal MI training (2-3 day workshop)
- Supervised practice with feedback (reviewing recorded sessions with MI trainer)
- Peer consultation and practice with colleagues
- Reading foundational MI literature (Miller & Rollnick, "Motivational Interviewing: Helping People Change")
- Regular self-reflection and skill development over 6-12 months
