### Direction

Solutions introduced without first exploring client's own goals or preferences (e.g., "limiting how much you vape or only vaping with friends"). ### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal true collaboration demonstrated. Expert-driven stance adopted throughout. Pre-formed suggestions presented as questions rather than co-constructing goals (e.g., "Do you think it would be a good idea to set some goals for cutting back when basketball season starts? Maybe like limiting..."). Client responds to clinician's agenda rather than co-creating treatment plan. No moments where clinician asks "What ideas do you have?" or "What do you think would work best for you?" Session is clinician-driven, not client-centered. When client offers own ideas, clinician acknowledges but immediately adds more suggestions, maintaining control.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Autonomy frequently undermined through directive language and prescriptive advice. While not using overtly controlling language ("you must"), cumulative effect of repeated suggestions creates controlling environment minimizing sense of choice. Phrasing like "you might want to" and "do you think you could" creates implicit expectation of compliance. Direct exhortation to adhere ("Remember, it's important to stick with your plan") communicates judgment if non-adherence occurs. Never explicitly affirms right to choose differently or validates ambivalence as legitimate. One moment of autonomy support present ("don't be too hard on yourself") insufficient to offset pervasive directive stance.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal evocation demonstrated; reliance on advice-giving and problem-solving instead. Factual questions elicit information but do not evoke motivation. Advice-giving disguised as questions ("Do you think it would probably help if you tried to find other ways to relax?"). Client's spontaneous change talk not amplified or explored deeply. Strong change talk present ("I was already thinking I'd just stop completely when basketball starts 'cause I don't want to get kicked off the team") met with clinician suggestions rather than evocative exploration. Client-identified coping strategies acknowledged but not fully explored. Never asks: "What matters most to you?" "What would it feel like?" "What do you think you're capable of?" Strategic evocative questioning absent throughout.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Genuine partnership fails to establish; expert role assumed throughout. Hierarchical dynamic created from opening ("Usually, I like to start by..."). Clinician consistently positions self as expert with solutions. No moments where clinician conveys that wisdom about change resides within client or that clinician and client are equals. Language is clinician-centered ("I thought we could," "I like to start," "You might want to," "Maybe you should"). When client offers ideas, not positioned as client expertise but as starting point for clinician additions. True partnership would involve statements like "You know yourself best" or "What's your wisdom about this?" Partnership language and stance absent.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill in evoking and cultivating change talk despite multiple spontaneous instances from client. Client offers strong change talk: commitment language ("I was already thinking I'd just stop completely"), ability language ("I'm pretty sure I could stop for a month"), reason language ("I don't want to get kicked off the team"), and identification of coping strategies. When client states "I was already thinking I'd just stop completely when basketball starts," clinician responds with advice rather than amplification: "You might want to try keeping track." Missed opportunity to deepen: "Tell me more about that—what is it about your season that makes you want to do that?" Client-identified coping strategies acknowledged but not evoked deeper. No open-ended questions designed to elicit change talk. Advice-giving may actually suppress change talk by positioning clinician solutions as answer.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Inconsistent skill in handling sustain talk. Clinician avoids reinforcing minimization and does not become adversarial—appropriate approach preventing session from becoming adversarial. However, fails to use sophisticated MI techniques (double-sided reflections, reframing, strategic questioning) to explicitly soften defensiveness. When client states "I don't think it's going to hurt me," clinician responds with factual question rather than double-sided reflection. Defensive statement "It's not like I'm addicted or anything" not addressed. Clinician does not get stuck on sustain talk and pivots toward change talk and problem-solving. Avoidance of reinforcement adequate but misses opportunities for explicit softening through strategic reflection and reframing.

### Overall Clinical Summary

**Session Score: 2.2/5 – Below-Average MI Competence**

#### **Strengths:**
- Warm, non-judgmental opening establishing safe environment for disclosure
- Maintains appropriate focus on target behavior (vaping) and basketball performance
- Avoids confrontation and does not argue with client minimization
- Acknowledges client's own coping strategies when offered
- Normalizes struggle and reduces shame in final exchange
- Client remains engaged throughout and agrees to try suggested strategies

#### **Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- **Excessive advice-giving:** Nearly every exchange includes clinician-generated suggestions; functions as expert delivering solutions rather than collaborative partner
- **Failure to amplify change talk:** Multiple spontaneous instances of strong change talk not strategically deepened or reinforced
- **Minimal exploration of ambivalence:** Clear ambivalence (fun with friends vs. basketball performance; minimization vs. fear of consequences) not reflected or explored
- **Lack of genuine collaboration:** Goal-setting and problem-solving clinician-driven; client asked to agree rather than co-construct
- **Limited autonomy support:** Cumulative effect of repeated suggestions undermines sense of choice and self-determination
- **Superficial empathy:** Accurate reflections lack emotional depth; do not capture nuance of ambivalence or meaning of experience
- **Missed opportunities for evocation:** Does not ask questions drawing out client's own wisdom, motivations, or resources
- **Inadequate softening of sustain talk:** While not reinforced, defensive statements not addressed through sophisticated MI techniques

#### **Impact on Client Motivation:**
While Anika remains engaged and agrees to try suggested strategies, intrinsic motivation for change may not be fully explored or strengthened. Prescriptive approach risks creating external compliance based on clinician suggestions and fear of consequences rather than internal commitment based on client's own values and self-determination.

#### **Recommendations Priority:**
1. Shift from expert-driven to evocative stance (ask rather than tell)
2. Amplify and deepen client's spontaneous change talk
3. Reflect and explore ambivalence through double-sided reflections
4. Establish true collaboration by inviting client to generate goals first
5. Strengthen autonomy support through choice-affirming language
6. Deepen empathy through complex reflections capturing emotional nuance
7. Use strategic questions to soften sustain talk and explore minimization
8. Position client as expert on her own life and resources
9. Reduce clinician talking time; increase strategic silence
10. Integrate MI fidelity monitoring into ongoing practice

---

**Likelihood of Sustained Change:** Moderate. Client leaves with external plan and some motivation, but intrinsic motivation may be limited. More evocative, collaborative approach would likely result in stronger, more sustainable motivation for change.
