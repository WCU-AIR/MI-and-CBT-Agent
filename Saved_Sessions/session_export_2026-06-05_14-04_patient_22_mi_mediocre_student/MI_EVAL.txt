# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates baseline respect and warmth with a welcoming greeting ("It's nice to meet you. I'm glad you came in today"). However, acceptance remains surface-level. Multiple opportunities to affirm Camila's current efforts (walking, yoga, trying despite energy challenges) are missed. When Camila expresses caution about burnout, the psychiatrist does not affirm this self-awareness as a strength but instead pivots to prescriptive advice. Language occasionally carries implicit judgment (e.g., "confidence is a bit moderate"). True acceptance requires deeper affirmation of inherent worth and normalization of ambivalence, which are insufficiently demonstrated.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Empathy is minimal and largely factual rather than emotional. Reflective listening is sparse; the psychiatrist primarily restates content ("you walk and do some light yoga, but some days you feel low energy") followed by questions rather than reflections demonstrating deep understanding. When Camila mentions feeling "discouraged if I'm not seeing big changes"—a significant emotional statement—the psychiatrist does not reflect or explore this feeling. Complex reflections that capture emotional nuance and underlying perspective are absent. The psychiatrist fails to explore the emotional dimensions of Camila's experience, representing missed opportunities for empathic connection.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains adequate strategic focus on increasing physical activity throughout the session. Early exploratory questions about barriers demonstrate directional clarity. However, as the session progresses, direction becomes increasingly prescriptive rather than collaboratively maintained. The psychiatrist shifts from exploration ("What do you think might help?") to directive suggestion ("you should try," "you might want to"). The final summary frames the plan as already decided ("you're going to try") rather than collaboratively constructed. This is problematic given Camila's moderate confidence (6/10) and expressed uncertainty about adherence.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Collaboration is minimal; the psychiatrist assumes an expert role with Camila positioned as recipient of guidance. The collaborative opening quickly erodes once diagnostic questioning begins. The psychiatrist asks most questions while Camila primarily responds, creating an asymmetrical dynamic. When solutions are offered (smaller goals, scheduling walks, using apps), there is no genuine invitation for Camila to co-construct these ideas. Camila's responses reflect agreement with psychiatrist suggestions rather than co-creation of the plan. True collaboration would involve asking "What do you think might help?" and positioning Camila as the expert on her own life before offering suggestions.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Autonomy is frequently undermined through directive language. Problematic phrases include "you should try," "probably help if," and "you're going to try," which subtly pressure Camila toward predetermined solutions rather than genuinely supporting her freedom to choose. The psychiatrist frames recommendations as logical solutions and seeks compliance rather than supporting autonomous choice. While Camila expresses willingness, this reflects compliance with psychiatrist suggestions rather than autonomous decision-making. One autonomy-supportive statement ("it's okay if you don't get it perfect right away") appears late and does not offset earlier directive stance. Explicit affirmation of Camila's right to choose is absent.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Evocation is central to MI but largely absent here. The psychiatrist relies heavily on telling rather than drawing out Camila's own motivations, values, and resources. When Camila expresses enjoyment of walking and yoga, the psychiatrist does not explore what appeals to her or how these activities make her feel. When she states belief that activity benefits health, the psychiatrist does not explore deeper reasons or values. Critical missed opportunities include not exploring her resilience when managing discomfort, not deepening her importance rating (7/10), and not asking what would increase her confidence. Instead of evoking, the psychiatrist tells: "setting smaller goals," "schedule short walks," "try setting alarms." Camila's own ideas are minimally generated; solutions are psychiatrist-provided.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Partnership is undermined by the psychiatrist's expert-driven stance. The opening is somewhat collaborative, but this quickly shifts to an information-gathering dynamic where the psychiatrist asks most questions and Camila responds. Solution-provision occurs without first exploring Camila's own ideas, positioning the psychiatrist as expert with solutions and Camila as recipient. The final plan summary ("you're going to try") frames the plan as psychiatrist-constructed rather than jointly created. Language patterns ("you should try," "you might want to," "it would probably help if") subtly position psychiatrist judgment as superior to Camila's wisdom about her own life. A more partnership-oriented approach would explicitly invite Camila's ideas and acknowledge her expertise about herself.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Multiple instances of Camila's change talk are not cultivated or reinforced. She offers desire language ("I'm open to talking about ways I might improve"), positive reinforcement ("I enjoy it, and it helps me feel okay"), reason language ("I think being active does make a difference"), belief language ("I do believe that being more active would be good for my health"), commitment language ("I'm willing to try," "I'm interested to see how it goes"), and hope language ("I'm feeling pretty hopeful about trying this"). None of these are reflected, reinforced, or amplified. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on barriers and problems, offering solutions without first amplifying Camila's own motivation. The session misses critical opportunities to strengthen her intrinsic motivation through cultivation of change talk.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist avoids confronting sustain talk, which is appropriate. When Camila expresses concern about burnout ("I don't want to get discouraged or burn out"), the psychiatrist incorporates this concern into the recommendation ("start with easy steps and don't push too hard too fast"), effectively softening the sustain talk. However, when Camila expresses doubt ("I'm not totally sure if I'd stick with it"), the psychiatrist does not explore this or use it as an opportunity to deepen understanding. Double-sided reflections acknowledging both difficulty and capacity are absent. When Camila mentions discouragement about not seeing big changes, the psychiatrist does not explore or reframe this. The psychiatrist demonstrates adequate skill in not arguing with sustain talk but misses opportunities for deeper exploration and strategic reframing.

## **Overall Session Score: 2.3/5**

---

### **Summary of Findings**

#### **Strengths:**
- Warm, respectful opening that creates a non-threatening environment
- Clear and consistent focus on the change goal (increasing physical activity)
- Avoids confrontation with client ambivalence or sustain talk
- Incorporates client concerns (burnout) into recommendations
- Normalizes imperfection ("it's okay if you don't get it perfect right away")
- Elicits importance and confidence ratings

#### **Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- **Minimal reflective listening:** Relies on questions and advice rather than reflections demonstrating understanding
- **Prescriptive rather than evocative:** Offers solutions ("you should try," "you might want to") rather than evoking client's own ideas
- **Expert-driven rather than collaborative:** Assumes expert role; positions client as recipient of guidance
- **Fails to cultivate change talk:** Multiple opportunities to amplify and reinforce client's desire, belief, commitment, and hope are missed
- **Insufficient exploration of ambivalence:** Does not explore doubt or concern deeply when expressed
- **Autonomy undermined:** Directive language subtly pressures client toward predetermined solutions
- **Missed empathic connection:** Emotional dimensions of client's experience are not reflected or explored

#### **Impact on Client Engagement:**
While Camila remains cooperative and agrees with recommendations, the session is unlikely to significantly enhance her intrinsic motivation for change. Her moderate confidence (6/10) and expressed uncertainty ("I'm not totally sure if I'd stick with it") suggest compliance rather than commitment. The psychiatrist's prescriptive approach may undermine her autonomy and self-efficacy. Research demonstrates that client-generated solutions are more likely to be sustained than therapist-prescribed solutions. The session has not optimized the opportunity to strengthen Camila's intrinsic motivation; likelihood of sustained behavior change is moderate at best.

#### **MI Competency Level:**
The psychiatrist is operating at a **below-average to adequate level** of MI competence (2-3/5). While demonstrating respect and avoiding confrontation, the session lacks core MI elements of evocation, collaboration, and cultivation of change talk. The psychiatrist has not yet internalized the fundamental MI principle that motivation for change resides within the client and must be drawn out rather than installed through advice-giving.
