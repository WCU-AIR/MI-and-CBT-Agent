
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda

**Summary:**

Agenda-setting was initiated collaboratively but remained vague, lacked time allocation, and was abandoned mid-session in favor of unstructured conversation. The therapist did not establish concrete session goals or maintain focus on the identified priority.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated virtually no systematic feedback-seeking throughout the session. Evidence-based CBT requires regular elicitation of patient feedback to ensure understanding, assess session helpfulness, and adjust interventions. The therapist never asked: "Does this make sense?" "How are you feeling about what we've discussed?" "Is this approach helpful to you?" "Do you have questions about what we talked about?" ### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated surface-level empathy but fundamentally misunderstood Carlos's clinical presentation and the mechanisms driving his difficulties. This is a serious clinical error with direct implications for treatment effectiveness.

What the therapist *did* understand: Carlos struggles with concentration, sleep, and worry; he feels stressed about college performance; he experiences racing thoughts; he feels out of place as the only Dominican student.

What the therapist *missed* or minimized:

1. **The systemic nature of Carlos's difficulties**: Carlos has documented ADHD (inattentive type), Specific Learning Disorder (reading fluency/comprehension and written expression deficits), and mild depression with panic symptoms. The therapist's repeated suggestion to "remind yourself you're capable" and "think positive" fundamentally misses that Carlos's concentration difficulties have neurobiological and learning-based components requiring structured accommodation and skill-building, not positive self-talk.

A CBT-trained therapist would recognize this as an opportunity to explore the *content* of racing thoughts (automatic thoughts about failure, inadequacy, social rejection) and develop cognitive restructuring or thought records, not dismiss the concern with platitudes.

These are significant psychosocial stressors that intersect with his academic anxiety. The therapist acknowledged this superficially ("feeling out of place is something a lot of people experience") but did not explore how identity-related stress might be amplifying his anxiety or contributing to his sense of hopelessness. The therapist offered hydration and positive thinking but never helped Carlos map this cycle, identify intervention points, or develop a behavioral plan to interrupt it (e.g., sleep hygiene, stimulus control, behavioral activation).

The therapist's suggestion to "think of study breaks like game levels" is somewhat helpful but was not grounded in understanding Carlos's specific executive function deficits or connected to evidence-based ADHD management strategies (time-blocking, external structure, task breakdown).

The therapist's repeated self-disclosure ("When I was in school...," "When I couldn't focus...," "When I was super stressed...") further demonstrates misunderstanding. The therapist appears to be treating Carlos's difficulties as universal stress management problems rather than recognizing the specific clinical presentation of a 19-year-old with ADHD, SLD, depression, and acculturation stress requiring tailored intervention.

**Summary:**
