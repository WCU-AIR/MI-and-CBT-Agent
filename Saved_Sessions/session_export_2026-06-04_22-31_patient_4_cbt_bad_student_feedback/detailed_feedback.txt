
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda

No agenda was established at any point in this session. The therapist opened with an unstructured, vague invitation ("Hey there! How's it going today? Just glad you could make it. ## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish Clear Session Agenda and Structure (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action**: At the beginning of each session, collaboratively set a specific agenda with 2-3 prioritized topics and time allocation. Explicitly state the session structure and how time will be used.
- **Example**: "I'd like to make sure we use our time well today. I'm hearing concerns about worry about test results, sleep problems, and feeling withdrawn from activities you enjoy. Which of these feels most important to address today? We have about 45 minutes, so we could spend 20 minutes on [priority 1], 15 on [priority 2], and 10 on [priority 3]. Does that work for you?"
- **Practice**: Before each session, write down 3 potential agenda items. Practice stating the agenda aloud and asking for patient input. Record yourself and review for clarity and collaboration.

### 2. **Apply Cognitive Restructuring to Catastrophic Thoughts (CRITICAL)**
- **Action**: Identify the patient's specific catastrophic thoughts about disease progression and test results. Use Socratic questioning to help her examine evidence, consider alternatives, and develop more balanced thinking.
- **Example**: "I hear that you're worried the tests might show the cancer has spread. That's a very understandable concern given what you're facing. When you have that thought, what evidence do you have that it's true? What evidence might suggest it might not be true? What would be a more balanced way to think about this uncertainty?"
- **Practice**: Study cognitive restructuring protocols. Practice identifying automatic thoughts in patient statements. Practice asking Socratic questions that help patients generate their own alternative thoughts rather than telling them what to think.

### 3. **Develop Behavioral Experiment to Address Reassurance-Seeking (CRITICAL)**
- **Action**: Recognize that repetitive questioning of nurses is anxiety-driven reassurance-seeking that maintains the anxiety cycle. Propose a behavioral experiment to test the hypothesis that reassurance provides only temporary relief.
- **Example**: "I notice you mention asking the nurses the same questions even though you know the answers. That's actually a very common anxiety pattern. When you get reassurance, how long does it help before the worry comes back? What if we did an experiment where you notice what happens to your anxiety when you ask versus when you don't ask? This could help us understand what's maintaining the worry."
- **Practice**: Study behavioral experiments in CBT manuals. Practice identifying reassurance-seeking patterns in patient presentations. Practice proposing experiments collaboratively rather than prescriptively.

### 4. **Implement Behavioral Activation for Withdrawal and Depression (CRITICAL)**
- **Action**: Systematically schedule valued activities to counter the patient's withdrawal from previously enjoyed activities (crossword puzzles, reading, etc.). Use activity scheduling as a structured intervention, not casual suggestion.
- **Example**: "I notice you mentioned that crossword puzzles don't hold your attention anymore, and you're not doing the things you used to enjoy. Let's pick one activity you used to enjoy and schedule a specific time this week to do it for 15 minutes. What would feel manageable?"
- **Practice**: Learn activity scheduling protocols. Practice creating specific, time-bound activity plans with patients. Practice explaining the rationale for behavioral activation (that mood often improves after doing valued activities, not before).

### 5. **Teach Anxiety Management and Psychoeducation (CRITICAL)**
- **Action**: Provide explicit psychoeducation about how anxiety works, the relationship between thoughts/feelings/behaviors, and how CBT addresses these patterns. Teach specific anxiety management techniques (grounding, progressive muscle relaxation, worry time scheduling).
- **Example**: "Let me explain how anxiety works. When we face uncertainty—like waiting for test results—our mind tries to protect us by imagining worst-case scenarios. This triggers physical anxiety symptoms like racing heart and trouble sleeping. The more we try to get reassurance or avoid thinking about it, the more anxious we become. CBT helps by teaching us to think about uncertainty more realistically and to manage the physical anxiety symptoms. Here's a technique that can help..."
- **Practice**: Study anxiety psychoeducation materials. Practice explaining the cognitive model clearly and simply. Practice teaching grounding techniques (5-4-3-2-1 sensory technique, progressive muscle relaxation, box breathing) and practice them yourself first.

### 6. **Implement Sleep-Focused Intervention (IMPORTANT)**

- **Example**: "Your sleep problem seems to be racing thoughts and worry at night. Let's try something called 'worry time.'
