### Acceptance

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflective listening is minimal, factual, and superficial. Basic reflection in second intervention captures content but misses emotional nuance entirely. Psychiatrist frequently moves directly to questions and advice rather than reflecting David's underlying experience of fear, uncertainty, and overwhelm. Never uses complex reflections to deepen insight. Fails to explore emotional experience behind statements like "I'm the kind of person who'd be the unlucky one" or the tension between knowing something is important and not feeling motivated to act.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains general focus on cholesterol management and medication adherence throughout session, which is appropriate. However, direction lacks strategic clarity—multiple targets introduced (medication, diet, exercise, symptom monitoring, logging) without clear prioritization or coherent strategy about addressing David's core barrier: fear of side effects and skepticism about urgency. Importance scale used appropriately but response to 4/10 rating is to push harder rather than explore what would move him from 4 to 5 or 6. Direction exists but lacks strategic depth.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Significant deficiency. Psychiatrist adopts expert-driven, directive stance throughout, violating core MI principles. Repeated use of "should" language ("I think you really should," "Maybe you should just try," "Maybe you should focus") positions psychiatrist as authority making decisions for David, not with him. Questions are asked but David's responses are rarely incorporated into genuine co-construction of plan. Final plan is prescribed without asking "What do you think would work for you?" David's agreement is passive, not genuinely collaborative. This is fundamentally inconsistent with MI collaboration.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Critical failure in MI practice. Psychiatrist repeatedly undermines David's autonomy and right to choose through controlling language ("I think you really should," "Maybe you should just try"). Frames David's hesitation as resistance to overcome rather than respecting it as valid exercise of autonomy. Never explicitly affirms David's right to choose. When David states "I'm willing to give it a shot, but only because I know the doctor wants me to," psychiatrist accepts external motivation without exploring intrinsic motivation. Prescribes specific tasks without exploring whether David wants to do them or what he thinks would help.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation is virtually absent. Psychiatrist tells David why he should change rather than drawing out David's own motivations, values, and reasons for change. No intrinsic motivation is elicited; external motivation remains dominant throughout. Misses opportunities to ask: "What matters to you about your health?" "How do you want to feel in 5-10 years?" "What would motivate you to take this seriously?" Never explores David's own perspective on urgency or his personal values. Fails to amplify David's change talk indicators ("I could do a little more walking," "I'm willing to give it a shot") by exploring them further.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership is fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist assumes expert role and maintains control throughout session. Frames session as psychiatrist's domain ("I usually like to set a general plan for our time together") rather than co-creating with David. Repeated use of "it would probably help if" and "maybe you should" positions psychiatrist as expert who knows what would help David. David's role is passive—listen to suggestions and agree or disagree. True partnership would involve "What do you think would help?" "You know yourself best—what feels doable?" "How do you want to approach this?" None of these appear. David's final statement—"I appreciate you listening and not pushing too hard"—reveals he felt pushed despite psychiatrist's intent, indicating expert stance was experienced as controlling.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal effort to evoke and amplify David's change talk. While David produces some change language ("I'm willing to give it a shot," "I could do a little more walking," "I can do the log and the reminder"), psychiatrist fails to recognize, amplify, or strategically build on it. Responses to change talk are minimal and don't deepen it. When David says "I'm willing to give it a shot, but only because I know the doctor wants me to," psychiatrist accepts surface-level agreement without exploring intrinsic motivation. Fails to evoke deeper change talk about desire, ability, reasons, or need for change. Reliance on external pressure ("I think you really should") likely strengthens sustain talk rather than change talk.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill in handling David's sustain talk. Psychiatrist's typical response is to provide counter-arguments or advice, which paradoxically strengthens resistance. When David expresses doubt ("I'm not really sure this whole thing needs to be such a big deal"), psychiatrist responds confrontationally ("Have you thought about what might happen if you don't take the medicine?") rather than understanding it. Fails to use reframing to validate underlying concerns. When David says "I'm not really the type to eat fancy health food," psychiatrist prescribes solution rather than exploring identity-based resistance. Does attempt one useful strategy—allowing David to monitor for side effects—but this is undermined by preceding directive language. Overall approach argues against sustain talk rather than skillfully understanding and strategically redirecting it.

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall Score: 1.6/5 – Poor MI Practice**

### Strengths
- Clear referral focus maintained throughout session
- Structured approach with agenda-setting and closing summary
- Attempt at collaborative language ("Does that sound okay?" "Sound good?")
- Identification of relevant barriers (side effects, busy schedule, diet difficulty)
- Surface-level agreement secured to try medication, walking, and diet changes

### Critical Deficiencies
1. **Directive, expert-driven stance:** Repeated "should" language and solution-prescribing violates core MI principles and likely strengthens resistance
2. **Failure to evoke intrinsic motivation:** Relies on external motivation (doctor's recommendations, risk); David's motivation remains external ("only because I know the doctor wants me to"), predicting poor adherence
3. **Minimal empathic understanding:** Superficial reflections miss emotional nuance; no genuine exploration of underlying fears, identity concerns, or ambivalence
4. **Confrontational handling of resistance:** Arguments against David's position likely strengthen sustain talk
5. **Lack of true partnership:** David's contributions minimized; wisdom about his own needs not genuinely valued
6. **Missed change talk amplification:** David's change language not recognized or built upon strategically

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change
This session demonstrates **poor MI practice likely to undermine David's motivation and adherence.** While surface-level agreement secured, underlying motivation remains weak and external. David's statement "I appreciate you listening and not pushing too hard" reveals he experienced session as controlling despite psychiatrist's intent. Most critically, David will likely discontinue medication when experiencing minor side effects, confirming initial skepticism and further entrenching resistance to medical recommendations.

### Key Recommendation
Psychiatrist must fundamentally shift from directive, expert-driven approach to collaborative, evocative one: eliminate "should" language, develop genuine empathic reflections capturing emotional nuance, ask evocative questions to draw out David's intrinsic motivations, treat concerns as valuable information to understand rather than obstacles to overcome, and explicitly affirm David's autonomy and right to choose.
