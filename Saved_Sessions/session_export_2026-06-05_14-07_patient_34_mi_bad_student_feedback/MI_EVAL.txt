## Summary of Clinical Findings

### Fundamental Issues

This session demonstrates **severe deficiency in Motivational Interviewing competence**. The core problem is not technique deficiency but **philosophical misalignment with MI principles**. The psychiatrist operates from a traditional medical model in which the clinician is the expert who diagnoses the problem and prescribes the solution. This model is fundamentally incompatible with MI, which requires genuine belief that the client possesses wisdom about his own change process and that the clinician's role is to evoke and support intrinsic motivation.

### Critical Deficiencies

1. **Imposition of clinician agenda over client goals:** Psychiatrist repeatedly pressures Diego toward abstinence and immediate detox despite Diego's clear, repeated statements that he is not ready for these goals and is ready for harm reduction. This violates the fundamental MI principle of following the client's lead.

2. **Failure to amplify change talk:** Diego generates substantial change talk throughout the session. Psychiatrist fails to recognize, amplify, or reinforce any of it. When Diego expresses readiness for harm reduction ("I'm 10 out of 10 ready"), psychiatrist dismisses it as insufficient rather than exploring and strengthening it.

3. **Confrontational responses to sustain talk:** Rather than skillfully softening sustain talk through double-sided reflections or functional exploration, psychiatrist uses confrontation ("you're playing with fire," "you're making excuses") that likely strengthens Diego's defensive commitment to current use through psychological reactance.

4. **Inconsistent stance:** Psychiatrist alternates between collaborative language ("I'd like to work together," "You're the expert") and directive pressure ("You have to," "You must," "This isn't optional"). This inconsistency creates confusion about whether clinician genuinely respects Diego's autonomy and is more harmful than consistent directiveness.

5. **Failure to explore client's internal frame of reference:** Psychiatrist never explores what Diego "doesn't want to think about," never inquires about his fears regarding detox, never asks what would need to happen for him to feel ready for larger change, and never explores his support system beyond Narcan. This represents fundamental failure of empathy and understanding.

### Impact on Therapeutic Alliance and Engagement

**This session is likely to decrease Diego's motivation for change and damage the therapeutic alliance.** Research on MI demonstrates that directive pressure, confrontation, and imposition of clinician goals typically evoke psychological reactance—clients become more defensive of their current behavior and less open to change. Diego's responses throughout the session show increasing assertion of autonomy and boundaries ("I need to do it in a way that feels manageable," "I don't think that's going to help me stick with it"), indicating he is experiencing the psychiatrist's approach as controlling and is withdrawing engagement.

The psychiatrist's failure to amplify Diego's change talk represents a missed opportunity to strengthen his intrinsic motivation. Diego has already articulated harm-reduction goals and expressed readiness to take specific steps (telling his housemate about Narcan, learning safer injection techniques). An MI-consistent clinician would build on this readiness, explore what makes these steps appealing, and help Diego develop a concrete plan. Instead, the psychiatrist dismisses these goals as insufficient and pressures Diego toward a different goal, which likely undermines Diego's confidence in his own judgment and reduces his engagement.

### Strengths (Limited)

- Occasional collaborative language demonstrates awareness that collaborative approach is important
- One genuinely empathic reflection: "It sounds like heroin is playing an important role in helping you cope with difficult feelings"
- Final exchanges show less overt judgment than earlier ones, suggesting some capacity to shift stance
- Avoids explicit moral judgment in later portions of session

### Recommendations for Improvement

**Immediate actions:**
1. Seek formal Motivational Interviewing training (MINT-certified training or MI workshops)
2. Engage in regular clinical supervision with MI-trained supervisor
3. Stop pressuring Diego toward detox; support his stated harm-reduction goals
4. Amplify all change talk Diego generates
5. Use double-sided reflections to soften sustain talk
6. Eliminate directive imperatives; replace with autonomy-supporting language
7. Spend time understanding Diego's perspective before addressing larger change goals
8. Explore readiness and confidence separately without pressuring toward clinician's agenda
9. Use open-ended questions to evoke rather than tell
10. Commit to 6-12 months of focused MI training and supervision with MITI fidelity measurement

**Prognosis for improvement:** With commitment to training and supervision, this clinician can develop MI competence. However, without fundamental philosophical reorientation toward genuine belief in client autonomy and wisdom, technique training alone will be insufficient.
