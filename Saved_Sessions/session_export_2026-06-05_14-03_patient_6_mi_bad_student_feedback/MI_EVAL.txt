# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal acceptance and significant judgment throughout the session. Patient's reasonable requests for information and collaborative decision-making are repeatedly framed as "making excuses" and "hesitating," pathologizing appropriate caution. Language such as "you really shouldn't be hesitating," "small steps won't cut it," and "anything less is just not acceptable" conveys disapproval and conditional regard. No affirmation of patient's strengths (proactive medication safety engagement, willingness to follow up with doctor, existing exercise efforts) or acknowledgment of competing life demands. Tone is consistently authoritarian and disapproving, particularly when patient appropriately pushes back against unrealistic demands.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no empathetic engagement. No reflective listening used to demonstrate understanding of patient's perspective. When patient requests clarification about serotonin syndrome and drug interactions, clinician interprets this as "hesitating" rather than recognizing it as appropriate health literacy-seeking. Emotional content is ignored—when patient expresses feeling "overwhelmed" and "pressured," response is dismissive rather than validating. Real-world barriers (busy schedule, family demands) are not explored; instead, demands for more discipline are repeated. Patient's direct statement that pressure undermines her motivation ("putting it like that makes me feel kind of pressured, and that doesn't help me stick with it") is completely ignored. Final statement about need for autonomy ("I need some say in how it's done, so I don't give up") is met with removal of negotiation entirely.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains some focus on specific targets (medication safety, exercise frequency, dietary habits) with consistency across the session. However, approach is so directive and coercive that collaborative nature necessary for effective MI is undermined. No collaborative goal-setting occurs—targets are imposed without exploring patient's values or realistic feasibility. Rigid, non-negotiable framing ("No more negotiation") explicitly rejects collaborative process. Competing priorities mentioned by patient (busy schedule, work and family demands) are never explored. Specificity is premature—exact frequencies (three times weekly exercise, balanced meals daily) are prescribed without understanding patient's baseline, preferences, or constraints. Direction present but delivered in manner likely to trigger reactance and disengagement rather than intrinsic motivation.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration is fundamentally absent from this session. Psychiatrist positions herself as sole authority on what is "acceptable" and what patient "must" do, establishing hierarchical expert-driven dynamic. When patient proposes collaborative approach ("Can't progress be about steady improvement rather than instant discipline?"), psychiatrist doubles down on demands rather than exploring suggestion. Explicit rejection of partnership occurs with statement "No more negotiation." Patient's repeated contributions (asking about drug interactions, proposing realistic goals, expressing what would help her stay committed) do not substantially influence session direction. Psychiatrist writes plan "to follow exactly as I say," positioning patient as passive recipient of instructions rather than co-creator. Regarding medication decision, while information is provided, no exploration of patient's preferences, concerns, or role in decision occurs. Power imbalance is established and maintained throughout.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is entirely absent. Psychiatrist actively minimizes choice through statements "This isn't optional" and "No more negotiation." Coercive environment created through repeated use of "must," "have to," and "you need to" (e.g., "You must avoid starting citalopram," "You must exercise at least three times a week"). Shaming language used to control behavior: "Anything less is failing yourself" undermines intrinsic motivation and is psychologically harmful. Patient's stated preferences for realistic, sustainable plans are dismissed rather than respected. Direct feedback about what undermines motivation ("putting it like that makes me feel kind of pressured, and that doesn't help me stick with it") is ignored, and same pressuring approach is repeated. Unilateral plan imposition removes patient's agency entirely. Patient's increasingly assertive assertions of autonomy need are pathologized as "making excuses" rather than recognized as predictable response to autonomy violation.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation—the core of MI—is essentially absent. Psychiatrist tells rather than asks, imposes rather than explores. Patient's intrinsic motivation (asking about medication safety, expressing willingness to follow up with doctor) is not evoked or amplified; instead, directives are offered. No exploration of patient's values, priorities, or what would motivate her to make changes. When patient mentions busy schedule and competing demands, barriers are not explored collaboratively; instead, demand for more discipline is repeated. No questions about feasibility or preferences ("What would realistic exercise look like for you?" "What barriers do you anticipate?" "What approach do you think would work best?"). Past successes are not explored. Patient's own ideas are dismissed—when she proposes "steady improvement rather than instant discipline," this is rejected rather than explored. Primarily closed questions used ("Are you going to call them today?" "Are you exercising regularly?") that elicit yes/no responses rather than open exploration. Only genuine evocative moment occurs in final exchange ("What's on your mind about your health?"), immediately followed by directive, non-evocative responses.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership is entirely absent. Psychiatrist positions herself as sole expert on what is "acceptable," what patient "must" do, and what constitutes "real improvement." Language consistently prescriptive: "You must exercise at least three times a week," "This isn't optional," "You need to." Patient positioned as passive recipient of clinician's directives rather than partner in creating solutions. Explicit rejection of shared power: "No more negotiation" is direct statement that clinician will not share decision-making authority. Patient's expertise about her own life is dismissed—when she shares knowledge of her schedule and constraints, this is not incorporated into plan. Patient's contributions throughout session do not substantially influence direction; responses are predetermined and unresponsive to patient's input. Hierarchical language ("you must," "you have to," "you need to") establishes parent-child dynamic rather than adult-adult partnership. No acknowledgment of patient's demonstrated competence in proactive medication safety engagement, willingness to follow up with doctor, or existing self-care efforts.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no skill in cultivating change talk. Patient offers substantial change talk: "I want to understand more about this medication," "I want to make sure I'm doing the right thing for my health," "I will definitely follow up with my doctor today," "I do try to take care of myself," "I want to change, but I need to do it in a way that fits my life," "I'm serious about my health," "I want to get better." Rather than amplifying and reinforcing these statements, psychiatrist dismisses them as insufficient or as "excuses." When patient says "I want to change, but I need to do it in a way that fits my life," response is "No, you really have to stop making excuses"—directly contradicting and suppressing change talk. When patient says "I'm serious about my health," psychiatrist responds by demanding more rather than affirming commitment. No reflective statements used to demonstrate understanding of patient's change language. No strategic amplification of patient's change language to strengthen motivation. No exploration of patient's confidence in making changes. When patient demonstrates commitment ("I'll call them today"), this is not reinforced; instead, more demands are added. Psychiatrist's pressuring approach creates conditions for sustain talk by forcing patient to defend autonomy and realistic self-assessment.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist does not actively reinforce sustain talk, but pressuring approach predictably generates sustain talk from patient, which is then mishandled. Patient's initial sustain talk is minimal—primarily knowledge gaps rather than resistance to change. As psychiatrist becomes increasingly pressuring and dismissive, patient begins articulating concerns about approach itself: "putting it like that makes me feel kind of pressured, and that doesn't help me stick with it" and "Being told 'no negotiation' makes me feel like my situation isn't being considered." Rather than recognizing these as feedback about what undermines motivation, psychiatrist labels them as "making excuses" and "hesitating." No double-sided reflections used to acknowledge both sides of patient's ambivalence. No reframing attempted to support change. Patient's concerns about approach are not explored; instead, same pressuring approach is repeated. Psychiatrist does not actively reinforce sustain talk by agreeing with reasons against change, and does attempt to redirect toward action (calling doctor, starting exercise), though method is coercive rather than collaborative. Missing: double-sided reflections, reframing, exploration of sustain talk, and strategic pivoting of patient's language toward change.

## Overall Session Assessment

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.1/5**

**Summary:** This session demonstrates a fundamental failure to implement Motivational Interviewing. Across all nine MI domains, the psychiatrist demonstrates poor to absent competence. The clinician employs a directive, coercive, authoritarian approach that is directly contrary to MI principles and evidence-based practice for behavior change.

**Does this session embody MI spirit?** No. The psychiatrist demonstrates a controlling, judgmental stance antithetical to MI's core values of partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation.

**Likelihood of evoking client motivation for change:** Low. Patient entered with demonstrated intrinsic motivation (proactive medication safety engagement, willingness to follow up with doctor, existing self-care efforts). Psychiatrist's approach replaced intrinsic motivation with external pressure. Research demonstrates external pressure is less effective for sustained behavior change than intrinsic motivation. Damage to therapeutic relationship may reduce patient's future healthcare engagement.

**Critical deficiencies:** Fundamental failure across all domains. Most critical improvements needed: (1) shift from directive to collaborative stance, (2) development of genuine reflective listening and empathy, (3) elimination of coercive and shame-based language, (4) active evocation and amplification of change talk, (5) support for patient autonomy and collaborative goal-setting.

**Recommendation:** Clinician requires formal MI training and ongoing supervision before continuing to provide counseling in this framework. Current approach risks harm to therapeutic relationship and undermines evidence-based practice for behavior change.
