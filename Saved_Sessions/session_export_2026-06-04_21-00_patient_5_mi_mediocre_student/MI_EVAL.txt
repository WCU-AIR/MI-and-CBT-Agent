### Acceptance

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Maintains clear, consistent direction throughout: helping Aizada advocate more effectively for medical testing and specialist referral. This focus is strategically coherent and progressively clarified. However, direction is problematic from MI perspective because it is clinician-imposed rather than collaboratively developed. Psychiatrist never explores what Aizada actually wants to work on beyond surface-level symptom management. Fails to explore ambivalence about biomedical model, underlying fears, values, goals, or readiness for behavioral change. Direction is narrow (biomedical action steps) and misses broader clinical picture. Potentially iatrogenic—by consistently reinforcing pursuit of more tests, may inadvertently strengthen health anxiety and reinforce patterns maintaining distress.

### Collaboration

**Comments:** Profound absence of genuine collaboration. Psychiatrist adopts expert-driven, directive stance throughout with minimal true partnership or shared decision-making. Unilateral problem definition never collaboratively explored or negotiated. Nearly every response contains prescriptive advice (15+ instances of "You might want to," "Maybe you should," "It would probably help if") without genuine inquiry into Aizada's perspective, readiness, or preferences. Rhetorical questions masquerade as collaboration but function as subtle directives. Client resistance ignored—when Aizada expresses hesitation, psychiatrist does not explore concerns but reframes them as obstacles to overcome. No co-construction of solutions; fully formed action plan presented for agreement rather than joint creation. Power imbalance pervasive—psychiatrist positioned as expert with answers; Aizada positioned as compliant patient needing guidance.

Session characterized by telling rather than asking; clinician-generated solutions rather than client-elicited wisdom. Minimal genuine open-ended questions designed to evoke Aizada's own thinking. No exploration of values, what matters most, or what "getting better" means to her. Missed opportunities to amplify spontaneous change talk—when Aizada notes stress-symptom connections ("During wedding I was too busy to notice pain"), psychiatrist does not explore or amplify but uses as factual data point. No exploration of client strengths, coping strategies, or existing wisdom. Every solution psychiatrist-proposed; Aizada never asked "What do you think might help?" No exploration of ambivalence through curiosity. No elicitation of intrinsic motivation beyond external pressure to get doctors to listen.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Fundamental absence of true partnership. Clinician consistently positions self as expert with answers; Aizada positioned as compliant patient needing guidance. No sense that expertise and wisdom about change reside within client. Expert positioning pervasive: "It would probably help if..." (implying psychiatrist knows what helps), "That's probably the best way..." (implying psychiatrist knows best approach), "I'm confident you'll make progress" (implying confidence in prescribed plan). Unilateral agenda-setting without genuine collaboration. Psychiatrist frames self as problem-solver; Aizada as passive recipient of solutions. Minimal power-sharing—psychiatrist makes decisions about focus, steps, and procedure; Aizada invited to agree or comply, not co-create. Dismissal of client hesitation—not treated as valuable information but as something to overcome. No acknowledgment that Aizada is expert on her own experience, body, fears, or needs. Relationship fundamentally asymmetrical and non-collaborative.

While Aizada spontaneously offers change talk (acknowledging stress-symptom connections), psychiatrist fails to evoke, amplify, or reinforce this language. Focus remains on external action steps rather than deepening intrinsic motivation. No exploration of values or what "getting better" means. No systematic exploration of dimensions of change talk: reasons for change, ability to change, need for change, or commitment to change. Clinician assumes these and prescribes action. Potential reinforcement of sustain talk—by focusing on need for more medical testing, may inadvertently reinforce Aizada's sustain talk (reasons to maintain medical help-seeking and symptom focus) rather than cultivating change talk toward addressing underlying stress or psychological wellbeing.

### Summary of Findings

#### **Strengths:**
- Clear, consistent direction toward medical advocacy and specialist referral
- Practical, concrete suggestions (prepare list, write questions, practice with son, bring diary)
- Professional, respectful tone without overt dismissiveness
- Acknowledgment that Aizada's concerns and fears are real and understandable

#### **Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Fundamental departure from MI principles:** Expert-driven, directive problem-solving rather than collaborative, client-centered exploration
- **Absence of genuine collaboration and partnership:** Psychiatrist tells rather than asks; prescribes rather than evokes; positions self as authority
- **Failure to explore ambivalence:** Aizada's simultaneous conviction of physical illness and acknowledgment of stress connection never explored as foundation for deeper understanding
- **Minimal evocation:** Spontaneous change talk missed; psychiatrist never asks about motivations, values, resources, or ideas
- **Inadequate autonomy support:** Subtle pressure and prescriptive language rather than genuine support for autonomous decision-making
- **Potential iatrogenic effects:** Consistent reinforcement of medical help-seeking may strengthen health anxiety and medical focus—the very patterns maintaining distress in somatized symptoms
- **Lack of empathic depth:** Surface-level reflections fail to capture emotional nuance, validate vulnerability, or demonstrate deep understanding

#### **Clinical Implications:**
While Aizada appears compliant and agrees to action plan, this compliance may reflect deference to authority rather than genuine intrinsic motivation. Psychiatrist has not evoked own reasons for change, explored readiness, or helped develop autonomous commitment. Session does not address core psychological needs: feeling heard, validated, understood; having fears taken seriously; developing agency and control; exploring mind-body connection. By focusing exclusively on medical advocacy, psychiatrist misses opportunity to help Aizada develop integrated, psychologically-informed understanding of health and wellbeing. Risk of continued medical help-seeking, ongoing health anxiety, and limited improvement in overall functioning and quality of life.

#### **Competency Level:**
**Below basic MI competence.** Psychiatrist must fundamentally shift from expert-driven problem-solving to collaborative, client-centered exploration. Requires learning to ask rather than tell, evoke rather than prescribe, explore ambivalence rather than resolve quickly, and support autonomy rather than subtly pressure compliance. Recommend prioritizing MI training, supervision, and deliberate practice before continuing work with health anxiety and somatized symptoms, where non-MI approaches may cause harm.
