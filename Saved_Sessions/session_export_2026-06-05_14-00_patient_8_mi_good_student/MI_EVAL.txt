# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates strong unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance throughout. Consistently validates Amina's boundaries and autonomy with statements such as "This is your space, and you get to decide what feels safe and important to talk about" and "I respect that boundary deeply." Warmth and support are genuine rather than performative. Minor gap: could have more explicitly normalized discomfort in first session with stranger, particularly given involuntary referral context.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Basic reflective listening is present but remains surface-level and lacks complexity. Early reflections accurately capture obvious emotional content ("It sounds like you might be feeling a bit unsure") but do not explore deeper emotional or relational meaning. Missed opportunities to investigate what boundaries protect or what drives resistance. Reflections sometimes veer into assumption rather than pure empathy, projecting clinician hope onto minimal client data rather than genuinely exploring ambiguity.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Session lacks coherent strategic direction. Psychiatrist shifts focus repeatedly without establishing clear target: general concerns → process → health/well-being → values → hypotheticals → change possibilities. Particularly problematic given Amina's precontemplation status and minimal communication. No connection to presenting concern (household conflict, caseworker involvement). Drifts across abstract explorations disconnected from actual life situation, which may feel irrelevant to already-disengaged client.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Surface-level collaboration demonstrated through explicit invitations to share and respect for autonomy. However, lacks genuine co-construction of session agenda. Psychiatrist interprets Amina's minimal responses ("Okay," "Mm-hmm") and continues with new questions rather than genuinely exploring what client means or wants. No invitation to co-create session structure or goals. Psychiatrist remains in subtle expert role while Amina remains passive; true collaboration requires more balanced power-sharing.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently affirms Amina's right to choose and actively fosters perception of control. Explicitly reinforces agency through statements like "you get to decide what feels safe" and "I'm here to support you at your own pace." Avoids directive language or pressure; uses permissive language throughout. Minor limitation: could more explicitly acknowledge involuntary nature of referral and tension this creates while emphasizing choice within constraint.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Critically weak. Psychiatrist asks questions intended to evoke but execution is poor and largely ineffective. Questions often assume content not established (e.g., assumes Amina has been thinking about change). Does not skillfully handle minimal responses; interprets "Mm-hmm" and "Okay" as openings and projects motivation onto them rather than genuinely exploring. Fails to evoke Amina's own reasons for change, values, strengths, or resources. No exploration of what client actually thinks or wants from her perspective.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Weak partnership demonstrated. While psychiatrist avoids overtly authoritarian stance, clinician subtly maintains expert role throughout. Repeatedly interprets Amina's minimal responses without checking in or collaborating. Clinician-driven agenda with psychiatrist setting topics while Amina remains reactive. Lacks genuine curiosity about Amina's expertise on her own life. Never asks "What do you think?" or "What's your perspective?" True partnership would position Amina as expert on her own experience and invite genuine power-sharing.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Essentially absent. No meaningful effort to evoke change talk regarding desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment. Questions are premature and assume change contemplation without evidence. When Amina provides minimal responses, psychiatrist does not amplify or reinforce as potential change talk. Questions are too abstract and disconnected from actual situation. No strategic use of open questions grounded in her life to elicit change language. This represents critical MI deficiency.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist skillfully avoids confronting sustain talk ("It's not really any of your business," repeated three times) and validates boundaries without defensiveness or pressure. This prevents amplification of resistance. However, lacks strategic pivoting toward change or exploration of ambivalence. Approach is respectful but passive—validates and moves on rather than using double-sided reflections or exploring function of resistance. Does not strategically work with sustain talk to deepen engagement.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 2.8/5**

### Key Strengths
- Consistent respect for autonomy and genuine unconditional positive regard
- Nonjudgmental stance; avoids pathologizing resistance
- Avoidance of confrontation prevents escalation
- Warm, supportive tone that is authentic rather than clinical
- Explicit validation of boundaries and privacy

### Critical Areas for Improvement
- **Direction:** Session drifts across multiple unfocused topics without connecting to presenting concern
- **Evocation:** Minimal responses interpreted rather than explored; clinician-driven rather than client-centered
- **Partnership:** Subtle expert positioning maintained; Amina remains passive
- **Change Talk:** No meaningful cultivation of client's own language about change
- **Empathy:** Surface-level reflections lack emotional and relational depth

### Clinical Impact
This session is unlikely to significantly increase Amina's intrinsic motivation for change or deepen therapeutic engagement. While the psychiatrist's respectful approach prevents further resistance escalation, it fails to strategically engage Amina or help her move toward contemplation. She remains passive, minimally communicative, and unengaged throughout.

### Recommendations for Improvement
1. Establish clear strategic direction connected to presenting concern (household conflict, caseworker involvement)
2. Develop genuine curiosity about Amina's actual thoughts and wants; explore minimal responses rather than interpreting them
3. Evoke change talk through strategic, grounded open questions rooted in her life situation
4. Position Amina as the expert on her own life and experience
5. Develop more complex, emotionally attuned reflections that capture meaning beneath surface statements
6. Build rapport and trust before exploring change; for precontemplation clients, engagement precedes change focus
7. Acknowledge involuntary nature of referral to normalize caution and potentially reduce resistance
8. Use double-sided reflections to explore ambivalence underlying resistance

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation for Change:** Low. More strategically directed, evocative approach necessary.
