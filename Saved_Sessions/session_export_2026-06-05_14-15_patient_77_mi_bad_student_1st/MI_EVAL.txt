# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Profound absence of unconditional positive regard. Psychiatrist repeatedly communicates judgment and disapproval ("you really should have quit by now," "stop making excuses," "you can't just say you're not strong enough"). Most critically, invalidates client's core experience of pain and exhaustion, responding with "you can't keep using tiredness or pain as an excuse" and "you have to stop feeling broken." Uses shaming language and ultimatums ("This is your last chance"). No warmth, affirmation of worth, or genuine support. Stance is punitive and controlling throughout. Conditional regard based entirely on behavioral compliance.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Zero reflective listening or perspective-taking. No reflective statements capture client's emotional experience. Repeatedly dismisses stated experience—when client says "I'm just tired," psychiatrist responds with negation rather than understanding. Client describes somatic pain and self-harm function; psychiatrist does not reflect or explore, instead pivots to demands about patches. Never reflects client's exhaustion, feeling "stuck," fear that "nothing else seems to help," or sense of being "broken." Complete absence of curiosity about client's internal world, barriers to change, or why self-harm feels necessary. No single empathic reflection occurs throughout entire session.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Maintains clear focus on target behaviors (smoking cessation and stopping self-harm), which prevents score of 1. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. Psychiatrist states direction as non-negotiable ("This is not a negotiation," "This is not a choice," "End of story") and uses ultimatums rather than exploring client's own goals or ambivalence. Conflates two separate issues without exploring whether client sees them as connected or what her priorities might be. Assumes patches are solution without exploring previous attempts, barriers, or preferences. No progressive clarification of direction; repetitive demand without adaptation to client response.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Fundamentally anti-collaborative. Psychiatrist asserts unilateral authority throughout: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do," "You have to," "You must," "This is not a negotiation," "This is not a choice," "End of story." These statements explicitly reject collaboration and assert psychiatrist's power over client's decision-making. Never asks client what she thinks might help, what she has learned from past attempts, what barriers she faces, or what her own ideas are about change. When client states patches didn't work for her, psychiatrist dismisses this as excuse rather than exploring. No joint problem-solving, no seeking of client input, no co-construction of solutions. Stance is "I know what's best; you must comply."

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Actively undermines autonomy through coercion, pressure, and explicit denial of choice. Uses language that directly removes choice: "This is not a negotiation," "This is not a choice," "You have to," "You must," "No exceptions," "End of story." Employs pressure and threat: "This is your last chance," "If you don't, you're just choosing to suffer more," "If you keep cutting and smoking, you're only making your life worse." When client expresses current readiness level ("I'm not ready to start patches today"), psychiatrist responds with "you can't keep saying you're not ready" and "This isn't about feeling ready; it's about doing what's necessary." Directly invalidates client's assessment of her own readiness and denies autonomy to make decisions about own care.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Demonstrates no evocation whatsoever. Tells rather than asks; prescribes rather than explores. Never asks open-ended questions designed to elicit client's own reasons for change, values, strengths, or ideas about what might help. No questions such as "What concerns you most?" "What would be different if you weren't cutting?" "What have you tried that helped?" "What matters most to you?" Instead uses closed questions that are really directives ("Have you tried quitting before?" followed by telling what to do; "Are you willing to commit?" as ultimatum, not exploration). Fails to evoke or amplify change talk when client expresses "I don't want to keep hurting myself"—this is ignored rather than explored and amplified.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Actively assumes expert role and explicitly rejects partnership. Opening statement—"I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to quit smoking for good"—establishes hierarchical, expert-driven dynamic. Reinforces throughout: "You have to," "You must," "This is not a negotiation," "End of story." Never acknowledges client as expert on her own experience. When client describes what has and hasn't worked ("Patches or gum never felt like they actually did anything for me"), psychiatrist dismisses as excuse rather than treating as valuable information. No inclusive language ("we," "together," "let's explore"). Language is directive and unilateral ("you have to," "you must," "I'm telling you"). Psychiatrist's contributions do not reflect client input; agenda dominates entirely.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Demonstrates no skill in evoking, amplifying, or reinforcing change talk. Client expresses change talk ("I don't want to keep hurting myself," "I don't want to keep doing that," recognition that cutting is harmful), but psychiatrist ignores these statements and continues with demands and ultimatums. Never asks questions designed to evoke change talk: "What concerns you most about continuing to cut?" "What would be benefits of finding another way?" "What strengths do you have?" "What have you done that helped before?" Fails to build confidence or self-efficacy. When client expresses doubt ("I'm not sure I'm strong enough"), psychiatrist responds with shame ("you have to be strong," "you can't just say you're not strong enough") rather than exploring and building confidence. Approach actively suppresses motivation through shame and demand.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Attempts to address sustain talk through repeated challenging and insistence on change ("you have to stop," "you must quit," "no more delays"), which prevents score of 1. However, approach is entirely confrontational and coercive, explicitly contraindicated in MI. Does not use double-sided reflections, reframing, or strategic responses to minimize sustain talk while maintaining collaboration. Instead uses direct confrontation ("you can't keep saying that's an excuse") and ultimatum ("you have to stop now"). Psychiatrist's approach may temporarily suppress sustain talk through intimidation, but does not actually soften it or help client develop alternative perspectives. Client continues expressing same sustain talk throughout session despite repeated confrontation, indicating ineffectiveness of approach.

### Summary of Critical Findings

**Strengths:**
- Maintains consistent focus on target behaviors (smoking cessation and self-harm cessation)
- Communicates appropriate concern about severity of issues

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Complete absence of acceptance, empathy, and unconditional positive regard
- Coercive, controlling stance with explicit rejection of collaboration and client autonomy
- No evocation of client's own motivation, values, or resources
- Failure to amplify change talk when expressed
- Confrontational approach to sustain talk that increases defensiveness
- Fundamental misunderstanding of precontemplation stage and readiness assessment
- Active invalidation of client's core experience (exhaustion, pain, self-harm function)

**Impact on Therapeutic Relationship and Client Engagement:**
This session is **actively harmful** to client motivation and likelihood of change. Client enters already expressing hopelessness, exhaustion, and reliance on self-harm. Psychiatrist's coercive, invalidating approach:
- Increases defensiveness and entrenchment in sustain talk
- Damages therapeutic alliance; client experiences psychiatrist as punitive and dismissive
- Reinforces hopelessness by invalidating concerns and demanding impossible change
- Increases risk of disengagement from care and potential escalation of self-harm

**Session would likely result in client disengagement from treatment, not engagement in change.**

---

### Immediate Recommendations

1. **Foundational Shift Required:** Adopt genuine collaborative, client-centered stance before continuing with this client
2. **Formal MI Training:** Engage in certified MI training and regular clinical supervision
3. **Eliminate Coercive Language:** Replace all "you have to/must" statements with open-ended questions
4. **Develop Acceptance and Empathy:** Validate client experience before introducing change agenda
5. **Explore Rather Than Prescribe:** Ask about previous attempts, barriers, and client priorities
6. **Recognize Stage of Change:** Client is in precontemplation; work to move toward contemplation, not demand action
7. **Amplify Change Talk:** Listen for and explore client's own motivation when expressed
8. **Address Countertransference:** Examine frustration with client's ambivalence; recognize it signals need for approach change

**Clinical Recommendation:** Psychiatrist should not continue seeing this client without significant MI training and supervision. If unable to shift to MI-consistent approach, client should be referred to clinician trained in MI or other evidence-based approaches respecting client autonomy.
