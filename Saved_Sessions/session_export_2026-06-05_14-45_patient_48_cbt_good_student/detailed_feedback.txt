
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

This document outlines a structured framework for assessing therapist competence in Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT). 
It is used for training, supervision, and evaluation. Each item is scored on a 0–6 scale:

0 = Poor
2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

## Recommendations

### 1. Introduce Formal Thought Records and Worksheets
- **Action:** Provide the patient with a written thought record form that includes columns for: Situation, Automatic Thought, Evidence For, Evidence Against, Balanced Thought, and Anxiety Rating (Before/After). Explain that this is a tool he can use independently whenever he notices health-related worry.
- **Example:** "Here's a form you can use at home. When you notice the thought 'Is this the Lyme coming back?' write down what happened, the thought, evidence on both sides, and a more balanced thought. Rate your anxiety before and after. This helps your brain learn the new, more balanced way of thinking."
- **Practice:** Have the patient complete one thought record during the session with your guidance, then assign him to complete 2-3 additional records during the week using the same situation (fatigue/confusion) to build skill and confidence.

### 2. Explicitly Address Information-Seeking Behavior with Specific Intervention
- **Action:** In the next session, develop a specific plan to reduce or modify health-related information-seeking. Options include: (1) delaying symptom research for 15 minutes when the urge arises to allow anxiety to naturally decrease, (2) limiting symptom research to once per day at a scheduled time, (3) replacing symptom research with alternative activities (exercise, hobbies, time with wife), or (4) reading only pre-approved, balanced health information rather than searching the internet.
- **Example:** "I noticed you mentioned that looking up symptoms usually makes your anxiety worse. What if we set a goal to delay symptom research for 15 minutes when you feel the urge? Often anxiety naturally decreases if we don't act on it immediately. We could track how your anxiety changes when you delay versus when you research right away. What do you think?"
- **Practice:** Have the patient choose one information-seeking reduction strategy and commit to it for one week. Track the urge to research, the delay/alternative activity, and the resulting anxiety level.

### 3. Develop Panic-Specific Interventions
- **Action:** In the next session, explicitly address the panic symptoms (chest tightness, difficulty breathing, shakiness). Introduce interoceptive exposure (deliberately inducing mild panic sensations to learn they are not dangerous) and/or breathing retraining. Explain that panic symptoms are part of the anxiety disorder and can be addressed separately from health anxiety.
- **Example:** "I noticed you mentioned feeling a tight chest and difficulty breathing when you get anxious. These are panic symptoms, and they're actually quite treatable. We can work on helping your body learn that these sensations aren't dangerous. One way to do this is through breathing exercises or by deliberately creating mild versions of these sensations in a safe way so your body learns they pass naturally."
- **Practice:** Teach diaphragmatic breathing or 4-7-8 breathing technique. Have the patient practice daily and use it when panic symptoms arise. Consider interoceptive exposure exercises (e.g., hyperventilation, spinning, holding breath) to habituate to panic sensations.

### 4. Explicitly Check Internalization of Balanced Thoughts
- **Action:** Before moving from cognitive work to behavioral planning, explicitly check whether the patient genuinely believes the balanced thought or merely intellectually agrees with it. Ask: "On a scale of 0-100, how much do you believe this balanced thought right now? What percentage of you still worries about Lyme?" If belief is low (<50%), spend more time on evidence examination or use behavioral experiments to build conviction.
- **Example:** "You came up with a really balanced thought: 'stress and tiredness are causing how I feel.' On a scale of 0-100, how much do you actually believe that right now? If it's not very high, that's okay—sometimes our emotions lag behind our thinking. We can use the activities you're going to try to help your whole self, not just your thinking mind, learn that this is true."
- **Practice:** Regularly assess belief in balanced thoughts using percentage scales. Use behavioral experiments specifically to build emotional conviction, not just intellectual agreement.

### 5. Develop Specific Intervention for Reassurance-Seeking
- **Action:** Identify the patient's reassurance-seeking behaviors (calling wife, seeking medical investigations, requesting lab tests) and develop a plan to gradually reduce them. Explain that reassurance temporarily reduces anxiety but perpetuates the disorder by preventing the patient from learning that his symptoms are not dangerous.
- **Example:** "I notice you called your wife when you felt anxious, and that helped temporarily. But reassurance actually keeps health anxiety going because it prevents you from learning that you can tolerate the anxiety without reassurance. What if we gradually reduced how often you call your wife when anxious? Maybe you could wait 10 minutes first, or try a coping strategy on your own. This would help your confidence grow."
- **Practice:** Have the patient track reassurance-seeking behaviors (frequency, who he seeks reassurance from, what he asks). Develop a graded plan to reduce reassurance-seeking by 10-20% per week.
