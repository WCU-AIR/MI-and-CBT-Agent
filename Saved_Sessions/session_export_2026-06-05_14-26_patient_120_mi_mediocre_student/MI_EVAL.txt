# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal acceptance demonstrated. While opening conveys basic warmth, the psychiatrist quickly shifts to judgment and directive authority. Fails to normalize Selene's struggle with adherence or validate her experience of feeling fine. Prescriptive language ("you should," "you might want to") communicates conditional regard based on compliance rather than unconditional positive regard. Selene's closing comment—"I appreciate you listening and not pushing too hard"—reveals the session was perceived as more controlling than collaborative, suggesting the psychiatrist's actual approach was even more directive than Selene's polite framing acknowledges.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Empathy is superficial, relying on factual summarization rather than genuine empathic reflection. When Selene expresses overwhelm ("it's already overwhelming"), the psychiatrist responds with logistics rather than emotional validation. Critical emotional cues are missed—when Selene worries about adding "another thing I have to do every day," the psychiatrist moves directly to problem-solving instead of exploring the anxiety. Complex reflections capturing emotional nuance and ambivalence are entirely absent. No reflection of Selene's core ambivalence: that part of her knows staying healthy matters, while right now it doesn't feel urgent or manageable alongside everything else she's carrying.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clear focus on target behavior (medication adherence, diet, exercise, support systems) is maintained throughout the session. However, direction is imposed through repeated prescriptions rather than collaboratively explored. Psychiatrist decides what the target should be (alarms, meal planning, walks, spouse involvement) and guides Selene toward predetermined solutions. Misses opportunities to explore Selene's own reasons for change, which would create intrinsic direction. Fails to strategically evoke Selene's motivation by asking what staying healthy means to her or what would be different if she managed her diabetes well. Previous attempts (writing things down) are not explored to understand barriers or what Selene learned.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration is fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist operates from expert-driven, hierarchical stance throughout. Prescriptive language dominates: "you should," "you might want to" (repeated 4 times), "it would probably help if" (repeated 5 times), "don't you think" (rhetorical questions). Unilateral decision-making evident—by session end, psychiatrist has determined Selene should: set alarms, schedule walks, talk to spouse, use phone reminders with goal notes. Selene presented with completed plan rather than invited to co-construct one. When Selene expresses worry about overwhelm, psychiatrist dismisses with "Sometimes people just need to push through the hard parts"—overriding her concern rather than collaboratively exploring it. No genuine shared problem-solving occurs.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is virtually nonexistent. No language emphasizing Selene's freedom to choose appears in the session. Phrases like "It's entirely up to you," "What feels right to you?", or "You get to decide" are absent. Statement "Sometimes people just need to push through the hard parts" communicates that Selene's hesitation is an obstacle to overcome rather than valid information about her readiness and capacity. Predetermined solutions remove her agency in designing her own change strategy. When Selene rates confidence at 5/10, psychiatrist reduces scope of plan rather than inviting Selene's input into what that one thing should be. No explicit permission given that Selene can choose not to follow recommendations or modify them. Closing statement—"Let's plan for you to try these steps this week"—frames plan as already decided rather than collaborative proposal.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no evocation demonstrated. Selene offers genuine change talk multiple times ("I do worry about staying healthy," "I want to stay healthy," "I'm willing to give it a try this week") but these are not amplified or deepened. When Selene says "I want to stay healthy," psychiatrist moves directly to prescribing solutions instead of asking what that means to her or what would be different if she managed her diabetes well. Session dominated by 8+ specific recommendations rather than asking Selene to generate ideas. No exploration of values, previous successes, or what Selene has learned from attempts to change. Advice-giving replaces evocation as dominant strategy. Change talk present but systematically missed as opportunities to strengthen intrinsic motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership is fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role and explicitly positions themselves as authority with correct answers. Repeated use of "you should," "you might want to," "it would probably help if" communicates "I know what's best for you." Session structure reinforces hierarchical power dynamic—psychiatrist asks questions, receives answers, prescribes solutions; Selene listens and complies. No mutual influence; Selene's ideas do not shape psychiatrist's recommendations. When Selene expresses concern about overwhelm and difficulty maintaining habits, psychiatrist does not treat this as valuable information about her capacity and needs. Instead, dismisses it: "Sometimes people just need to push through the hard parts." No acknowledgment that Selene has valuable knowledge about herself, her barriers, or her strengths. Treatment plan imposed rather than co-created.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no skill demonstrated in evoking or amplifying change talk. Selene's change talk ("I want to stay healthy," "I'm willing to try") is not explored, deepened, or reinforced. When Selene says "I want to stay healthy," psychiatrist does not ask follow-up questions to strengthen this motivation. Questions asked are diagnostic ("How often would you say that happens?") rather than evocative. No strategic questioning designed to draw out reasons for change. Sustain talk ("I feel fine," "it's not a big deal," "it's hard to stick to," "it's tough to fit in," "I worry I'll fall back") is largely ignored or met with advice, allowing it to persist unchallenged. When Selene expresses willingness to try, psychiatrist does not reinforce or deepen commitment. Advice-giving dominates rather than drawing out client's intrinsic reasons for change.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill demonstrated in softening sustain talk. Selene offers multiple instances of sustain talk (feeling fine, no symptoms, hard to stick to plans, already overwhelming, worried about consistency, feels like a lot) that are largely unaddressed or met with dismissive advice. Missed opportunity for double-sided reflection when Selene expresses both desire for health and concern about overwhelm. Psychiatrist's statement—"Sometimes people just need to push through the hard parts"—is confrontational and dismissive, likely to strengthen rather than soften sustain talk. No reframing of barriers in ways that support change. While psychiatrist does redirect toward solutions (alarms, meal planning), this is done through directive advice rather than skillful MI techniques like double-sided reflections or strategic redirection to client's own reasons for change.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.7/5**

**Assessment:** This session demonstrates **fundamental misalignment with Motivational Interviewing principles** across nearly every domain. The psychiatrist operates from a traditional expert-driven, directive medical model rather than an MI-based collaborative model. While the clinician demonstrates basic counseling skills (warmth, focus, practical problem-solving), core MI competencies are largely absent.

**Key Strengths:**
- Clear, consistent focus on presenting problem
- Basic human warmth and respect for client's time
- Acknowledgment of some client perspectives
- Practical, reasonable suggestions offered

**Critical Gaps:**
- Authoritarian rather than collaborative stance
- Advice-giving dominates; evocation absent
- Sustain talk unaddressed; change talk not amplified
- Autonomy constrained through prescriptive language
- Empathy superficial; emotional content missed
- Partnership not established; power not shared

**Impact on Client Engagement:** Selene's surface-level compliance masks moderate confidence (5/10) and unresolved ambivalence. Without exploration of intrinsic motivation or genuine resolution of her concerns about overwhelm, likelihood of sustained behavior change is **low to moderate**. Selene may attempt recommended strategies briefly but is likely to revert to previous patterns when initial effort becomes difficult.

**Recommendation:** Seek formal MI training, supervision, or consultation to develop core MI competencies systematically. Focus particularly on: (1) shifting from expert-driven to collaborative stance, (2) developing genuine empathic reflections, (3) evoking and amplifying change talk, (4) supporting autonomy explicitly, and (5) addressing sustain talk skillfully using double-sided reflections.
