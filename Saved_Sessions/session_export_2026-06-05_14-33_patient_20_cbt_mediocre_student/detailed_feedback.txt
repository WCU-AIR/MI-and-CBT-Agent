
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **URGENT: Conduct comprehensive suicide risk assessment and develop safety plan**
- **Action:** In the next session, explicitly assess suicidal ideation, intent, plan, and access to means. Use a structured assessment tool (e.g., Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale). Develop a written safety plan with crisis resources, emergency contacts, and coping strategies.
- **Example:** "I want to check in about something important. You mentioned earlier that you've thought about death. Can you tell me more about that? Are you having thoughts of harming yourself right now? Do you have a plan? Have you thought about how you might do it? Do you have access to [means]? Let's develop a safety plan together so you know what to do if these thoughts get stronger."
- **Practice:** Review suicide assessment protocols. Practice asking directly about suicidal thoughts without fear or discomfort. Develop a template safety plan to use with patients.

### 2. **Reconceptualize the case as Bipolar II depression, not anxiety disorder**
- **Action:** Develop a clear case formulation that recognizes the primary disorder (Bipolar II depression) and secondary features (anxiety, perfectionism). Adjust treatment strategy accordingly. Prioritize depressive symptoms and mood monitoring over anxiety-focused interventions.
- **Example:** "Based on what you've shared, it sounds like you're experiencing a depressive episode—low mood, low energy, sleeping more, eating more, feeling guilty, and having thoughts about death. The worry about work is part of that, but the main issue is the depression. Let's focus on getting your mood and energy back up, and the work worries will likely improve too."
- **Practice:** Review CBT protocols for depression vs. anxiety. Study the differences in treatment approach. Consult with a supervisor about case conceptualization.

### 3. **Establish a multi-session treatment plan with explicit phases and priorities**
- **Action:** Develop a written treatment plan that includes: (1) Safety/stabilization phase (suicide risk management, mood monitoring), (2) Behavioral activation phase (scheduling valued activities, increasing engagement), (3) Cognitive work phase (addressing depressive thoughts), (4) Relapse prevention phase. Share this plan with the patient.
- **Example:** "Here's my plan for our work together. First, we need to make sure you're safe and that we're monitoring your mood carefully. Second, we're going to help you get more active and engaged in things that matter to you—that often helps depression. Third, we'll work on the thoughts that keep you stuck. And finally, we'll develop a plan to prevent future episodes. Does that make sense? What questions do you have?"
- **Practice:** Develop treatment plans for different disorders. Practice explaining plans to patients in clear, collaborative language.

### 4. **Implement behavioral activation as the primary intervention for depression**
- **Action:** Replace distraction-based suggestions with structured behavioral activation. Help the patient identify valued activities (work, family, hobbies, self-care) and schedule them systematically. Start with small, achievable activities and gradually increase. Track mood and activity to identify patterns.
- **Example:** "Let's think about things that matter to you or that you used to enjoy. What are some activities you value? [Patient lists: time with family, reading, gardening, exercise]. Let's schedule 3-4 of these for specific times this week. For example, Tuesday evening 7-8pm, you'll spend time with your family. Thursday morning, you'll do 15 minutes of gardening. We'll track how your mood is before and after each activity to see if it helps."
- **Practice:** Learn behavioral activation protocols. Practice identifying valued activities with patients. Develop activity scheduling worksheets.

### 5. **Implement mood monitoring and sleep/wake cycle tracking**
- **Action:** Have the patient track mood 2-3 times daily (0-10 scale) and note activities, sleep, and sleep quality. For Bipolar II, sleep is a critical early warning sign. Discuss sleep hygiene and consistent sleep/wake times.
- **Example:** "I'd like you to track your mood three times a day—morning, afternoon, evening—on a 0-10 scale. Also note how much you slept and how you feel about your sleep. And let's aim for a consistent bedtime and wake time, even on weekends. Sleep is really important for your mood. Here's a tracking sheet to use."
- **Practice:** Develop mood and sleep tracking forms. Practice explaining the importance of sleep for mood regulation in bipolar disorder.

### 6. **Use guided discovery and Socratic questioning instead of direct suggestion**
- **Action:** When addressing thoughts, use questions to help the patient discover insights rather than telling them what to think. Ask about evidence, alternatives, and consequences. Invite the patient to be an active scientist testing their thoughts.
- **Example:** Instead of "Maybe it would help to think about whether those thoughts are totally true," ask: "Let's look at that thought together—'I'm not good enough.' What evidence do you have that this is true? What evidence do you have against it? Has there been a time when you did something well at work? What happened? How did people react? What would you tell a friend who had this thought?"
- **Practice:** Study Socratic questioning techniques. Practice asking open-ended questions. Record yourself and listen for how often you tell vs. ask. Practice with a supervisor.

### 7. **Develop structured thought records for depressive cognitions**
- **Action:** Use thought records to systematically examine depressive thoughts (hopelessness, worthlessness, self-blame) rather than just work-related perfectionism. Help the patient identify evidence, alternative thoughts, and behavioral experiments to test thoughts.
- **Example:** "Let's use a thought record. You had the thought 'I'm letting everyone down.' Let's write that down. What evidence do you have for that thought? What evidence do you have against it? What's another way to look at this situation? What could you do to test whether this thought is true?"
- **Practice:** Learn thought record protocols. Practice completing thought records with patients. Develop thought record worksheets.

### 8. **Establish clear, specific, and collaborative homework with written instructions**
- **Action:** Assign homework with explicit rationale, specific instructions, and written tools. Ensure the patient understands and is committed. Review homework in every session and adjust based on what works.

Getting active helps break that cycle. Can you commit to this? What might get in the way? How can we problem-solve that?"
- **Practice:** Develop homework assignment templates. Practice explaining rationale clearly. Practice reviewing homework systematically.

### 9. **Elicit and respond to feedback systematically throughout sessions**
- **Action:** Ask for feedback regularly: "Does this make sense?" "How are you feeling about what we've discussed?" "Is this helpful?" "What's been most useful so far?" "Is there anything I've missed?" Adjust based on feedback.
