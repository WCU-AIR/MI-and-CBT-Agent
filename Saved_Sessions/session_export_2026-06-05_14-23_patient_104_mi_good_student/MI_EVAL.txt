### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates solid empathic understanding with several well-executed complex reflections. A particularly strong example captures multiple layers: "It sounds like this wait not only disrupts your day but also stirs up a worry about how it might affect your responsibilities and reputation at work, which feels like a heavy weight to carry. At the same time, there's a sense that these delays might be just part of the system, something out of your control, which can feel pretty discouraging." The clinician accurately reflects Nabil's ambivalence between motivation and hopelessness. However, opportunities exist to sit longer with emotional content before pivoting to solutions. When Nabil expresses discomfort about "admitting defeat or weakness" at work, the psychiatrist reflects this but doesn't explore the deeper shame or vulnerability underlying that concern.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains general focus on appointment management and Nabil's frustration but fails to clearly establish or collaboratively define the specific change target. Early questions are appropriately open ("What would feel most helpful for you to focus on today?"), but the direction becomes increasingly prescriptive as the session progresses. The psychiatrist never clarifies whether the actual goal is reducing Nabil's anger response, improving coping strategies, changing the system itself, or managing emotional regulation. When Nabil signals misalignment ("It just feels like a band-aid on a bigger problem"), the psychiatrist doesn't pause to recalibrate. The distinction between managing emotions versus addressing systemic concerns remains unresolved, weakening directional clarity.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates partial collaboration in early and middle portions of the session through open-ended questions that position Nabil as a contributor. However, collaboration deteriorates significantly when transitioning to solution-offering. The statement "Would it be okay if I shared some information about ways others have found helpful..." appears collaborative but functions as a gateway to expert-driven advice-giving. The psychiatrist presents three pre-formulated strategies without first exploring what Nabil has already tried, what barriers exist, or what solutions Nabil himself might generate. True collaboration would involve co-constructing solutions rather than asking Nabil to evaluate psychiatrist-generated options. By the final exchange, the psychiatrist is directing Nabil toward specific action steps rather than exploring what Nabil genuinely wants to try.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates inconsistent autonomy support. Early questions respect Nabil's choice and position him as the decision-maker about session content. However, autonomy support weakens considerably when shifting into solution-offering mode. The framing of "Would it be okay if I shared some information..." is presented as a question but functions as a directive. Nabil's agreement appears somewhat obligatory given social pressure in the clinical context. More problematically, the psychiatrist presents three specific strategies without first asking what ideas Nabil has considered. When Nabil expresses discomfort about notifying work of delays, the psychiatrist acknowledges this but then gently pressures him toward a specific behavior rather than exploring his full range of choices. The final assumption that Nabil has committed to "these steps" represents a subtle erosion of autonomy.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates some evocation but inconsistently and with limited depth. Early evocative questions successfully draw out Nabil's core motivations: "It's about respect and fairness more than anything else. I just want to be treated like the professional I am." However, the psychiatrist fails to strategically amplify or deepen this meaningful change talk. After Nabil articulates these core motivations, the psychiatrist moves quickly to a scaling question rather than exploring further. Most significantly, the psychiatrist abandons evocation entirely when transitioning to solution-offering, providing pre-formulated suggestions instead of asking "What ideas do you have?" The psychiatrist does evoke existing coping strategies but doesn't deeply explore how they might be expanded or adapted—another missed evocative opportunity.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist begins with a partnership stance but progressively assumes an expert role, particularly in the latter half of the session. This represents a significant departure from MI spirit. Early partnership indicators include open-ended questions and affirmation of Nabil's resilience. However, the critical shift occurs when the psychiatrist frames himself as the expert with "information" and "ways" that have worked for "others," implicitly positioning Nabil as someone needing expert guidance. The psychiatrist presents three pre-formulated strategies without first exploring Nabil's own ideas or barriers. When Nabil expresses skepticism ("It just feels like a band-aid on a bigger problem"), the psychiatrist reframes rather than genuinely reconsidering the approach with Nabil. The final exchange assumes the psychiatrist and Nabil have jointly identified "these steps," but in reality, the psychiatrist identified them and Nabil reluctantly agreed. Power dynamics become increasingly unequal as the session progresses.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates some ability to evoke change talk but fails to strategically cultivate and amplify it throughout the session. Powerful change talk emerges early: "The biggest reason is just not wanting to feel like I'm being disrespected or treated like some inconvenience... I just want to be treated like the professional I am." However, the psychiatrist's response focuses on reflection and validation rather than deepening this emotionally resonant change talk. Nabil generates ability talk when discussing coping strategies, but the psychiatrist affirms without amplifying by asking how these strategies have helped or what else he's been able to do. When Nabil expresses commitment talk ("Maybe the easiest thing for me to try would be asking the clinic..."), the psychiatrist doesn't strengthen this commitment by exploring confidence and barriers. The session lacks consistent, skillful cultivation of change talk throughout.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal skill in addressing sustain talk and, in some instances, inadvertently reinforces it. Sustain talk appears throughout: "I'm not sure there's much that can be done," "It just feels like a band-aid on a bigger problem," and "Whether it sticks, I guess we'll see." When Nabil expresses hopelessness, the psychiatrist reflects this without strategically pivoting toward possibility. When Nabil says "I'm not getting my hopes up," the psychiatrist acknowledges both sides of ambivalence but doesn't strategically emphasize the change side. Most problematically, when Nabil expresses core sustain talk ("It just feels like a band-aid on a bigger problem"), the psychiatrist reframes cognitively rather than addressing it directly, essentially validating Nabil's resignation by agreeing the system is out of his hands. When Nabil expresses skepticism ("I'm warning you, I'm not easy to convince"), the psychiatrist doesn't explore this resistance. The final sustain talk is met with logistical problem-solving rather than exploration of confidence or commitment.

## Overall Session Score: **3.2/5**

### Summary Assessment:

This session demonstrates foundational MI competence with solid acceptance, empathy, and initial evocation, but significant deficiencies in maintaining MI spirit throughout. The psychiatrist successfully engages Nabil's core values and generates meaningful change talk early on, but then undermines this momentum by shifting to expert-driven advice-giving and failing to strategically cultivate or amplify the change talk that emerged.

**Key Strengths:**
- Strong initial acceptance and validation of Nabil's frustration
- Solid reflective listening with complex reflections capturing ambivalence
- Effective affirmation of existing resilience and coping strategies
- Successful evocation of core values (respect, professional identity, fairness)
- Appropriate use of scaling questions

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- Premature shift from partnership to expert role undermines MI spirit
- Failure to strategically amplify and deepen meaningful change talk
- Inadequate softening of sustain talk; hopelessness is validated rather than redirected
- Unclear change target conflates emotional management with systemic concerns
- Reduced autonomy support through subtle pressure toward psychiatrist-generated solutions
- Diminished partnership as power dynamics become increasingly unequal

**Impact on Client Engagement:**

Nabil's final statement—"Whether it sticks, I guess we'll see"—reflects low confidence and commitment, suggesting the psychiatrist's approach has not successfully evoked intrinsic motivation for change. The psychiatrist's solutions address surface-level coping rather than Nabil's core concern: feeling disrespected and powerless within a system that doesn't value his time. By offering band-aid solutions without exploring this deeper issue, the psychiatrist inadvertently validates Nabil's sustain talk and may actually reduce his motivation for change.

**Recommendation:**

The psychiatrist must maintain evocation and partnership throughout the session, resist the urge to offer pre-formulated solutions, strategically amplify change talk when it emerges, and skillfully address sustain talk through redirection rather than validation. Most importantly, explore the deeper issue underlying Nabil's frustration—his need for respect, autonomy, and agency—rather than focusing solely on appointment logistics. With focused practice on these areas, this clinician could become a skilled motivational interviewer.
