### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is virtually nonexistent in early clinical work. Clinician employs directive language minimizing Dylan's choice: "You really must quit smoking immediately," "You have to work out regularly," "You have to commit to at least 30 minutes a day, no exceptions," "Just start today—no excuses. Can you promise me that?" These statements communicate Dylan has no choice. Question "Can you promise me that?" is demand for compliance framed as question, pressuring Dylan to commit to something he has not agreed is necessary. Language "no more 'I'm fine' talk" and "stop making excuses" further diminishes autonomy by invalidating Dylan's self-assessment. Critical shift occurs with statement "Maybe instead of focusing on what you 'should' do, we can explore what health goals matter most to you." Phrase "we can keep this conversation open for whenever you want to explore changes" explicitly affirms Dylan's right to choose.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out client's own motivations, values, and reasons for change—is largely absent from early work. Clinician tells Dylan what he should do rather than asking about his perspectives. Questions are often rhetorical or accusatory: "Why haven't you quit already?" "Is it laziness or just a lack of willpower?" These do not invite exploration of own motivations; they imply Dylan is deficient. Genuine evocative moment occurs with question "What are some things about your health or habits that you'd like to improve or maintain?" Dylan responds with valuable information about balancing social life and avoiding trouble. Follow-up question "have you thought about any specific steps you might want to take?" is somewhat leading. Psychiatrist does not strategically amplify Dylan's change talk when it appears ("I'd maybe want to avoid getting into trouble or messing up my future"). This represents critical missed opportunity for evocation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership—fundamental MI stance that expertise about change resides within client—is entirely absent from early clinical work. Clinician explicitly assumes expert role: "I want to talk about some important changes you need to make" and "I'll be guiding you through what you should do." This is hierarchical expert-client relationship, not partnership. Language throughout early portion reinforces power imbalance: "You really must," "You have to," "Just stop it completely—that's the only way." When Dylan explicitly states "It just feels like you're lecturing me instead of understanding my side," psychiatrist continues prescribing rather than pausing to engage. Meaningful shift occurs with "I realize I was too quick to jump to conclusions" and "Maybe instead of focusing on what you 'should' do, we can explore what health goals matter most to you." Use of "we" and invitation to "explore" together suggests shared expertise. Statement "we can keep this conversation open for whenever you want to explore changes" suggests true partnership. However, final repetition of opening questions raises concerns about stability.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal skill in cultivating change talk. In early portion, clinician does not strategically evoke Dylan's own language in favor of change. Instead, psychiatrist imposes reasons: "If you don't push yourself now, you're going to regret it later" and "You're at a critical age where habits form." These are clinician-generated reasons, not client-generated change talk. When Dylan produces change talk—"I'd maybe want to avoid getting into trouble or messing up my future because of dumb decisions"—psychiatrist does not amplify or reinforce it. This represents critical missed opportunity. Clinician does ask evocative question "What are some things about your health or habits that you'd like to improve or maintain?" inviting change talk, and Dylan responds with some. However, clinician does not strategically amplify this language. Follow-up question "have you considered setting limits on drinking?" is somewhat leading and does not purely evoke Dylan's own change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates poor skill in handling Dylan's sustain talk (arguments for maintaining status quo). Clinician's approach often reinforces sustain talk rather than softening it. Dylan's sustain talk includes: "I'm almost 21, not some old guy worrying about health stuff," "I don't really see any big problem here to fix right now," "Everyone I know goes out, hangs with friends, maybe drinks a bit." Rather than using MI techniques to soften this, psychiatrist confronts directly: "you really need to stop downplaying the risks," "no more 'I'm fine' nonsense," "Is it laziness or just a lack of willpower?" These confrontational responses typically strengthen sustain talk by triggering defensive reactions. When Dylan says "I don't see why I have to change something that isn't really causing me trouble," psychiatrist responds with fear-based arguments rather than double-sided reflections or strategic reframing. In latter portion, psychiatrist demonstrates improved handling by simply accepting sustain talk without confrontation: "For now, it sounds like you're managing well, and we can keep this conversation open for whenever you want to explore changes." This softer approach does not reinforce defensiveness.

### Summary of Key Findings

**Strengths:**
- Demonstrates capacity to recognize when approach is not working and shift toward more MI-consistent practice
- Shows self-awareness with statement "I realize I was too quick to jump to conclusions"
- Employs genuine reflective statements in latter portion demonstrating understanding of Dylan's perspective
- Later statements explicitly affirm Dylan's right to choose whether and when to engage with change
- Uses collaborative language ("we," "explore together") in latter half

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- Pervasive directiveness and prescription throughout opening and early-middle portions ("you must," "you have to," "just do this")
- Assumption-based assessment without first understanding Dylan's actual presentation or concerns
- Confrontation of sustain talk strengthens rather than softens resistance
- Failure to evoke and amplify Dylan's change talk when it appears
- Inconsistent application of MI principles with dramatic mid-session shift followed by return to directive questioning
- Rare use of genuine exploratory questions; questions often rhetorical or accusatory
- Lack of strategic amplification using MI techniques (double-sided reflection, amplified reflection, strategic reframing)

**Impact on Therapeutic Relationship:**
This session is likely to decrease Dylan's motivation for change and damage therapeutic relationship. Early-session approach—directive, prescriptive, and dismissive—triggers defensiveness and communicates disrespect for Dylan's autonomy. Dylan's explicit statement "It just feels like you're lecturing me instead of understanding my side" indicates approach is counterproductive. While later shift partially repairs relationship, significant damage has been done. Failure to evoke Dylan's own change talk represents critical missed opportunity, as research demonstrates client-generated change talk is far more predictive of behavior change than clinician-imposed reasons.

---

### Clinical Recommendations

**Priority Actions:**
1. Eliminate directive language; replace with collaborative phrasing ("I wonder if," "What do you think about," "How might")
2. Begin sessions with genuine listening rather than predetermined assessment
3. Develop reflective listening skills with goal of 3-4 reflections per client statement
4. Strategically evoke and amplify change talk when it appears
5. Use double-sided reflections to soften sustain talk rather than confronting it
6. Demonstrate genuine acceptance and unconditional positive regard consistently
7. Ask genuine exploratory questions rather than rhetorical or accusatory ones
8. Maintain consistent MI spirit throughout entire session from beginning
9. Develop autonomy support as core clinical stance
10. Seek MI-certified supervision and training; record sessions for feedback

**Training Needs:**
- Structured MI-specific training (10-15 hours minimum)
- Supervision with MI-certified trainer through recording review
- Practice in controlled settings with feedback before clinical application
- Development of MI as automatic clinical stance rather than inconsistently applied technique
