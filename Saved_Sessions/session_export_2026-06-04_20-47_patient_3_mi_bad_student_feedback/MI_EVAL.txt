# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard. Opening with directive language ("We need to talk about some changes you have to make") and immediately framing the patient as having failed ("why haven't you stopped yet?"). Repeatedly uses judgmental framing such as "You're just making excuses because you don't have enough willpower" and "No more excuses." Shows no affirmation of patient's strengths (concern for her son, willingness to consider reduction). The statement "Stress is no excuse to keep smoking" invalidates the patient's legitimate concerns. No genuine warmth or support conveyed; the message is consistently "You are failing and lack willpower."

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session contains almost no evidence of accurate reflective listening or genuine effort to understand the client's perspective. When the patient explains concerns about withdrawal, stress, and competing responsibilities, the clinician responds with dismissal ("Stress is no excuse") rather than reflection or validation. No complex reflections capture emotional nuance. One brief moment of empathic language ("It sounds like smoking is part of your daily routine, and quitting right away feels overwhelming. That's completely understandable") is immediately undermined by return to directive pressure. Psychiatrist never reflects back what the patient has shared about her life context, fears, or emerging motivation.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains focus on smoking cessation as a goal, which is appropriate. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively established and lacks strategic nuance. Direction is rigid and non-negotiable: "You have to quit completely, no ifs or buts." No exploration of patient's own goals, values, or readiness. When patient proposes harm-reduction goal (cutting from 6 to 3 cigarettes), psychiatrist rejects it rather than exploring it strategically. One brief moment attempts collaborative direction ("How do you think about setting a goal together to gradually reduce your smoking?") but is immediately abandoned when psychiatrist reverts to "you just need to tough it out." Strategic direction would involve exploring patient's goals, understanding stage of change, and collaboratively developing aligned plan—none of which occurs.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates an almost complete absence of collaboration. Psychiatrist assumes expert, authority-based stance from opening: "We need to talk about some changes you have to make." Makes unilateral decisions about what is best: "You have to quit completely," "You should just use nicotine gum or patches," "It's the only way you'll succeed." When patient proposes alternative plan (cutting back gradually), psychiatrist rejects it: "You can't just cut back and expect that to fix things." Never asks "What do you think would work best for you?" or invites patient's input. Power dynamic is entirely one-sided: psychiatrist holds all expertise and authority; patient is expected to comply. This is the opposite of collaboration.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session actively undermines autonomy at every turn. Psychiatrist uses controlling language throughout: "You have to quit completely," "You need to start now," "You should just use nicotine gum or patches," "Can you promise me you'll start using those aids this week?" These are demands framed as imperatives, not invitations or options. Explicitly rejects patient's proposed alternative and insists on psychiatrist's preferred approach. When patient expresses hesitation about patches or gum, psychiatrist responds with "you just need to tough it out," dismissing concerns and pressuring compliance. Uses guilt and fear to pressure compliance: "Being a mom is exactly why you need to stop right now—your son depends on you!" and "If you keep delaying, you're risking your health and your family's future." No acknowledgment of patient's right to make her own decisions.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates virtually no evocation. Psychiatrist tells patient why she should change rather than drawing out her own motivations: "it's the only way you'll feel better," "it's the only way you'll improve your health." When patient spontaneously offers change talk ("Being a mother, I don't want something to happen to me that would leave my son without a mom"), psychiatrist does not evoke or amplify it; instead weaponizes it to pressure compliance. When patient proposes realistic goal (cutting back in evenings), psychiatrist rejects it rather than exploring it. Never asks open-ended questions designed to elicit patient's own reasons for change: "What concerns you most about your smoking?" "What would be different if you made a change?" "What matters most to you?" Psychiatrist acts as though motivation must be installed from outside through directive pressure rather than believing motivation resides within the client.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates the opposite of partnership. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role and positions patient as deficient in judgment and willpower. From opening ("We need to talk about some changes you have to make"), establishes hierarchical, expert-driven dynamic. Makes pronouncements about what is necessary, what will work, and what patient must do. Explicitly attributes patient's difficulty to personal deficiency: "You're just making excesses because you don't have enough willpower." This frames problem as residing within patient's character rather than in complexity of her situation. Never invites patient's expertise about her own life, coping strategies, or knowledge of what might work for her. One brief moment shifts toward partnership ("I want to work with you on this") but is immediately undermined by return to expert pronouncements. Language throughout is "I know what's best for you, and you need to comply" rather than "Let's figure this out together."

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session contains minimal change talk from patient, and what emerges is not cultivated or amplified. Patient offers several instances of change talk: "Being a mother, I don't want something to happen to me that would leave my son without a mom"; "I could maybe try to cut back in the evenings, from six to three cigarettes"; "That seems more manageable right now"; "I'm willing to try cutting back in the evenings to three cigarettes"; "I'm willing to consider patches or gum if it feels like it could actually help." In each case, psychiatrist fails to evoke, amplify, or reinforce. When patient expresses concern about her son, psychiatrist weaponizes it rather than exploring it. When patient proposes cutting back, psychiatrist rejects it. When patient expresses willingness to try gradual reduction, psychiatrist dismisses it. When patient expresses willingness to consider patches or gum, psychiatrist pressures her. No strategic effort to strengthen patient's own language in favor of change.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Session demonstrates minimal skill in softening sustain talk. Patient offers sustain talk in several forms: "I don't think I smoke or drink that much compared to people I work with"; "I'm not sure I'm ready to just quit cold turkey"; "I haven't tried patches or gum, and I don't know if they'll really work for me"; "It just feels like a lot to change all at once." Psychiatrist's response is primarily confrontational rather than skillfully redirected. When patient minimizes behavior, psychiatrist ignores it and moves to directive pressure. When patient expresses doubt about readiness, psychiatrist responds with "You're just making excuses because you don't have enough willpower"—confrontational and likely to entrench sustain talk. When patient expresses skepticism about patches, psychiatrist responds with "you just need to tough it out," dismissing concern rather than exploring or reframing it. One moment briefly softens sustain talk ("It sounds like smoking is part of your daily routine, and quitting right away feels overwhelming. That's completely understandable") but is immediately followed by return to directive pressure, undermining the softening. Lacks consistent use of double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic redirection.

## OVERALL ASSESSMENT

**Overall Session Score: 1.2/5**

**MI Competency Level:** Minimal/Inadequate

**Summary:**

This session represents a **fundamental failure to implement Motivational Interviewing principles** and demonstrates a **confrontational, directive, expert-driven approach** that is actively counterproductive to evoking client motivation for change. The psychiatrist violates nearly every core MI principle across all nine domains.

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Absence of acceptance and unconditional positive regard
- Minimal empathy and perspective-taking
- Authoritarian rather than collaborative stance
- Active undermining of patient autonomy
- Failure to evoke intrinsic motivation
- Confrontational handling of resistance
- Rejection of harm-reduction goals
- Dismissal of patient's change talk
- Judgmental framing and shame-based language

**Impact on Patient Engagement:**

This session is likely to **decrease** the patient's motivation for change and increase defensiveness and resistance. The patient enters with emerging motivation and a realistic, achievable goal; rather than building on this foundation, the psychiatrist dismisses it and demands complete cessation immediately. This approach will result in increased defensiveness, decreased intrinsic motivation, reduced likelihood of follow-through, and damaged therapeutic relationship.

**Recommendation:**

This clinician requires **immediate, structured MI training and ongoing supervision** to develop competence in this evidence-based approach. The current approach is not only ineffective—it is actively harmful to the therapeutic relationship and the patient's motivation for change.
