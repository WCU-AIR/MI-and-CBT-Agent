# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance

**Comments:** Minimal unconditional positive regard demonstrated. Clinician uses problem-focused framing and conditional support tied to compliance with medical recommendations. Lacks explicit affirmation of client's independence, cultural traditions, and values. Judgmental undertones present through presuppositional questioning ("Do you think the drinking is causing any problems?"). ### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2

Missed opportunities to reflect client's core emotional content: tension between autonomy and responsibility, fear of judgment, and protective mechanisms. ### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clear focus maintained on alcohol reduction throughout session. Progressive clarification from drinking patterns to barriers to confidence assessment to action steps. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively established. Premature narrowing of focus without exploring client's own concerns or goals. Direction maintained through subtle pressure and clinician authority rather than through evoking client's intrinsic motivation. Missed opportunity to explore how competing values (independence and baby's health) might be aligned.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Expert-driven stance predominates throughout. Unilateral advice-giving without seeking client input: "Maybe you should try cutting back," "It would probably help if you asked your doctor," "Maybe you should try switching to non-alcoholic drinks." Power imbalance evident in language ("should," "probably," "would help"). Client's reasoning dismissed rather than engaged with. No co-construction of solutions. Minimal incorporation of client's own ideas about involving partner and gathering information. Client positioned as recipient of guidance rather than partner in problem-solving.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Critical deficiency. Client explicitly states need for autonomy ("I'm independent," "I want to do it on my terms," "I need to feel in control of my own decisions"), yet clinician consistently undermines through repeated directive language. Conditional autonomy support only at session end ("Making changes is your decision") feels hollow after sustained subtle coercion. No exploration of client's own decision-making process. Paradox between external pressure and client values never addressed. Client's closing statement ("Thanks for not pushing too hard") is ironic—suggests compliance rather than genuine autonomy. This represents fundamental MI failure.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal evocation present. Client spontaneously offers significant change talk ("I really want to have a healthy baby," "I want to do what's best for the baby," "I want to make sure I do right by this baby") but clinician acknowledges minimally without deepening through strategic questioning. Questions asked primarily to gather information or elicit agreement rather than to genuinely explore client's thinking. No exploration of client's values, resources, or past successes with change. Reliance on telling rather than asking. Missed opportunities to evoke self-efficacy, ability, and intrinsic motivation. Confidence scaling used but not strategically to evoke more change talk.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Clinician operates from expert stance throughout, positioning self as authority on health facts, what's best for client, and change process. Repeated suggestions ("Maybe you should," "It would probably help if," "You might want to") center clinician's perspective. No acknowledgment of client's expertise about her own life, body, family, and values. Power imbalance in language ("I think," "It's important," "It would probably help"). Minimal incorporation of client's contributions into session direction. True partnership—where client's wisdom is genuinely valued and shapes the session—largely absent.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk present in client's speech but largely unaddressed by clinician. Client offers desire ("I really want to have a healthy baby"), need ("I want to do what's best"), commitment ("I want to make sure I do right by this baby"), and willingness ("I'm willing to ask my doctor," "trying non-alcoholic drinks doesn't feel like too big a stretch"). Clinician acknowledges minimally without amplification or strategic deepening. No reflections that reinforce or strengthen change talk. Prescriptive advice substituted for evocative questioning. Missed opportunities to ask about meaning, importance, and commitment. Confidence scaling present but not used strategically to evoke more change talk.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clinician avoids direct confrontation and maintains respectful tone when client expresses sustain talk ("I don't see problems," "I feel fine," "Evidence isn't clear," "One glass won't hurt"). Appropriate redirection toward change talk and action steps rather than debate. However, misses sophisticated MI techniques: no double-sided reflections acknowledging both sustain talk and change talk simultaneously; no reframing of sustain talk as aligned with deeper values; no exploration of protective function of resistance; no strategic open questions to soften sustain talk. Relationship preserved but opportunity for deeper work missed.

### Overall Clinical Summary

**Session Score: 2.1/5 – Below Average MI Competence**

**Strengths:**
- Maintained respectful, non-judgmental tone throughout
- Avoided direct argument or confrontation with client's perspective
- Acknowledged some of client's ideas (partner involvement, information gathering)
- Gentle redirection from sustain talk toward change talk
- Closing statement contained autonomy-supporting language

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Expert-driven stance undermines MI spirit:** Clinician operates from assumption of knowing what's best rather than trusting client's wisdom
- **Autonomy consistently undermined:** Despite client's explicit statements about needing independence, clinician continues with subtle directives
- **Minimal evocation of intrinsic motivation:** Client's spontaneous change talk acknowledged but never deepened; clinician relies on telling rather than asking
- **Lack of true collaboration:** Client positioned as recipient of advice rather than partner in problem-solving
- **Superficial empathy:** Reflections lack emotional depth and fail to capture ambivalence and values
- **Missed value alignment:** Clinician never helps client see how informed decision-making about baby's health expresses her independence and love for child

**Impact on Client Engagement:**
Session likely produces **compliance without genuine commitment.** Client agrees to steps primarily to satisfy external pressure rather than from resolved ambivalence and intrinsic motivation. Client's statement "Thanks for not pushing too hard" suggests experienced pressure despite clinician's attempt to be gentle, indicating autonomy was not genuinely honored. Risk of reactance and return to status quo once external pressure decreases.

**Immediate Priorities for Improvement:**
1. Eliminate directive language; replace with genuine curiosity
2. Shift from telling to asking—use open questions to evoke client's own thinking
3. Develop complex reflections capturing emotional nuance and ambivalence
4. Strategically amplify client's change talk through reflections and follow-up questions
5. Develop true collaboration by seeking client's input on direction and solutions
6. Explicitly affirm client's values and help align change with these values
7. Use confidence scaling strategically to evoke change talk
8. Develop sophisticated responses to sustain talk using double-sided reflections
9. Demonstrate genuine acceptance and unconditional positive regard
10. Seek ongoing MI-specific supervision and training

**Recommendation:** This clinician requires focused training and supervision on core MI principles, particularly autonomy support, evocation, and collaboration. With deliberate practice and feedback, significant improvement in MI competence is achievable.
