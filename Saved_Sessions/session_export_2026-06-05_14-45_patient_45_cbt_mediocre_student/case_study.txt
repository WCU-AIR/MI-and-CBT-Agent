--- DISORDER ---
Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)

--- CASE STUDY ---

Peggy Isaac, a 41-year-old administrative assistant, was referred for evaluation due to feeling “always on edge.” She lived alone, had never married or had children, and had no prior psychiatric history. Eight months earlier, her long-term boyfriend abruptly ended their relationship, triggering significant anxiety about routine tasks, work performance, finances, and being a burden to others. She moved to a less expensive apartment and sought frequent reassurance from coworkers and her mother.

Over the past three months, Ms. Isaac began avoiding leaving her apartment, initially at night and later during the day, due to fears of something bad happening and being unable to summon help. She developed strategies such as having groceries delivered but felt increasingly vulnerable outside her home. At home, she could relax and enjoy leisure activities.

Ms. Isaac reported lifelong nervousness and early separation anxiety, crying during kindergarten drop-offs and seeing a counselor at age 10 during her parents’ divorce. She had long-standing difficulty being alone, maintaining frequent romantic relationships since age 16, and had functioned well socially and professionally until the recent breakup. She denied depressive symptoms beyond transient sadness after the breakup. Her GAD-7 score indicated severe anxiety.

On examination, she appeared anxious but cooperative. Cognition, insight, and judgment were intact. There were no psychotic symptoms, psychomotor changes, or suicidality.

Discussion: Ms. Isaac meets DSM-5 criteria for GAD, characterized by excessive anxiety, difficulty controlling worries, restlessness, fatigue, impaired concentration, and reassurance-seeking. Her recent avoidance of leaving the home suggests early agoraphobic behaviors, though the duration is shorter than the 6-month criterion for DSM-5 agoraphobia. The onset of her symptoms coincides with psychosocial stressors and may be compounded by a lifelong insecure attachment style. Cognitive-behavioral therapy and/or pharmacotherapy (e.g., SSRI or SNRI) are appropriate treatment options.
