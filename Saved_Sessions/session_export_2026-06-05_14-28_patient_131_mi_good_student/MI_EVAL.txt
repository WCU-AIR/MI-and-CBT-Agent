### Acceptance

**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance throughout. Validates Viktor's perspective early ("It sounds like you're feeling a bit unsure") and affirms self-awareness ("That's a really insightful way to look at it"). ### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong reflective listening with accurate, multi-dimensional reflections. Particularly skillful: "You're describing your drug use as a kind of pressure valve" captures both functional and emotional dimensions. Sophisticated reflection on ambivalence: "It sounds like there's a part of you that's open to change if the commitment is strong enough, but also a part that's cautious." Consistently captures emotional experience and tension between marital peace and reliance on coping. Could deepen by exploring underlying fears and vulnerabilities more explicitly (e.g., when Viktor expresses worry about losing his coping mechanism).

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintains clear, strategic focus on drug use and its relationship to stress management and marital conflict. Session progresses logically: exploration of ambivalence → developing discrepancy → exploring strengths → concrete first steps. Does not allow drift into tangential topics. However, could be more explicit about target behavior earlier. When Viktor introduces "deeper issues" as cause of fights, psychiatrist accepts framing somewhat uncritically rather than gently exploring whether drug use is *part* of larger pattern. Minor loss of directional clarity here.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong collaborative stance with consistent invitation of Viktor's perspective. Opening question immediately establishes partnership: "What would you most like to focus on?" Uses collaborative language throughout ("It sounds like," "You're describing"). Asks Viktor directly about confidence level and what would support him rather than prescribing solutions. However, subtle moments where collaboration shifts toward gentle guidance: asking about past stress management without first exploring what Viktor thinks would be helpful. Offering specific suggestions (phone alarm, telling wife) rather than asking what Viktor envisions.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Moderate autonomy support with notable gaps. Positive elements: opening question invites choice; avoids prescriptive language; frames first step as "if you felt ready." However, does not explicitly affirm Viktor's right to *not* change—implicit directional pull toward change throughout. When Viktor expresses low motivation (3/10), psychiatrist could have said: "It sounds like right now you don't see a compelling reason to change, and that's completely your choice." Instead, moves forward with exploring strengths, subtly communicating change is expected. Missing explicit acknowledgment that Viktor might decide not to try first step or might try and decide it doesn't work.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong evocation skills with consistent drawing out of Viktor's own motivations and resources rather than imposing reasons for change. Excellent questions: "What do they mean to you?" (about fights); "What do you think your drug use means to you?" (about function); "What would you say motivates you to keep things as they are?" (about reasons). Invites self-discovery rather than clinician-imposed insight. However, could deepen: when Viktor rationalizes deeper issues cause fights, could ask "What would need to happen for you to consider your drug use might be worth addressing?" When Viktor expresses uncertainty, could ask "What do you think you'd need to feel more confident?"

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong partnership with active power-sharing and positioning of Viktor as expert on his own experience. Uses inclusive language ("It sounds like," "You're describing") that reinforces collaboration. Validates insights and problem-solving capacity. Excellent opening establishes shared agenda-setting. Asks about confidence and support, positioning Viktor as decision-maker. However, subtle moments weaken partnership: guiding Viktor toward specific memory (successful coping without drugs) rather than asking what would be helpful to explore. Offering solutions rather than co-constructing. Does not explicitly invite Viktor's feedback about session direction or ask "Does this feel like a useful direction to you?"

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Moderate skill in evoking change talk but with significant missed opportunities and inconsistent amplification. Change talk emerges ("sometimes it causes fights," "I suppose those skills could help," "I'd say I'm about a 6 on confidence") but psychiatrist reflects accurately without strategically deepening it. When Viktor says fights happen, could ask follow-up questions: "Tell me more about those fights—what happens? How does that affect you?" When Viktor articulates first step, could strengthen commitment: "How important is this to you? What makes you think you could actually do this?" Most significantly, does not strategically evoke change talk related to *reasons* for change or *need* for change. Missing deep exploration of what marriage means to Viktor, what he values, what he fears losing.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Moderate skill in handling sustain talk with notable gaps in strategic response. Psychiatrist appropriately reflects sustain talk without directly challenging ("I don't think it's a problem," "I'm managing it," "It's not easy to change"). However, does not strategically pivot toward change talk or use double-sided reflections to soften resistance. When Viktor says "stopping wouldn't magically fix everything," could use double-sided reflection: "On one hand, you're right there are deeper issues, and on the other hand, you've noticed your drug use does sometimes contribute to fights." When Viktor expresses low motivation (3/10), does not explore what would move him to 4 or 5. Does not use reframing to shift perspective. Accepts sustain talk somewhat passively rather than skillfully softening through MI-specific techniques.

### **Overall MI Competency Assessment**

**Current Session Score: 3.7/5**

**Summary:** This session demonstrates solid foundational MI competence with strong reflective listening, genuine warmth, and collaborative stance. Psychiatrist successfully avoids confrontation, maintains nonjudgmental approach, and creates environment where Viktor feels heard and respected. Session structure is logical and appropriate, progressing from exploration of ambivalence to strengths to concrete first steps.

However, session falls short of excellent MI practice in several critical areas: (1) Change talk is evoked but not consistently amplified or deepened through strategic follow-up questions; (2) Sustain talk is accepted somewhat passively rather than skillfully softened through double-sided reflections and reframing; (3) Autonomy support is undermined by subtle directional pull toward change; (4) Exploration of intrinsic motivation and values is limited; (5) Clinician occasionally shifts from true collaboration toward gentle advice-giving.

**Competency Level:** **Competent but not yet expert MI practice.** Clinician has mastered foundational skills (empathy, acceptance, collaboration) but has not yet developed sophisticated skills of strategic change talk amplification, skillful sustain talk management, and deep value exploration. This is solid clinical work that will help Viktor feel understood and may gently move him toward contemplation, but unlikely to powerfully evoke intrinsic motivation for change.

**Most Critical Improvements Needed:**
1. Strategic amplification of change talk through follow-up questions
2. Explicit autonomy support affirming right to choose
3. Deeper exploration of values and intrinsic motivation
4. Skillful softening of sustain talk through double-sided reflections

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation:** Viktor is likely to feel respected and understood, but motivation remains externally driven (wife's pressure) rather than internally driven. With recommended improvements—particularly deeper exploration of what marriage means, what he fears losing, what he values—psychiatrist could significantly increase likelihood of genuine, intrinsic motivation for change.
