# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Complete absence of unconditional positive regard. Psychiatrist repeatedly communicates disapproval and judgment ("you're wrong to downplay it," "you're just making it worse for yourself," "stop making excuses"). No warmth, affirmation, or acknowledgment of patient's legitimate concerns. Repeated ultimatums ("Yes or no?") communicate contempt for patient's internal experience. Fundamentally incompatible with MI spirit.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Zero reflective listening demonstrated. Psychiatrist never reflects back patient's experience or validates concerns despite patient's clear articulations of feeling pressured and shut down. No genuine effort to understand patient's perspective, fears, or values. Dismisses emotional experience as irrelevant ("This isn't about what you want to feel; it's about what you need to do"). Active dismissal of patient's vulnerability rather than empathic response.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Maintains clear focus on target behavior (alcohol cessation) but through authoritarian coercion rather than collaborative guidance. Rigid, non-negotiable stance ("This is not a negotiation," "There are no other options") prevents genuine exploration of patient's motivations and readiness. Patient demonstrates openness to change but psychiatrist's inflexibility prevents any meaningful direction-setting. Focus maintained but method fundamentally violates MI principles.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Explicitly rejects collaborative partnership. Psychiatrist states "This is not a negotiation" and "No, that's not okay" when patient requests dialogue. Treats interview as expert-driven hierarchy rather than equal partnership. Patient repeatedly invites collaboration ("Can we maybe talk about it more?") and is shut down each time. Demonstrates complete rejection of power-sharing and joint problem-solving.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Active suppression of autonomy through coercion and control. Psychiatrist explicitly undermines choice ("You have to stop making excuses and just commit," "No compromises," "There's no time for more talking"). Patient clearly articulates autonomy need ("I want to feel like I'm making the choice because it makes sense to me, not just because someone's pushing me") and psychiatrist responds with further pressure. Approach is psychologically coercive and triggers predictable reactance.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Zero evocation of patient's own motivations, values, or resources. Psychiatrist imposes reasons for change rather than drawing them out. No open questions asked to explore patient's perspective. Patient produces change talk ("I'm willing to think about making some changes," "I want to take care of myself") which is dismissed as "excuses" rather than amplified. Opposite of MI approach—tells rather than asks.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Complete rejection of partnership model. Psychiatrist opens with "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do" and maintains rigid expert positioning throughout. Dismisses patient's knowledge of their own life and readiness ("This isn't about what you want to feel; it's about what you need to do"). Explicitly rejects power-sharing ("This is not a negotiation"). Positions psychiatrist as sole authority with only correct answer.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Actively suppresses rather than cultivates change talk. Patient's statements of willingness and values are dismissed as "excuses" and "hesitation." Psychiatrist confronts rather than explores, shames rather than encourages. No reinforcement or amplification of patient's own change language. Approach decreases rather than increases motivation. Patient's change talk becomes progressively more entrenched in sustain talk by session end.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Confrontational approach to sustain talk amplifies rather than softens resistance. Direct confrontation ("No, no, you're wrong to downplay it," "You have to stop fooling yourself") triggers predictable reactance. Patient's sustain talk becomes progressively more entrenched throughout session. Maintains some focus on target behavior (accounts for score of 2 rather than 1), but method is counterproductive. Research demonstrates this approach increases rather than decreases resistance.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.1/5**

**MI Competency Level:** Minimal to no MI competence. This session represents **anti-MI practice** rather than MI with gaps.

---

### Critical Findings

**Fundamental Violations:**
- Authoritarian, expert-driven approach throughout
- Active suppression of patient autonomy and choice
- Confrontational stance that triggers psychological reactance
- Complete absence of reflective listening and validation
- Imposition of solutions rather than evocation of patient's own motivation
- Dismissal of legitimate autonomy and readiness concerns as "excuses"

**Impact on Patient Engagement:**
Patient enters session with genuine openness to change ("I'm willing to think about making some changes," "I want to take care of myself") and exits more resistant and entrenched ("I can't just say yes or no right now"). **Session actively decreased intrinsic motivation and increased resistance.**

---

### Strengths (Limited)
- Maintains consistent focus on target behavior (alcohol use and health)
- Clearly communicates concern for patient's wellbeing (though method is counterproductive)

---

### Areas Requiring Immediate Improvement

**Priority 1: Fundamental Reorientation to MI Spirit**
- Shift from expert-driven to collaborative partnership model
- Develop genuine acceptance and unconditional positive regard
- Recognize that expertise about change resides primarily within the client

**Priority 2: Develop Reflective Listening Skills**
- Learn to reflect back patient's experience before responding
- Validate concerns and demonstrate genuine understanding
- Practice: 2 reflections for every 1 directive

**Priority 3: Replace Confrontation with Exploration**
- Approach sustain talk with curiosity rather than judgment
- Use open questions to explore ambivalence
- Normalize ambivalence as healthy and intelligent

**Priority 4: Support Autonomy Explicitly**
- Eliminate "have to," "must," "need to" language
- Affirm patient's right to choose
- Invite collaborative problem-solving

**Priority 5: Evoke Rather Than Impose**
- Ask open questions about patient's own reasons for change
- Amplify and reinforce patient's change talk
- Draw out values and motivations rather than imposing them

**Priority 6: Eliminate Shame and Judgment**
- Replace judgmental language with warm, respectful curiosity
- Validate patient's concerns and self-awareness
- Model respect for patient's autonomy and intelligence

### Formal Recommendations

1. **Mandatory Formal MI Training:** Enroll in MINT-certified Motivational Interviewing workshop (2-3 days minimum)
2. **Ongoing Supervision:** Participate in MI consultation group or individual supervision with MI expert
3. **Session Recording and Coding:** Record sessions and have them coded using MITI (Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity) scale
4. **Supervised Practice:** Do not continue behavior change counseling until demonstrating MI competence through supervised practice
5. **Self-Reflection:** Review this transcript and identify every instance of judgment, confrontation, and autonomy suppression; rewrite using MI-consistent responses
