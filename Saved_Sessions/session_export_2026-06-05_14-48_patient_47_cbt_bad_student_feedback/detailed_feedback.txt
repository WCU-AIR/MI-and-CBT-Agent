
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Feedback: Therapist Evaluation

---

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist only attempted to set an agenda near the end of the session (after approximately 80% of the interaction had already occurred). When the agenda was finally proposed—"spend some time talking about how the clutter is affecting your mood, then chat about your sleep and energy levels, and finally think about some small steps you might want to try"—it was vague, lacked time allocation, and failed to establish clear, measurable session goals. The agenda mentioned three broad topics but did not prioritize which problem would receive focus, did not establish specific objectives (e.g., "identify three automatic thoughts related to discarding," "develop a behavioral experiment"), and did not clarify how much time would be devoted to each area.

Most critically, the therapist spent the majority of the session engaging in unstructured, meandering conversation dominated by personal anecdotes before attempting any agenda-setting. This violates a fundamental CBT principle: establishing structure and direction at the session's outset. A proper agenda would have been collaborative, specific, and established within the first 2-3 minutes. For example: "Wendy, I'd like to understand how your difficulty discarding items connects to your low mood. Then we'll identify one specific thought that gets in the way when you try to discard something, and develop a plan to test that thought. Does that sound helpful? We have about 45 minutes." The late and vague agenda-setting demonstrates minimal competence in this foundational skill.

**Summary:**
Agenda-setting occurred too late in the session and lacked specificity, measurable goals, or time allocation. This represents a critical failure in CBT structure and session management.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made virtually no attempt to elicit feedback from the patient throughout the session. There were no explicit questions checking whether the patient understood the therapist's reasoning, no requests for the patient's reactions to suggestions, no clarification of whether interventions were helpful, and no summary of key points at the session's end to verify the patient's understanding or agreement.

The therapist offered numerous suggestions (drink more water, go to bed earlier, watch funny shows, go for walks, use the phrase "this too shall pass") without ever asking: "Does that make sense to you?" "How do you feel about trying that?" "What barriers might get in the way?" or "What did you find most helpful from today?" The patient's responses indicate growing passivity and acceptance rather than active engagement—the patient says things like "I'll try" and "Thanks for the encouragement," but the therapist never verifies whether these commitments are genuine or whether the patient actually understands the rationale.

In evidence-based CBT, feedback elicitation is essential for ensuring collaboration, identifying misunderstandings, and adjusting the approach based on the patient's perspective. The complete absence of this skill represents a fundamental gap in therapeutic competence. A competent therapist would have asked at minimum: "How are you feeling about what we've discussed today?" "What's one thing that resonated with you?" "What concerns do you have about trying these steps?"

**Summary:**
No systematic feedback elicitation occurred. The therapist failed to check understanding, verify patient agreement, or adjust based on patient reactions—a critical omission in CBT practice.

### 3. Understanding

However, the repeated self-disclosure, minimization, and advice-giving without exploration represent significant interpersonal problems that would likely reduce the patient's sense of being truly understood and could damage the therapeutic alliance.

### 5. Collaboration
