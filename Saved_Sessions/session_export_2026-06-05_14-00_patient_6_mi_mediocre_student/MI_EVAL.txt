## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.6/5**

### Strengths
- Demonstrates basic politeness and does not use confrontational language
- Provides accurate medical information about serotonin syndrome and its risks
- Avoids arguing with or dismissing the patient's concerns
- Uses some MI-consistent questions (scaling questions about importance and confidence)
- Does not reinforce sustain talk or pressure the patient into compliance
- Offers follow-up care, showing some continuity of treatment

### Critical Areas for Improvement
- **Fundamental MI spirit violation:** Imposes own agenda (lifestyle changes) rather than following the patient's stated concern
- **Minimal collaboration:** Maintains expert-driven stance with prescriptive advice rather than joint problem-solving
- **No evocation:** Provides information and solutions rather than drawing out the patient's own motivations, ideas, and resources
- **Missed change talk amplification:** Multiple instances of Alessia's change talk are not recognized or amplified
- **Superficial empathy:** Reflections are factual rather than capturing emotional nuance or deeper meaning
- **Directive language:** Consistent use of "should," "probably," "might want to" that subtly pressures rather than supports autonomy
- **No exploration of patient expertise:** Never asks what Alessia already knows, what her ideas are, or what her concerns are beyond surface clarification
- **Missed opportunity to address underlying fears:** Does not explore Alessia's fear of disappointing her doctor or seeming "difficult"—a critical barrier to self-advocacy

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change
While this session does not harm the patient, it significantly underutilizes the opportunity to strengthen Alessia's intrinsic motivation and self-efficacy. Alessia enters with good change talk and exits with a prescriptive plan rather than a collaboratively developed one, potentially reducing her sense of ownership. Most concerning, the psychiatrist does not address Alessia's fear of disappointing her doctor—a critical barrier to her actually advocating effectively with her prescribing physician. The expert-driven stance may inadvertently reinforce her tendency to defer to authority rather than trust her own judgment. Alessia will likely follow the advice because she is compliant, but not because she has deepened her own conviction. This reduces the likelihood of sustained self-advocacy in future medical encounters.

### Primary Recommendation
The psychiatrist must fundamentally shift from an expert-driven, prescriptive stance to a collaborative, evocative one. The most critical priority is learning to **evoke rather than provide** and to **follow the patient's lead** rather than imposing the clinician's agenda. This is the foundation of MI practice and is currently absent from this clinician's work. Engagement in MI-specific training, supervision, and recorded session review with feedback is strongly recommended.
