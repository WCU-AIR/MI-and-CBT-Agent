# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates profound absence of unconditional positive regard. Repeatedly judges Oliver's emotional experience as an obstacle ("stop feeling guilty," "why do you keep letting your feelings get in the way?"). Oliver's ambivalence and care for his flatmate are framed as character flaws rather than understandable human conflicts. No affirmation of Oliver's strengths, loyalty, self-awareness, or willingness to seek help. Implicit message: "Your feelings are the problem; eliminate them and comply."

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal effort to understand Oliver's perspective or internal experience. When Oliver articulates his core conflict (caring vs. independence), clinician does not reflect or explore ambivalence; instead responds prescriptively. No clarifying questions to deepen understanding ("What does the guilt feel like?" "What are you most worried about?"). No complex reflections capturing emotional depth. Approach is transactional, focused on extracting compliance rather than understanding the person behind the problem.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains exceptionally clear, consistent direction throughout: Oliver must tell flatmate to leave by specific date, using firm language, with no softening or negotiation. Repeats directive across every exchange with remarkable consistency ("You must do that immediately," "You have to be firm and unyielding," "When exactly will you tell him?"). However, direction is imposed unilaterally with no collaborative input. In MI context, direction should be collaborative, not weaponized as tool of control.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Operates from rigid expert-driven, authoritarian stance. No joint problem-solving or shared decision-making. Oliver's preferences, values, and concerns never solicited or incorporated. When Oliver mentions wanting to "remind him gently but firmly," psychiatrist immediately shuts down: "No, you can't be gentle." Language consistently controlling ("You have to," "You must," "You need to") with no invitation for Oliver's agency. Zero power-sharing or co-construction of intervention.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Systematically undermines Oliver's autonomy through controlling language and prescriptive directives. Frames Oliver's choices as predetermined and non-negotiable ("You have to set boundaries now; otherwise, this will just keep dragging on"). When Oliver expresses hesitation or concern about approach, psychiatrist pressures rather than explores ("You have to stop overthinking this"). No acknowledgment that Oliver might choose different approach or has right to make own decisions. Oliver positioned as passive recipient of instructions rather than active agent in change process.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Demonstrates virtually no evocation—the cornerstone of MI. Never asks questions inviting Oliver to explore own reasons for change, values, or resources ("What's most important to you about having your own space?" "What strengths have you used in past difficult conversations?"). Instead imposes motivation: "You have to be more assertive." When Oliver spontaneously generates change talk ("My job is done," "I have to be more assertive"), clinician accepts as compliance and moves on without amplifying. Approach is "installing" motivation rather than evoking it.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Assumes expert role entirely with no genuine partnership. Clinician positions himself as authority who knows exactly what Oliver needs; Oliver is compliant patient. No invitation for Oliver's expertise about own situation ("You know your friend better than I do. What do you think would work?"). No acknowledgment of Oliver's growing insight or collaborative exploration of obstacles. When Oliver expresses worry about "fallout," psychiatrist dismisses rather than explores. No shared responsibility for outcome. Stance is entirely hierarchical and expert-driven.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Oliver spontaneously generates substantial change talk ("My job is done," "I'm starting to believe friendlier approach has delayed inevitable," "I'm absolutely sure I'll stick to this plan"), but psychiatrist fails to amplify or develop it. Clinician simply accepts as compliance and moves to next directive. No exploration of Oliver's confidence, strengths, or intrinsic motivation. Psychiatrist extracts commitment through pressure ("When exactly will you tell him? You must commit") rather than cultivating genuine motivation. Approach risks undermining Oliver's intrinsic change talk by replacing it with external pressure.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Oliver expresses sustain talk throughout (guilt, worry, care for flatmate, concern about fallout). Psychiatrist's approach is confrontational and dismissive rather than skillful. Responds to guilt with: "You really need to stop feeling guilty" and "Guilt and worry are just excuses." This attacks sustain talk and invalidates emotional experience rather than softening it. No use of double-sided reflections or strategic reframing. One positive element: clinician does redirect Oliver away from sustain talk through repeated return to action plan. However, this is done through pressure and control rather than skillful MI techniques that maintain collaborative relationship.

---

### **Overall Session Score: 1.3/5**

---

## Summary Assessment

**Fundamental Violation of MI Spirit:** This session represents near-complete abandonment of Motivational Interviewing principles. The approach is directive, controlling, and authoritarian rather than collaborative, evocative, and client-centered.

**Key Deficits:**
- Absence of acceptance and empathy; judges Oliver's emotional experience as obstacle
- Systematic undermining of autonomy through controlling language and prescriptive directives
- Failure to evoke; tells rather than asks; imposes motivation rather than drawing it out
- Confrontational handling of ambivalence; attacks feelings rather than validating them
- No genuine partnership; clinician assumes expert role entirely
- Missed opportunities to explore Oliver's values, strengths, and intrinsic motivation

**Impact on Client:** While Oliver complies with directives and commits to action, compliance appears driven by external pressure rather than intrinsic motivation. Risks include: reduced intrinsic motivation, increased ambivalence, damaged therapeutic relationship, missed opportunity for deeper work, and risk of relapse in future boundary-setting situations.

**Competency Level:** This session does not meet standards for competent MI practice. Clinician requires substantial retraining in MI principles, particularly evocation, collaboration, autonomy support, and genuine empathic understanding. Fundamental shift from directive to evocative stance is required.
