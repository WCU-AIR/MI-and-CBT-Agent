
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Eliminate Confrontational Language and Adopt Collaborative Framing (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Remove all prescriptive language ("have to," "must," "need to," "should") and replace with collaborative inquiry and autonomy-supportive language.
- **Example:** 
  - **Instead of:** "You really need to quit now before it's too late. You have to stop making excuses and take action."
  - **Use:** "I'm hearing that you're thinking about quitting, and you also have concerns about whether now is the right time. What do you think would need to happen for you to feel ready to make a change?"
- **Practice:** Record yourself conducting a mock MI session and count instances of prescriptive language. Aim for zero. Practice replacing each prescriptive statement with an open-ended question that invites the client's perspective.

---

### 2. **Implement Consistent Autonomy Support from Session Onset**
- **Action:** Begin every session by explicitly inviting the client to set the agenda and shape the session direction. Consistently affirm the client's right to choose and their ownership of the change process.
- **Example:**
  - **Opening:** "I'm glad you're here. What would you like to focus on today? What's most important to you right now?"
  - **When client expresses ambivalence:** "It sounds like you're weighing both sides of this—the reasons to change and the reasons to stay the same. That's a really important process. What feels most important to you right now?"
  - **When client resists:** "I hear that you're not ready to commit to patches this week. That makes sense. What do you think might help you feel more ready?"
- **Practice:** In every session, explicitly ask the client what they want to focus on and affirm their autonomy at least three times. Record yourself and listen for instances where you override the client's stated preference or timeline.

---

### 3. **Develop Skillful Evocation Through Strategic Questioning**
- **Action:** Replace clinician-provided reasons for change with open-ended questions designed to evoke the client's own motivations, values, and concerns. Use the "DARN-CAT" framework to systematically evoke change talk.
- **Example:**
  - **Desire:** "What would be different if you quit smoking? What appeals to you about that?"
  - **Ability:** "If you decided to cut back, what do you think you could do? What have you tried before that worked, even a little?"
  - **Reasons:** "What are the main reasons you're thinking about quitting? What concerns you most about your smoking?"
  - **Need:** "How important is it for you to address your smoking? What would happen if you didn't make any changes?"
  - **Commitment:** "On a scale of 0-10, how committed are you to making a change? What would it take to move from a [X] to a [X+1]?"
- **Practice:** In your next three sessions, use only open-ended questions about change for the first 10 minutes. Do not provide any reasons for change yourself. Record and listen for how many times the client articulates their own reasons vs. how many times you provide them.

---

### 4. **Amplify and Reinforce Emerging Change Talk**
- **Action:** When the client expresses change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment), immediately reflect it back, explore it further, and reinforce it. Use strategic reflections that deepen and strengthen the client's own motivation.
- **Example:**
  - **Client:** "I guess I could try cutting back in the evening when I smoke the most."
  - **Instead of:** "That sounds like a reasonable first step. Do you think you can set a specific goal?"
  - **Use:** "So you've identified the evening as a time when you smoke most, and you're thinking about cutting back then. What appeals to you about starting there? What do you think you could do instead in the evening?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, identify every instance of change talk the client expresses. For each instance, write down: (1) what the client said, (2) what you said in response, (3) whether your response deepened or weakened the change talk. Aim for 100% deepening responses.

---

### 5. **Master Double-Sided Reflections to Soften Sustain Talk**
- **Action:** When the client expresses sustain talk (reasons against change, concerns about readiness), use double-sided reflections that validate both the client's concern and their motivation for change, then redirect toward change talk.
- **Example:**
  - **Client:** "I know smoking is bad for me, but I'm not ready to quit right now. It helps me deal with stress."
  - **Instead of:** "You need to realize that smoking is dangerous. Why haven't you tried quitting already?"
  - **Use:** "So on one hand, you recognize that smoking isn't good for your health, and on the other hand, you're not sure you're ready to make a change right now because smoking helps you manage stress. That's a really important point—stress management. If you were to quit, what do you think you'd do instead to handle stress?"
- **Practice:** In your next three sessions, when the client expresses sustain talk, pause and construct a double-sided reflection before responding. Record yourself and listen for whether your reflection validates both sides and redirects toward change talk.

---

### 6. **Explore the Function of the Behavior Before Proposing Solutions**
- **Action:** Before suggesting interventions (patches, gum, etc.), deeply explore why the client engages in the behavior, what needs it meets, and what barriers exist to change. This understanding informs more effective, personalized solutions.
- **Example:**
  - **Client:** "Smoking helps me deal with stress."
  - **Instead of:** "You should try nicotine patches or gum."
  - **Use:** "Smoking sounds like an important way you manage stress. Tell me more about that. What kinds of situations make you want to smoke? What happens when you smoke—how does it help? If you were to quit, what do you think would be the hardest part about managing stress differently?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, spend at least 10 minutes exploring the function of the target behavior before suggesting any interventions. Ask at least five open-ended questions about why the client engages in the behavior and what needs it meets.

---

### 7. **Establish Genuine Partnership by Positioning the Client as Expert**
- **Action:** Consistently communicate that the client is the expert on their own experience, values, and what will work for them. Invite the client to co-create the treatment plan rather than prescribing it.
- **Example:**
  - **Instead of:** "Here's what you need to do: start patches this week, cut down by one cigarette per day, and call me in two weeks."
  - **Use:** "You know yourself better than anyone. Based on what you've told me about your smoking and what helps you manage stress, what do you think might be a good first step? What feels doable to you? What would make it more likely that you'd stick with it?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, use the phrase "You know yourself best" or "What do you think would work for you?" at least five times. Record yourself and listen for how often you position yourself as expert vs. the client.

---

### 8. **Develop Consistency in MI Practice Throughout the Session**
- **Action:** Recognize that MI is not a technique to be applied selectively but a consistent stance and spirit that should permeate the entire session. Practice maintaining collaborative, autonomy-supportive, evocative language from opening to closing.
- **Example:**
  - **Opening:** "What would you like to focus on today?"
  - **Throughout:** Open-ended questions, reflections, affirmations, summaries that invite client input
  - **Closing:** "What feels like the most important thing you're taking away from today? What's your next step, if any?"
- **Practice:** Record an entire session and listen for consistency. Identify moments where you shift out of MI stance (confrontation, prescription, expert positioning). Analyze what triggered the shift and practice alternative responses.

---

### 9. **Implement Structured MI Training and Supervision**
- **Action:** Engage in formal MI training (e.g., MINT-certified training) and regular clinical supervision focused on MI fidelity. Use the Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity (MITI) scale to assess your own sessions and receive feedback.
- **Example:**
  - Attend a 2-day MI training workshop
  - Record sessions and have them coded using MITI
  - Participate in peer consultation groups focused on MI practice
  - Review recordings with a supervisor and identify specific areas for improvement
- **Practice:** Schedule MI training within the next 3 months. Commit to recording and reviewing at least one session per week with a focus on MI fidelity.

---

### 10. **Recognize and Manage Your Own Reactions to Client Ambivalence**
- **Action:** Develop awareness of your own frustration, impatience, or judgment when clients express ambivalence or resistance. These reactions often trigger confrontational responses. Practice self-compassion and curiosity instead.
- **Example:**
  - **Internal reaction:** "This patient keeps making excuses. I need to push harder."
  - **Reframe:** "This patient is expressing ambivalence, which is normal. My job is to understand their perspective and help them explore their own motivations, not to convince them."
- **Practice:** After each session, reflect on moments where you felt frustrated or impatient with the client. Write down what triggered the reaction and what you could have done differently. Discuss these moments with a supervisor or peer.
