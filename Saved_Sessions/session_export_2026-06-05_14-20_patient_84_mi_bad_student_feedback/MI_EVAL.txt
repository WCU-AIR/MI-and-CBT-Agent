### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Clinician demonstrates inconsistent acceptance throughout the session. Early exchanges (1-4) show significant deficiency with judgmental language ("You need to stop making excuses," "Waiting for the 'right time' is just an excuse"), communicating disapproval of patient's ambivalence rather than unconditional positive regard. Beginning in exchange 5, acceptance improves markedly with validation ("It sounds like you're really weighing the decision carefully, which is important"). ### Empathy

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clinician maintains strategic focus on smoking cessation throughout but executes direction inconsistently. Early exchanges (1-4) maintain focus through confrontation and prescription rather than collaborative exploration, violating MI principles. Direction is imposed rather than negotiated ("You really should cut down immediately," "You have to quit now"). Later exchanges (5 onward) demonstrate improved strategic focus with collaborative inquiry ("Can you tell me about your smoking habits? When do you usually smoke?"). However, subtle directiveness persists even in later exchanges ("How about we start by identifying one small change...?"). Overall, clinician demonstrates understanding of target behavior but inconsistent skill in maintaining direction through collaborative rather than authoritarian means.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Clinician demonstrates severely deficient collaboration in early exchanges (1-4) with unilateral agenda-setting ("We're going to talk about your smoking and how you really need to quit"), expert-driven language ("You have to realize," "You must just commit"), and authoritarian stance ("Are you going to commit to using patches this week or not?"). Patient's repeated expressions of ambivalence are met with counter-arguments rather than collaborative problem-solving. Significant improvement begins in exchange 5 with explicit partnership language ("What do you think would help...? We could address together"). Exchanges 5 onward show genuine collaboration with shared decision-making and joint exploration. However, the abrupt shift suggests clinician recognized failure of confrontational approach rather than implementing MI skillfully from onset. Early damage to therapeutic alliance is substantial.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Clinician demonstrates severe and persistent violation of autonomy support, representing the most significant deficiency in the session. Early exchanges (1-4) contain repeated prescriptive language ("need to," "must," "have to") that frames change as non-negotiable requirement rather than client choice. Patient's autonomy-related concerns ("I'm not sure I'm ready," "pushing me to decide right this second just makes me feel pressured") are explicitly dismissed ("This isn't about feeling ready—it's about doing what's necessary"). Clinician presents binary choices as ultimatums rather than options. Partial improvement in exchanges 5 onward with collaborative framing ("Would you be interested in...?") and explicit affirmations ("This is your journey"). However, pressure language reappears in exchange 7 ("Remember, you have to be serious about this to see results," "are you ready to commit...starting today?"). Inconsistent autonomy support throughout session represents fundamental MI violation.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Clinician demonstrates minimal evocation in early exchanges (1-4), asking factual questions but immediately providing reasons for change rather than drawing out patient's own motivations. Clinician tells patient how they will feel ("You'll feel so much better once you stop") and provides health risks rather than evoking patient's values. Developing evocation in exchanges 5 onward with open-ended questions ("What do you think would help you feel more confident?" "What kind of support would work best for you?"). However, critical deficiency persists: when patient expresses change talk ("I'll do my best to stick to that goal"), clinician responds with pressure ("Remember, you have to be serious") rather than amplifying motivation. Patient's identification of stress as trigger is not explored for alternative coping strategies. Evocation remains underdeveloped with insufficient strategic amplification of emerging change talk.

### Partnership

Clinician positions themselves as expert on what patient "has to realize" and judges patient's reasoning ("Why haven't you tried quitting already?"). ### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Clinician demonstrates minimal change talk cultivation in early exchanges (1-4) with confrontational responses that suppress rather than encourage patient's own language favoring change. When patient expresses ambivalence, clinician responds with counter-arguments rather than exploring change talk. Developing skill in exchanges 5 onward with open-ended questions designed to evoke change talk ("What do you think would help you feel more confident?"). However, critical deficiency persists throughout: insufficient amplification and reinforcement of emerging change talk. When patient states "I'll do my best to stick to that goal" (strong commitment language), clinician responds with pressure rather than deepening motivation. When patient identifies stress as trigger (important insight), clinician does not explore alternative coping strategies or ability to manage stress differently. Reactive rather than proactive cultivation of change talk represents significant missed opportunity to strengthen patient's intrinsic motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clinician demonstrates poor sustain talk handling in early exchanges (1-4) by directly confronting patient's arguments against change ("You need to stop making excuses," "Waiting for the 'right time' is just an excuse to keep smoking"). Direct confrontation of sustain talk is contraindicated in MI and typically strengthens resistance. Significant improvement in exchanges 5 onward with validation and redirection. When patient expresses concern about readiness, clinician responds with double-sided reflection ("It sounds like you're weighing the decision carefully, which is important") and redirects toward change talk ("What do you think would help you feel more confident?"). Clinician validates sustain talk about overwhelm and explores support needs rather than confronting. However, approach remains somewhat reactive rather than proactive—clinician waits for patient to express sustain talk before softening it, rather than preventing sustain talk through skillful evocation of change talk from session onset. Delayed implementation and reactive nature represent moderate deficiency.

## **OVERALL SESSION ASSESSMENT**

### **Global MI Competence Score: 2.4/5 (Below Average)**

---

### **Summary of Key Findings:**

**Critical Bifurcation in Session Quality:**
This session demonstrates a stark division between severely deficient MI practice in exchanges 1-4 and significantly improved MI-consistent practice in exchanges 5 onward. The early exchanges violate fundamental MI principles through confrontation, prescription, dismissal of autonomy, and judgment of ambivalence. These deficiencies likely **damage the therapeutic alliance** and **suppress intrinsic motivation**, which are counterproductive to MI goals. The patient's explicit statements of feeling "pressured," "overwhelmed," and "shut down" indicate the early approach is ineffective.

**Mid-Session Course Correction:**
Beginning in exchange 5, the clinician demonstrates a marked shift toward MI-consistent practice with collaborative language, validation, exploration of barriers, and autonomy support. This suggests the clinician **recognized the failure** of the confrontational approach and attempted repair. By the end of the session, the patient is more engaged and willing to explore smoking patterns, indicating that **skillful MI practice can recover from early deficiencies**.

**Inconsistency and Reactivity:**
The clinician's MI competence is **inconsistent and reactive rather than proactive**. The clinician demonstrates knowledge of MI principles (evidenced by improved later exchanges) but fails to implement them consistently from session onset. Pressure language reappears even in later exchanges ("Remember, you have to be serious about this to see results"), suggesting incomplete internalization of autonomy-supportive principles.

---

### **Strengths:**

- **Late-session course correction:** Clinician recognizes ineffective approach and pivots to collaborative stance
- **Improved reflective listening:** Later exchanges include accurate reflections demonstrating understanding
- **Explicit autonomy affirmations:** Exchanges 5-9 include "this is your journey" and collaborative framing
- **Patient resilience:** Despite early deficiencies, patient remains engaged and expresses willingness to work toward change
- **Collaborative problem-solving:** Later exchanges demonstrate genuine partnership and shared decision-making

---

### **Critical Areas for Improvement:**

1. **Eliminate confrontational language** from session onset (highest priority)
2. **Implement consistent autonomy support** throughout entire session
3. **Develop skillful evocation** through strategic questioning rather than clinician-provided reasons
4. **Amplify emerging change talk** rather than allowing it to pass unnoticed
5. **Establish genuine partnership** by positioning client as expert from opening
6. **Explore behavior function** before proposing solutions
7. **Master double-sided reflections** to soften sustain talk without confrontation
8. **Maintain MI consistency** throughout session rather than reactive course correction
9. **Engage in formal MI training** and regular supervision with MITI coding
10. **Develop awareness** of personal reactions to ambivalence that trigger confrontation

---

### **Impact on Client Engagement:**

The session demonstrates a **critical therapeutic rupture followed by partial repair**. Early exchanges likely suppress intrinsic motivation through confrontation and pressure. The clinician's course correction in exchange 5 begins to repair the alliance and evoke patient's own motivation. By exchange 9, patient is more engaged and willing to explore triggers. This suggests **skillful MI practice can recover from early deficiencies**, but the damage to the alliance and initial resistance represent significant cost.

---

### **Recommendation:**

This clinician demonstrates **knowledge of MI principles but inconsistent implementation**. With commitment to formal MI training, regular supervision with session recording and MITI coding, and deliberate practice of the recommendations above, this clinician has potential to become an effective motivational interviewer. However, current practice falls substantially short of MI competence and requires immediate, sustained improvement in consistency, proactivity, and skillfulness.
