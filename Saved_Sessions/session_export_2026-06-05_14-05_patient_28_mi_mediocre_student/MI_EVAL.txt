### Acceptance

**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal unconditional positive regard and fails to establish genuine warmth or nonjudgmental stance. While cordial opening is present, clinician quickly pivots to problem-focused questioning without establishing emotional safety. ### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2

Basic reflection offered ("So, you're worried about how your friends might react, but you also feel lonely") is factual rather than emotionally attuned. Clinician misses deeper emotional content: fear of being seen as "weak," desire to be accepted for who she is (not her diagnosis), and profound loneliness of carrying this secret. ### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains clear focus on target behavior—disclosure of diabetes to peers—which is appropriate and necessary. Session does not wander aimlessly; coherent thread exists from exploring ambivalence to developing action plan (talking to Emily after school). However, direction becomes increasingly narrow and prescriptive rather than collaboratively refined. Clinician moves quickly from exploration to solution-building without fully understanding Daniela's perspective or allowing her to articulate her own goals. By third response, psychiatrist already suggesting specific language and recommending particular friend before patient has fully explored her own thinking. Direction lacks strategic depth; clinician assumes goal is disclosure to Emily by specific time without exploring whether Daniela's actual goal might be different (feeling less isolated, managing diabetes more effectively, reducing anxiety). Clinician never asks "What would be most helpful for you right now?" or "What does success look like to you?"

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Significant deficiency. Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal genuine collaboration and instead adopts expert-driven, directive stance throughout. True collaboration means clinician and client are equal partners; here, clinician consistently uses prescriptive language: "Maybe you should think about starting small," "Maybe you should try to prepare what you want to say first," "It would probably help if you picked one friend." This is not collaborative exploration—it is clinician telling patient what to do. Most egregiously, when Daniela asks for help, psychiatrist immediately provides scripted response ("Sure, you might want to say something like, 'Hey, I've been dealing with something health-wise...'") rather than co-constructing language with her. When patient practices script, clinician evaluates it and suggests modifications, maintaining expert role. Final response exemplifies lack of collaboration: "Your next steps could be practicing the conversation a bit more and then choosing a good time to talk to Emily" tells patient what her next steps are, then seeks agreement rather than genuinely collaborating. No moment where clinician asks "What do you think would be most helpful?" or "How would you like to approach this?" Power dynamic is entirely clinician-directed.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively undermines autonomy support throughout session. Rather than emphasizing Daniela's right to choose and her ownership of change process, clinician consistently implies disclosure is expected outcome and patient should follow clinician's recommendations. Key autonomy-undermining language includes: "Maybe you should think about starting small," "It would probably help if you picked one friend," "Maybe you should try to prepare what you want to say first, so it feels less scary," "Do you think you could try saying something like that?" (framed as question but functionally directive), "Your next steps could be practicing the conversation a bit more and then choosing a good time to talk to Emily." When Daniela states "I'm not totally ready to do it yet," psychiatrist responds with "Keep up the good work," which subtly pressures toward action rather than respecting current readiness level. Clinician never explicitly affirms Daniela's right to choose whether, when, or how to disclose. No language like "This is entirely your decision," "You know yourself and your friends best," or "Whatever you decide, I support you." Implicit message is: "Here's what you should do, and here's how to do it."

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources—is largely absent. Psychiatrist asks some open-ended questions early on ("What's been the hardest part about sharing this with your friends?") but quickly pivots to closed questions and advice-giving rather than continuing to evoke Daniela's own thinking. Scaling questions used ("On a scale from 1 to 10, how important do you think it is...?") but not followed by exploration of numbers; clinician does not explore why patient rated importance at 6/10 or confidence at 3/10. These are rich opportunities to evoke her own reasons for change and ideas about increasing confidence, but psychiatrist immediately responds with prescriptive suggestions. Clinician never asks Daniela to articulate her own reasons for disclosure, her own values around honesty and friendship, or her own ideas about approach. When Daniela shows change talk—"Maybe I should figure out how to explain it better" and "Maybe I could say it without making it awkward"—clinician does not amplify or reinforce this language. Missed opportunity to ask "What makes you think explaining it better could help?" or "Tell me more about what would make it feel less awkward." Psychiatrist fails to evoke Daniela's own resources and strengths; she mentions friends are "really receptive" and she's good at storytelling—assets that could be built upon—but clinician treats her as problem to be solved rather than resourceful person with existing strengths.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership—understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within client—is fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role and maintains it throughout, with patient in passive, recipient position. Clinician positions herself as authority on what Daniela should do, how she should say it, when she should do it, and what to expect. Psychiatrist provides scripted language, suggests specific friends, recommends timing, and outlines next steps. This is expert-driven intervention, not partnership. Key indicators of absent partnership: clinician provides specific script rather than asking Daniela to generate her own language; clinician suggests Emily as friend to approach without exploring Daniela's own assessment of her friendships; clinician recommends "after school one day when we're just hanging out" without asking when patient thinks would be best; clinician tells Daniela to "think about how you want to respond if Emily has questions" rather than exploring this with her; clinician concludes with "your next steps could be..." rather than asking "what do you think your next steps should be?" In true partnership, clinician would ask: "You know your friends better than I do—who do you think would be most understanding?" or "What feels like the right time and place to you?" or "What do you think Emily might ask, and how would you want to respond?" Final statement—"Taking this step could really improve how you feel about your diabetes and your friendships"—is clinician making claim about outcomes rather than exploring with Daniela what she hopes might happen.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal skill in evoking, recognizing, and reinforcing change talk. While Daniela produces some change talk throughout session, clinician largely fails to amplify or strengthen it. Patient's change talk includes: "Maybe I should figure out how to explain it better," "Maybe it would make things better because they'd understand me more," "I think I might feel better if I practice it here first," "That felt kind of weird but also a little okay," "I think that makes sense," "I'm getting closer." In response to these statements, psychiatrist does not use strategic techniques to amplify change talk. When Daniela says "Maybe I should figure out how to explain it better," clinician does not ask "What makes you think that?" or "Tell me more about that" or "How would explaining it better help?" Instead, clinician moves to asking closed questions about problems and barriers. When patient practices script and says "That felt kind of weird but also a little okay," clinician responds with "Yeah, that's a good start. Maybe you should add something like..." This is evaluative and directive rather than evoking more change talk. More skillful response would be: "It felt a little okay—what made it feel okay?" or "What would make it feel even more comfortable?" Psychiatrist does not use open-ended questions to draw out Daniela's own reasons for change. Questions like "What would be good about telling Emily?" or "How might your life be different if you shared this?" or "What do you value about honesty in friendships?" would evoke deeper change talk. When Daniela says "I'm getting closer," this is significant change talk indicating movement in readiness; clinician's response is to outline next steps rather than exploring what "getting closer" means and what's contributing to that shift.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates inconsistent skill in handling Daniela's sustain talk (reasons against change, arguments for status quo). There are moments of appropriate redirection but also missed opportunities and some inadvertent reinforcement. Patient's sustain talk includes: "That one I haven't shared yet," "I don't want to make things weird or seem different," "I'm scared they'll treat me differently or think I'm weak," "I keep it to myself because that feels safer," "I'm still really worried it could make things worse," "I don't feel very sure that I can actually do it without things getting weird or uncomfortable," "It's just hard to take that step when I'm not sure what will happen afterward." Psychiatrist does attempt to redirect some sustain talk; when Daniela expresses fear of judgment, clinician asks "What's stopping you the most from opening up? Is it fear of judgment or something else?" This is attempt to explore and potentially reframe barrier. However, clinician does not use sophisticated techniques like double-sided reflection or strategic reframing. More skillful approach would involve reflecting sustain talk first to show understanding, then gently exploring other side. Psychiatrist tends to move quickly past sustain talk to problem-solving. When Daniela says "I'm not totally ready to do it yet," clinician responds with "Keep up the good work," which does not address sustain talk or explore what readiness would look like. One positive moment: psychiatrist does suggest preparing and practicing, which can reduce anxiety driving sustain talk. However, clinician does not explore deeper fears or help Daniela develop confidence that might naturally reduce sustain talk. Questions like "What would need to happen for you to feel more ready?" or "What would give you more confidence that your friends would respond well?" would be more skillful.

## Summary of Feedback

### **Strengths:**
- Maintains clear focus on target behavior (disclosure to peers)
- Asks some open-ended questions early in session
- Attempts to use scaling questions to assess importance and confidence
- Suggests behavioral rehearsal, which can reduce anxiety
- Maintains respectful, non-hostile tone
- Offers practical suggestions for timing and approach

### **Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- **Fundamental MI spirit violation:** Session is expert-driven and prescriptive rather than collaborative and evocative. Psychiatrist tells Daniela what to do rather than drawing out her own motivations and ideas.
- **Minimal acceptance and empathy:** Clinician fails to validate Daniela's fears, normalize her struggle, or demonstrate genuine warmth and understanding of her emotional experience.
- **Undermines autonomy:** Psychiatrist prescribes solutions and implicitly pressures Daniela toward action rather than supporting her right to choose and her ownership of process.
- **Weak evocation:** Clinician does not draw out Daniela's own reasons for change, her values, or her existing strengths and resources.
- **No genuine partnership:** Clinician assumes expert role and maintains it throughout, positioning Daniela as passive recipient rather than as expert on her own life.
- **Insufficient change talk cultivation:** While Daniela produces some change talk, clinician does not strategically amplify or reinforce it.
- **Superficial handling of ambivalence:** Psychiatrist does not deeply explore Daniela's competing motivations or help her resolve her ambivalence through her own reflection.

### **Impact on Client Engagement and Change:**
While Daniela remains engaged and cooperative, this engagement may be more compliance than genuine motivation. Her confidence remains low (3/10), and she explicitly states "I'm not totally ready to do it yet." Psychiatrist's response does not address this readiness gap or explore what would help her feel more ready. Session provides short-term solution but does not build deeper self-efficacy and intrinsic motivation that MI aims to cultivate.
