### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Profound absence of unconditional positive regard. Psychiatrist repeatedly labels patient responses as "excuses" ("You really have to stop making excuses," "You're just making excuses," "There's no excuse not to reduce"). Ambivalence is pathologized as stubbornness rather than normalized as universal human experience. No affirmation of patient strengths, no recognition of autonomy. Disapproving language throughout ("You have to take this seriously," "You have to stop being so stubborn," "Enough hesitation"). Final statement explicitly abandons acceptance: "I'm setting this plan for you, and you have to follow it." Patient's direct feedback about ineffectiveness is ignored.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal reflective listening and no genuine effort to understand patient's internal frame of reference. One factual reflection early on ("Okay, so you're drinking several times a week") immediately followed by judgment. Patient explicitly articulates perspective multiple times ("It's part of hanging out with my friends," "Drinking with friends is how I relax and be social," "It feels like it's being decided for me"), but psychiatrist never reflects these statements with curiosity or understanding. Instead, explanations are dismissed as "excuses." No complex reflections capturing emotional experience, social identity, or ambivalence. Patient's emerging concern ("sounds like a lot") is not explored or amplified.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Maintains focus on target behavior (alcohol use) with no topic drift, demonstrating some strategic direction. However, direction is imposed rather than collaborative. Predetermined goal (complete abstinence, then reduction to once weekly) pursued through repeated yes/no questions and directives regardless of patient responses. Linear and rigid approach: "Will you stop completely? No? Then will you cut to once weekly? No? Then I'm setting this plan for you." No exploration of patient's own goals, values, or readiness. No inquiry into what might make change feel feasible or meaningful. Direction entirely clinician-driven, not co-constructed with patient.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Explicitly hierarchical, expert-driven stance from opening statement: "I'm here to help you get on the right track" and "we're going to focus on what you need to do to improve." Questions are directives disguised as questions ("Will you stop drinking completely starting today? Yes or no?"), not collaborative inquiries. When patient expresses hesitation, psychiatrist responds with "You have to just do it" and "You're just making excuses" rather than "Let's think through this together." When patient provides direct feedback—"It feels like it's being decided for me, and that just makes me less sure I'll stick with it"—psychiatrist does not pause, reflect, or adjust. Instead repeats same directive approach. Zero joint problem-solving or co-construction of change plan.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Systematically undermines autonomy at every turn through language minimizing choice: "you need to," "you have to," "you must," "no excuses," "non-negotiable," "I'm setting this plan for you, and you have to follow it." Patient explicitly asserts autonomy multiple times ("I don't know if I can just stop completely," "I'm not ready to quit completely today," "I'm not ready to promise once a week or less," "I'm still not ready to say yes to that yet"). Each assertion is overridden with pressure. Patient's direct statement that lack of autonomy support is counterproductive—"It feels like it's being decided for me, and that just makes me less sure I'll stick with it"—is completely ignored. No communication that patient has right to choose or that change is patient's decision to make.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Core of MI is absent. No open-ended questions designed to elicit patient's own thinking about change. All questions are closed yes/no questions designed to secure compliance, not evoke motivation: "Are you currently smoking? Yes or no?" "Will you stop drinking completely starting today? Yes or no?" "Will you reduce your drinking to once a week or less? Yes or no?" No inquiry into patient's reasons for drinking, concerns about health, ideas about what might work, or personal values. When patient offers change talk—"I guess, it sounds like a lot when you say it, though"—psychiatrist does not amplify or reinforce it. Instead immediately pivots to judgment and pressure. Missed opportunity to evoke and strengthen emerging concern about drinking frequency.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Explicitly rejects partnership and asserts clinician expertise and authority throughout. Opening statement positions psychiatrist as expert who knows what is "right" and what patient "needs to do." Unilateral assumption of expert role: "That's definitely something you need to cut back on immediately," "You need to have more willpower and discipline," "Drinking frequently will ruin your progress and your life," "I'm setting this plan for you, and you have to follow it." Patient's contributions are not valued or incorporated. When patient says "It's kinda part of hanging out with my friends," psychiatrist dismisses as excuse rather than saying "Let's think about how we can work with that." When patient provides feedback about ineffectiveness, psychiatrist does not incorporate this wisdom but repeats same approach. No co-creation of change plan.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal change talk from patient, and psychiatrist does nothing to evoke or amplify it. Patient's only statement approximating change talk is "I guess, it sounds like a lot when you say it, though"—mild expression of concern about drinking frequency and seed of motivation for change. Critical MI moment: psychiatrist should respond with curiosity and amplification. Instead, psychiatrist acknowledges change talk but immediately pivots to judgment and pressure ("That's definitely something you need to cut back on immediately"), which suppresses further change talk. Patient does not elaborate on concern; becomes defensive and resistant instead. Throughout remainder of session, patient expresses sustain talk and ambivalence but no additional change talk emerges. Psychiatrist's directive, pressuring approach activates psychological reactance rather than evoking intrinsic motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Directly confronts patient's sustain talk by labeling it as "excuses" rather than skillfully softening it. Patient expresses sustain talk throughout: "It's kinda part of hanging out with my friends," "It's mostly with friends, and that's part of my routine," "Drinking with friends is kind of how I relax and be social," "It's not like I'm drinking all day, every day." In MI, sustain talk should be acknowledged and gently reframed without confrontation. Instead, psychiatrist confronts directly ("You're just making excuses"), which activates defensive responding and strengthens sustain talk. Patient becomes more entrenched: "It's not like I'm drinking all day, every day" and "It's not really that simple for me." By final exchanges, patient explicitly defending current behavior and resisting directives: "I'm not ready to say yes to once a week starting today." Confrontational approach has intensified sustain talk rather than softened it. No attempt to acknowledge underlying values (friendship, relaxation, normalcy) or gently reframe.

## Overall Session Summary

### **Overall MI Competency Score: 1.0/5**

**Session Strengths:**
- Maintains consistent focus on target behavior (alcohol use) with no topic drift
- Clearly communicates genuine concern about patient's health
- Demonstrates persistence in addressing the issue despite repeated patient refusals

**Critical Deficiencies:**

This session represents a **fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles and spirit**. Rather than discrete areas for improvement, this session requires comprehensive reorientation to the MI approach.

**Pervasive violations across all nine MI domains:**
- Acceptance replaced with judgment and disapproval
- Empathy replaced with dismissal of patient perspective
- Collaboration replaced with hierarchical authority
- Autonomy support replaced with coercion and directive language
- Evocation replaced with prescription and closed yes/no questions
- Partnership replaced with unilateral expert authority
- Change talk not cultivated or amplified
- Sustain talk confronted rather than softened

**Specific Harmful Patterns:**

1. **Repeated yes/no questions designed to secure compliance** rather than explore ambivalence or invite genuine dialogue
2. **Judgmental labeling** of patient responses as "excuses" and "stubbornness," communicating disrespect for patient's perspective
3. **Pressure and directive language** ("you have to," "you must," "non-negotiable") that activates psychological reactance
4. **Ignoring direct patient feedback** about ineffectiveness—when patient states "It feels like it's being decided for me, and that just makes me less sure I'll stick with it," psychiatrist does not pause or adjust but repeats same approach
5. **Failure to evoke or amplify change talk**—patient's one statement of emerging concern is not explored
6. **Confrontation of sustain talk** rather than softening, which intensifies resistance

**Impact on Patient Engagement and Motivation:**

By session end, patient is explicitly disengaged and resistant. Patient states: "I don't really feel like I'm on board with it yet" and "It just makes me want to hold on to what feels normal for me now." Psychiatrist's approach has backfired. Rather than evoking intrinsic motivation for change, the approach has activated defensive resistance and reduced likelihood of patient following through on any change plan. Patient's final statement—"It feels like it's being decided for me, and that just makes me less sure I'll stick with it"—is direct feedback that approach is counterproductive. This feedback is ignored.

**Current MI Competency Assessment:**

Psychiatrist is operating at **pre-MI level**. This is not MI with deficiencies; this is traditional directive counseling explicitly contrary to MI principles. Psychiatrist has not internalized MI spirit of partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation. **Significant retraining and practice required.**

**Likelihood of Evoking Patient Motivation for Change:**

**Very Low.** This session has actively reduced patient's motivation for change. If this approach continues, patient is unlikely to make changes to drinking and likely to disengage from treatment entirely. An MI-consistent approach would have explored ambivalence, amplified emerging concern, and supported autonomy, thereby increasing likelihood of genuine motivation aligned with patient's own values and goals.

## Key Recommendations for Improvement

**Immediate Priority Actions:**

1. **Shift from expert-driven to client-centered approach** — Adopt genuine collaborative stance; position patient as expert on their own life
2. **Replace judgment with acceptance** — Eliminate judgmental language; communicate unconditional positive regard
3. **Develop genuine curiosity** — Ask open-ended questions designed to understand, not convince
4. **Respond to patient feedback** — Be responsive to patient's experience; adjust approach based on what patient tells you
5. **Enroll in formal MI training** — This session demonstrates fundamental misalignment with MI principles; formal training through Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers strongly recommended

**This session is not salvageable through minor adjustments. Comprehensive reorientation to MI philosophy and practice is required.**
