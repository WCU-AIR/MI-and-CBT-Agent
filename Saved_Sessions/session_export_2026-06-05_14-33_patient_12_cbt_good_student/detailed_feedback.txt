
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS)
This document outlines a structured framework for assessing therapist competence in Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT). 
It is used for training, supervision, and evaluation. Each item is scored on a 0–6 scale:

0 = Poor
2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Conduct Functional Analysis of Self-Injury (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Before the next session, systematically assess the function of Ashley's wrist biting through direct observation and caregiver interview. Identify antecedents (what triggers it), the behavior itself, and consequences (what happens after). Determine whether self-injury serves emotion regulation, sensory stimulation, escape/avoidance, or attention-seeking functions.
- **Example:** Ask Ashley's parents: "When Ashley bites her wrist, what happens right before? What does she seem to be feeling or needing? What happens after she bites her wrist—does she seem calmer, does she get attention, does she avoid something?" Document patterns across multiple incidents.
- **Practice:** Use a simple ABC (Antecedent-Behavior-Consequence) chart to organize observations. Review literature on self-injury in ASD and intellectual disability to understand common functions and evidence-based interventions.

### 2. **Develop Behavioral Intervention Plan for Self-Injury**
- **Action:** Based on the functional analysis, develop a comprehensive behavioral plan that includes: (1) environmental modifications to reduce triggers, (2) alternative coping behaviors that serve the same function as self-injury, (3) reinforcement of non-injurious responses, and (4) safety planning if self-injury occurs.
- **Example:** If self-injury serves emotion regulation, teach Ashley alternative sensory/regulatory strategies: deep pressure (weighted blanket, squeezing a stress ball), rhythmic movement (rocking, swinging), or use of comfort objects (license plate cards). If it serves escape/avoidance, use gradual exposure with coping strategies rather than allowing avoidance.
- **Practice:** Collaborate with Ashley's parents and school staff to identify which alternative behaviors are most feasible and reinforcing for Ashley. Practice implementing the plan across multiple settings (home, school, community).

### 3. **Prioritize Environmental and Behavioral Interventions Over Cognitive Techniques**
- **Action:** Shift the treatment strategy to emphasize environmental modification, behavioral skills, and sensory regulation rather than cognitive restructuring. For Ashley's developmental level, concrete, behavioral interventions are more likely to be effective.
- **Example:** Instead of asking Ashley to challenge her thought about the bus, help her prepare for the bus delay through: (1) advance notice ("The bus might be late today"), (2) a structured waiting routine (e.g., look at license plates, sit in a quiet area), (3) a sensory regulation strategy (e.g., deep breathing, pressure), and (4) a reward for managing the wait without self-injury.
- **Practice:** Review CBT literature on intellectual disability (e.g., Sturmey, 2004; Vereenooghe & Langdon, 2013) to understand evidence-based adaptations. Attend training on behavioral intervention for self-injury in ASD.

### 4. **Involve Caregivers and School Staff as Treatment Partners**
- **Action:** Schedule a meeting with Ashley's parents and school staff to: (1) share the functional analysis findings, (2) teach them the behavioral intervention plan, (3) establish consistency across settings, and (4) identify barriers to implementation.

If she uses the stress ball instead of biting, give her a preferred reward (e.g., 5 minutes with license plates)."
- **Practice:** Conduct in-vivo coaching with parents and teachers to ensure they can implement the plan correctly. Establish a communication system (e.g., daily log, weekly check-in) to monitor progress and troubleshoot barriers.

- **Action:** Create visual supports (pictures, symbols, written instructions) to help Ashley remember and implement coping strategies. Leverage her documented strength in visual memory and rote learning.
