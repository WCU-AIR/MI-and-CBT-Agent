
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **Conduct a comprehensive diagnostic assessment to clarify the underlying condition**
- **Action**: In the next session, systematically assess the timeline, pattern, and nature of cognitive and functional decline. Use a structured cognitive screening tool (e.g., Montreal Cognitive Assessment, Mini-Cog). Explicitly explore whether the patient experiences cognitive symptoms as thoughts he can challenge (depression) or as a loss of capacity (neurocognitive disorder).
- **Example**: "I want to understand more about the changes you've noticed. When did you first realize your memory was different? Has it gotten worse over time? Can you give me a specific example of something you forgot recently? How does it feel when that happens—is it something you think about and worry about, or does it feel like your brain just isn't working the way it used to?"
- **Practice**: Review the diagnostic criteria for major neurocognitive disorder and depression. Practice distinguishing between depressive rumination ("I'm worthless") and cognitive decline ("I can't remember things"). Consult with a neuropsychologist or geriatric psychiatrist if uncertain.

### 2. **Establish a clear, collaborative agenda and treatment plan aligned with the actual diagnosis**
- **Action**: At the start of the next session, explicitly state your understanding of the patient's condition and propose a treatment plan. For example: "Based on what you've told me and the evaluation you had, it seems like you may be experiencing some cognitive changes along with mood changes. I'd like to focus on: (1) understanding these changes better, (2) helping you and your wife adapt to them, and (3) finding ways to maintain your quality of life. Does that sound right to you? What would you most like to focus on?"
- **Example**: "Today I'd like to talk about three things: first, the changes in your memory and thinking that you've noticed; second, how these changes have affected your daily life; and third, what support you and your wife might need. We have about 45 minutes. Does that work for you?"
- **Practice**: Practice writing out a clear treatment plan that specifies: (1) the diagnosis or working hypothesis, (2) the maintaining factors, (3) the treatment goals, (4) the specific interventions, and (5) the timeline. Share this plan with the patient and obtain agreement.

### 3. **Eliminate excessive self-disclosure and refocus on the patient**
- **Action**: In future sessions, minimize personal anecdotes. When tempted to share a personal experience, ask yourself: "Does this help the patient understand their situation better, or does it shift focus to me?" If the latter, don't share it. Instead, use the time to explore the patient's experience more deeply.
- **Example**: Instead of "When I was feeling down, I used to remind myself that every day is a new chance," say: "I'm curious about what happens when you try to think positive. You mentioned earlier that your mind keeps going back to worrying. Tell me more about that. What are you worrying about?"
- **Practice**: Record yourself in role-plays or actual sessions. Count the number of times you use "I" or share personal information. Set a goal to reduce this by 50%. Ask a supervisor to provide feedback on self-disclosure.

### 4. **Implement systematic guided discovery and Socratic questioning**
- **Action**: Replace advice-giving with skillful questioning. When tempted to tell the patient what to do, instead ask: "What do you think might help?" "What have you tried before?" "What would happen if you...?" "How could we test that idea?"
- **Example**: Instead of "Maybe you could try not to be so hard on yourself," ask: "You mentioned worrying about forgetting things and messing up simple tasks. When that happens, what do you tell yourself? What thoughts go through your head? Where do you think those thoughts come from?"
- **Practice**: Practice Socratic questioning in role-plays. Use the "downward arrow" technique to explore core beliefs. Ask open-ended questions and resist the urge to provide answers. Record yourself and review for quality of questioning.

### 5. **Conduct a thorough behavioral and cognitive assessment**
- **Action**: Systematically identify: (a) specific automatic thoughts and core beliefs, (b) behavioral patterns (avoidance, inactivity, withdrawal), (c) maintaining factors, (d) the patient's strengths and resources, and (e) barriers to change. Use this information to develop a targeted treatment plan.
- **Example**: "You mentioned that you've stopped playing golf and cards. What was it about those activities that you enjoyed? When did you stop? What happened that made you decide not to do them anymore? What would it take to go back to them?"
- **Practice**: Develop a comprehensive case formulation for this patient that specifies: (1) the presenting problem, (2) the precipitating factors, (3) the maintaining factors, (4) the patient's strengths, and (5) the treatment targets. Share this formulation with a supervisor for feedback.

### 6. **Involve the patient's wife as a collaborative partner in treatment**
- **Action**: Invite the wife to a session or at least provide her with psychoeducation about the patient's condition and strategies for support. Discuss how she can help with behavioral activation, memory support, and emotional support. Assess her own needs and stress level.
- **Example**: "Your wife has been very supportive. I'd like to involve her in our work together. Would it be okay if she came to our next session? I'd like to talk with both of you about what's been happening and how we can work together to help you feel better."
- **Practice**: Develop a caregiver psychoeducation handout that explains the patient's condition, common challenges, and evidence-based strategies for support. Practice delivering this information in a compassionate, non-alarmist way.

### 7. **Develop a structured behavioral activation plan adapted to the patient's cognitive capacity**
- **Action**: Rather than vague suggestions to "take walks" or "spend time with your wife," develop a specific, written plan. Identify activities the patient values, break them into manageable steps, schedule them concretely, and plan for barriers. For a patient with cognitive deficits, provide written reminders and involve the wife in implementation.
- **Example**: "Let's think about activities you used to enjoy. You mentioned golf and cards. What was it about those that you liked? Let's start smaller—maybe you could play cards with your wife for 20 minutes on Tuesday and Thursday evenings at 7 PM. We'll write this down and put it on the refrigerator. Your wife can remind you. How does that sound?"
- **Practice**: Develop a detailed behavioral activation plan for this patient that specifies: (1) the activities, (2) the frequency and duration, (3) the specific time and day, (4) the barriers and how to overcome them, and (5) how progress will be monitored.

### 8. **Provide psychoeducation about the patient's condition and realistic expectations**
- **Action**: Educate the patient about his condition (whether depression, neurocognitive disorder, or both) in clear, compassionate language. Explain what to expect, what interventions can and cannot do, and what the patient can do to maintain quality of life. Avoid false reassurance ("things will get better") but also avoid hopelessness.
- **Example**: "Based on what we've learned, it seems like you're experiencing some changes in your memory and thinking, along with mood changes. These changes have been gradual over a few years. It's important to know that while we can't reverse these changes, we can help you adapt to them and maintain your quality of life. We can also help your wife support you. Let's focus on what you can still do and what brings you joy."
- **Practice**: Develop a psychoeducation handout for this patient that explains his condition, the expected course, and evidence-based strategies for coping. Practice delivering this information in a way that is honest but not demoralizing.

### 9. **Implement systematic cognitive work if appropriate, or acceptance work if not**
- **Action**: Determine whether the patient's cognitions are amenable to cognitive restructuring (typical of depression) or whether acceptance and adaptation are more appropriate (typical of neurocognitive disorder). If the former, use structured thought records and evidence examination. If the latter, focus on acceptance, meaning-making, and adaptation.
- **Example**: If depression: "You mentioned worrying about forgetting things. Let's look at that thought. What evidence do you have that you're forgetting things? What evidence do you have against it? What's a more balanced way to think about it?" If neurocognitive disorder: "You're right that you're experiencing some memory changes. That's frustrating and scary. Let's talk about how you can adapt to these changes and still have a good quality of life. What matters most to you?"
- **Practice**: Learn to distinguish between thoughts that are amenable to cognitive restructuring and situations that require acceptance and adaptation. Practice both cognitive restructuring and acceptance-based interventions.
