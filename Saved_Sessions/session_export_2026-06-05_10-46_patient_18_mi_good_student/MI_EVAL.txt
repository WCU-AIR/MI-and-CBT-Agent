### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and validation throughout the session. Early affirmations of Anisa's courage ("It's really courageous to keep coming back despite the setbacks") and normalization of her ambivalence are evident. When Anisa expresses caution about nicotine replacement therapy, the clinician validates her concerns as reasonable. However, opportunities exist to more actively affirm Anisa's inherent worth independent of success or failure, and to more explicitly normalize her protective coping strategies (keeping things to herself) before exploring alternatives. The clinician maintains warmth and avoids judgmental language consistently, but could deepen acceptance through more explicit affirmation of her identity and value beyond smoking behavior.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** This is the psychiatrist's strongest domain. Sophisticated, accurate reflective listening with consistent emotional attunement is demonstrated throughout. Complex reflections capture interconnected emotional experiences: "I hear you describing this deep entanglement where smoking feels woven into your identity and your coping with stress." The clinician uses Anisa's own language ("leper in a non-leper colony," "mountain to climb," "cloud hanging over me") and reflects it back, demonstrating genuine listening. The reframe of her 3-4 confidence level ("there's also a spark of hope and willingness to try again") captures both ambivalence and resilience. Empathy is consistent, accurate, and emotionally attuned across all exchanges, capturing not just what Anisa says but what she feels and means.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains appropriate focus on smoking cessation and related motivations throughout. The opening question establishes direction collaboratively rather than imposing it. However, direction could be more strategic in several ways: after identifying the daughter as a key motivator, deeper exploration could occur before moving to confidence scaling; when Anisa expresses caution about nicotine replacement therapy, the psychiatrist provides psychoeducation but doesn't strategically guide her to narrow focus or commit to a specific approach; the final goal-setting is concrete but lacks specific timelines, measurable outcomes, or contingency plans if cravings become severe. The psychiatrist could be more directive in helping Anisa establish concrete, measurable change goals with specific implementation details.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong collaborative stance is demonstrated throughout most of the session. The opening question genuinely invites Anisa to set the agenda. Permission-seeking before offering psychoeducation ("If it's okay with you, I'd like to share some information...") respects autonomy and positions the psychiatrist as a partner. Frequent invitations for input ("How does that fit with what you've heard?") invite co-construction rather than prescription. However, the psychoeducation section is somewhat one-directional; the psychiatrist could have asked what Anisa already knows about quitting approaches before presenting information. When Anisa expresses concern about patches being "a crutch," the psychiatrist validates but doesn't deeply explore this concern collaboratively. The final goal-setting affirms Anisa's suggestion but could have invited her to articulate and refine her plan before affirming it.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist consistently respects and supports Anisa's autonomy. The opening question invites her to choose the focus. Permission-seeking before offering advice respects her right to choose. When Anisa expresses hesitation about support groups and counseling, the psychiatrist validates her concern without pushing: "that makes a lot of sense given how tough this has been." Goal-setting language prioritizes her agency: "what do you think might be a first step that feels doable and respectful of your feelings and strengths?" However, opportunities exist to more explicitly reinforce that the choice to quit—and how to quit—belongs entirely to her. When Anisa says "I don't know if I can," the psychiatrist could ask: "What would need to be true for you to feel ready to try?" to emphasize that the psychiatrist is a supporter, not a persuader. Additionally, the clinician could explicitly ask whether her commitment is intrinsic rather than compliance-based.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Solid evocation is demonstrated with consistent use of open questions to draw out Anisa's own motivations, values, and resources. Questions like "What do you think has made quitting feel like such a mountain?" and "what makes you want to keep trying?" invite Anisa to articulate her own understanding. Strategic questioning about strengths ("What strengths have you drawn on to keep trying?") evokes her resilience and self-efficacy. However, evocation could be deeper: after identifying her daughter as a key motivator, more probing questions ("What specifically about making your daughter proud motivates you?") would deepen intrinsic motivation. When Anisa mentions her "stubborn streak," the psychiatrist affirms it but doesn't explore how she might harness this strength specifically for quitting. The psychoeducation section is somewhat didactic; stronger evocation would first draw out Anisa's experiential knowledge ("What have you already tried? What worked, even a little?") before presenting expert information.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong partnership is demonstrated with consistent collaborative language and respect for Anisa's expertise. The clinician positions her as the expert on her own experience and explicitly invites her input. Language is inclusive: "It's okay with you," "what do you think," "how does that fit with you." The psychiatrist avoids expert positioning when Anisa expresses caution about nicotine replacement therapy, validating her perspective rather than dismissing it. However, a subtle shift toward expert positioning occurs in the psychoeducation section. The statement "What I can share are some evidence-based approaches..." presents information in a somewhat top-down manner. A more partnership-oriented approach would first explore Anisa's existing knowledge. Additionally, when Anisa identifies her goal, the psychiatrist affirms it but doesn't invite her to refine or adjust it collaboratively. Stronger partnership would include: "What would make this plan feel even more doable for you? What might get in the way, and how could we problem-solve that together?"

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistent, skillful cultivation of change talk is demonstrated. Strategic questions evoke substantial change talk: "What makes you want to keep trying?" elicits powerful motivation ("It's mostly my daughter... I want her to have a mom who's there... I want to feel free"). Questions about past attempts and strengths draw out resilience and determination. The confidence scaling question appropriately invites Anisa to assess her own readiness. The psychiatrist reinforces change talk through affirmations: "It's really courageous," "That stubborn streak is a powerful resource," "Your love for your daughter really fuels your motivation." However, opportunities exist to more strategically amplify specific types of change talk: when Anisa says "I've been wanting to quit for a long time," deeper exploration ("What is it about quitting that appeals to you?") would strengthen desire talk. When she rates confidence at 3-4, the psychiatrist could ask: "What would move you from 3-4 to 5-6?" to evoke ability talk. When identifying her goal, asking "How confident are you that you can do this?" would evoke commitment talk.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** This is a notable weakness. Anisa generates significant sustain talk—fear of failure, worry about cravings and mood swings, concern about patches being a "crutch," discomfort with sharing—and the psychiatrist's handling is inconsistent. The typical response is to validate and reflect sustain talk back without strategically shifting focus toward change. When Anisa expresses fear of failure, the psychiatrist responds: "That fear of shame and failure seems to be a persistent companion," which validates but doesn't reframe. When she expresses concern about patches being "a crutch," the response validates without exploring: "I appreciate your openness and the caution you bring from past experiences—that makes a lot of sense." When she mentions discomfort with sharing, the psychiatrist accepts this without exploring whether this protective stance might hinder change efforts. Stronger softening would use double-sided reflections ("On one hand you're worried about failing; on the other hand, you're here today and you've kept trying"), reframing ("What do you think the long-term impact of continued smoking might be on your relationship with your daughter?"), or exploratory questions ("What would it mean to use a patch? How would you know if it was helping?"). The psychiatrist eventually helps Anisa move toward action, but this happens organically rather than through strategic softening of sustain talk.

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall Score: 4.0/5**

### Strengths
- **Exceptional empathy** with sophisticated reflective listening capturing emotional nuance and validating ambivalence
- **Strong collaborative stance** with genuine power-sharing and invitation of Anisa's input throughout
- **Consistent autonomy support** respecting her pace, choices, and right to self-determination
- **Solid change talk evocation** through strategic questioning and affirmation
- **Acceptance and warmth** with nonjudgmental stance and genuine support
- **Concrete goal-setting** helping Anisa identify a specific, manageable first step

### Areas for Improvement
- **Sustain talk management** (HIGHEST PRIORITY): Barriers are validated but not strategically softened or reframed; sustain talk sometimes inadvertently reinforced
- **Strategic direction**: Could be more directive in narrowing options and establishing measurable outcomes with contingency plans
- **Depth of evocation**: Change talk evoked but could explore intrinsic motivations more fully and invite Anisa to problem-solve her own concerns
- **Psychoeducation approach**: Information presented somewhat didactically; could first explore Anisa's existing knowledge and experience
- **Confidence exploration**: The 3-4 rating affirmed but not strategically explored; missing opportunity to ask what would increase confidence
- **Commitment clarification**: Goal-setting collaborative but doesn't explicitly ensure commitment is intrinsic rather than compliance-based

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change
Anisa leaves feeling supported and validated with a concrete goal, which supports engagement and rapport. However, her sustain talk remains largely unaddressed and could resurface as barriers during change attempts. Her relatively low confidence (3-4) combined with lack of contingency planning for anticipated obstacles (intense cravings, mood swings) leaves her vulnerable to relapse. The session demonstrates solid MI practice with strong relational elements but misses opportunities to strategically amplify motivation and address barriers. With attention to the recommendations provided—particularly strategic softening of sustain talk, deeper exploration of intrinsic motivation, and contingency planning—the psychiatrist could significantly increase the likelihood of successful sustained change.
