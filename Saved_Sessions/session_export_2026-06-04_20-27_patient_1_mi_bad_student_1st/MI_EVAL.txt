### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard throughout the session. Explicit judgment and disapproval permeate the interaction, with dismissive language including "You're just making excuses," "Nobody is going to take your health seriously if you keep whining," and "Don't overthink it." The opening directive stance ("I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do") immediately establishes a controlling, non-accepting framework. ### Empathy

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** While the psychiatrist maintains consistent focus on smoking and drinking cessation, preventing topic drift, the manner of maintaining direction is antithetical to MI principles. Direction becomes coercion through repeated ultimatums ("you have to quit now," "no ifs, ands, or buts," "no more delays") rather than collaborative exploration. The psychiatrist never explores Adrian's values, barriers to change, or what would help him feel ready. There is no progressive clarification of direction—only repetitive demands with increasing intensity. A more skillful approach would maintain focus while strategically exploring ambivalence and collaboratively setting goals. The rigidity and pressure-based approach undermines the collaborative exploration necessary for sustainable change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The session demonstrates an authoritarian, expert-driven stance from the opening statement: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do." The psychiatrist makes unilateral pronouncements, dismisses Adrian's input as invalid ("You're just making excuses"), refuses to incorporate his perspective into the discussion, and uses interrogative language demanding compliance rather than inviting exploration. When Adrian attempts to share his perspective ("It's not that simple," "It's more complicated than just a yes or no"), the psychiatrist responds with dismissal rather than curiosity. There is no joint problem-solving, no shared decision-making, and no sense that Adrian's ideas have value in shaping the session. Adrian is treated as a problem to be solved rather than a partner in problem-solving. The power dynamic is entirely one-directional, with no true collaboration present.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The session is characterized by systematic undermining of autonomy through pressure, ultimatums, and removal of choice. The psychiatrist repeatedly uses language that eliminates choice: "you have to quit," "you need to stop," "you must," "no more delays," "no excuses." These statements communicate that Adrian has no legitimate choice. When Adrian exercises his autonomy by declining to commit to immediate cessation ("No, I'm not going to quit everything today"), the psychiatrist responds with escalated pressure rather than respect. The repeated "Yes or no?" demands attempt to force Adrian into a binary choice that does not reflect his actual readiness or values—this is coercive, not autonomy-supportive. Fear-based language ("Do you want lung cancer or liver failure?") functions as psychological coercion designed to override Adrian's autonomous decision-making. Adrian's consistent, respectful refusals are treated as defiance rather than legitimate expressions of self-determination.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The session contains virtually no evocation. The psychiatrist relies entirely on telling Adrian what he needs to do rather than asking questions designed to draw out his own motivations, values, and resources. Adrian provides multiple opportunities for evocation that are completely missed: implicit change talk ("I don't want lung disease," "I know it's important"), barriers and ambivalence ("It's the only break I get," "It helps me relax"), and contemplation ("Maybe someday," "Maybe I'll think about cutting back"). Rather than exploring these with curiosity, the psychiatrist dismisses them as "whining" and "excuses." No open-ended questions are asked to elicit Adrian's own motivation. The only questions posed are closed, directive, and designed to force compliance ("Yes or no?"). The psychiatrist tells Adrian what to think, what to feel, and what to do, which is the opposite of evoking his own wisdom and motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist actively assumes the expert role and communicates that Adrian's perspective and self-knowledge are irrelevant. From the opening statement, a hierarchical, expert-driven dynamic is established. The implicit message throughout is: "I know what's best for you, and you need to comply." Adrian's self-knowledge is dismissed ("You're just making excuses," "That kind of thinking is exactly why people stay unhealthy"), his input is refused incorporation into session direction, and his stated readiness is repeatedly overridden. When Adrian asserts his own expertise about his situation ("I'm not there yet," "It's not that simple for me"), the psychiatrist responds with dismissal rather than curiosity. The message is clear: Adrian's understanding of his own situation is wrong, and the psychiatrist's understanding is correct. This approach prevents Adrian from developing self-efficacy and damages the therapeutic alliance. True partnership—where the client's expertise about his own situation is valued—is entirely absent.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** No strategic cultivation of change talk occurs. Adrian provides several instances of change talk that should be amplified: implicit desire ("I don't want lung disease," "I'm not saying I want to get sick," "I know it's important"), ability (Adrian has thought about change: "Maybe I'll think about cutting back"), and reasons (Adrian acknowledges health risks and shows concern about consequences). Instead of amplifying these statements, the psychiatrist responds with fear-mongering and pressure ("Do you want lung cancer or liver failure?"), which research shows increases defensiveness and sustain talk, not change talk. The psychiatrist never asks questions designed to evoke change talk such as "What concerns you most?" or "What would be different if you made a change?" Adrian's implicit change talk is ignored or suppressed through the fear-based approach. The psychiatrist's strategy actively undermines change talk by increasing defensiveness rather than supporting Adrian's own motivation for change.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist does attempt to address sustain talk by repeatedly challenging Adrian's minimization ("If you're smoking even sometimes," "That's still a problem"). However, the approach is entirely confrontational rather than skillful. The psychiatrist uses direct challenge and pressure rather than sophisticated MI techniques such as double-sided reflection or strategic reframing. When Adrian expresses sustain talk ("It's the only break I get," "It helps me relax"), the psychiatrist dismisses it as invalid ("You're just making excuses") rather than validating both sides of his ambivalence. A more skillful approach would use double-sided reflection: "So on one hand it helps you relax, and on the other hand you're worried about your health—both are real." The psychiatrist does maintain some focus away from sustain talk through repeated redirection to the need for change, which prevents extended exploration. However, this is achieved through pressure and ultimatums rather than through MI techniques that would reduce defensiveness and increase openness to change.

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall Score: 1.2/5**

### Strengths:
- Maintains consistent focus on target behavior (smoking and drinking cessation)
- Prevents session from drifting aimlessly
- Attempts to address sustain talk and minimize justifications

### Critical Deficiencies:
- **Fundamental absence of MI spirit:** The session is characterized by confrontation, coercion, and control rather than partnership, acceptance, and evocation
- **No reflective listening:** Zero reflective statements; no demonstration of understanding Adrian's perspective
- **Systematic undermining of autonomy:** Repeated ultimatums and removal of choice throughout
- **Suppression of change talk:** Adrian's implicit motivation is ignored or suppressed through fear-based pressure
- **Judgment and shaming:** Dismissive language communicates disapproval and devalues Adrian's worth
- **Expert-driven stance:** Psychiatrist assumes unilateral authority rather than partnering with Adrian
- **Research-contraindicated strategy:** Pressure-based approach produces defensiveness and resistance, not behavior change
- **Missed opportunities:** Multiple opportunities to evoke Adrian's own motivation are completely missed

### Impact on Client Engagement:
This session is likely to produce the opposite of the intended effect. Adrian demonstrates appropriate ambivalence and some implicit motivation for change. However, the psychiatrist's confrontational approach will likely increase his defensiveness, entrench his sustain talk, and damage his willingness to engage in future treatment. Adrian's consistent, respectful refusals ("No, I'm not going to quit everything today") reflect healthy self-determination, not defiance. By treating these refusals as problems to overcome through increased pressure, the psychiatrist is likely to push Adrian away from treatment and reinforce his defensive stance.

### Immediate Recommendations:
1. **Adopt the MI spirit:** Shift from expert-driven to collaborative, client-centered approach
2. **Implement reflective listening:** Use reflections as primary tool before offering advice
3. **Eliminate fear and pressure:** Replace ultimatums with open-ended evocative questions
4. **Respect autonomy:** Support Adrian's right to choose; eliminate "have to" language
5. **Amplify change talk:** Strengthen Adrian's own motivation rather than suppress it
6. **Use double-sided reflections:** Address sustain talk without confrontation
7. **Explore ambivalence:** Treat Adrian's mixed feelings as normal, not problematic
8. **Seek formal MI training:** This psychiatrist requires accredited training in Motivational Interviewing

**This psychiatrist should seek immediate training in Motivational Interviewing from an accredited MI trainer. The current practice is inconsistent with evidence-based standards and is likely to harm patients.**
