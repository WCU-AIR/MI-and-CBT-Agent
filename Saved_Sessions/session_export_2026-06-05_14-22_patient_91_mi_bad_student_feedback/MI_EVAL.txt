# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates a profound failure in communicating unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance. Repeated use of accusatory language ("Why didn't you keep up with your medications?"), explicit rejection of the client's experience ("There's no excuse for not following your treatment plan"), and dismissal of client values ("The dog situation doesn't matter as much as your health") convey disapproval and conditional regard. No affirmations of Marcus's strengths, normalization of his struggles, or validation of his barriers are present. The client's increasing defensiveness ("I'm not trying to make excuses, just... being honest about where I'm at") indicates acceptance was actively undermined rather than communicated.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Empathy is virtually absent throughout the session. The psychiatrist talks *at* Marcus rather than *with* him, using interrogative and accusatory questions ("Why haven't you been keeping up with your medications?") rather than exploratory reflection. Zero instances of accurate empathic reflection are present. When Marcus expresses overwhelm, the response is pressure. When he expresses attachment to his dog, the response is dismissal. No complex reflections capture Marcus's emotional state, values, or internal conflict. The psychiatrist never demonstrates genuine effort to understand Marcus's perspective, barriers, or lived experience—a critical MI failure that prevents therapeutic alliance.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** While the psychiatrist maintains appropriate focus on asthma management as the target behavior, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. No exploration of Marcus's own goals or readiness occurs. When Marcus identifies a realistic first step ("trying to remember my inhaler more regularly"), the psychiatrist immediately undermines it: "But you really need to stop making excuses and just take your inhaler every day, no matter what." Interventions are not staged according to readiness; smoking cessation and dog removal are demanded before inhaler adherence is established. The psychiatrist dictates what must happen rather than guiding Marcus toward his own prioritized change, undermining client autonomy and readiness-based staging.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Collaboration is fundamentally absent. The opening statement explicitly frames the psychiatrist as the decision-maker: "I'll be telling you exactly what you need to do to get better." Repeated directives ("You must stop missing doses," "You have to avoid triggers," "You must quit immediately," "You have to be all in right now") establish a hierarchical, expert-driven stance. No joint problem-solving occurs around Marcus's financial barriers, overwhelm, or emotional attachment to his dog. The psychiatrist decides what matters, what Marcus must do, and Marcus's readiness—removing choice and treating Marcus as a non-compliant patient requiring correction rather than a partner in his own care.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Autonomy support is actively undermined throughout the session. Explicit pressure and demands permeate the interaction ("You really need to get that under control immediately," "No, you can't just take it slow," "There's no room for 'not ready'"). When Marcus expresses ambivalence about smoking cessation, the response is a yes/no demand ("Are you ready to quit today?") rather than exploration of choice. When Marcus identifies a realistic first step, the psychiatrist adds overwhelming demands he did not choose. Language minimizes client agency ("You can't keep letting this slide," "If you don't quit smoking, your lungs will keep getting worse"). Marcus's withdrawal of autonomy language by session end indicates he has been pressured into defensiveness rather than supported in making his own choices.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Evocation is nearly absent. The psychiatrist tells rather than asks, imposes rather than explores. Few open questions are asked, and those that are posed are rhetorical or accusatory ("Why didn't you keep up with your medications?" "Why haven't you done this already?") rather than exploratory. Marcus's change talk is present but completely uncultivated: "I know it's serious... I don't want to end up in the hospital again," "I want to get better," "I'm willing to try and take my meds more regularly." The psychiatrist never reflects, reinforces, or deepens this change talk. Instead, new demands are immediately added, shifting focus away from Marcus's own stated motivation. Missed opportunities to evoke Marcus's values, past successes, resources, and intrinsic motivation are numerous throughout the session.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Partnership is completely absent. The psychiatrist assumes exclusive expertise and treats Marcus as a non-compliant patient requiring correction. The explicit statement "I'll be telling you exactly what you need to do to get better" frames the psychiatrist as the sole decision-maker. Client contributions are dismissed: when Marcus identifies a realistic first step, it is rejected; when he expresses attachment to his dog, it is dismissed as unimportant; when he expresses ambivalence about smoking cessation, he is pressured rather than heard. The psychiatrist decides what matters, what Marcus must do, and Marcus's readiness—removing the client's voice from the process. This is fundamentally anti-MI and prevents collaborative change. Marcus's responses show increasing passivity and defensiveness, indicating he has been positioned as a problem to be fixed rather than a partner in his own care.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** While Marcus offered multiple instances of change talk ("I don't want to end up in the hospital again," "I want to get better," "I'm willing to try and take my meds more regularly," "I can try to be more consistent with it"), the psychiatrist failed to recognize, reflect, reinforce, or build upon any of it. When Marcus stated "I'm willing to try and take my meds more regularly—I want to get control of this asthma," the psychiatrist responded by adding new demands ("Are you ready to quit today?") rather than deepening his commitment. No reflections of change talk occur. No follow-up questions explore what would support Marcus's stated willingness. No celebration of his motivation is present. Instead, the psychiatrist's pressure likely activated sustain talk and decreased the change talk Marcus had offered, undermining the development of intrinsic motivation for change.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Sustain talk was not softened; it was directly confronted and rejected. When Marcus expressed ambivalence ("I don't know if I'm ready to quit today. It just feels like too much right now"), the psychiatrist responded with confrontation: "No, you can't just take it slow. There's no room for 'not ready' or 'too much.'" When Marcus expressed barriers ("It's hard when everything feels so overwhelming"), the response was pressure and threat rather than validation and exploration. No double-sided reflections were used to validate both Marcus's desire for change and his legitimate concerns. No reframing of barriers as understandable occurred. Research demonstrates that confronting sustain talk increases resistance; this approach likely amplified Marcus's defensiveness rather than softened it. Marcus's final response ("I'm not trying to make excuses, just... being honest about where I'm at") reflects a client pushed into a corner, not one whose concerns have been validated and explored.

## Overall Session Summary

### **Overall MI Competency Score: 1.2/5**

**Assessment Level:** Pre-MI / Fundamentally Non-MI Approach

---

### Key Findings

**Critical Failures:**
- Authoritarian, directive stance throughout the entire session
- Absence of empathy, acceptance, and autonomy support
- Active undermining of client autonomy through pressure and demands
- Failure to evoke client motivation; instead, motivation was likely decreased
- Confrontation of sustain talk rather than skillful softening
- Dismissal of client values, barriers, and emotional experience
- Repeated use of "must," "have to," "need to" language
- Rejection of client's realistic first step and addition of overwhelming demands

**Impact on Client Engagement:**
Marcus moved from engagement ("I want to get better") to defensiveness ("I'm not trying to make excuses, just... being honest about where I'm at"). The therapeutic alliance was damaged. Intrinsic motivation for change was likely **decreased** rather than increased.

**Likelihood of Behavior Change:**
Very low. Research demonstrates that directive, pressuring approaches are less effective than collaborative, evocative approaches. This session exemplifies the ineffective approach and may have entrenched resistance.

---

### Immediate Recommendations

1. **Adopt MI Spirit:** Shift from expert/directive to collaborative/evocative stance. Embody partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation.

2. **Eliminate Directive Language:** Replace all "must," "have to," "need to," "should" language with open questions and collaborative exploration.

3. **Develop Empathic Reflection:** Learn to reflect client statements in ways that demonstrate understanding of their perspective, emotions, and values. Use complex reflections that deepen insight.

4. **Recognize and Cultivate Change Talk:** Identify when clients express desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment to change. Reflect, reinforce, and deepen this change talk.

5. **Support Autonomy:** Consistently communicate respect for the client's right to choose. Offer choices and emphasize their ownership of the change process.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:** Use double-sided reflections and reframing to validate concerns while gently exploring change possibilities. Never confront or reject sustain talk.

7. **Seek Formal MI Training:** Enroll in certified MI training with a qualified trainer. Engage in regular supervision with feedback on recorded sessions. This is not a skill that can be learned from reading alone.

---

### Supervisor Note

**The psychiatrist is operating at a pre-MI level and should not continue conducting MI sessions without formal training and supervision.** The current approach is fundamentally different from MI (directive/expert-driven vs. collaborative/evocative) and is ineffective for evoking client motivation. Immediate enrollment in MI training is strongly recommended before continuing with this client or others.
