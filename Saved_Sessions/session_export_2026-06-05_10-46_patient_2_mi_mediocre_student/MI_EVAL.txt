# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal unconditional positive regard. While opening is warm, the clinician quickly pivots to problem-focused agenda without validating Ahmed's perspective. Repeatedly dismisses Ahmed's stated contentment ("I'm good as it is") with gentle but persistent pressure rather than acceptance. Fails to affirm Ahmed's strengths (managing physically demanding job, maintaining family relationships, adequate sleep). No normalization of his experience or acknowledgment that resistance to additional activity may be reasonable given his fatigue. Surface-level politeness undermines therapeutic alliance and violates MI spirit.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflective listening is present but superficial and factual. Psychiatrist captures content ("It sounds like work is pretty demanding") but misses emotional nuance and Ahmed's internal experience of fatigue and concern about adding burden. Never explores Ahmed's perspective on why he resists additional activity. Fails to recognize and explore Ahmed's ambivalence entirely—his low confidence (2-3) and internal conflict are not addressed. No attempt to understand Ahmed's values, what matters to him about current life, or what would need to shift for change. Empathy remains at basic level without deepening understanding.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains clear target behavior (increasing physical activity/stress management) and generally keeps conversation focused on this goal. Direction is present but somewhat rigid—consistently steers toward same solution (stretching, walking, light exercise) without exploring whether this aligns with Ahmed's goals or values. Lacks strategic progression; returns repeatedly to same suggestion without building on responses or adjusting strategy based on resistance. Final summary ("Let's check in on this next time") shows some attempt to maintain direction across sessions. Adequate focus but lacks collaborative flexibility.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration is essentially absent. Session is entirely expert-driven and unilateral. Psychiatrist repeatedly imposes solutions without seeking Ahmed's input: "I think you might want to try," "It would probably help if you," "Maybe you could start with." Never invites genuine joint problem-solving ("What do you think might help?" or "Let's think together about what might work for you?"). Only moment approaching collaboration is confidence scale question, but this is used to reinforce psychiatrist's agenda rather than explore Ahmed's perspective. Ahmed's responses consistently ignored or overridden. No co-construction of any aspect of treatment plan.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy is systematically undermined through persistent gentle pressure and implicit judgment. Psychiatrist frames suggestions as recommendations client "should" follow: "I think you might want to try," "It would probably help if you," "Maybe you should try." Soft directives communicate that psychiatrist knows what's best and Ahmed should comply. When Ahmed clearly states "I don't see a big need to change. I'm doing okay," psychiatrist does not respect this choice but continues suggesting alternatives, implying Ahmed's decision is incorrect. Never explicitly affirms Ahmed's right to choose differently. Ahmed's low confidence and repeated resistance suggest he feels pressured rather than supported in autonomy. No genuine choice or ownership offered.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation is essentially absent—the heart of MI is missing. Psychiatrist never asks about Ahmed's own motivations, values, or reasons for change: "What matters most to you?" "What would help you feel better?" "What do you value about your current routine?" Instead, psychiatrist tells Ahmed what should help: "It would probably help if you found time to unwind." Never explores Ahmed's own ideas, strengths, or resources. Fails to elicit any change talk—Ahmed never articulates his own reasons, confidence, or commitment to change. Psychiatrist's suggestions remain external impositions rather than Ahmed's own motivations. No genuine curiosity about Ahmed's perspective demonstrated.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership requires conveying that expertise about change resides within client, not clinician. This session demonstrates opposite—psychiatrist assumes expert role throughout and actively minimizes Ahmed's expertise about his own life. Repeatedly positions self as authority: "I think you might want to try," "It would probably help if you," "Maybe you should try." No language suggesting Ahmed is expert on his own life. Ahmed explicitly states his expertise ("I'm worn out already," "I don't see how I can fit even a little extra in," "I'm already doing plenty physically"), but psychiatrist does not honor this self-knowledge. Power dynamic entirely unequal. Ahmed's contributions not used to shape session direction. No shared decision-making; psychiatrist decides target, approach, and follow-up plan. Clinician-driven, not client-centered.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** No change talk is cultivated. Ahmed produces only sustain talk throughout: "I don't really have any big complaints," "I don't really see any problems there," "I'm good as it is," "I don't see a big need to change." Psychiatrist never strategically responds to evoke change talk. When Ahmed says "I get it might help, but I just don't see how I can fit even a little extra in," psychiatrist responds with prescription rather than exploring the part of Ahmed that sees potential benefit. Confidence scale question is only moment that could elicit change talk, but Ahmed's response (2-3) is not used to explore his own reasons or resources. No attempt to amplify or reinforce any change talk. Ahmed never articulates desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment to change.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist does not reinforce Ahmed's sustain talk, which is positive. However, does not skillfully soften it either. Uses gentle contradiction and repeated prescription that may actually strengthen Ahmed's resistance. Responses to sustain talk include repeated use of "but": "But maybe even small changes," "But it would probably help if you just kept an open mind." This confrontational move can entrench sustain talk and strengthen defensiveness. More skillful approach would use double-sided reflection: "On one hand, you're doing okay and don't see a need to change. On the other hand, you mentioned work can be tough sometimes." Mild attempt to redirect focus ("Maybe you should try to notice how you feel after work") is still prescriptive rather than collaborative. No strategic softening techniques demonstrated.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.4/5 – Poor MI Practice**

### Strengths
- Warm, genuine opening establishes basic rapport
- Maintains consistent focus on target behavior without wandering
- Avoids reinforcing sustain talk, preventing entrenchment
- Attempts motivational assessment via confidence scale
- Establishes follow-up plan showing continuity of care

### Critical Deficiencies
The session fundamentally violates MI spirit by prioritizing psychiatrist's agenda over Ahmed's autonomy and perspective. Operates from expert-driven, problem-focused model rather than collaborative, evocative approach.

**Core deficiencies:**
1. No genuine collaboration—imposes solutions rather than co-constructing
2. Minimal empathy—superficial reflections miss emotional nuance and ambivalence
3. No evocation—tells Ahmed what should help rather than drawing out his motivations
4. Undermined autonomy—persistent pressure communicates compliance expectation, not genuine choice
5. No change talk—Ahmed produces only sustain talk; no strategic effort to evoke motivation
6. Confrontational responses—repeated "but" statements may strengthen resistance

### Clinical Outcome
Ahmed's low confidence (2-3) and final statement ("I'll think about it. No promises though") indicate he feels pressured rather than motivated. Likelihood of behavior change is very low. Session reinforces Ahmed's position that change is unnecessary rather than facilitating movement from precontemplation to contemplation.

### Recommendation
Psychiatrist requires MI training focused on core competencies: collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support. Recommended interventions include role-play practice with feedback, audio/video review of sessions, and deliberate practice on specific MI techniques (open-ended questions, reflective listening, evoking change talk). Current MI competency is below threshold for effective motivational interviewing.

---

**Evaluator Notes:** This session reflects traditional medical model (clinician diagnoses, prescribes solution) rather than MI approach (collaborative exploration of ambivalence and evoking intrinsic motivation). Fundamental reorientation toward client-centered, collaborative stance is essential for improved outcomes.
