
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 7. Guided Discovery

### 1. **URGENT: Conduct Comprehensive Medical Assessment and Risk Evaluation**

### 2. **Establish Coordination with Medical Team**
- **Action:** Do not treat this patient in isolation. Establish regular communication with her primary care physician and/or an eating disorders medical specialist. Share treatment plans, monitor medical status, and adjust psychological treatment based on medical needs.

### 3. **Conduct Thorough Eating Disorder Assessment (MOST CRITICAL FOR TREATMENT PLANNING)**
- **Action:** Systematically assess: (1) Current food/fluid intake (detailed dietary recall), (2) Barriers to eating (appetite, postprandial distress, financial, cultural, family, medical), (3) Compensatory behaviors (exercise, purging), (4) Medical complications, (5) Motivation/ambivalence about recovery, (6) Family context and support.
- **Example:** "I need to understand exactly what you're eating and what makes it hard. Can you walk me through a typical day? What do you eat for breakfast? How much? What happens after you eat? What makes it hard to eat more? Are there foods you can tolerate better? What would help you eat more?"
- **Practice:** Use structured eating disorder assessment tools (e.g., Eating Disorder Examination). Ask specific, detailed questions about intake. Don't accept vague answers. Understand the difference between restriction driven by fat phobia vs. other factors.

### 4. **Develop Collaborative Case Conceptualization**
- **Action:** Share your understanding of her eating disorder with Valerie and invite her input. Explain: "Here's how I understand what's happening: Your body is very malnourished, which is causing appetite suppression and making you feel tired. When you do eat, you feel bloated and uncomfortable, which makes you want to eat less. You also mentioned financial limitations and not wanting to burden your family. I think all of these things are keeping you stuck in restriction. Do you see it the same way? What would you add?"
- **Example:** "I don't think your problem is that you have bad thoughts about being weak. I think your problem is that your body is starving, and we need to help you eat more so your body can heal. Once you're eating enough, the tiredness and headaches will improve, and the negative thoughts will be easier to manage. Does that make sense?"
- **Practice:** Develop the ability to formulate eating disorders cases in terms of maintaining factors rather than just symptoms. Understand non-fat-phobic anorexia nervosa. Ask yourself: "What is actually keeping this person from eating?"

### 5. **Shift to Behavioral Intervention: Structured Meal Planning**
- **Action:** Move away from cognitive restructuring and toward behavioral change. Develop a specific meal plan with: (1) Target caloric intake (likely 1500-2000+ calories/day to achieve weight gain), (2) Specific meals and snacks with portion sizes, (3) Regular eating times, (4) Foods that are tolerable, (5) Strategies to manage postprandial distress.
- **Example:** "Let's create a meal plan together. I want you to eat three meals and two snacks every day. For breakfast, you'll eat [specific food] at [specific time]. For lunch, you'll eat [specific food] at [specific time]. We'll start with amounts you can manage and gradually increase. We'll also work on managing the bloating and discomfort. Does this feel doable?"
- **Practice:** Learn to develop individualized meal plans for eating disorder patients. Understand how to gradually increase intake. Know strategies for managing postprandial distress (eating slowly, staying upright, gentle movement, medical evaluation if severe).

### 6. **Use Behavioral Experiments to Test Beliefs About Eating**
- **Action:** Design specific behavioral experiments to test Valerie's beliefs about eating. For example: "You think eating makes you feel worse. Let's test that. Eat a small meal and carefully monitor what actually happens. Write down your predictions before eating and your actual experience after eating."
- **Example:** "This week, I want you to eat a small meal (e.g., rice and beans) and carefully notice what happens. Before you eat, write down: 'I predict I will feel very bloated and uncomfortable.' After you eat, write down: 'What actually happened?' Let's see if your prediction matches reality. This will help us understand what's really going on."
- **Practice:** Learn to design behavioral experiments for eating disorders. Understand how to help patients test their beliefs through direct experience rather than just talking about it.

### 7. **Address Barriers to Eating Systematically**
- **Action:** For each barrier to eating, develop specific solutions. For example:
  - **Appetite suppression:** Psychoeducation about malnutrition-induced appetite loss; small, frequent meals; foods that are easier to eat
  - **Postprandial distress:** Medical evaluation; eating slowly; staying upright; gentle movement; possible medical intervention

  - **Cultural/religious dietary restrictions:** Understand what foods are acceptable; work within those constraints
  - **Family burden:** Explore family involvement; help Valerie communicate her needs
- **Example:** "You mentioned financial limitations. Let's problem-solve that. What foods are affordable and that you can tolerate? Are there community resources that could help? Can your family help? Let's make a list of affordable foods you can eat."
- **Practice:** Develop problem-solving skills specific to eating disorders. Understand how to address practical barriers (financial, cultural, medical) that maintain restriction.

### 8. **Involve Family in Treatment**
- **Action:** Include Valerie's family in treatment. Provide psychoeducation about anorexia nervosa and malnutrition. Explain that family support is essential for recovery. Discuss how family can help (meal support, encouragement, monitoring).
- **Example:** "Valerie's recovery depends on her eating more. I'd like to meet with your family to explain what's happening and how you can help. Family support is one of the most important things for recovery."
- **Practice:** Learn family-based treatment approaches for eating disorders. Understand how to engage families as allies in treatment. Know how to address family dynamics that might maintain restriction.

### 9. **Provide Psychoeducation About Anorexia Nervosa and Malnutrition**
- **Action:** Educate Valerie about: (1) How malnutrition causes appetite suppression, fatigue, poor concentration, and other symptoms, (2) Why postprandial distress occurs and how it improves with adequate nutrition, (3) Medical complications of anorexia nervosa, (4) What recovery requires (adequate nutrition, weight restoration, medical monitoring), (5) Why cognitive work alone is insufficient.
- **Example:** "When your body is starving, it shuts down appetite to conserve energy. That's why you don't feel hungry even though you need to eat. When you start eating more, your appetite will come back. The bloating and discomfort you feel after eating is your stomach adjusting to food after being empty. This will improve as you eat more regularly. The tiredness and headaches are from malnutrition—they'll improve as you gain weight."
- **Practice:** Develop the ability to explain eating disorder pathophysiology in clear, compassionate language. Help patients understand that their symptoms are medical consequences of starvation, not character flaws.

### 10. **Modify Activity Level if Compensatory Exercise is Present**
- **Action:** Clarify whether Valerie's 3-4 hours of daily walking is compensatory exercise (driven by need to burn calories) or necessary transportation. If compensatory, gradually reduce it. If necessary transportation, explore alternatives (public transit, rides from family) to reduce energy expenditure.
- **Example:** "You mentioned you walk 3-4 hours daily because you don't have other transportation. I'm concerned that this much activity, combined with your low food intake, is making your malnutrition worse. Let's think about alternatives. Can your family give you rides sometimes? Can you use public transportation? Can we reduce the walking so your body can use its energy for healing instead of exercise?"
- **Practice:** Understand the distinction between compensatory exercise and necessary activity. Know how to address compensatory exercise in eating disorders.

### 11. **Abandon Ineffective Cognitive Restructuring; Use Cognitive Work Strategically**
- **Action:** Stop using generic cognitive restructuring ("think positive thoughts"). Instead, use cognitive work strategically to address specific beliefs that emerge as eating increases. For example, as Valerie eats more, she may develop anxiety about weight gain—then cognitive work is appropriate.
- **Example:** "Right now, your main problem is not your thoughts—it's that you're not eating enough. Once you're eating more and your body is healing, we can work on any thoughts or worries that come up. But first, we need to focus on getting food in."
- **Practice:** Understand when cognitive work is appropriate in eating disorders (after behavioral change has begun) vs. when behavioral intervention is the priority (when restriction is severe). Learn to use cognitive work strategically rather than as a default intervention.

### 12. **Establish Clear Treatment Goals and Monitor Progress**
- **Action:** Collaboratively establish specific, measurable treatment goals: (1) Target weight/BMI, (2) Target caloric intake, (3) Reduction in restriction behaviors, (4) Improvement in medical markers (menstrual function, energy, concentration). Monitor progress weekly. If progress is not occurring, adjust treatment.
- **Example:** "Our goal is for you to reach a healthy weight of [X pounds] and eat [X calories] per day. We'll weigh you weekly and track your intake. If you're making progress, we'll continue this plan. If you're not making progress, we'll figure out what's getting in the way and adjust."
- **Practice:** Learn to set specific, measurable goals in eating disorder treatment. Understand how to monitor progress objectively. Know when to adjust treatment based on lack of progress.

### 13. **Develop Competence in Eating Disorder Treatment**
- **Action:** This session demonstrates significant gaps in eating disorder knowledge and skills. Pursue specialized training in eating disorder treatment. Consider: (1) Attending eating disorder workshops or conferences, (2) Reading evidence-based eating disorder treatment manuals (e.g., CBT-E, family-based treatment), (3) Seeking supervision from an eating disorder specialist, (4) Obtaining certification in eating disorder treatment.
- **Example:** Recommended resources: "Cognitive Behavior Therapy-Enhanced (CBT-E) for Eating Disorders" by Fairburn; "Family-Based Treatment of Adolescent Eating Disorders" by Lock & Le Grange; Academy for Eating Disorders (www.aedweb.org).
- **Practice:** Recognize that eating disorder treatment requires specialized knowledge. Commit to ongoing training and supervision.
