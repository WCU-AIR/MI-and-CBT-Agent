
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly communicates judgment, dismissal, and disapproval throughout the interaction. Most critically, when Tariq explicitly states he does not smoke or use substances—a factual clarification—the psychiatrist ignores this and continues to lecture him about quitting smoking and drugs anyway ("you really should quit smoking if you do," "Have you tried quitting before?"). This is not only ineffective but actively disrespectful, suggesting the psychiatrist is not listening to the client's actual presentation.

The psychiatrist's language is overtly judgmental and shaming: "feeling stressed doesn't cut it as an excuse," "that's just weakness," "stop feeling sorry for yourself," "you're just whining." These statements communicate contempt for Tariq's emotional experience and violate the fundamental MI principle of accepting the client as a person of inherent worth. When Tariq attempts to explain his struggle—"I'm human too, you know?"—the psychiatrist responds with "This isn't about being human or having feelings—it's about following the rules." This is a direct rejection of Tariq's humanity and emotional validity.

There is no affirmation of Tariq's strengths (he identifies as a "model resident" and demonstrates genuine motivation to be released and reunite with family). The psychiatrist never normalizes the struggle of adolescence in detention or acknowledges the legitimacy of wanting to contact family. Instead, every response frames Tariq as the problem—someone who needs to "get his act together," "be tougher," and "stop making excuses."

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance. The session is characterized by judgment, dismissal of the client's experience, and active disrespect for Tariq's emotional reality. This directly violates MI spirit and creates a confrontational rather than collaborative dynamic.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective. This psychiatrist demonstrates minimal to no empathy throughout the session. Tariq repeatedly and clearly states his primary concern: "I just want my call, that's it" and "I just want to talk to my mom." He contextualizes this within meaningful family circumstances (his father's birthday tomorrow) and explains how the loss of phone privileges is affecting his emotional state and compliance.

The psychiatrist never reflects back what Tariq is actually saying. Instead, the psychiatrist imposes a predetermined agenda (smoking cessation, substance use, willpower) that has nothing to do with Tariq's presenting concern. When Tariq clarifies "I don't smoke or drink, alright? I'm not into that stuff," the psychiatrist continues to lecture about quitting smoking and drugs—demonstrating either that the psychiatrist is not listening or is deliberately ignoring the client's words.

There are no reflective statements that capture Tariq's emotional experience. The psychiatrist never reflects any of this. Instead, the psychiatrist interprets Tariq's emotional expression as "weakness," "whining," and "making excuses"—the opposite of empathic understanding.

A single empathic response might sound like: "It sounds like losing phone privileges feels really unfair, especially with your dad's birthday coming up and wanting to stay connected to your mom. That's weighing on you right now." The psychiatrist never attempts anything resembling this.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no genuine empathy. There is no reflective listening, no attempt to understand Tariq's perspective, and active mischaracterization of his emotional experience as weakness rather than understandable human struggle.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI means maintaining strategic focus on a specific target behavior while remaining client-centered. This session has direction, but it is entirely imposed and disconnected from the client's actual concerns or the clinical context.

The psychiatrist maintains a rigid focus on three topics: (1) smoking cessation, (2) substance use, and (3) "trying harder" to follow rules. However, this direction is problematic for several reasons. First, Tariq has clearly stated he does not smoke or use substances—these are not relevant targets. The psychiatrist's insistence on these topics despite repeated clarification suggests either poor listening or a predetermined script unresponsive to the client's actual presentation.

Second, the direction lacks strategic coherence with Tariq's actual change goals. Tariq's implicit goals are: (1) regaining phone privileges, (2) feeling heard and fairly treated, (3) maintaining family connection, and (4) successfully completing his detention and being released. A strategically directed MI session would explore these goals and help Tariq identify how his behavior (bed-making, attitude management) connects to achieving them. Instead, the psychiatrist lectures about willpower and "trying harder" without exploring what Tariq actually wants or what barriers he faces.

Third, the direction is maintained through confrontation rather than collaboration. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands commitment ("Will you commit to behaving better from now on?") without first understanding Tariq's perspective or building motivation collaboratively. This is directive in the worst sense—it's prescriptive and coercive rather than strategically guiding toward change.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains direction but it is rigid, disconnected from the client's actual concerns, and imposed rather than collaboratively developed. The focus on irrelevant topics (smoking/drugs) and failure to connect behavioral change to Tariq's actual goals represents poor strategic direction.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful. This session demonstrates the opposite—a hierarchical, expert-driven, authoritarian stance throughout.

The psychiatrist repeatedly uses commanding language: "you really should," "you have to," "you need to," "you must." The psychiatrist makes unilateral declarations about what Tariq should do without seeking his input, perspective, or agreement. Tariq explicitly requests collaboration: "It helps if someone actually listens to where I'm coming from instead of just telling me to fix it." The psychiatrist's response is to continue telling him to fix it without listening. This is a direct rejection of Tariq's request for partnership.

The power dynamic is entirely one-sided. The psychiatrist holds all authority and uses it punitively ("if you don't, you'll only make your situation worse"). There is no co-construction of solutions, no shared decision-making, and no genuine invitation for Tariq's ideas to shape the session.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no collaboration whatsoever. The session is entirely expert-driven and authoritarian, with the psychiatrist imposing solutions and demanding compliance rather than partnering with Tariq to explore his concerns and co-create change strategies.

### 5. Autonomy Support
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Autonomy support means respecting the client's right to choose and emphasizing their ownership of the change process. This session actively undermines autonomy through coercion, pressure, and threat.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish Genuine Acceptance and Empathy (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Begin every session by communicating unconditional positive regard and demonstrating accurate empathic understanding of the client's perspective before addressing any behavioral concerns.

**Example:** 
- Instead of: "You need to stop feeling sorry for yourself and just do what's necessary"
- Use: "It sounds like losing your phone privileges feels really unfair, especially with your dad's birthday coming up. And you're worried about staying connected to your family while you're here. That's weighing on you. Help me understand more about what that's like."

**Practice:** 
- Record yourself conducting a session and count how many times you use reflective listening statements vs. directive statements. Aim for at least 70% reflective listening in the first half of the session.
- Practice reflecting back what you hear without judgment: "So what I'm hearing is..." before offering any guidance.
- Identify one strength in every client and explicitly affirm it early in the session: "I notice you've been a model resident most of the time you've been here—that shows real commitment to following the rules."

---

### 2. **Listen to the Client's Actual Presenting Concern and Abandon Predetermined Agendas**

**Action:** When a client clarifies their concern ("I don't smoke or use drugs"), accept that clarification and redirect your focus to what actually matters to them. Never continue lecturing about irrelevant topics.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "Have you tried quitting before? You really need to take this seriously. Bad habits like that will only make your situation worse."
- Use: "Got it—so substance use isn't something you're dealing with. That's good to know. So what's really on your mind right now is getting your phone privileges back so you can talk to your mom. Tell me more about why that matters so much."

**Practice:**
- Before each session, write down your predetermined agenda, then consciously set it aside. Commit to following the client's lead for the first 10 minutes.
- When a client corrects you, pause and say: "Thank you for clarifying that. Let me focus on what's actually important to you."
- Ask yourself: "Is this topic relevant to what the client came here to talk about?" If not, redirect.

---

### 3. **Shift from Directive Demands to Collaborative Exploration**

**Action:** Replace "you have to," "you need to," and "you must" with open-ended questions that invite the client to problem-solve with you.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "You have to get your act together if you want to get out in three weeks. You can't let losing phone privileges mess with you—that's just weakness."
- Use: "You mentioned you want to get out on time and see your family. What do you think would help you manage the frustration about losing phone privileges while you're working toward that goal?"

**Practice:**
- In your next 5 sessions, count how many times you use directive language ("have to," "need to," "must," "should"). Set a goal to reduce this by 50%.
- For every directive statement you would normally make, pause and ask an open-ended question instead: "What do you think...?" "How would you...?" "What would help...?"
- Practice collaborative language: "We could explore..." "What if we looked at..." "I'm wondering if..."

---

### 4. **Evoke Rather Than Impose Motivation**

**Action:** Ask strategic questions designed to draw out the client's own reasons for change, values, and resources. Build on their change talk rather than lecturing.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "You need to try harder and stop making excuses"
- Use: "You mentioned you want to get out on time and go home to your family. What does that mean to you? What would it be like to see your mom and dad again?"
- Then: "You also mentioned you're usually a model resident. What helps you follow the rules when you're doing well?"

**Practice:**
- Develop a set of evocative questions and practice them until they feel natural:
  - "What matters most to you about [change goal]?"
  - "What would be different if you [made this change]?"
  - "What are you already doing well that we could build on?"
  - "What would help you manage [barrier] while you're working toward [goal]?"
- In your next session, ask at least 5 open-ended questions before offering any advice or direction.
- When the client expresses change talk, pause and ask a follow-up question to deepen it: "Tell me more about that."

### 5. **Amplify and Reinforce Change Talk**

**Action:** When the client expresses motivation for change, reflect it back, explore it further, and build on it rather than continuing to lecture.

**Example:**

- Instead of: "You need to stop feeling sorry for yourself and start following the rules no matter what."
- Use: "That's important to you—trying to behave better so you can get out on time. What do you think would help you manage your frustration when you're feeling picked on?"

**Practice:**

- Practice reflective responses that strengthen change talk: "So getting out on time and seeing your family is really important to you," "You've shown you can follow the rules—what helps you do that?"

### 6. **Use Autonomy Support Instead of Coercion and Threat**

**Action:** Replace threats and demands with language that emphasizes the client's choice and ownership of the change process.

**Example:**

### 7. **Skillfully Address Sustain Talk with Double-Sided Reflection and Reframing**

**Practice:**
