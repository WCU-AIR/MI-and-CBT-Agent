--- DISORDER ---
Personality Disorders

--- CASE STUDY ---

Henry, a 19-year-old college sophomore, was referred to the student health center by a teaching assistant who noticed he appeared odd, worried, and preoccupied, and that his lab notebook contained bizarrely threatening drawings.

Henry arrived on time for the consultation and, although suspicious of the referral, explained that he generally “followed orders.” He reported feeling that some classmates were undermining him, believing they told instructors he was “a weird guy” and did not want him as a lab partner. He interpreted this referral as confirmation of his suspicions.

Henry described magical thinking, believing coins could predict the future and that his thoughts often came true. He transferred to an out-of-town university at his parents’ request but refused to participate in dorm or social activities, finding them a waste of time.

Collateral information from his mother revealed that Henry had been quiet, shy, and reserved since childhood, with no close friends, no dating history, and minimal desire for social interaction. He had been fascinated by ghosts, telepathy, and witchcraft since junior high and believed he could influence natural events with his thoughts. He denied substance use, and previous drug screens were negative. A family history included a grandfather who died in an asylum.

On examination, Henry was tall and thin, dressed casually, alert, wary, nonspontaneous but cooperative. He denied depression, confusion, suicidal or homicidal ideation, hallucinations, obsessions, compulsions, panic attacks, or phobias. Intellectual skills appeared above average, and Mini-Mental State Examination score was 30/30.

Discussion: Henry exhibits social and interpersonal deficits, eccentricities, and cognitive distortions, including magical thinking, suspiciousness, ideas of reference, and social withdrawal. These features meet DSM-5 criteria for schizotypal personality disorder (SPD). Additionally, his suspicion of others’ intentions and tendency to bear grudges support a diagnosis of paranoid personality disorder (PPD). Though only 19, he is at risk for progression to schizophrenia or other psychiatric disorders, though no active psychotic or mood symptoms are present.
