
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

## 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a complete absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly uses judgmental language and confrontational framing that communicates disapproval of the patient's autonomy and perspective. Specific examples include: "Are you just making excuses or what?" (confrontational, assumes dishonesty), "Why don't you want to commit... Are you just being stubborn?" (labeling and judgment), and "Look, you can't just keep making excuses" (repeated dismissal of patient's stated experience). 

The psychiatrist frames the patient's hesitation as moral failure rather than ambivalence—a core feature of precontemplation. There is no affirmation of the patient's strengths (e.g., he has tried quitting before, he acknowledges marital impact, he shows some insight into his behavior). Instead, the psychiatrist communicates that the patient is irresponsible, in denial, and failing to take health seriously. The repeated use of "you have to," "you need to," and "you must" conveys coercion rather than respect for the patient as a person of worth.

Most critically, when the patient expresses legitimate concerns about forced change ("I need to feel like it's my choice, not just because it's being forced on me"), the psychiatrist responds by actually *prescribing* the patches unilaterally—the opposite of acceptance. This is authoritarian medicine, not MI. There is zero warmth, no genuine curiosity about the patient's perspective, and no communication that the psychiatrist values the patient as an equal human being.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates hostile judgment toward the patient's autonomy and perspective, uses shaming language, and ultimately violates the patient's stated need for choice by unilaterally prescribing treatment. This is fundamentally incompatible with MI's core value of acceptance.

## 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective. This session contains virtually no reflective listening and demonstrates a profound failure to understand or validate the patient's experience. 

The patient repeatedly states: "I'm not ready," "I need to feel like it's my choice," "I'm here because my wife made me come," and "Pushing me just makes me more resistant." The psychiatrist never reflects back any of these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist consistently dismisses them: "No, you can't just keep putting this off. This isn't about what you feel like doing." This is the opposite of empathy—it is explicit rejection of the patient's internal experience.

You want to feel like this is your decision." This would demonstrate understanding. Instead, the psychiatrist responds with "you have to take this seriously" and "you need to get your act together"—language that communicates the psychiatrist does not understand or care about the patient's perspective.

The patient shows a flicker of change talk ("I guess sometimes I wonder if maybe I should try something different"), but the psychiatrist does not reflect or explore this. There is no attempt to understand what the patient values, what his concerns are about patches, or what would make change feel possible to him. The psychiatrist operates from a position of assumed expertise and moral superiority, not genuine curiosity about the patient's world.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no reflective listening, explicitly rejects the patient's stated experience, and shows no genuine effort to understand his perspective. This is a fundamental failure of empathy and represents a barrier to engagement rather than a bridge to it.

## 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI means maintaining strategic focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation) while remaining responsive to the client's process. This session does maintain focus on smoking and nicotine replacement therapy—the psychiatrist never wanders from the topic. However, the execution is severely flawed.

The psychiatrist has a rigid, predetermined direction: "You must start nicotine replacement therapy today." This is not strategic direction; it is inflexible prescription. True direction in MI would involve: (1) exploring the patient's own goals and values, (2) developing discrepancy between those goals and current behavior, (3) progressively clarifying what change might look like, and (4) allowing the patient to move toward that direction at his own pace.

Instead, the psychiatrist uses the same demand repeatedly across eight turns: "Are you willing to try nicotine replacement therapy today?" → "You need to start nicotine replacement therapy today" → "I'm prescribing the patches for you today." This is repetitive coercion, not strategic direction. The psychiatrist does not explore: What does the patient want for his health? What are his concerns about patches? What would make quitting feel possible? What does he value about smoking? What are his past attempts telling us?

The direction is also counterproductive. The patient explicitly states that pressure makes him more resistant ("Pushing me to jump into patches or gum today just makes me more resistant"), yet the psychiatrist responds by increasing pressure. This demonstrates a failure to adjust direction based on client response—a core MI skill.

**Summary:**

While the session maintains topical focus on smoking, the direction is rigid, predetermined, and counterproductive. The psychiatrist prescribes rather than guides, and fails to adjust approach when the patient signals that pressure increases resistance.

## 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful. This session demonstrates zero collaboration—it is entirely authoritarian and expert-driven.

The psychiatrist opens with: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you should do to quit." This explicitly rejects collaboration from the first sentence. Throughout the session, the psychiatrist uses unilateral language: "You have to," "You need to," "You must," "I'm prescribing." Collaborative moments would include: "What do you think might help?" "What have you learned from your past quit attempts?" "What concerns do you have about patches?" When the patient expresses a legitimate concern ("I need to feel like it's my choice"), the psychiatrist responds by removing choice entirely: "I'm prescribing the patches for you today." This is the antithesis of collaboration. The patient is not a partner; he is an object to be managed.

The psychiatrist also dismisses the patient's knowledge of himself: "This isn't about what you feel like doing — it's about what you have to do." The patient knows his own readiness, his own barriers, his own motivations. The psychiatrist assumes the patient's self-knowledge is irrelevant. This is not collaboration; it is contempt for the patient's expertise about his own life.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist operates from a position of unilateral authority, explicitly rejects collaboration, and treats the patient as a passive object rather than an equal partner. This is fundamentally incompatible with MI.

## 5. Autonomy Support
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Autonomy support means respecting the client's right to choose and emphasizing their ownership of the change process. This session is a systematic violation of autonomy at every turn.

The patient explicitly states his need for autonomy multiple times: "I don't want to feel like I'm being forced into something," "I need to feel like it's my choice, not just because it's being forced on me," "I don't like feeling rushed or pushed around." - "You need to quit now before it gets worse."
- "You have to take this seriously or you'll regret it later."

Critically, the patient's resistance is a direct response to autonomy violation. He states: "I don't like feeling rushed or pushed around... forcing me into patches or gum today isn't going to change that." ## Recommendations

### 1. **Adopt a Collaborative, Partnership-Based Stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Begin the session by explicitly positioning the patient as a partner and expert on his own life. Communicate that you are here to understand his perspective and work together, not to tell him what to do.

**Example:** 
- Instead of: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you should do to quit."
- Use: "I appreciate you coming in today. I know your wife encouraged you to be here. I'm curious to understand your perspective on your smoking—what's your take on it? What matters to you about this?"

This immediately communicates respect, partnership, and genuine curiosity rather than judgment and prescription.

**Practice:** 
- Before each session, remind yourself: "My job is not to convince this patient to change. My job is to help them explore their own reasons for change."
- Record yourself and listen for how many times you use "you have to," "you need to," "you must." Replace these with "I wonder..." "What do you think..." "Tell me more about..."
- Practice with a colleague: have them play a resistant patient and practice opening statements that communicate partnership rather than prescription.

---

### 2. **Use Reflective Listening to Demonstrate Empathy and Build Alliance**

**Action:** After the patient speaks, reflect back what you heard—both the content and the emotion. This shows you understand and validates their experience.

**Example:**
- Patient: "I don't think it's a big problem. I can control it."
- Instead of: "You have to stop thinking it's no big deal because it definitely is."
- Use: "So from your perspective, you've got it under control and it's not something you're worried about right now. That makes sense."

Then, after validating, you can gently introduce discrepancy: "And at the same time, you mentioned it sometimes causes fights at home. So there's a part of you that notices it's affecting your relationship."

**Practice:**
- For every patient statement, pause and reflect before responding. Make it a habit.
- Use the formula: "So what I'm hearing is [content] and it sounds like [emotion]. Is that right?"
- Practice complex reflections that capture both sides of ambivalence: "On one hand... and on the other hand..."

---

### 3. **Ask Open Questions to Evoke the Patient's Own Motivations**

**Action:** Replace directive statements with genuine questions designed to help the patient explore his own reasons for change. Ask about values, goals, concerns, and past experiences.

**Example Questions:**
- "What's your take on your smoking? How do you see it?"
- "What would be different if you quit?"
- "What matters to you most in your life right now?"
- "How does smoking fit with what matters to you?"
- "You mentioned it sometimes causes fights at home. Tell me more about that."
- "What have you learned from your past quit attempts?"
- "What concerns do you have about quitting?"
- "What would need to happen for you to feel ready to make a change?"

**Practice:**
- Write out 10 open questions related to smoking and motivation for change. Practice asking them naturally.
- In sessions, aim for a 70/30 ratio: 70% patient talking, 30% clinician talking. If you're talking more than 30%, you're telling too much.
- After asking a question, wait. Resist the urge to fill silence. Let the patient think and respond.

---

### 4. **Recognize and Amplify Change Talk**

**Action:** When the patient expresses any openness to change, desire for change, ability to change, or reasons for change, immediately recognize it and explore it further. This is how you build motivation.

**Example:**
- Patient: "I guess sometimes I wonder if maybe I should try something different."
- Instead of: Ignoring it and continuing with prescription.
- Use: "You've had some thoughts about trying something different. Tell me more about that. What's that about?"

Then reflect and explore: "So you're thinking there might be another way to handle this. What appeals to you about that?"

**Practice:**
- Listen for change talk: desire ("I want to..."), ability ("I could..."), reasons ("It would help because..."), need ("I should..."), commitment ("I will...").
- When you hear it, pause and explore it. Ask follow-up questions: "Tell me more." "What else?" "How would that help?"
- Reflect it back to strengthen it: "So one reason you might want to quit is to improve things with your wife. That's important to you."

---

### 5. **Respect Autonomy and Avoid Coercion**

**Action:** Explicitly communicate that the patient has the right to choose, and that change is ultimately their decision. When you sense resistance, reduce pressure rather than increase it.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "You have to start nicotine replacement therapy today."
- Use: "There are different options for quitting—patches, gum, going cold turkey, counseling. Some people find one approach works better than others. What do you think might work for you, if you decided to make a change?"

Or: "This is ultimately your decision. I can share what research shows, but whether and when you use patches is completely up to you."

**Practice:**
- Notice when you feel the urge to pressure or convince. That's a signal to step back and increase autonomy support instead.
- When a patient says "I'm not ready," resist the urge to argue. Instead, reflect: "You're not feeling ready right now. That's okay. What would need to happen for you to feel ready?"
- Use language that emphasizes choice: "You could..." "You might..." "It's up to you..." "What feels right to you?"

---

### 6. **Develop Discrepancy Without Confrontation**

**Action:** Help the patient see the gap between their values/goals and their current behavior, but do this through reflection and exploration, not argument. Use double-sided reflections to validate ambivalence.

**Example:**
- Patient: "I don't think it's a big problem. I can control it."
- Instead of: "You have to stop thinking it's no big deal because it definitely is."
- Use: "So on one hand, you feel like you've got it under control, and on the other hand, you've mentioned it's causing some tension at home. Both of those things are true for you right now."

Then explore: "How do you make sense of that? What do you think about that?"

**Practice:**
- When you notice discrepancy (patient says smoking isn't a problem but also says it causes fights), reflect both sides: "So... and at the same time..."
- Ask the patient to make sense of the discrepancy rather than telling them: "What do you make of that?" "How do you put those two things together?"
- Avoid arguing. Your job is to help them see the discrepancy, not to convince them it exists.

---

### 7. **Soften Sustain Talk Through Strategic Responses, Not Confrontation**

**Action:** When the patient expresses reasons not to change, don't argue against them. Instead, use techniques like reflection, reframing, and strategic questioning to gently shift perspective.

**Example:**
- Patient: "I'm not ready to quit. I can handle it on my own."
- Instead of: "No, you can't just keep putting this off. You need to take this seriously."
- Use: "You feel like you can manage it yourself right now. And you've also mentioned it's causing some fights at home. So there's a part of you that's managing it, and a part of you that's noticing it's affecting your relationship. What do you think about that?"

Or: "You've tried quitting before and it didn't stick. What did you learn from that? What would be different this time?"

**Practice:**
- When you hear sustain talk, pause. Don't argue.
- Use a double-sided reflection: "On one hand... and on the other hand..."
- Ask a strategic question that gently shifts perspective: "What would your wife say about that?" "How do you think this affects your health long-term?" "What would be different if you quit?"
- Remember: the more you argue against sustain talk, the more the patient argues for it. Your job is to soften it, not fight it.

---

### 8. **Avoid Unilateral Prescription; Collaborate on Treatment Planning**

**Action:** Instead of prescribing treatment, explore options together and let the patient choose what feels right to them. This increases adherence and respects autonomy.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "I'm prescribing the patches for you today. You have to use them as directed."
- Use: "There are several options that research shows can help with quitting: nicotine patches, gum, prescription medications, counseling, or a combination. Some people find one works better than others. What have you heard about these? What do you think might work for you?"

Then: "If you decided to try patches, when do you think would be a good time to start? What would help you stick with it?"

**Practice:**
- In treatment planning, ask the patient: "What do you think would help?" "What feels doable to you?" "What are you willing to try?"
- Offer options rather than directives: "You could try... or you could try... What appeals to you?"
- If the patient isn't ready, that's okay. Explore what would need to happen: "What would need to happen for you to feel ready?"

---

### 9. **Recognize and Respond to Psychological Reactance**

**Action:** When you sense resistance or the patient explicitly states that pressure makes them more resistant, immediately reduce pressure and increase autonomy support. This is a critical skill.

**Example:**
- Patient: "Pushing me just makes me more resistant."
- Instead of: Increasing pressure ("You have to do this now").
- Use: "I hear you. You don't like feeling pushed. That makes sense. This is your decision to make. What do you think would help?"

**Practice:**
- Notice when a patient is becoming more resistant. That's a signal to change your approach.
- When you hear "I'm not ready," "I don't want to be forced," "I need to do this my way," these are signals to reduce pressure and increase autonomy support.
- Remember: resistance is not stubbornness or denial. It's a signal that your approach isn't working. Adjust.
