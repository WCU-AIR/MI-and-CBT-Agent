# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal unconditional positive regard demonstrated. While opening is warm, the psychiatrist quickly shifts to a directive, expert-driven approach. Luka's progress (cutting down drinking, increased partner communication) is not meaningfully affirmed. Partial change is treated as insufficient rather than legitimate. Luka's ambivalence about complete abstinence is framed as a problem to solve rather than a normal part of change. When Luka expresses concern about social consequences of avoiding his mates, this legitimate concern is dismissed rather than validated. Overall stance communicates implicit judgment that Luka's current efforts are inadequate.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Superficial and inconsistent empathy. Minimal reflective listening and limited demonstration of accurate understanding of Luka's internal experience. When Luka describes the subjective experience of anger ("it's like something switches inside me"), the psychiatrist does not reflect this emotional insight back. Complex reflections are absent; reflections offered are simple and factual. Luka's emotional experience, ambivalence, and legitimate concerns (fear of social exclusion, difficulty with follow-through) are not accurately reflected. The psychiatrist asks diagnostic questions but does not use reflections to deepen understanding of Luka's perspective, values, or emotional experience.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clear focus on target behaviors (reducing drinking and managing anger) is maintained appropriately. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. The psychiatrist identifies problems and prescribes predetermined solutions (avoid risky places, practice deep breathing, counting to ten) without exploring Luka's own thinking about direction and change. The final treatment plan is presented as if it were Luka's plan, but it was largely constructed by the psychiatrist through directive suggestions. Luka's agreement represents compliance rather than genuine ownership of direction.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Profound absence of genuine collaboration. Session structure is consistently: psychiatrist asks questions → psychiatrist interprets answers → psychiatrist suggests solutions → patient complies. No joint problem-solving occurs. When Luka identifies triggers, the psychiatrist immediately moves to prescriptive suggestions rather than exploring collaboratively. The psychiatrist never asks "What do you think would help?" or "What ideas do you have?" Power dynamic is entirely unequal—psychiatrist holds expertise and dispenses it; Luka receives and complies. When psychiatrist says "How about setting a goal to try deep breathing or counting to ten...?" this is a directive dressed as a question, not genuine collaboration.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is virtually absent. Language is repeatedly prescriptive: "you might want to think about," "you should try," "you might want to give those a shot," "you should try setting some clear goals." When Luka expresses legitimate concern about social consequences ("if I'm not there, they'll think I'm avoiding them"), the psychiatrist overrides this concern rather than supporting his autonomy to weigh it and make his own decision. Psychiatrist never says "What do you think is best for you?" or "You know your situation best—what feels right?" Luka is not genuinely being asked whether he wants to follow the plan; he is being asked to agree to the psychiatrist's plan. No genuine respect for Luka's right to choose or his expertise about his own life.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources—is almost entirely absent. Luka spontaneously offers substantial change talk ("I've cut down on the drink," "Talking more with my partner has helped," "I want to get better at that," "I'm willing to give it a shot"), but psychiatrist does not evoke or amplify this. When Luka says "I've cut down," psychiatrist does not explore: "Tell me more about how you managed that." Instead, psychiatrist asks diagnostic questions ("Do you find it hard to stick to your plans?") that invite elaboration on problems rather than strengths. Psychiatrist never asks "What do you want for yourself?" or "What matters most to you?" or "What strengths do you have that could help?" Psychiatrist tells rather than asks; directs rather than evokes.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership—the understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client—is fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role throughout. Language consistently positions psychiatrist as expert and Luka as recipient: "It would probably help if you could identify those triggers," "Maybe setting some limits on where you go," "You might want to practice those techniques." Psychiatrist never invites Luka to be co-expert. Does not say "I'm wondering what you think about..." or "You might have some ideas about..." or "What's your sense of...?" When Luka expresses uncertainty, psychiatrist generates strategies rather than inviting Luka to generate them. Final summary presents psychiatrist's plan as if it were Luka's plan. No acknowledgment or invitation of Luka's expertise about his own life and change process.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Luka offers substantial spontaneous change talk throughout session, but psychiatrist does little to cultivate, amplify, or reinforce it. Critical missed opportunity. When Luka says "I've cut down on the drink," psychiatrist does not reflect and amplify this progress. When Luka says "Talking more with my partner has helped too," psychiatrist does not explore further but instead pivots to problems ("But honestly, the anger part is harder"). When Luka rates confidence at 6/10 and mentions progress made, psychiatrist does not amplify: "So you've already made real changes—what else might help you move higher?" When Luka says "I'm willing to give it a shot," this commitment language is not strengthened. Psychiatrist does ask one scaling question but does not use response to evoke further change talk.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist does not skillfully soften sustain talk. When Luka expresses resistance or difficulty, psychiatrist often reinforces it by focusing on the difficulty or overrides it by dismissing concerns. When Luka says "It's not always easy" regarding avoiding risky places, psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflection or reframe; instead essentially dismisses concern. When Luka expresses legitimate fear about social consequences, psychiatrist does not validate or explore; instead tells Luka to avoid situations anyway. Minimal use of reframing or strategic redirection toward change talk. One area handled reasonably well: psychiatrist does not argue with sustain talk or use confrontation. However, absence of confrontation is not the same as skillfully softening resistance.

---

### Overall Clinical Summary

**Session Score: 1.6/5**

**Strengths:**
- Clear, consistent focus on target behaviors (reducing drinking, managing anger)
- Absence of confrontational language; does not argue with client
- Warm, welcoming opening that sets positive tone
- Acknowledgment of partner's supportive role
- Offers concrete, evidence-based strategies (deep breathing, counting to ten)

**Critical Deficiencies:**
This session represents a fundamental departure from motivational interviewing principles and spirit. The psychiatrist operates from a traditional expert, directive model rather than a collaborative, evocative MI model. Core MI elements—collaboration, evocation, autonomy support, and genuine empathy—are largely absent.

**Impact on Client Engagement:**
This session likely undermines Luka's intrinsic motivation for change. While Luka enters with genuine change talk and willingness, the psychiatrist's directive approach may trigger reactance—the psychological response to perceived threats to autonomy. Luka's agreement to the final plan appears to be compliance rather than genuine commitment. The session misses critical opportunities to build on Luka's existing strengths, explore his ambivalence, and help him develop his own sustainable plan for change.

**Recommendations for Development:**
1. **Shift from expert to collaborative stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY):** Fundamentally reframe role from expert who prescribes to collaborative partner who helps client discover solutions.
2. **Evoke and amplify change talk:** When client offers change talk, pause and explore it rather than pivoting to problems.
3. **Use complex reflections:** Move beyond simple factual reflections to capture emotional nuance and ambivalence.
4. **Validate ambivalence:** When client expresses concerns, validate and explore collaboratively rather than override.
5. **Ask before telling:** Always invite client ideas first before offering suggestions.
6. **Affirm progress:** Explicitly affirm changes already made and strengths demonstrated.
7. **Use autonomy-supportive language:** Replace directive language with language that respects client's right to choose.
8. **Develop confidence rulers skillfully:** Use scales as evocative tools, asking what's working and what would help move higher.
9. **Reduce advice-giving:** Minimize unsolicited advice; increase collaborative problem-solving.
10. **Attend to MI spirit:** Reflect regularly on whether you embodied partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation.

**MI Competency Assessment:** Minimal MI competency demonstrated. Psychiatrist demonstrates foundational skills (warmth, non-confrontational stance, clear focus) but needs to fundamentally shift from expert, directive model to collaborative, evocative model. Formal MI training with feedback on recorded sessions is strongly recommended.
