
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Feedback: Therapist Evaluation

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made minimal effort to establish a structured, collaborative agenda. In the opening, the therapist offered vague language: "I thought maybe we could just chat a bit about how things have been going for you recently, maybe focus on any problems or worries you've had." This is not agenda-setting—it's an invitation to free-form discussion. No specific target problems were identified collaboratively. No time allocation was discussed. No prioritization occurred.

Critically, the therapist repeated this identical opening in the second session ("So, maybe we can just talk a bit about how things have been going for you lately, and if there's anything bothering you or causing some worries"), suggesting no learning or progression between sessions. For a 15-year-old with Acute Stress Disorder presenting with multiple impairing symptoms (sleep disturbance, intrusive images, avoidance, hypervigilance, concentration problems, irritability), a structured agenda is essential. The therapist should have explicitly identified: (1) specific target symptoms to address today, (2) time allocation, (3) whether this session would focus on psychoeducation, symptom monitoring, or skill-building, and (4) how today's work connects to overall treatment goals.

The absence of agenda-setting is particularly problematic because Dylan is avoidant ("I don't really like talking about what happened") and the session drifted into general supportive conversation rather than focused CBT work. A clear agenda would have provided structure and helped Dylan understand the rationale for discussing difficult material.

**Summary:**
Agenda was not established collaboratively or explicitly. The therapist offered open-ended invitations rather than structured problem identification, and this pattern repeated identically in session two, indicating no progress or learning.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated basic comprehension of Dylan's explicit statements but missed critical nuances and the underlying emotional reality. The therapist accurately reflected that Dylan experiences intrusive images, hyperarousal, and avoidance: "So, it sounds like the accident and those memories are really bothering you." This is surface-level empathy.

However, the therapist missed Dylan's core struggle: the disconnect between intellectual knowledge ("I know we're safe now") and emotional/somatic reality ("it's hard to really believe it in the moment"). Dylan explicitly articulated this: "Even though I know we're safe now, it's hard to really believe it in the moment. My heart just races and I feel like I need to get away." This is a sophisticated observation about the nature of trauma—that rational reassurance alone cannot override conditioned fear responses. A therapist with deeper understanding would have validated this insight and explained why simple cognitive restructuring ("just tell yourself you're safe") is insufficient for trauma, and why exposure-based or somatic interventions are needed.

Dylan also expressed frustration about feeling "stuck"—unable to act despite the urge to escape. The therapist acknowledged this ("That sounds really tough, and it's understandable you'd feel stuck and scared") but did not explore what "stuck" means or how it relates to his avoidance patterns and safety behaviors.

Trying not to think about it, and then feeling angry or upset when I do." The therapist did not explore the relationship between avoidance efforts and symptom maintenance—a core CBT concept for ASD.

**Summary:**
The therapist grasped explicit content but missed Dylan's deeper struggle with the gap between knowing and feeling, the role of avoidance in maintaining symptoms, and the somatic nature of his fear responses.
