
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

His main difficulties involve exaggerated fight-or-flight responses, hypervigilance, and re-experiencing trauma through intrusive memories and nightmares. His reactions are involuntary, sudden, and disproportionate, affecting social, family, and recreational functioning. Treatment planning should consider his independence, avoidance tendencies, and the importance of building trust for psychotherapy.

## Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Feedback: Therapist Evaluation

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

No agenda was established at any point in this session. The therapist opened with a casual, unstructured greeting ("Hey there! How's it going today?") without collaboratively identifying session goals, target problems, or priorities. For a patient with severe, decades-long PTSD presenting with hypervigilance, rage responses, nightmares, and intrusive memories, the absence of agenda-setting is a critical failure. The session drifted aimlessly through multiple tangential topics: the patient's morning noise trigger, gardening, woodworking, sleep difficulties, caffeine intake, counting sheep, warm baths, and hydration—without any coherent treatment plan or prioritization. A proper agenda would have identified that Eric presents with four distinct PTSD symptom clusters requiring targeted intervention: (1) intrusive memories/nightmares, (2) hypervigilance and startle response, (3) avoidance behaviors, and (4) irritability/rage. Instead, the therapist never established which symptom to address first, what the session's specific goals were, or how time would be allocated.

The therapist also failed to establish priorities collaboratively. Eric explicitly stated, "I don't like feeling on edge all the time" and "the nightmares wake me up," yet the therapist never asked, "Which of these concerns would be most helpful to focus on today?" or "What would make the biggest difference in your life right now?" This represents a fundamental violation of CBT structure.

**Summary:**

The therapist completely failed to establish a collaborative agenda, identify target problems, or prioritize treatment goals. This session lacked basic structural organization essential to effective CBT.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made minimal, inadequate attempts to elicit feedback throughout the session. While the patient asked the therapist direct questions multiple times ("How about you?" "Do you think it's normal to feel that way sometimes?" "Do you have any tricks for calming down?" "How do you usually keep your spirits up?"), the therapist never once asked Eric for feedback about the session itself, his understanding of interventions, or whether suggestions were helpful or realistic.

- "Just keep your chin up and think positive"

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated poor understanding of Eric's internal reality and the nature of his PTSD. While the therapist reflected some explicit statements ("Being around nature is great," "You've been keeping busy"), these reflections were superficial and missed the profound psychological mechanisms underlying Eric's symptoms.

Critical failures in understanding:

1. **Misunderstanding trauma-related hyperarousal:** Eric explicitly described physiological panic responses ("my heart races, and sweat breaks out") and involuntary startle reactions to unexpected stimuli. The therapist normalized these as universal sleep difficulties ("Everyone has those nights when sleep just doesn't come easy") rather than recognizing them as PTSD hyperarousal symptoms requiring trauma-specific intervention.

2. **Minimizing intrusive memories:** Eric stated, "the nightmares wake me up, and no amount of thinking positive helps then" and "the memories hit out of nowhere—they pull me back." This is a direct statement that positive thinking is ineffective for his trauma symptoms. The therapist's repeated suggestion to "think positive" and "keep your chin up" demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding that PTSD intrusive symptoms are involuntary and not amenable to simple cognitive reframing.

3. **Missing the avoidance pattern:** Eric said, "I don't talk much about those because it feels like opening a door I want to keep closed" and "sometimes I just want to get to bed quickly so the nightmares don't catch me." These are classic avoidance behaviors central to PTSD maintenance. The therapist never explored this or recognized that avoidance perpetuates symptoms.

4. **Failing to recognize shame and guilt:** The case formulation explicitly notes Eric experiences "shame, guilt, and distress" related to witnessing soldiers' deaths. The therapist never inquired about these emotional sequelae or their impact on his willingness to engage in treatment.

5. **Misattributing symptoms to universal stress:** The therapist repeatedly compared Eric's PTSD to personal anecdotes ("I stayed up all night before a big exam," "I was feeling really down and took a walk"). This demonstrates a fundamental failure to recognize that combat trauma and PTSD are qualitatively different from normal stress.

The therapist's empathy was surface-level ("I totally get where you're coming from") without demonstrating genuine comprehension of Eric's lived experience as a combat veteran with decades of untreated trauma.

**Summary:**

The therapist demonstrated poor understanding of PTSD pathophysiology, repeatedly normalizing trauma symptoms as universal stress and failing to recognize the involuntary nature of intrusive memories and hyperarousal.

**Practice:**
