# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no unconditional positive regard or nonjudgmental stance. Repeatedly communicates disapproval and conditional regard based on compliance ("No excuses, no second chances"). Frames Jordan's legitimate concerns about isolation as personal weakness rather than normalizing human struggle. Explicitly rejects supportive engagement ("I'm not here to coddle you"), violating MI's foundational principle of acceptance.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Minimal empathic understanding demonstrated. Uses pseudo-empathy (token acknowledgments followed by dismissal: "I understand it feels hard, but you really have to push through that fear"). Never uses complex reflections capturing emotional depth or Jordan's core conflict between desire to change and fear of isolation. Dismisses legitimate concerns as "excuses" rather than exploring them. No evidence of accurate reflective listening or genuine effort to understand Jordan's internal experience.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains focus on target behaviors (shoplifting, substance use, avoiding friends) but imposes direction rather than collaboratively exploring it. Direction is non-negotiable and externally imposed. No exploration of Jordan's own goals, values, or vision for change. Abrupt mid-session shift to smoking/substance use suggests checklist-driven approach rather than strategic focus. Direction should emerge from client's motivations; here it is dictated by clinician.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Explicitly rejects collaborative partnership. Opens with "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do" and states "I'm not here to coddle you." Uses authoritarian language throughout ("you have to," "you must," "you need to," "I expect you to follow through"). Treats Jordan as passive recipient of directives rather than equal partner. Never solicits Jordan's input on solutions or co-constructs plan. Unilateral expert stance dominates entire session.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Systematically undermines autonomy through pressure, threats, and conditional regard. Uses coercive language: "You have to cut off those friends," "You can't afford to wait," "If you don't commit to cutting ties, you're just digging your own hole." Pressures Jordan despite his explicit statement of unreadiness ("I'm not ready to commit to cutting them off just yet"). Final compliance appears to be pressure-driven rather than autonomous choice. Research shows externally motivated change is less sustainable than intrinsically motivated change.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Tells rather than asks. Never asks open-ended questions about Jordan's own reasons for change or explores his values and resources. When Jordan offers change talk ("I want to stop, I really do," "I'm tired of feeling stuck and guilty"), psychiatrist fails to amplify or explore it, instead pivoting to demands. No exploration of Jordan's ideas for solutions. Never asks about self-efficacy or confidence. Imposes solutions (cut off friends, quit cold turkey) without evoking client's own wisdom or motivation.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Explicitly assumes expert role and rejects partnership. Communicates that expertise and wisdom reside solely with clinician, not client. Never treats Jordan's concerns as valid expertise about his own life. Never asks "What do you think?" or "Let's figure this out together." Positions himself as authority figure and Jordan as passive recipient. Fundamental violation of MI principle that client is expert on own change process.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Fails to evoke, amplify, or reinforce Jordan's multiple instances of change talk: "I want to stop, I really do," "I'm tired of feeling stuck and guilty," "I'm willing to start trying," "I don't wanna keep living like this." When Jordan offers change talk, psychiatrist responds with pressure and demands rather than deepening exploration. Never asks follow-up questions ("Tell me more about that") or celebrates change talk. Directive language may actually suppress intrinsic motivation by replacing it with external pressure.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Handles sustain talk through confrontation and dismissal rather than skillful exploration. When Jordan expresses fear of isolation ("If I just cut them off, I'd be alone"), psychiatrist responds with pressure ("You have to be stronger") rather than exploring legitimate concern. Frames sustain talk as weakness ("You can't let it be an excuse") instead of validating it. One attempt at redirect ("Just try to avoid those friends for a while") is slightly better than pure confrontation but remains directive. Research shows confronting sustain talk directly often strengthens resistance through psychological reactance.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.1/5**

**Session Characteristics:**
This session represents a fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles and spirit. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance characterized by pressure, threats, and conditional regard. Rather than evoking intrinsic motivation through collaboration and empathy, the clinician imposes directives and demands compliance.

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Authoritarian stance explicitly rejecting partnership and collaboration
- Repeated use of pressure language ("you have to," "you must," "you can't")
- Dismissal of legitimate concerns as "excuses" and "weakness"
- Failure to evoke or amplify Jordan's change talk
- Confrontation of sustain talk rather than skillful exploration
- Pseudo-empathy followed by invalidation
- Conditional regard ("No excuses, no second chances")
- Apparent compliance achieved through coercion, not genuine motivation

**Impact on Client Engagement:**
- Reduced intrinsic motivation for change
- Risk of psychological reactance and increased resistance
- Damaged therapeutic alliance
- Unaddressed ambivalence regarding core conflict (desire to change vs. fear of isolation)
- Increased shame and defensiveness
- Low likelihood of sustained behavior change

**Prognosis for Sustained Change:** Poor. External pressure-driven compliance is less sustainable than intrinsically motivated change. Jordan is likely to either superficially comply and relapse, experience reactance and actively resist, or disengage from treatment.

**Immediate Recommendations:**
1. Fundamental shift from expert-driven to collaborative, partnership-based stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
2. Development of genuine empathy and validation for ambivalence
3. Elimination of pressure, threats, and conditional regard language
4. Learning to evoke rather than impose change talk
5. Formal training in Motivational Interviewing through MINT or equivalent program
6. Supervised practice with MI trainer and regular session review

**Whether Session Embodies MI Spirit:** No. This session actively violates MI spirit through authoritarian control, pressure tactics, and dismissal of client autonomy. Immediate retraining is essential.
