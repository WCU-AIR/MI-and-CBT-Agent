
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda

### 3. Understanding

### 7. Guided Discovery
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist relied almost exclusively on direct advice and reassurance rather than guided discovery. This is a fundamental failure in CBT technique. Guided discovery involves asking skillful questions that help the patient reach insights independently, examine evidence, consider alternatives, and test beliefs. The therapist did the opposite—he told the patient what to think and do.

**Evidence of failure to use guided discovery:**

1. **Direct advice instead of questions:** 
   - Therapist: "Maybe it would help to try thinking about those thoughts differently, like reminding yourself that you did what you could and that some things are beyond your control."
   - This is direct advice. A guided discovery approach would be: "When you think, 'If I had pushed her more, she'd still be here,' what evidence do you have for that thought? What evidence is there against it? What else might have contributed to her death?"

2. **Leading questions that suggest answers:**
   - Therapist: "When you notice those thoughts about her, what usually goes through your mind? Like, what kind of thoughts pop up? Maybe something like 'I can't stop thinking about her,' or 'I'm worried something bad will happen'? Or is it more like feeling guilty or sad?"
   - This is a leading question that suggests answers rather than inviting genuine discovery. A guided discovery question would be: "When those thoughts about your grandmother come up, what goes through your mind? What do you notice?"

3. **Reassurance instead of exploration:**
   - Therapist: "That sounds really tough, carrying that guilt and sadness. It's understandable to feel that way."
   - While empathic, this does not facilitate discovery. A guided discovery response would be: "I hear that the guilt feels very real to you. Let's explore that together. What specifically do you feel guilty about? What would it mean if you were responsible?"

4. **Prescriptive suggestions without exploration:**
   - Therapist: "Maybe it would help to do some activities that distract you or make you feel a bit better, like going for a walk or listening to music."
   - This is prescriptive. Guided discovery would involve: "What have you found helpful in the past when you're feeling this way? What activities used to bring you joy? What might be a small step you could take?"

5. **No examination of beliefs:**
   - The patient stated, "I know logically it's not all my fault, but I can't get those thoughts out of my head." This is a critical moment for guided discovery—exploring the gap between intellectual and emotional belief. The therapist could have asked: "What would it take for you to really believe, not just know, that you're not responsible? What evidence would convince you? What does your heart say versus your head?" Instead, the therapist offered reassurance.

6. **No behavioral experiments or hypothesis testing:**
   - The therapist did not suggest testing beliefs through behavioral experiments. For example: "You mentioned that distraction helps temporarily. What if we tried something different—what if you allowed yourself to sit with the sadness for 5 minutes without trying to push it away? What do you think would happen? Let's try it and see."

7. **Acceptance suggestion without exploration:**
   - Therapist: "Maybe you could try noticing the feelings without judging them, just letting them be there for a bit."
   - This is a prescriptive suggestion. Guided discovery would involve: "You mentioned that when you let yourself feel the sadness fully, you freeze up or shut down. What do you think would happen if we explored that together? What are you afraid might happen if you sit with the sadness?"

8. **No Socratic questioning in Session 2:**
   - When the patient expressed doubt about acceptance ("I'm not sure about just letting them sit there without judging—I guess it sounds nice in theory, but I'm worried it would just make me feel worse"), the therapist did not use Socratic questions to explore this concern. Instead, the therapist moved on without addressing it.

**Summary:**

The therapist abandoned guided discovery in favor of direct advice, reassurance, and prescriptive suggestions. This represents a critical failure in CBT technique and prevents the patient from developing insight or agency in his treatment.
