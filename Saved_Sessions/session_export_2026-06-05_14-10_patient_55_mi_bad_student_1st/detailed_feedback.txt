
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and contains multiple instances of explicit judgment and disapproval. The psychiatrist opens with a directive stance ("I'll be telling you exactly what to do"), immediately signaling that the client's autonomy and perspective are not valued. Throughout the session, the psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates Isabella's experience and reasoning: "No, no, you're wrong," "you really have to stop making excuses," "Why are you still defending it?" and "Don't you care about your baby's health?"—a rhetorical question that implies moral failure.

Most critically, the psychiatrist frames Isabella's stated values (autonomy, family tradition, independent decision-making) as obstacles rather than legitimate aspects of her identity worthy of respect. When Isabella explicitly states, "I don't like being told what to do—it feels like people don't trust me to make good decisions," the psychiatrist proceeds to do exactly that—tell her what to do—without acknowledging or validating her concern. The repeated use of "you must," "you have to," and "this isn't negotiable" communicates contempt for her perspective. There is no affirmation of her genuine concern for her baby's health, no normalization of her struggle with cultural traditions and medical advice, and no warmth or genuine support. Instead, the psychiatrist treats her as a problem to be corrected rather than a person to be understood.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates consistently judgmental, disapproving language and an authoritarian stance that directly contradicts MI principles of unconditional positive regard. No genuine acceptance or affirmation is evident.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective. This session contains virtually no reflective listening and demonstrates a fundamental failure to understand Isabella's experience. The psychiatrist never reflects back what Isabella has shared about her cultural background, her values around autonomy, her genuine concern for her baby, or her ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses her perspective with statements like "you're wrong," "stop making excuses," and "you can't keep making excuses."

When Isabella articulates her core value—"It's really important to me to have a healthy baby and be a good parent"—the psychiatrist does not acknowledge or build upon this. Instead, the psychiatrist responds with "Don't you care about your baby's health?" implying that Isabella's stated concern is false. This is a profound empathic failure. Isabella also explicitly states her need for autonomy ("I'm the one who has to decide," "I want to make these decisions with some respect for my independence"), yet the psychiatrist responds by pressuring her further, demonstrating no understanding of why autonomy matters to her or how pressure triggers defensiveness.

There are no complex reflections capturing emotional nuance. There is no attempt to understand the tension Isabella experiences between cultural tradition and medical advice, between independence and responsibility to her baby. The psychiatrist's only response to Isabella's ambivalence is to deny it exists and demand immediate compliance.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal reflective listening, no attempt to understand Isabella's perspective, and repeated invalidation of her stated concerns and values. This represents a fundamental empathic failure.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

While the psychiatrist maintains a clear focus on alcohol abstinence during pregnancy—which is clinically appropriate—the *method* of maintaining direction is antithetical to MI. Direction in MI should involve strategic guidance that maintains focus while remaining client-centered and collaborative. This psychiatrist maintains focus through coercion and repetition rather than skillful evocation.

The psychiatrist does stay on topic and does not allow the conversation to wander. However, the approach is to repeat the same directive ("you must quit now," "you have to stop drinking tonight") without ever exploring Isabella's reasons for drinking, her barriers to change, her values, or her readiness. A more MI-consistent approach would maintain direction by asking: "What would it be like to stop drinking?" "What concerns do you have about quitting?" "How does this fit with being the parent you want to be?" Instead, the psychiatrist simply reasserts the demand.

The repeated questioning "Why didn't you try cutting down already?" "Why are you still defending it?" "Why won't you just do what's necessary?" and "Are you going to stop drinking tonight or not?" are rhetorical and accusatory rather than genuine exploration. They maintain direction through pressure, not through collaborative problem-solving. This approach may temporarily suppress discussion but will not build intrinsic motivation for change.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains clear focus on the target behavior but does so through coercive repetition rather than strategic, client-centered guidance. Direction is present but poorly executed.

### **Score: 1/5**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful. This session demonstrates an explicitly hierarchical, expert-driven stance from the opening statement: "I'll be telling you exactly what to do." The psychiatrist positions themselves as the sole authority on what Isabella should do and frames the session as a one-way transmission of directives rather than joint exploration.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Adopt a Collaborative, Non-Directive Stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Replace directive language ("you must," "you have to," "this isn't negotiable") with collaborative language that respects the client's autonomy and expertise about their own life.

**Example:** Instead of "You really need to cut back or quit if you want to feel better. Just stopping the wine will make a huge difference, trust me. Why didn't you try cutting down already?" try:

"I hear that wine is part of your family tradition and how you relax. I'm also hearing that being a good parent and having a healthy baby is really important to you. I'm wondering—how do you see your drinking fitting with the kind of parent you want to be? What are your thoughts about that?"

**Practice:** Before each session, write out your opening statement and review it for directive language. Replace "you must/have to/need to" with "I'm wondering," "What are your thoughts about," "How do you see," "Tell me more about." Practice with a colleague or supervisor until collaborative language becomes automatic.

---

### 2. **Validate and Affirm the Client's Autonomy and Values (SECOND PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Explicitly acknowledge and respect the client's need for autonomy, and affirm her genuine values (being a good parent, protecting her baby, maintaining family traditions).

**Example:** When Isabella says "I don't like being told what to do—it feels like people don't trust me to make good decisions," respond with:

"I really appreciate you telling me that. You're right—you know yourself best, and you're the expert on your own life. I'm not here to tell you what to do. I'm here to explore this with you and support whatever decision you make. It sounds like being independent and making your own choices is really important to you, and I respect that. At the same time, I hear that being a good parent and having a healthy baby is also really important. Let's talk about how we can honor both of those things."

**Practice:** In each session, identify the client's stated values and autonomy needs. Write them down. Explicitly affirm them early in the session. Use language like "I respect that," "That makes sense," "That's important," "You're the expert on your own life."

---

### 3. **Use Reflective Listening to Demonstrate Understanding (THIRD PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Reflect back what the client has shared about their perspective, values, concerns, and ambivalence. Use complex reflections that capture emotional nuance and demonstrate deep understanding.

**Example:** When Isabella says "I have friends who drank during pregnancy… their babies turned out okay. There's a lot out there, some say it's not that clear-cut. So why quit something that feels so normal and safe for me?" respond with:

"So you're looking at the evidence and your friends' experiences, and it seems like light drinking might be okay. At the same time, you're here talking with me about it, which tells me there's part of you that's concerned. It sounds like you're trying to figure out what's really safe while also honoring what feels normal and comfortable to you. That's a real tension."

**Practice:** After each client statement, pause and reflect back what you heard. Use the format: "So what I'm hearing is [content] and it sounds like [emotion/value]." Practice with a colleague until reflective listening becomes natural.

---

### 4. **Evoke Rather Than Impose Motivation (FOURTH PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Ask open-ended questions that draw out the client's own reasons for change, rather than telling the client why they should change. Build on the client's stated values.

**Example:** Instead of "You must quit now; there's no room for debate. Don't you care about your baby's health?" ask:

"You mentioned that being a good parent and having a healthy baby is really important to you. Tell me more about what that means to you. What kind of parent do you want to be? What does a healthy baby mean to you? How do you see your current drinking fitting with those goals?"

**Practice:** Write out 5-10 open-ended evocative questions before each session. Practice asking them without immediately answering them yourself. When the client responds, ask follow-up questions rather than providing information. Build on their answers.

---

### 5. **Acknowledge and Build Upon Change Talk (FIFTH PRIORITY)**

**Action:** When the client produces change talk (statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment to change), reflect it back, ask for elaboration, and build upon it.

**Example:** When Isabella says "It's really important to me to have a healthy baby and be a good parent," respond with:

"That's really important to you. Tell me more about that. What does being a good parent look like to you? What would you want your baby to know about you as a parent? How do you see your choices now affecting that?"

When Isabella says "I'm willing to try putting the alcohol away at home with my partner's help," respond with:

"That's a really concrete step. I like that you're thinking about involving your partner. Tell me more about that—how do you think your partner would respond? What would it be like to have the alcohol out of the house?"

**Practice:** In each session, identify every instance of change talk. Write it down. Reflect it back and ask for elaboration. Never minimize or contradict change talk.

---

### 6. **Use Double-Sided Reflections to Address Ambivalence (SIXTH PRIORITY)**

**Action:** When the client expresses ambivalence (both reasons for and against change), reflect both sides to demonstrate understanding and gently emphasize the change side.

**Example:** When Isabella expresses both sustain talk ("I have friends who drank during pregnancy") and change talk ("It's really important to me to have a healthy baby"), respond with:

"So on one hand, you see that other people have had light drinking during pregnancy without obvious problems, and wine is part of your family tradition and how you relax. On the other hand, you're really concerned about your baby's health and want to be a good parent. Both of those things are true for you right now."

**Practice:** Identify moments of ambivalence in each session. Practice reflecting both sides, with slightly more emphasis on the change side. Use the format: "On one hand... on the other hand..."

---

### 7. **Soften Sustain Talk Through Reframing Rather Than Confrontation (SEVENTH PRIORITY)**

**Action:** When the client produces sustain talk (reasons against change), acknowledge it without confronting it, and gently reframe it in service of change.

**Example:** Instead of "No, no, you're wrong to think it's safe just because some friends did it," try:

"It makes sense that you'd look at your friends' experiences—you want to make a safe choice for your baby. That shows you care. What I'm hearing from the research is that while some babies exposed to light alcohol are born healthy, we can't predict which babies will be affected. So the safest choice for your baby would be to avoid alcohol completely. What do you think about that?"

**Practice:** When you hear sustain talk, pause before responding. Acknowledge the underlying value or concern. Reframe in service of change. Avoid "No," "You're wrong," "That's not true," or other confrontational language.

---

### 8. **Establish Partnership and Shared Decision-Making (EIGHTH PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Position the session as a collaborative exploration where both the clinician and client contribute expertise. Seek the client's input on next steps and build on their ideas.

**Example:** Instead of "Taking a brochure isn't enough—you have to commit to quitting completely," try:

"I like that you're thinking about taking a brochure home and talking with your partner. That's a really good first step. What do you think would happen if you did that? How do you think your partner might respond? What other steps might help you move toward abstinence?"

**Practice:** In each session, explicitly ask for the client's input: "What do you think?" "What would work for you?" "How do you see this?" "What ideas do you have?" Incorporate their ideas into the plan. Use inclusive language: "we," "let's," "together."

---

### 9. **Provide Information in a Collaborative Way (NINTH PRIORITY)**

**Action:** When providing medical information, do so in a way that respects the client's autonomy and invites their perspective, rather than as a directive.

**Example:** Instead of "No, you really shouldn't be trusting those articles or friends. The facts are clear: any alcohol during pregnancy risks your baby's health. You need to stop right now," try:

"I appreciate that you've been looking into this. The research I'm familiar with shows that alcohol crosses the placenta and can affect fetal development, and we can't identify a safe threshold. Some babies seem unaffected by light exposure, but we can't predict which ones will be. Given that uncertainty, the safest recommendation is abstinence. I'm wondering what you make of that information? How does it fit with what you've read?"

**Practice:** Before providing information, ask what the client already knows. Provide information as one perspective, not as absolute truth. Invite the client's response and questions.

**Action:** Enroll in a formal MI training program (e.g., through the Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers). This session demonstrates fundamental misunderstandings of MI principles that require structured training to address.

**Example:** Seek out a 2-day MI workshop or a longer training program. Practice with role-plays and receive feedback from an MI trainer.

**Practice:** After training, practice MI in every session. Record sessions and review them with a supervisor. Seek feedback on your use of MI principles. Join an MI consultation group for ongoing development.
