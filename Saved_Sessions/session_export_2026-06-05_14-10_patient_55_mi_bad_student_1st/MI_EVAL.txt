# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates profound absence of unconditional positive regard with explicit judgment and disapproval throughout. Opens with directive stance ("I'll be telling you exactly what to do"), immediately signaling client's autonomy is not valued. Repeatedly invalidates client's experience with statements like "No, no, you're wrong," "you really have to stop making excuses," and "Don't you care about your baby's health?" Frames client's stated values (autonomy, family tradition, independence) as obstacles rather than legitimate aspects of identity. No affirmation of genuine concern for baby's health, no normalization of struggle with cultural traditions and medical advice, and no warmth or support. Treats client as a problem to be corrected rather than a person to be understood.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session contains virtually no reflective listening and demonstrates fundamental failure to understand client's perspective. Psychiatrist never reflects back client's cultural background, values around autonomy, genuine concern for baby, or ambivalence. When client articulates core value—"It's really important to me to have a healthy baby and be a good parent"—psychiatrist does not acknowledge or build upon this; instead responds with "Don't you care about your baby's health?" implying stated concern is false. No complex reflections capturing emotional nuance. No attempt to understand tension between cultural tradition and medical advice, or between independence and responsibility. Only response to client's ambivalence is to deny it exists and demand immediate compliance.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains clear focus on alcohol abstinence during pregnancy, which is clinically appropriate. However, method of maintaining direction is antithetical to MI—uses coercion and repetition rather than skillful evocation. Stays on topic but does not explore client's reasons for drinking, barriers to change, values, or readiness. Repeated questioning ("Why didn't you try cutting down already?" "Why are you still defending it?" "Why won't you just do what's necessary?") is rhetorical and accusatory rather than genuine exploration. Maintains direction through pressure rather than collaborative problem-solving. This approach may temporarily suppress discussion but will not build intrinsic motivation for change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates explicitly hierarchical, expert-driven stance from opening statement: "I'll be telling you exactly what to do." Positions psychiatrist as sole authority on what client should do and frames session as one-way transmission of directives rather than joint exploration. Client attempts to collaborate by offering perspective ("I've looked into it," "I have friends who drank," "I'm willing to take a brochure home"), but psychiatrist systematically dismisses these contributions with "No, no, you're wrong" and "you really have to stop making excuses." Never incorporates client's ideas into session direction. When client proposes concrete step (putting alcohol away at home with partner's help), psychiatrist rejects it: "Taking a brochure isn't enough." No shared decision-making, no co-construction of solutions, and no genuine teamwork. Power dynamic entirely one-sided.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session is masterclass in autonomy violation. Psychiatrist explicitly states intention to tell client "exactly what to do," then proceeds to do so repeatedly: "You really need to cut back or quit," "You must quit now," "You have to stop drinking tonight," "This isn't negotiable." Client explicitly communicates need for autonomy in opening: "I don't like being told what to do—it feels like people don't trust me to make good decisions." Psychiatrist's response is to immediately violate this stated need by telling her what to do. When client attempts to assert autonomy ("I'm gonna do what I want," "I want to make these decisions with some respect for my independence"), psychiatrist responds with increased pressure and moral judgment rather than respecting right to choose. Frames client's autonomy as irresponsibility: "This isn't about your feelings or timing—it's about your baby's health."

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session contains virtually no evocation. Psychiatrist tells client what to do rather than asking what she thinks, what matters to her, or what she might be willing to try. Client spontaneously offers substantial change talk multiple times: "It's really important to me to have a healthy baby and be a good parent," "I'm seriously thinking about cutting back and eventually stopping," "I'm willing to try putting the alcohol away at home with my partner's help as a first step." These are golden opportunities for evocation that are ignored or dismissed. Questions asked are accusatory and rhetorical ("Why didn't you try cutting down already?" "Don't you care about your baby's health?" "Why won't you just do what's necessary?") rather than exploratory. No open-ended questions like "What do you value most about being a parent?" or "What would need to happen for you to feel ready to make a change?" Psychiatrist assumes motivation must be installed from outside rather than drawn from within.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session explicitly rejects partnership from opening: "I'll be telling you exactly what to do." Psychiatrist positions self as sole authority on what client needs, what is safe, what is necessary, and what client should do. When client offers perspective ("I've looked into it," "I have friends who drank," "I want to make decisions on my own terms"), psychiatrist dismisses her expertise about her own life with "No, no, you're wrong" and "you really have to stop making excuses." Does not acknowledge that client has legitimate knowledge about cultural background, values, readiness for change, relationship with partner, or capacity for decision-making. Repeated use of "you must," "you have to," and "this isn't negotiable" communicates that client's input is irrelevant. When client proposes concrete step, psychiatrist rejects it without asking "What do you think would work?" or "How could we build on that?" No co-creation of change process. Psychiatrist actively discourages power-sharing and positions client as passive recipient of expert directives.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Client spontaneously produces multiple instances of change talk throughout session: "It's really important to me to have a healthy baby and be a good parent," "I'm not blindly carrying on without thinking," "I have thought about cutting back, yeah," "I'm seriously thinking about cutting back and eventually stopping," "I'm ready to start taking steps, like talking it over with my partner and putting the alcohol away at home," "I'm moving towards cutting back for the baby," "I want to be a good parent, and that means making changes." These are powerful statements of desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment. In MI, clinician would amplify these by reflecting back, asking for elaboration, and building upon them. Instead, psychiatrist systematically ignores or undermines them. When client says "I'm willing to try putting the alcohol away at home with my partner's help," psychiatrist responds: "Taking a brochure isn't enough—you have to commit to quitting completely." When client says "I'm seriously thinking about cutting back and eventually stopping," psychiatrist responds: "No, you can't just 'move towards' cutting back." Never asks client to elaborate on change talk or reflects it back to strengthen it.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Client produces sustain talk throughout session: "I have friends who drank during pregnancy with no problems," "There are articles online saying the link between light drinking and harm isn't proven," "I don't see the evidence," "I want to make these decisions with some respect for my independence." Psychiatrist's response is direct confrontation: "No, no, you're wrong to think it's safe just because some friends did it. You really have to stop making excuses." "No, you really shouldn't be trusting those articles or friends. The facts are clear." While psychiatrist does attempt to redirect away from sustain talk by reasserting medical facts, this is done through confrontation rather than skillful softening. Does not use double-sided reflections, does not reframe sustain talk in service of change, and does not strategically pivot toward change talk. Simply contradicts client and reasserts directive. This approach may temporarily suppress sustain talk but does not address underlying ambivalence or build motivation for change. In fact, by confronting sustain talk so directly, psychiatrist likely strengthens it—client becomes more defensive and more committed to her position.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Current MI Competency Level:** Minimal/Absent (1.1/5)

**Session Embodies MI Spirit:** No. This session demonstrates authoritarianism, judgment, lack of compassion for client's perspective, and imposition of motivation. The spirit of MI—partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation—is entirely absent.

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation for Change:** Very Low. Client enters with ambivalence and some openness to change ("I'm willing to talk about the drinking," "I'm seriously thinking about cutting back," "I'm ready to start taking steps"). By end of session, client has become more defensive and more committed to her position ("I can't promise to stop drinking tonight just because I'm being told to," "I want to do it my way"). Psychiatrist's approach violates client's explicitly stated need for autonomy, triggering psychological reactance—the tendency to resist when one's freedom is threatened. Rather than building intrinsic motivation for change, psychiatrist has created adversarial dynamic where client must defend autonomy by defending drinking.

**Clinical Strengths:**
- Clear clinical focus on alcohol abstinence during pregnancy (clinically appropriate)
- Accurate medical information regarding risks of alcohol during pregnancy
- Persistence in maintaining topic focus

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- Shift from directive to collaborative stance (foundational)
- Develop genuine reflective listening skills
- Learn to evoke rather than impose motivation
- Understand psychological reactance and autonomy needs
- Receive formal MI training before continuing behavior change work

**Immediate Recommendation:** Psychiatrist should seek formal Motivational Interviewing training before continuing to work with clients on behavior change. Current approach is likely to be harmful to client outcomes and may damage therapeutic relationship. With training and practice, this clinician could become effective MI practitioner. Without it, current approach should be discontinued.
