
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

- **Practice:** Learn to prioritize problems with patients. Practice translating vague complaints into specific, measurable goals. Develop skill in collaborative goal-setting.

### 3. **Develop and Communicate a Clear CBT Conceptualization and Treatment Strategy**
- **Action:** Develop a written conceptualization of the patient's problems using the CBT model: How do her thoughts, feelings, and behaviors interact to maintain depression and suicidal ideation? How does this relate to her BPD? Communicate this to the patient in clear language. Explain the treatment strategy: "Here's how we'll work together to help you feel better. First, we'll... - **Example:** "I think your depression is being maintained by several things: isolation (you're spending most of your time in bed), avoidance (you're avoiding difficult feelings by shopping and watching TV), and negative thoughts about yourself (you believe you're a mess and can't be fixed). These things feed each other—the more you isolate, the worse you feel; the worse you feel, the more you avoid. Here's what we'll do: We'll work on identifying and changing the negative thoughts. We'll gradually increase your activities and social contact. We'll develop skills to manage intense emotions. Over time, this should help you feel less depressed and less suicidal."
- **Practice:** Study the cognitive model of depression and BPD. Practice explaining the model to patients in simple language. Develop skill in creating written treatment plans.

### 4. **Eliminate Excessive Therapist Self-Disclosure and Maintain Professional Boundaries**
- **Action:** Recognize that personal anecdotes shift focus from the patient to the therapist and can undermine the therapeutic relationship, especially with BPD patients. When tempted to share a personal story, ask yourself: "Does this serve the patient's treatment goals, or does it serve my need to connect or be understood?" Redirect focus back to the patient. Use phrases like "That's interesting, but let's focus on your experience" or "I appreciate you asking, but I want to make sure we're using our time to help you."
- **Example:** Instead of "I once had a really rough time too, felt like nothing was going right. But then I just decided to keep going, day by day, and eventually things started to look up," say: "I hear that you're struggling with hopelessness. That's a common experience with depression. Let's explore what's making you feel hopeless and what might help shift that."
- **Practice:** Record yourself in role-plays and count instances of self-disclosure. Set a goal of zero personal anecdotes per session. Practice redirecting to the patient's experience.

### 5. **Apply Specific CBT Techniques: Cognitive Restructuring for Core Negative Thoughts**
- **Action:** Identify the patient's core negative thoughts (e.g., "I'm a mess," "I can't fix myself," "I don't deserve to feel better"). Use Socratic questioning to examine these thoughts: What evidence supports this thought? What evidence contradicts it? What would someone who cares about you say? What would you say to a friend who had this thought? Develop alternative, more balanced thoughts. Use thought records to practice this skill between sessions.
- **Example:** "You said 'I'm a mess that I can't fix.' Let's examine that thought. What makes you think you're a mess? What evidence do you have? Is there any evidence that contradicts that? What would it mean if you could fix even one small thing? Let's write this down and practice examining this thought when it comes up."
- **Practice:** Learn the cognitive restructuring protocol. Practice Socratic questioning. Develop skill in helping patients generate alternative thoughts.

### 6. **Apply Specific CBT Techniques: Behavioral Activation for Depression and Isolation**
- **Action:** Assess the patient's values and what activities bring meaning or pleasure. Develop a behavioral activation plan: schedule specific activities (even small ones like taking a shower, going for a walk, calling a friend) at specific times. Start with very small, achievable goals. Monitor mood before and after activities to help the patient see the connection between behavior and mood. Gradually increase activity level.
- **Example:** "Let's think about what matters to you. What brings you joy, even a little? What would you like to be doing? Let's start small. This week, I'd like you to: (1) Get out of bed by 10 AM three days this week, (2) Take a 10-minute walk outside once, (3) Call one person you know. Let's track how you feel before and after each activity."
- **Practice:** Learn the behavioral activation protocol. Practice assessing values. Develop skill in creating achievable behavioral goals and monitoring outcomes.

### 7. **Apply Specific CBT Techniques: Functional Analysis of Impulsive Behaviors**
- **Action:** Analyze the function of the patient's impulsive behaviors (online shopping, excessive TV watching, lying in bed). For each behavior, identify: What emotion or situation triggers it? What does it accomplish in the short term? What are the consequences in the long term? What need is it meeting? Develop alternative behaviors that meet the same need in healthier ways.
- **Example:** "You mentioned that you shop online a lot, even though you don't have the money. Let's understand this better. What are you feeling right before you shop? What does shopping accomplish for you? How do you feel during shopping? How do you feel after? What need is shopping meeting? What else could you do to meet that need?"
- **Practice:** Learn functional analysis. Practice identifying triggers, functions, and consequences of behaviors. Develop skill in generating alternative behaviors.

### 8. **Teach Emotion Regulation and Distress Tolerance Skills for BPD**
- **Action:** Recognize that the patient's impulsive behaviors (shopping, isolation, self-harm) are attempts to regulate intense emotions.
