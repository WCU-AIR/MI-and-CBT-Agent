### Summary of Clinical Findings

#### **Strengths:**
- Clear focus maintained throughout session; no significant topic drift
- Concrete action steps attempted (looking up courses, asking Donna for help)
- Warm, inviting opening that sets positive tone
- Acknowledgment of client's stated goals and aspirations
- Basic familiarity with MI tools (scaling questions)
- Attempt to move toward specific, measurable goals

#### **Critical Deficiencies:**

**1. Expert-Driven Stance Dominates (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
Psychiatrist operates from expert role throughout, telling client what to do rather than exploring what client thinks would work. This violates core MI principle of partnership and collaboration. Prescriptive language ("you should," "you might want to") used 15+ times.

**2. Advice-Giving Replaces Evocation (SECOND PRIORITY)**
Instead of asking questions that draw out client's own motivations, values, and ideas, psychiatrist provides solutions. This is opposite of MI. Psychiatrist tells rather than asks.

**3. Autonomy Undermined (THIRD PRIORITY)**
Prescriptive language and false choice presentation subtly pressure client toward psychiatrist's predetermined solutions rather than supporting client's right to choose.

**4. Change Talk Not Cultivated (FOURTH PRIORITY)**
While client produces substantial change talk (especially about wanting to be different father), psychiatrist does minimal work to amplify, explore, or reinforce it. Powerful statements acknowledged but not deepened.

**5. Sustain Talk Not Softened (FIFTH PRIORITY)**
Psychiatrist uses advice-giving and problem-solving rather than MI techniques (double-sided reflections, reframing, exploration) to address barriers. Can inadvertently reinforce sustain talk.

**6. Empathy Surface-Level (SIXTH PRIORITY)**
Reflective listening captures facts but misses emotional nuance. Psychiatrist never reflects shame, vulnerability, or genuine ambivalence client is experiencing.

**7. Partnership Absent (SEVENTH PRIORITY)**
Psychiatrist does not convey that client has wisdom about own life. Client input not genuinely sought or incorporated into decision-making.

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change

This session likely reduces client's intrinsic motivation for change. While client enters with substantial change talk and genuine ambivalence, psychiatrist's expert-driven, advice-giving approach may trigger reactance (resistance to being told what to do). By not exploring client's own reasons for change, psychiatrist misses opportunities to strengthen intrinsic motivation. Session ends with client saying "I'll try to keep that in mind"—suggesting compliance rather than genuine commitment. This is red flag indicating MI spirit not achieved.

---

### Key Recommendations for Improvement

**1. Shift from Expert-Driven to Collaborative Stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
Replace prescriptive language with genuine questions. Instead of "You should ask Donna for help," ask "What do you think about asking Donna for help? How does that feel to you?"

**2. Actively Evoke and Amplify Change Talk (SECOND PRIORITY)**
When client produces powerful statements about values and identity, pause and explore deeply. When client says "want to be a different kind of dad," ask: "Tell me more about that. What kind of father do you want to be? What would that look like?"

**3. Use Double-Sided Reflections to Honor Ambivalence (THIRD PRIORITY)**
When client expresses conflicting desires, reflect both sides without judgment. Example: "Part of you wants that excitement with your mates, and part of you wants to build something real with Donna. Both are real parts of you."

**4. Ask Questions Instead of Giving Advice (FOURTH PRIORITY)**
For every piece of advice tempted to give, ask question instead. Trust client has wisdom about own life. Your job is to ask questions that help access that wisdom.

**5. Deepen Reflective Listening to Capture Emotional Nuance (FIFTH PRIORITY)**
Move beyond factual reflections to complex reflections capturing emotion, meaning, and client's perspective. Reflect shame, vulnerability, and genuine struggle.

**6. Support Autonomy by Emphasizing Client Choice (SIXTH PRIORITY)**
Explicitly affirm client's right to choose. Add statements: "This is your decision. You get to choose. What feels right to you?"

**7. Explore Sustain Talk Rather Than Confronting It (SEVENTH PRIORITY)**
When client expresses barriers, explore with curiosity rather than trying to overcome. Example: "You mentioned needing action. Tell me more about that. What appeals to you about that?"

**8. Use Scaling Questions Strategically (EIGHTH PRIORITY)**
Follow up scaling questions with deepening questions. Instead of just asking "How important is it?" ask "What makes it an 8 and not a 5? What would it take to get to a 9?"

**9. Maintain Consistent MI Spirit Throughout (NINTH PRIORITY)**
Before each session, remind self of four MI principles: Partnership, Compassion, Evocation, Autonomy. Periodically check in: "Am I in partnership or expert mode? Am I evoking or telling?"

---

### Conclusion

This session represents significant departure from Motivational Interviewing practice. While psychiatrist demonstrates basic competence in maintaining focus and attempting concrete action steps, core MI principles—partnership, evocation, autonomy support, and collaboration—are largely absent. Psychiatrist operates from expert-driven, advice-giving stance rather than collaborative, evocative one. This likely reduces client's intrinsic motivation for change and may trigger reactance.

With focused practice on recommendations above, particularly shifting from expert to collaborative stance and replacing advice-giving with evocative questioning, this psychiatrist can develop genuine MI competence and significantly improve effectiveness in evoking client motivation for sustained behavior change. Foundation exists (focus, warmth, acknowledgment of goals), but execution requires substantial improvement to align with MI principles and spirit.
