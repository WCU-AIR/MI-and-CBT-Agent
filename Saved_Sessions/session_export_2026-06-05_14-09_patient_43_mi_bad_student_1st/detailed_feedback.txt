
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a near-complete absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly uses language that is overtly judgmental and dismissive of the client's experience. From the opening statement—"We need to talk about what you're doing that's harming you"—the tone establishes moral disapproval rather than respect for Elise as a person of worth. Throughout the session, the psychiatrist uses phrases such as "you're just making excuses," "you really have to stop making excuses," and "No, you need to stop hesitating," which directly communicate that the client's perspective is invalid and that her concerns about social connection are not legitimate. 

Most critically, the psychiatrist frames Elise's social bonding through smoking as a "bad habit you're clinging to" and a "crutch," explicitly pathologizing her stated values and sources of belonging. When Elise expresses that smoking helps her feel accepted and connected, the psychiatrist responds with "Socializing is no reason to keep smoking" and "It's just a bad habit you're holding onto"—responses that communicate rejection of her lived experience rather than curiosity about it. There is no affirmation of Elise's strengths, no normalization of her struggle, and no genuine warmth. The repeated ultimatums ("Are you going to commit to this right now or not?") create a coercive atmosphere incompatible with MI spirit.

The psychiatrist's stance is fundamentally authoritarian and punitive. Elise's hesitation and ambivalence—which are developmentally appropriate in the contemplation stage—are met with frustration and pressure rather than acceptance. There is zero evidence of the psychiatrist viewing Elise as a person worthy of respect independent of her compliance with medical directives.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no acceptance of the client. Judgmental language, dismissal of client concerns, pathologizing of values, and repeated confrontation create an atmosphere of disapproval that is antithetical to MI principles and likely to increase client defensiveness and resistance.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective from their internal frame of reference. This session contains virtually no reflective listening and demonstrates a consistent failure to understand or validate Elise's experience. The psychiatrist never reflects back what Elise has said about the social meaning of smoking, never explores her experience of belonging, and never demonstrates curiosity about why smoking feels so central to her identity and relationships.

When Elise states, "It's like instant friends" and "You bond with other smokers," the psychiatrist responds with directives ("You can't keep delaying this") rather than reflections that demonstrate understanding. When Elise expresses that quitting feels like "losing part of how I fit in," the psychiatrist dismisses this as an excuse rather than exploring what belonging means to her or acknowledging the genuine loss she anticipates. There are no complex reflections that capture emotional nuance—no statements like "It sounds like smoking is really about more than nicotine; it's about feeling like you belong somewhere" or "You're caught between wanting to protect your health and not wanting to lose the connections that matter to you."

The psychiatrist's responses are consistently factual and directive ("You need to exercise three times a week") rather than empathic. There is no attempt to understand Elise's barriers to exercise, her schedule constraints, or her emotional experience of feeling overwhelmed. The psychiatrist never asks open-ended questions designed to deepen understanding of her perspective. Instead, the psychiatrist imposes an external framework of what "should" happen without any genuine effort to see the world through Elise's eyes.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no empathic listening. There are zero reflective statements, no exploration of client perspective, and consistent dismissal of Elise's stated concerns. This represents a fundamental failure in the core MI skill of accurate empathy.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 5/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI refers to maintaining strategic focus on target behaviors while remaining client-centered. This psychiatrist demonstrates extremely clear and consistent direction—perhaps the only dimension where the session shows consistency. The target behaviors are explicitly identified: smoking cessation and increased exercise. The psychiatrist maintains unwavering focus on these goals throughout the entire session, repeatedly returning to them and refusing to be diverted.

However, it is critical to note that while the psychiatrist demonstrates strong *directiveness*, this is fundamentally different from MI-consistent *direction*. The psychiatrist maintains focus through coercion and prescription rather than through collaborative exploration. The direction is imposed rather than co-constructed. The psychiatrist never allows the session to explore Elise's own goals, values, or readiness; instead, the psychiatrist steamrolls toward predetermined outcomes with statements like "You have to quit smoking now—no ifs, ands, or buts" and "No more thinking or planning—just start."

In MI, direction should be maintained while preserving client autonomy and collaboration. This psychiatrist maintains direction through elimination of client choice. While the score reflects the presence of clear focus, this must be understood as directiveness without the collaborative, client-centered foundation that makes direction effective in MI.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains extremely clear and consistent focus on smoking cessation and exercise. However, this direction is achieved through coercion and prescription rather than collaborative exploration, which undermines MI effectiveness despite the clarity of focus.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires that the interview occur between two equal partners, with both parties' knowledge and perspectives valued in problem-solving. This session demonstrates a complete absence of collaboration. The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritarian stance from the opening statement and maintains it throughout. The psychiatrist makes unilateral decisions about what must happen ("You have to quit smoking now," "You must commit to exercising at least three times a week") without any input from Elise about her preferences, timeline, or approach.

The psychiatrist never asks, "What do you think might work for you?" or "How could we approach this in a way that feels manageable?" or "What would need to happen for you to feel ready?"

The power dynamic is entirely one-directional. The psychiatrist speaks; the client listens and is expected to comply. When Elise expresses hesitation, the psychiatrist interprets this as resistance to be overcome rather than as information to be understood collaboratively. The repeated question "Are you going to commit to this right now or not?" is a demand for compliance, not an invitation to collaborative decision-making.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates zero collaboration. The session is entirely expert-driven with unilateral decision-making, no joint problem-solving, and explicit rejection of client input. This represents a fundamental violation of MI partnership principles.

### 5. Autonomy Support

**Explanation:**

Autonomy support requires that the clinician actively foster the client's perception of choice and emphasize their ownership of the change process. ## Recommendations

### 1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance and Abandon Confrontation**
- **Action:** Fundamentally shift from an expert-directive approach to a collaborative partnership. Begin sessions by explicitly inviting the client's perspective: "I'd like to understand your experience with smoking and what it means to you. Can you tell me more?"
- **Example:** Instead of "You're just making excuses," say: "It sounds like smoking is about more than just nicotine for you—it's connected to feeling like you belong with your friends. Help me understand that better."
- **Practice:** Record yourself in practice sessions and count how many times you use directive language ("you have to," "you must," "you need to") versus collaborative language ("we could," "what do you think," "let's explore"). Aim to eliminate directive language in favor of collaborative framing. Practice with a colleague or supervisor who can provide real-time feedback on your stance.

---

### 2. **Develop Genuine Empathic Listening and Reflective Responses**
- **Action:** Make reflective listening your primary response mode. For every client statement, pause and reflect back what you heard, focusing on emotion and meaning rather than facts. Use complex reflections that capture the deeper significance of what the client is expressing.
- **Example:** When Elise says "It's like instant friends," reflect: "So smoking is a quick way to feel connected and accepted by others. That sense of belonging is really important to you." Then pause and listen rather than immediately pivoting to directives.
- **Practice:** In supervision, practice reflective listening with role-plays. Have a supervisor play the client and give you feedback on the accuracy and depth of your reflections. Aim to develop reflections that capture both the surface content and the underlying emotion/value. Record yourself and listen for how often you use reflections versus directives—reflections should comprise 60-70% of your responses.

---

### 3. **Recognize and Amplify Client Change Talk**
- **Action:** Train yourself to identify moments when the client expresses desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment to change. When you hear change talk, respond with reflections that deepen and strengthen it. Ask open-ended follow-up questions to evoke more change talk.
- **Example:** When Elise says "I do want to feel better and live longer," respond with: "That's important to you—feeling better and living longer. What would that look like for you? What would be different?" Then listen and reflect what you hear.
- **Practice:** In supervision, review session recordings and identify all instances of client change talk. For each instance, write down what you said in response. If you responded with a directive, rewrite your response as a reflection or open-ended question designed to amplify the change talk. Practice this skill until it becomes automatic.

---

### 4. **Explore and Validate the Client's Ambivalence Rather Than Attacking It**
- **Action:** When a client expresses sustain talk or resistance, respond with curiosity and validation rather than confrontation. Use double-sided reflections that acknowledge both the client's concerns about change and their concerns about the status quo.
- **Example:** Instead of "You're just making excuses," say: "So on one hand, you're worried about losing the social connection that smoking gives you—that sense of belonging is real and important. And on the other hand, you're aware that smoking is harming your health and you do want to feel better. You're caught between two things that matter to you. Tell me more about that."
- **Practice:** In role-plays, practice responding to sustain talk with double-sided reflections. Have a supervisor give you feedback on whether your reflection validates both sides of the ambivalence without arguing for change. Practice until you can respond to resistance with genuine curiosity rather than frustration.

---

### 5. **Support Client Autonomy and Emphasize Their Right to Choose**
- **Action:** Explicitly affirm the client's right to make their own decisions about change. Use language that emphasizes choice and ownership: "It's entirely up to you," "What feels right to you?" "You're the expert on your own life." Avoid directive language ("you have to," "you must").
- **Example:** Instead of "You have to quit smoking now," say: "Quitting smoking is something you're considering. What would need to happen for you to feel ready to make that change? What would help?"
- **Practice:** In supervision, review recordings and identify all instances of directive language. Rewrite each directive statement as an autonomy-supportive statement. Practice until autonomy-supportive language becomes your default.

---

### 6. **Ask Open-Ended Questions to Evoke the Client's Own Motivations and Resources**
- **Action:** Replace closed questions and directives with open-ended questions designed to draw out the client's own reasons for change, values, strengths, and resources. Use questions like: "What concerns you most about...?" "What would be good about...?" "What have you thought about...?" "Tell me about a time when you...?"
- **Example:** Instead of "Don't you want to feel better and live longer?" ask: "What matters most to you about your health? What would be different if you were healthier?"
- **Practice:** In supervision, practice generating open-ended questions for common client presentations. Have a supervisor role-play the client and give you feedback on whether your questions genuinely evoke the client's perspective or whether they're leading questions disguised as open-ended. Aim to ask questions you genuinely don't know the answer to.

---

### 7. **Explore the Meaning and Function of the Behavior Rather Than Dismissing It**
- **Action:** When a client describes a behavior as serving an important function (like smoking providing social connection), respond with curiosity about that function rather than dismissing it as an "excuse" or "bad habit." Explore what need the behavior is meeting and how that need might be met in other ways.
- **Example:** Instead of "It's just a bad habit you're clinging to," say: "So smoking is really about connection and belonging for you. Those are important human needs. I'm curious—what is it about smoking specifically that makes you feel connected? Are there other ways you might meet that need for belonging?"
- **Practice:** In supervision, practice exploring the function of behaviors. When a client describes a behavior, ask yourself: "What need is this meeting? What would the client lose if they gave this up? How can I acknowledge that loss while exploring alternatives?" Practice until you can hold both the recognition that change is needed AND the recognition that the behavior serves an important function.

---

### 8. **Develop Skill in Handling Resistance Without Confrontation**
- **Action:** Recognize that client resistance is information, not defiance. When a client resists, respond with curiosity rather than pressure. Use techniques like rolling with resistance, amplified reflection, and strategic redirection to soften resistance without direct confrontation.
- **Example:** When Elise says "I can't commit right now," instead of "Enough hesitation. You have to commit now," say: "You're not ready to commit right now. That makes sense—this is a big change. What would need to happen for you to feel more ready?"
- **Practice:** In supervision, practice responding to resistance with curiosity. Have a supervisor play a resistant client and practice responding without confrontation. Get feedback on whether your responses soften or amplify the resistance. Practice until you can remain calm and curious when faced with client resistance.

---

### 9. **Establish a Genuine Therapeutic Alliance Based on Partnership and Respect**
- **Action:** Fundamentally reconceptualize your role. You are not the expert who knows what the client should do; you are a partner who helps the client explore their own motivations, values, and resources for change. Communicate this through your language, tone, and behavior throughout the session.
- **Example:** Open with: "I'm here to support you in thinking about your health. I have some medical information that might be helpful, but you're the expert on your own life. Let's work together to figure out what makes sense for you."
- **Practice:** In supervision, discuss your beliefs about your role as a clinician. Examine any beliefs that position you as the expert who knows what's best for the client. Practice shifting to a collaborative stance in which you genuinely respect the client's right to make their own choices, even if those choices differ from your recommendations.

---

### 10. **Receive Training and Supervision in Motivational Interviewing**
- **Action:** Enroll in a formal MI training program (e.g., through the Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers). Participate in ongoing supervision with an MI-trained supervisor who can provide real-time feedback on your sessions.
- **Example:** Attend a 2-day MI workshop, then participate in monthly supervision groups where you present recordings of your sessions and receive feedback from peers and supervisors trained in MI.
- **Practice:** Commit to recording and reviewing your sessions regularly. Use the MITI (Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity) scale or similar tool to assess your MI adherence. Set specific goals for improvement and track your progress over time.
