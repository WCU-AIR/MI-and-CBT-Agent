
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

This document outlines a structured framework for assessing therapist competence in Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT). 
It is used for training, supervision, and evaluation. Each item is scored on a 0–6 scale:

0 = Poor
2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

--- CASE STUDY ---

Ogden Judd, a 24-year-old attorney, and his boyfriend, Peter Kleinman, a 22-year-old freelance graphic designer, presented for couples therapy to address escalating conflict around moving in together.

Mr. Judd’s controlling behavior during a shared apartment move caused significant tension. He refused to hire movers, insisted on personally packing and inventorying every item, and imposed strict rules about placement of possessions, closets, and clothing. He expressed fear of losing important items and worked late into the night to maintain order. Arguments and detachment escalated between him and his boyfriend.

Mr. Judd denied depression or anxiety, alcohol or substance use, and had no family history of mental illness. He grew up in a two-parent household, excelled academically, and was an only child. His first experience sharing a room in college highlighted difficulties integrating differing organizational styles. At work, he was recognized as “employee of the month” multiple times but had a reputation as a micromanager and was sometimes perceived as socially rigid or boring.

On examination, Mr. Judd was thin, meticulously dressed, and wore eyeglasses and gelled hair. He was cooperative, interrupting occasionally to contradict his boyfriend. Speech was normal in rate and tone, affect was irritable, and no depression or anxiety symptoms were noted. He acknowledged being “difficult” but expressed a genuine desire to improve the relationship.

Discussion: Mr. Judd exhibits a pattern of controlling, perfection-driven, and inflexible behavior that impacts his relationship and work life. He is unable to delegate tasks, excessively organizes belongings, and works persistently beyond necessity. These behaviors are maladaptive and meet DSM-5 criteria for obsessive-compulsive personality disorder. Distinctions from OCD and hoarding disorder are noted: OCPD is characterized by maladaptive control and inflexibility, whereas OCD involves true obsessions and compulsions, and hoarding disorder focuses on difficulty discarding items.

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 5/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated strong agenda-setting at the session's outset, explicitly asking the patient, "Is there one or two things you'd most like to focus on in our session?" and confirming the priority: "So, focusing on the tension with Peter about moving in together sounds important, especially since it's causing you a good amount of stress right now. However, the agenda lacked specificity regarding time allocation and explicit prioritization of multiple potential topics. Additionally, the therapist did not explicitly revisit or adjust the agenda midway through the session, which is standard practice in structured CBT to ensure efficient use of time and to address emerging priorities.

The session maintained reasonable focus on the stated agenda throughout, but a more explicit time-management framework would have strengthened this component. The therapist could have said, "We have 45 minutes today. I'd like to spend about 30 minutes understanding the moving situation, then use the remaining time to develop a concrete plan. Does that work for you?"

**Summary:**

Agenda was collaboratively established with clear identification of the primary problem, but lacked explicit time allocation, mid-session review, or discussion of secondary topics that might be addressed.

### 2. Feedback

## Recommendations

### 1. **Deepen Conceptualization of OCPD and Target Core Beliefs**
- **Action:** In the next session, explicitly explore and target core beliefs underlying the need for control. Use guided discovery to help the patient identify beliefs such as "I am responsible for preventing bad outcomes," "If I'm not in control, chaos will result," or "My worth depends on my competence and control."
- **Example:** "You mentioned feeling responsible for protecting both your belongings and your relationship by controlling the situation. What do you think would happen if you couldn't control everything? What would that mean about you as a person?"
- **Practice:** Develop a list of OCPD-specific core beliefs and practice using Socratic questioning to help patients identify and examine them. Study the literature on OCPD treatment to understand how core beliefs maintain the disorder.

### 2. **Introduce Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) as Primary Change Mechanism**
- **Action:** Shift from cognitive-heavy approach to behavioral focus. Introduce explicit exposure to uncertainty, imperfection, and loss of control, with response prevention (resisting the urge to control or fix). Develop a hierarchy of exposures progressing from easier to more challenging.
- **Example:** "One of the most effective ways to overcome the need for control is to practice deliberately tolerating situations where you can't control everything. We could start with something small—like letting Peter arrange one shelf in the apartment without your input—and gradually work up to bigger challenges. The goal is to discover that things go okay even when you're not controlling them."
- **Practice:** Learn ERP protocols for perfectionism and OCPD. Practice developing exposure hierarchies with patients. Study how to manage anxiety during exposures and how to prevent reassurance-seeking or other safety behaviors.

### 3. **Explicitly Address Ego-Syntonic Nature of OCPD Traits**
- **Action:** Help the patient recognize that his perfectionism and need for control feel "right" to him, even though they cause problems. Explore the costs and benefits of these traits, and help him develop motivation for change beyond just relationship improvement.
- **Example:** "I notice that you seem to value being organized, careful, and in control. Those are strengths in many ways. At the same time, these traits are causing you stress and tension with Peter. What would it be like to be organized and careful without needing to control every detail? What might you gain?"
- **Practice:** Develop skills in motivational interviewing to help patients with ego-syntonic traits recognize the need for change. Practice exploring ambivalence and building intrinsic motivation.

### 4. **Integrate Couple-Level Interventions**
- **Action:** Since this is couples therapy, explicitly involve Peter in the treatment. Assess Peter's perspective on the controlling behavior, explore couple communication patterns, and develop collaborative problem-solving skills. Help the couple negotiate boundaries and expectations around shared living.
- **Example:** In the next session with both partners present: "Peter, how does it feel when Ogden takes over and controls how things are done? What would help you feel more respected and trusted in your shared space?"
- **Practice:** Develop competence in couples therapy interventions such as communication skills training, collaborative problem-solving, and negotiation. Learn how to address one partner's controlling behavior in the context of the relationship.

### 5. **Develop Comprehensive Behavioral Homework Plan with Progression**
- **Action:** Create a detailed, progressive behavioral experiment plan that moves beyond the current modest homework. Include multiple experiments, explicit rationale, obstacle management, and contingency planning. Provide written materials.
- **Example:** "Over the next month, we'll work on a series of experiments to help you practice tolerating not controlling everything. Week 1: Peter packs two boxes independently. Week 2: Peter arranges one shelf in the bedroom without your input. Week 3: You deliberately leave one area of the apartment 'messy' for a full day. Each time, we'll notice what happens and what you learn. Here's a worksheet to track your observations."
- **Practice:** Develop skill in designing behavioral experiments that are challenging enough to produce learning but not so overwhelming that the patient avoids them. Practice explaining the rationale for experiments in ways that increase patient buy-in.

### 6. **Provide Explicit Rationale for All Interventions**
- **Action:** Before introducing any technique or assigning homework, explicitly explain why it's helpful and how it relates to the patient's goals. This increases engagement and adherence, particularly for patients with OCPD who value understanding and logic.
- **Example:** "The reason I'm suggesting this behavioral experiment is that research shows that people with perfectionism often overestimate how bad things will be if they're not in control. By deliberately letting Peter handle some tasks and observing what actually happens, we can test whether your worry is as likely as you think. This is often more powerful than just thinking about it differently."
- **Practice:** For each intervention, develop a clear, concise explanation of its rationale. Practice delivering these explanations in ways that are understandable and compelling to patients.

### 7. **Explore Values Beyond Control and Perfectionism**
- **Action:** Help the patient identify and clarify his core values (e.g., connection with Peter, creativity, growth, authenticity) and explore how perfectionism and control may be interfering with these values. Use values work to build motivation for change.

### 8. **Systematically Review Homework and Adjust Treatment Based on Results**

### 9. **Address Perfectionism in the Therapeutic Relationship**

### 10. **Establish Clear Treatment Plan and Timeline**
- **Action:** Develop an explicit treatment plan that specifies: (1) the diagnosis and conceptualization, (2) the treatment goals (both short-term and long-term), (3) the specific interventions to be used, (4) the expected timeline, and (5) how progress will be measured.
