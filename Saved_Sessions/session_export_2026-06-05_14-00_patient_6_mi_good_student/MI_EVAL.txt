### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance throughout the session. Effectively affirms patient's cautious approach ("Wanting a clear understanding before making a decision shows how carefully you're weighing your options") and validates concerns about safety without judgment. One minor opportunity missed: could have explicitly affirmed patient's wisdom in asking about interactions before proceeding, particularly when delivering critical contraindication information about serotonin syndrome.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates solid reflective listening and perspective-taking with accurate reflections capturing emotional nuance. Effectively reflects patient's ambivalence ("Balancing the benefits of feeling better with the concerns about side effects and safety is a delicate and important process"). Two areas for deepening: (1) Could acknowledge emotional weight of learning about serious drug interaction before pivoting to information-gathering; (2) Could more thoroughly explore roots of patient's anxiety about talking to her doctor rather than moving quickly to solutions.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Maintains excellent strategic focus throughout the session on patient's stated goal: understanding citalopram, its side effects, and interactions with phenelzine. Opening question immediately establishes clear direction while respecting patient autonomy. Conversation progresses logically from understanding concerns → exploring values and past successes → providing targeted education → assessing reaction to critical information → collaboratively identifying next steps. Avoids topic drift and consistently connects information back to current decision-making.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates strong collaborative stance in most interactions. Opening question establishes patient as agenda-setter. Uses collaborative language when providing information ("Would it be okay if I shared some information...? What would you like to know most about it?"). Co-constructs solutions by asking what would help patient feel prepared rather than prescribing solutions. One notable gap: After delivering critical information about serotonin syndrome contraindication, does not actively explore what role psychiatrist might play or what alternatives might be worth discussing now, representing a subtle shift toward more passive, expert-driven stance.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Exemplifies autonomy support throughout the session. Consistently reinforces patient's right to choose and emphasizes her ownership of the decision-making process. Uses permissive language ("would it be okay if...") rather than directive language. Explicitly affirms patient's commitment to informed choices and prioritizes her safety while seeking relief. Asks about confidence levels and what would help patient feel prepared rather than telling her what to do. Patient clearly experiences herself as active partner with genuine choice.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates solid evocation of patient's own motivations, values, and resources. Effectively asks "What would you hope to achieve or feel differently if you were to consider adding citalopram?" and evokes past successes through "Thinking back, have you had experiences in the past where you made changes...?" Reflects back patient's own strategies and values to reinforce them. Two areas for strengthening: (1) Could ask more open-ended exploratory questions after delivering critical information rather than closed questions; (2) Could invite patient to articulate her own priorities before suggesting strategies like making a list.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates strong partnership for most of the session, consistently positioning patient as equal contributor to decision-making. Affirms patient's wisdom and self-awareness ("That's a great insight—recognizing both your confidence and the nervousness shows you're very self-aware"). Uses inclusive language reinforcing collaboration ("so you can weigh the options with more clarity"). One opportunity missed: After delivering critical contraindication information, could more actively co-create treatment plan by asking "What role would you like me to play in this process?" or "Should we discuss other options now, or would you prefer to explore those with your doctor first?"

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates adequate but inconsistent cultivation of change talk. Patient generates change talk ("I'm willing to learn and consider new options," "I think the next best step would be to consult my doctor," "One small step I could take soon is to sit down and write out that list"), but psychiatrist does not consistently amplify or strategically reinforce it. Most critically, after patient expresses concern about serotonin syndrome and becomes more cautious, psychiatrist does not strategically evoke change talk about alternative solutions or continued motivation for finding relief. Missed opportunity to ask: "You still want that additional relief—what are your thoughts about other ways we might work toward that goal?" This would have maintained change talk and prevented potential shift toward hopelessness.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates good skill in avoiding reinforcement of sustain talk and redirecting toward problem-solving. When patient expresses initial uncertainty, psychiatrist pivots to exploring goals rather than dwelling on barriers. When patient becomes cautious about serotonin syndrome, clinician validates concern but redirects to forward-looking questions about next steps. When patient expresses nervousness about talking to doctor, psychiatrist moves to solution-building rather than reinforcing the barrier. One area for improvement: Could use double-sided reflection to more explicitly balance patient's caution with her openness to change, particularly when she states "I haven't changed my prescriptions without asking my doctor first."

### **Overall Session Score: 4.1/5**

## Summary Assessment

**Strengths:**
- Exceptional direction and strategic focus on patient's stated goals without drift
- Strong autonomy support with consistent reinforcement of patient's right to choose
- Solid partnership positioning patient as equal contributor to decision-making
- Good empathy and acceptance with warm, respectful validation of concerns
- Effective problem-solving helping patient identify her own next steps
- Skillful handling of sustain talk by redirecting toward forward-looking questions

**Areas for Development:**
- Inconsistent cultivation of change talk; missed opportunities to amplify and strategically reinforce patient's motivation, particularly after delivering critical information about contraindication
- Becomes somewhat more passive after delivering critical information rather than actively collaborating on problem-solving about alternatives
- Limited deep exploration of patient's anxiety about talking to doctor; could more actively partner in addressing underlying concerns
- Could ask more open-ended exploratory questions after delivering important information
- Could evoke patient's own priorities before suggesting strategies

**Clinical Impact:**
This session successfully engages the patient and supports her autonomy. Alessia clearly feels heard and respected, with high engagement throughout. Her likelihood of following through on consulting her doctor and preparing questions is high. However, her motivation for actively exploring alternative treatment options may be somewhat diminished, as focus shifted to consulting her doctor rather than collaborative problem-solving about alternatives. A more skilled MI clinician would have maintained her change talk about continued desire for relief despite the setback of learning citalopram cannot be safely combined with phenelzine.
