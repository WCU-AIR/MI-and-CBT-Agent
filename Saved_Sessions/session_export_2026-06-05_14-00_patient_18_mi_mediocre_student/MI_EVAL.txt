# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates surface-level warmth but lacks genuine acceptance of client's emotional experience, fears, and ambivalence. Dismissive responses to concerns about judgment and embarrassment undermine therapeutic relationship. No affirmation of client's courage or normalization of addiction struggle. Fails to communicate unconditional positive regard when client expresses shame ("like a leper in a non-leper colony").

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal empathic understanding; relies exclusively on factual reflection rather than emotional attunement. Misses powerful emotional content (shame, isolation, fear of judgment). Uses basic reflections ("I see, so it's both the habit and the stress") followed immediately by advice. Fails to explore complex emotions underlying low confidence (4/10) or to engage with metaphors expressing profound distress. No complex reflections that capture emotional nuance.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Maintains appropriate focus on smoking cessation throughout session. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. Quickly narrows focus to specific interventions (nicotine replacement, support groups, cutting down, tracking) rather than first exploring client's own motivations and values. Repeated use of "maybe you should" signals directive rather than evocative approach. Importance/confidence scaling used prescriptively rather than exploratively.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Profound absence of collaboration. Psychiatrist adopts expert-driven, authoritarian stance throughout. Pervasive prescriptive language ("maybe you should," "it would probably help if") without asking client's input. Unilateral decision-making about interventions. Dismisses client concerns about nicotine replacement and support groups rather than exploring them. No shared decision-making or co-creation. Power imbalance throughout: psychiatrist tells; client listens.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy actively undermined. Prescriptive language dominates, creating external pressure toward compliance rather than supporting client's right to choose. Ambivalence dismissed rather than honored. No explicit affirmation that client has right to choose own path. Emphasis on compliance with recommendations rather than client ownership of change process. Minimization of stated concerns about embarrassment and judgment. Client positioned as passive recipient of expert advice.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Largely absent despite substantial spontaneous change talk from client ("I want to try for real, for my daughter and for myself," "I'm tired of feeling like this"). Psychiatrist rarely asks questions that deepen or amplify client's own reasons for change. Focuses on barriers rather than evoking motivation. Importance/confidence scaling used but not followed by exploratory questions ("What makes it an 8?" "What would help you feel more confident?"). Missed opportunities to ask "Tell me more" or "What else?"

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Completely absent. Psychiatrist assumes expert role and positions self as authority who knows what client needs. Makes unilateral recommendations without exploring client's own ideas or preferences. Dismisses client expertise about own life and change process. No co-creation of session. Unilateral agenda-setting. Power imbalance pervasive. Client positioned as passive; psychiatrist as active and directive. No genuine partnership or shared problem-solving.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** While client provides substantial spontaneous change talk throughout session, psychiatrist does little to actively cultivate, evoke, or reinforce it. Misses numerous opportunities to deepen motivation through follow-up questions. Moves quickly past change talk into advice-giving without exploration. Asks about barriers (sustain talk) rather than amplifying reasons and desires for change. Brief acknowledgment of change talk ("It's good that you want to try for your daughter and yourself") but no strategic questioning to deepen commitment.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates basic competence in not reinforcing sustain talk and normalizes difficulty of behavior change ("It's normal to have some slip-ups"). Avoids direct confrontation with ambivalence. However, lacks sophistication in actively softening barriers. Sometimes inadvertently reinforces sustain talk by focusing on obstacles without exploring client's own resources and strengths. Does not use complex reflections, double-sided reflection, or strategic reframing. Could more effectively highlight past successes and build self-efficacy.

---

## Overall Session Assessment

### **Overall MI Competency Score: 1.9/5**

---

### Summary of Key Findings

**Strengths:**
- Warm, non-confrontational opening stance
- Maintains appropriate focus on smoking cessation
- Avoids arguing with or directly confronting ambivalence
- Normalizes difficulty of behavior change to some degree
- Uses importance/confidence scaling (MI tool awareness)

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Directive, prescriptive stance dominates entire session
- Minimal genuine empathy and acceptance of emotional experience
- Expert-driven rather than collaborative approach
- Limited evocation of client's own motivations and reasons for change
- Dismissal of client concerns rather than exploration
- Missed opportunities for complex reflection and emotional attunement
- Autonomy actively undermined through prescriptive language and pressure toward compliance
- Partnership entirely absent; client positioned as passive recipient of advice

---

### Clinical Implications

**Likelihood of Evoking Intrinsic Motivation:** Low to moderate. Client expresses willingness to comply with recommendations, but this appears to be external compliance rather than genuine intrinsic motivation. Psychiatrist has not effectively explored client's own reasons for change, addressed ambivalence, or supported autonomy. Client likely to experience recommendations as external pressure rather than own choice, reducing long-term adherence and success.

**Risk Factors:** Approach may inadvertently reinforce client's existing shame and self-doubt. Dismissal of concerns about support groups and medication may increase resistance. Lack of genuine partnership may undermine therapeutic alliance and reduce client's sense of agency in change process.

---

### Priority Recommendations for Improvement

1. **Eliminate prescriptive language** and replace with genuine collaborative questions
2. **Develop deeper empathic responses** to emotional content and ambivalence
3. **Actively cultivate change talk** through strategic follow-up questioning
4. **Explore and honor client concerns** rather than dismissing them
5. **Establish true partnership** by explicitly inviting collaboration and respecting autonomy
6. **Use complex reflections** to capture emotional nuance and deepen insight
7. **Affirm and normalize** client's experience and courage
8. **Deepen importance/confidence exploration** with follow-up questions
9. **Support autonomy** by emphasizing choice and client ownership throughout
10. **Develop skill in handling ambivalence** without resolving it prematurely

---

### Recommended Training Focus

Psychiatrist would benefit from substantial training and supervised practice in:
- Shifting from expert-driven to collaborative stance
- Developing genuine empathic attunement to emotional content
- Strategic questioning to evoke and amplify change talk
- Autonomy-supportive communication
- Complex reflection techniques
- Partnership and shared decision-making

**Current MI Competency Level:** Below average. While not actively harmful, session unlikely to effectively evoke client's intrinsic motivation for sustained behavior change.
