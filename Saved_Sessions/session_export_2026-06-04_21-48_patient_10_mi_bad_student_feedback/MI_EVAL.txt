# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates inconsistent acceptance with significant violations in early-to-middle phase (responses 2-3). Initial collaborative opening ("You know yourself best") is undermined by judgmental statements ("you really should quit," "Why didn't you just stop then?", "You must understand"). Patient explicitly responds to lack of acceptance: "it's kind of frustrating to hear." Recovery occurs in later responses with genuine empathy and respect ("It sounds like the stress and family situation are really overwhelming for you"), but the 40% segment of confrontation creates substantial therapeutic rupture. Unconditional positive regard is compromised by implied disapproval of patient's priorities and coping mechanisms.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Empathy is minimal in first half of session. Psychiatrist dismisses patient's primary concern (co-parenting stress) as secondary to smoking cessation and mischaracterizes legitimate barriers as "excuses." Patient's explicit statement "I need someone to hear that part too" indicates failure to accurately understand her internal frame of reference. Later responses (4-8) show improvement with basic reflective listening ("It sounds like the stress and family situation are really overwhelming for you"), but reflections remain surface-level and factual rather than emotionally attuned. No complex reflections that capture ambivalence or deepen insight. Psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore emotional depth of patient's love for son as motivational anchor.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Direction is problematic in early responses with rigid focus on smoking cessation despite patient's explicit statement that family stress is primary concern. Pressure toward immediate commitment ("Are you willing to commit to quitting today?") without exploring readiness. From response 4 onward, direction improves significantly as psychiatrist acknowledges stress as legitimate precursor to smoking change and explores stress management as pathway. However, critical strategic gap exists: psychiatrist never explicitly connects patient's core values (love for son, wanting best for him) back to smoking cessation as motivational anchor. Patient offers this connection implicitly, but psychiatrist fails to amplify or deepen this link strategically.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Collaboration demonstrates significant power imbalance, particularly in early-to-middle phase. Responses 2-3 contain multiple prescriptive, expert-driven statements ("You really should," "You must understand," "You must take responsibility," "You just have to be strong and committed"). Patient's response reflects rejection of expert stance: "it's kind of frustrating to hear." Later responses (4-8) show improvement with invitations to client expertise ("What do you think might help?", "What kinds of activities have you found helpful?"), but collaboration remains somewhat one-directional (clinician asks, client answers) rather than truly equal partnership. Psychiatrist does not fully share power in decision-making or deeply co-construct solutions.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Autonomy support is severely compromised in early responses through prescriptive language and pressure tactics. Statements like "You really should quit," "You must take responsibility," and "Are you willing to commit to quitting today?" remove client's sense of choice and minimize her right to determine timing. Patient explicitly asserts autonomy in response: "I'm not ready to say yes to quitting today." Later responses (4-8) improve with autonomy-supportive framing ("What do you think might help?", "when the time feels right"), but support remains incomplete. Psychiatrist does not explicitly state "This is your decision" or fully embrace client's right to choose any outcome, including continued smoking. Framing remains somewhat clinician-guided regarding eventual smoking cessation.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Evocation is minimal throughout session. Early responses rely on telling and lecturing ("Smoking damages your health and sets a bad example") rather than drawing out client's own motivations. Patient offers substantial change talk ("I love my son dearly," "I want to quit, believe me, I do"), but psychiatrist does not strategically evoke or deepen this. Later responses (4-8) shift to asking questions but fail to deeply explore patient's own motivations, values, and reasons for change. Critical missed opportunities: psychiatrist never asks about connection between son and smoking, never explores what "best for him" means, never asks about client's own awareness of smoking's impact. Patient's intrinsic motivation (love for son, parenting values) is never used as evocative anchor.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Partnership is severely compromised in early-to-middle phase through clear expert positioning and authority stance. Responses 2-3 position psychiatrist as authority judging client's accountability and prescribing solutions. Patient's response reflects rejection: "it's kind of frustrating to hear." Later responses (4-8) show improvement with questions that invite client expertise ("What do you think?", "What kinds of activities have you found helpful?"), but partnership remains incomplete. Psychiatrist does not explicitly acknowledge client's expertise about her own life, does not invite client to teach clinician, and does not fully embrace client as primary expert on her own change. Even in improved responses, clinician-led questioning predominates over truly co-constructed partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Cultivation of change talk is poor throughout session. Patient offers substantial change talk: "I love my son dearly," "I want to quit, believe me, I do," "I want to do better for my son," "I'm willing to take it slow." In responses 2-3, psychiatrist actively suppresses change talk through confrontation ("Why didn't you just stop then?") rather than reinforcing. Later responses (4-8) acknowledge change talk but fail to amplify or deepen it. When patient says "I want to try, though. That feels like a start," psychiatrist validates but does not strategically evoke more. No questions about ability ("What strengths do you have?"), reasons ("What else matters to you?"), or need ("How important is this?"). Psychiatrist misses opportunities to strengthen commitment language and deepen patient's own motivation through strategic reinforcement.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Softening sustain talk is adequate in later responses but initially amplified through confrontation. Early responses (2-3) confront sustain talk directly ("you really have to stop making excuses") rather than softening it, causing patient to become defensive and more entrenched. Later responses (4-8) demonstrate adequate softening through double-sided reflection ("stress makes quitting more complicated") and reframing sustain talk as solvable problem. Psychiatrist explores obstacles without confronting them and validates patient's thinking. However, more sophisticated techniques are absent: no amplified reflection to evoke alternatives, no deep exploration of smoking's function, no reframing that positions stress management as key insight. Softening is basic rather than strategically sophisticated.

### Overall Clinical Assessment

**Session Score: 2.4/5 – Below Average MI Competence**

**Summary of Key Findings:**

This session demonstrates **significant deficiencies in core MI principles**, particularly in the critical early phase. The psychiatrist violates acceptance, autonomy support, evocation, and partnership through confrontation, pressure, and expert positioning in responses 2-3. While recovery occurs in responses 4-8, the therapeutic alliance rupture is substantial and the session never achieves the depth needed to optimally evoke intrinsic motivation for change.

**Strengths Demonstrated:**
- Ability to recognize that initial approach is not working and shift strategy
- Later collaborative, exploratory stance with open-ended questions
- Validation and affirmation of patient's autonomy regarding timing
- Practical problem-solving to identify concrete, manageable first steps
- Recognition that stress management is legitimate precursor to smoking cessation

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- **Eliminate confrontation and pressure entirely** — Early responses actively harm alliance and reduce motivation
- **Develop skill in evoking intrinsic motivation** — Patient's core values (love for son) never strategically explored or connected to smoking cessation
- **Cultivate change talk throughout** — Offered change talk is acknowledged but not amplified or deepened
- **Create complex, emotionally attuned reflections** — Reflections remain surface-level and factual
- **Establish true partnership** — Expert positioning predominates, especially in early phase
- **Maintain consistent MI spirit** — Inconsistent embodiment of acceptance, compassion, and evocation

**Impact on Client Engagement:**
Patient demonstrates partial engagement with stress management plan (moderate confidence 6-7/10) but uncertain readiness for smoking cessation. Early confrontation created defensiveness that was only partially repaired. Patient leaves with concrete first step but without deep intrinsic motivation that would result from consistent MI practice. Connection between core values and smoking cessation goal remains unexplored.

**MI Competency Level:** Foundational knowledge with poor execution and insufficient skill in core techniques. Clinician demonstrates awareness and some flexibility but requires substantial skill development for consistent, depth-oriented MI practice.
