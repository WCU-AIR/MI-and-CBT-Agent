# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Clinician demonstrates near-complete absence of unconditional positive regard. Opening statement establishes judgmental, authoritarian stance ("I'll be telling you exactly what steps to take"). Repeated use of disapproving language ("stop making excuses," "playing with fire," "don't fool yourself") conveys disapproval rather than acceptance. No affirmation of Jason's worth, normalization of struggle, or acknowledgment of his perspective. Dismisses Jason's values around social connection rather than exploring them. Creates defensiveness rather than openness.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no reflective listening demonstrated. Clinician asks closed, leading questions that presume Jason is lying ("Are you sure you're not drinking more often than you're admitting?") rather than seeking genuine understanding. When Jason explains social pressure and calls roommate (harm reduction attempt), these are ignored entirely. No effort to understand Jason's emotional experience, values around friendship, or genuine ambivalence. Clinician lectures at Jason rather than with him. No complex reflections or attempts to capture Jason's worldview.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Clinician maintains clear focus on alcohol use and need to quit, but pursues this through authoritarian, non-collaborative means. Direction is entirely top-down ("you have to quit completely," "you need to commit right now") rather than MI-based. Repeats same demand for complete abstinence without exploring Jason's goals, values, or readiness. No progressive clarification through collaborative exploration. Minimal credit for maintaining focus on target behavior, but manner of maintaining focus is coercive and undermines change motivation.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Clinician explicitly rejects collaboration in opening statement ("I'll be telling you exactly what steps to take"). Adopts clear expert-driven, hierarchical stance throughout. Never seeks Jason's input on solutions or co-constructs any plan. When Jason offers information (called roommate, doesn't drink all the time), these are treated as obstacles rather than incorporated into understanding. No genuine partnership or power-sharing. Clinician positions himself as sole authority; Jason as non-compliant patient requiring correction.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Clinician systematically undermines autonomy through coercive language ("you have to," "you need to," "you must," "no ifs or buts"). False dichotomies used to pressure compliance ("Are you going to take this seriously or keep down this dangerous path?"). No acknowledgment of Jason's right to make decisions or respect for his agency. Particularly problematic given Jason's explicit statement at outset: "I'm not really into being told what to do." Clinician doubles down on telling rather than respecting autonomy concern, predictably increasing defensiveness.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Clinician imposes reasons for change rather than evoking Jason's own motivations. Tells Jason why he should change ("Drinking can cause serious health problems") without exploring Jason's perspective, concerns, or values. Missed opportunities for evocation: Jason's mention of calling roommate (harm reduction awareness), his concern about "ruining future" (values clarification), his difficulty saying no (social pressure exploration). Uses closed, leading questions rather than open questions. No genuine curiosity about Jason's internal motivation or resources for change.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Clinician actively assumes expert role and explicitly rejects partnership. Opening statement positions clinician as sole source of expertise; Jason as passive recipient of instructions. Jason's contributions (perspective on situation, values, concerns) treated as obstacles rather than incorporated into session direction. Implicit message: "Your perspective is wrong; my perspective is right." No reciprocal influence, co-creation of direction, or indication that Jason's wisdom has value. Power dynamic entirely one-directional: clinician → Jason.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session contains minimal change talk from Jason. Only weak commitments offered: "I'll be more careful, sure" and "I'll be smarter about it." Clinician does not reinforce, build upon, or explore these statements. Instead, rejects Jason's offer and demands something more extreme ("You have to commit to quitting now, no ifs or buts"), pushing Jason back into defensiveness. No open questions designed to evoke change talk. No strategic amplification or double-sided reflections to strengthen change talk. Result: Jason provides almost no change talk; increasingly provides sustain talk ("I'm not ready," "I'm not about to throw away my social life").

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Clinician directly confronts Jason's sustain talk rather than softening it, predictably strengthening defensiveness and resistance. When Jason states "It's just part of normal college stuff," clinician responds: "You really need to stop making excuses." Direct confrontation violates MI principles and increases resistance. No double-sided reflections, reframing, or strategic pivoting. Clinician never uses MI technique of rolling with resistance—accepting Jason's perspective and exploring it. Labels Jason's statements as excuses, minimization, and denial. By session end, Jason more entrenched: "I'm not ready to just stop cold turkey over one thing."

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Session Score: 1.0/5**

**MI Competency Level:** Pre-MI / Fundamentally Non-MI

**Key Findings:**

This session represents a fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles across all nine dimensions. The clinician adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance explicitly incompatible with MI. Rather than evoking Jason's intrinsic motivation, the clinician imposes external pressure and demands compliance. Rather than exploring Jason's perspective and values, the clinician dismisses them as excuses and minimization.

**Critical Deficits:**

- No genuine partnership or collaboration
- Systematic undermining of autonomy through coercive language
- Absence of reflective listening and empathy
- Confrontational approach to resistance
- Imposition of clinician's agenda rather than evocation of client's motivation
- Dismissal of client's perspective and values

**Impact on Client Engagement:**

This session actively undermines likelihood of behavior change. Jason enters with ambivalence; leaves more entrenched in sustain talk. Clinician's confrontational approach triggers Jason's defensiveness and autonomy concerns (which Jason explicitly mentioned at outset). Therapeutic relationship damaged; Jason now sees clinician as authority figure to resist rather than partner to work with.

**Most Critical Recommendations:**

1. **Fundamental stance shift:** Move from expert/authority role to genuine partnership
2. **Replace confrontation with curiosity:** Explore Jason's perspective rather than argue against it
3. **Develop reflective listening:** Listen and reflect rather than lecture and direct
4. **Evoke rather than impose:** Ask questions to help Jason discover his own reasons for change
5. **Seek formal MI training and supervision:** This session reveals significant gaps in MI competence

**Prognosis for Change Without Intervention:**

Poor. Without fundamental shift in clinician approach, Jason will likely disengage from treatment or become increasingly resistant. Current approach is counterproductive to behavior change goals.
