
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a near-complete absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. From the opening statement—"I'm here to help you get your health on track today. We're going to focus on what you need to do to improve, so I'll be telling you exactly what steps to take"—the clinician establishes a judgmental, authoritarian stance that fundamentally violates MI spirit.

Throughout the session, the psychiatrist repeatedly uses language that conveys disapproval and judgment: "you really need to stop making excuses," "you're playing with fire," "Don't fool yourself," and "you have to commit to quitting now, no ifs or buts." These statements communicate that Jason is irresponsible, deceptive, and in denial. Rather than normalizing the struggle or affirming Jason's worth as a person, the clinician pathologizes his behavior and treats him as someone who needs to be corrected.

Most critically, the clinician never acknowledges Jason's perspective, validates any of his concerns about social connection, or affirms his autonomy as a young adult navigating college life. When Jason expresses that socializing is important to him ("the whole point of college, right?"), the clinician dismisses this rather than exploring it. There is no warmth, no genuine support, and no communication that Jason is valued beyond his compliance with the clinician's directives.

**Summary:**

The clinician demonstrates consistently judgmental, disapproving language with no affirmation of Jason's worth or normalization of his struggle. This directly contradicts MI's foundational principle of unconditional positive regard and creates defensiveness rather than openness.

---

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective. This session contains virtually no reflective listening and demonstrates minimal effort to understand Jason's experience or worldview.

The clinician asks closed, leading questions ("Are you sure you're not drinking more often than you're admitting?") that presume Jason is lying rather than seeking to understand his actual experience. When Jason explains that saying no is difficult when peers are drinking, the clinician does not reflect this or explore the social pressure he faces. When Jason mentions calling his roommate (suggesting some attempt at harm reduction), this is ignored entirely.

The clinician never captures Jason's emotional experience, his values around friendship and belonging, or the genuine ambivalence he expresses. Instead, the clinician repeatedly lectures about consequences without ever demonstrating that he understands *why* Jason values parties and socializing, or what internal conflict Jason might be experiencing.

A single complex reflection might sound like: "So on one hand, you see this as pretty normal college stuff—everyone's doing it, and it's part of how you connect with your friends. On the other hand, you're here because campus security caught you, and now there's pressure to take this more seriously. That's a tough spot." This never occurs. Instead, the clinician talks *at* Jason rather than *with* him.

**Summary:**

The session contains no meaningful reflective listening, no effort to understand Jason's perspective, and no demonstration of accurate empathy. The clinician lectures rather than listens, creating distance rather than connection.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

While the clinician does maintain a clear focus on alcohol use and the need to quit, this focus is pursued in a way that is antithetical to MI. Direction in MI means strategically guiding the conversation toward change goals *while remaining client-centered*—not imposing direction through authority and repetition.

The clinician's direction is entirely top-down: "you have to quit completely," "you need to commit right now," "you have to take this seriously." This is directive counseling, not MI. The clinician repeats the same demand for complete abstinence across multiple turns without ever exploring Jason's own goals, values, or readiness for change.

There is no progressive clarification of direction through collaborative exploration. The clinician does not ask: "What would need to happen for you to feel ready to make a change?" or "What concerns you most about this situation?" or "What do you see as the real risks here?" Instead, the clinician imposes a predetermined outcome (complete abstinence now) and pressures Jason to accept it.

The minimal credit for a score of 2 (rather than 1) is that the clinician does maintain focus on the target behavior. However, the *manner* of maintaining focus is so coercive and non-collaborative that it actively undermines change motivation.

**Summary:**

While the clinician maintains focus on alcohol use, the approach is authoritarian and top-down rather than collaborative. Direction is imposed rather than co-constructed, which is inconsistent with MI principles.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have valuable knowledge. This session demonstrates the opposite: a clear expert-driven, hierarchical stance where the clinician positions himself as the authority who knows what Jason needs and will tell him exactly what to do.

The opening statement explicitly rejects collaboration: "I'll be telling you exactly what steps to take." This sets a tone of unilateral decision-making that persists throughout. The clinician never asks Jason for his input on solutions, never seeks his perspective on what might work, and never co-constructs any plan together.

When Jason offers information—"I called my roommate," "I don't drink all the time," "it's just when I'm out with friends"—the clinician does not build on these statements or incorporate them into understanding. Instead, these are treated as obstacles to overcome or excuses to dismiss.

Instead, the clinician repeatedly tells Jason what he "has to" do, positioning himself as the expert and Jason as the non-compliant patient.

**Summary:**

The clinician adopts an authoritarian expert role throughout, explicitly rejecting collaboration and treating Jason's input as irrelevant. This is fundamentally incompatible with MI's collaborative spirit.

### 5. Autonomy Support
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Autonomy support means respecting and actively fostering the client's perception of choice and ownership. This session systematically undermines autonomy through coercive language and pressure.

The clinician repeatedly uses language that minimizes choice: "you have to," "you need to," "you must," "no ifs or buts." These phrases communicate that Jason has no choice in the matter—the clinician will decide what happens. When Jason expresses hesitation about quitting completely, the clinician responds with pressure rather than respect: "Are you going to take this seriously or keep down this dangerous path?" This is a false dichotomy that pressures compliance.

The clinician never acknowledges Jason's right to make his own decisions, even poor ones. There is no statement like: "Ultimately, this is your choice. I can share what I think the risks are, but you get to decide what you want to do." Instead, the implicit message is: "You will do what I say, or you will suffer consequences."

This approach is particularly problematic with a 20-year-old who is already demonstrating resistance and who explicitly stated at the outset: "I'm not really into being told what to do." Rather than respecting this autonomy concern, the clinician doubles down on telling Jason what to do, which predictably increases defensiveness and sustain talk.

**Summary:**

The clinician systematically undermines autonomy through coercive language, pressure, and false dichotomies. There is no respect for Jason's right to choose or acknowledgment of his agency in the change process.

### 6. Evocation
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Evocation means drawing out the client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources through skillful questioning and listening. This session demonstrates almost no evocation. Instead, the clinician *imposes* reasons for change without ever exploring Jason's own reasons.

The clinician tells Jason why he should change: "Drinking can cause serious health problems and mess up your future." But the clinician never asks Jason what *he* thinks about his drinking, what concerns *he* has, or what *he* values that might be at stake. Questions like "What worries you about what happened at that party?" or "What do you hope for your future?" or "How do you see this situation affecting your goals?" are entirely absent.

Jason actually provides some openings for evocation that the clinician misses entirely. Jason says: "I don't think one night like this will ruin my health or future." This is an opportunity to explore: "You don't think it will ruin your future. What *do* you think could affect your future? What matters to you about your college experience?" Instead, the clinician dismisses this as minimization.

Jason also mentions calling his roommate, which suggests some awareness of risk and some attempt at harm reduction. Rather than exploring this—"Tell me about that. What made you think to call your roommate?"—the clinician ignores it.

The clinician asks closed, leading questions ("Are you sure you're not drinking more often than you're admitting?") rather than open questions that would evoke Jason's own thinking. ## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish Genuine Partnership and Collaboration (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Begin the session by explicitly inviting Jason into a collaborative process. Acknowledge his autonomy and position yourself as a partner, not an expert who will tell him what to do.

**Example:** 
- "Jason, I know you were referred here because of what happened at the party. I'm not here to lecture you or tell you what to do. You're the expert on your own life. What I'd like to do is understand your perspective on what happened and work together to figure out what makes sense for you. How does that sound?"

**Practice:** 
- Before your next session, write out your opening statement and ensure it includes: (1) acknowledgment of the client's autonomy, (2) explicit rejection of the expert role, (3) invitation to collaborate, (4) genuine curiosity about the client's perspective.
- Practice saying this opening aloud until it feels natural and genuine.

---

### 2. **Develop Genuine Empathy Through Reflective Listening**

**Action:** Replace lectures and closed questions with open-ended questions and reflective listening. Your goal is to understand Jason's world, not to convince him he's wrong.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** "Are you sure you're not drinking more often than you're admitting?"
- **Try:** "Help me understand what a typical weekend looks like for you. What does socializing usually involve?"

- **Instead of:** "You really need to stop making excuses."
- **Try:** "So from your perspective, this was just one party, and you're almost 21 anyway. Tell me more about how you see this situation."

**Practice:**
- In your next session, aim for at least 5-7 open-ended questions before offering any perspective.
- After each client statement, pause and reflect back what you heard before moving forward.
- Record yourself and count: How many reflections did you offer? How many lectures? The goal is reflections >> lectures.

### 3. **Explore Jason's Values and Discrepancy (Not Imposed Discrepancy)**

**Action:** Rather than telling Jason why he should change, ask questions that help *him* discover the discrepancy between his values and his behavior.

**Example:**
- "You mentioned that you value your friendships and social life. That's clearly important to you. What else matters to you about your college experience?"
- "You also got caught by campus security and you're here in this meeting. How do you feel about that? What does that mean for you?"
- "So on one hand, you see this as normal college stuff. On the other hand, you're dealing with a conduct violation. How do you make sense of that?"

**Practice:**
- Identify Jason's stated values (friendship, socializing, being a "normal" college student).
- Identify the discrepancy (he values not getting in trouble, but his behavior led to getting caught).
- Ask open questions that help Jason notice this discrepancy himself, rather than pointing it out.
- Avoid the phrase "but" when reflecting discrepancy—use "and" instead: "You value your social life AND you're concerned about the conduct violation."

---

### 4. **Stop Demanding Abstinence; Explore Readiness for Change**

**Action:** Replace demands for complete abstinence with genuine curiosity about Jason's readiness and ideas for change. Meet him where he is, not where you think he should be.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** "You have to commit to quitting completely right now."
- **Try:** "I hear that quitting completely right now doesn't feel realistic to you. What would feel realistic? What do you think you might be willing to try?"

- **Instead of:** "Are you going to take this seriously or keep down this dangerous path?"
- **Try:** "What concerns you most about what happened? What would you want to be different?"

**Practice:**
- Remove the word "have to" from your vocabulary in sessions. Replace it with "I wonder if..." or "What do you think about..."
- When a client resists your suggestion, pause and ask: "What would need to be true for that to feel doable for you?"
- Explore the client's own ideas for change before offering your own.

### 5. **Roll With Resistance; Don't Confront It**

**Action:** When Jason provides sustain talk or resistance, respond with acceptance and curiosity rather than confrontation. Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge both sides of his ambivalence.

**Example:**

- **Instead of:** "You're making excuses. You need to stop now."

- **Jason:** "I'm not about to give up my social life over one party."
- **Instead of:** "You're playing with fire."
- **Try:** "Your social life and your friendships are really important to you. That makes sense. At the same time, this party led to a conduct violation. How do you see yourself keeping both—your social connections and staying out of trouble?"

**Practice:**
- When you feel the urge to confront or argue, pause instead.
- Reflect back what you heard without judgment.
- Add the other side of the ambivalence using "and" not "but."
- Ask an open question to explore the discrepancy gently.

### 6. **Evoke Change Talk; Don't Impose It**

**Practice:**
