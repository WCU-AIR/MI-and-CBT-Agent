
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **URGENT: Recognize and Respond to Acute Mania**
- **Action:** Immediately conduct a comprehensive psychiatric assessment including: (1) detailed mood and sleep history, (2) assessment of grandiosity and possible psychotic features, (3) suicide/homicide risk assessment, (4) medication and substance use history, (5) determination of whether hospitalization is indicated.
- **Example:** "I want to make sure I understand what's happening. You mentioned you haven't slept in days but don't feel tired. That's unusual and concerns me medically. Tell me more about that. How many nights have you not slept? When you do try to sleep, what happens? And how are you feeling physically—any racing thoughts, lots of energy, or feeling like your mind is going too fast?"
- **Practice:** Review DSM-5 criteria for manic episode and bipolar disorder. Practice conducting a structured mood disorder assessment. Consult with a psychiatrist or bipolar disorder specialist about appropriate assessment and treatment planning for acute mania.

### 2. **Establish Appropriate Clinical Authority and Safety Planning**
- **Action:** Respectfully but firmly address the patient's poor insight and establish clinical concern as the foundation for treatment. Involve family/support system in safety planning and treatment coordination.
- **Example:** "I hear that you feel fine and that others don't understand. I also notice some things that concern me medically—you haven't slept in days but don't feel tired, your thoughts are racing, and you're making major financial decisions based on beliefs about predicting the stock market. These are signs of a medical condition called mania that needs treatment. I want to work with you and your husband to keep you safe. Can we talk about what that looks like?"
- **Practice:** Role-play delivering clinical concerns with warmth but firmness. Practice involving family members in treatment planning. Study motivational interviewing techniques for addressing ambivalence about treatment in patients with poor insight.

### 3. **Prioritize Psychiatric Stabilization Over Cognitive Therapy**
- **Action:** Refer for psychiatric evaluation and medication management. Do not attempt cognitive restructuring as the primary intervention until the patient is stabilized and has adequate insight. Psychoeducation about bipolar disorder should precede cognitive work.
- **Example:** "I think it would be helpful to have you see a psychiatrist who specializes in bipolar disorder. They can evaluate whether medication adjustments are needed and help us understand what's happening. Once you're feeling more stable, we can work together on managing stress and preventing future episodes. Does that make sense?"
- **Practice:** Develop relationships with psychiatrists and bipolar disorder specialists. Learn the role of medication in treating acute mania. Understand when CBT is appropriate (maintenance/relapse prevention) versus when it is not (acute mania with poor insight).

### 4. **Conduct Thorough Assessment of Medication Compliance and Substance Use**
- **Action:** Explore the patient's statement about her husband "telling me to stop taking my meds or drink less." Assess barriers to medication adherence, side effects, and substance use patterns. Develop a plan to address these barriers.
- **Example:** "You mentioned your husband saying something about your meds and drinking. Tell me more about that. Are you taking your fluoxetine as prescribed? What's your experience been with it? And how much alcohol are you drinking during these episodes? I want to understand what's getting in the way of taking care of yourself."
- **Practice:** Develop a structured medication adherence assessment. Learn about common barriers to adherence in bipolar disorder (side effects, denial of illness, substance use). Practice collaborative problem-solving around adherence.

### 5. **Establish a Collaborative Agenda That Addresses the Patient's Actual Concerns**
- **Action:** Acknowledge the patient's perspective while gently introducing clinical concerns. Work collaboratively to define problems and goals that address both the patient's stated concerns and clinical safety needs.
- **Example:** "I hear that you're excited about your blog and want to make it successful. That's important to you. I also want to make sure you're healthy and safe so you can keep working on things that matter to you long-term. Can we talk about what's been happening with your sleep and energy? Because sometimes when those things change, it can affect our judgment and decision-making. What do you think?"
- **Practice:** Study motivational interviewing and collaborative goal-setting. Practice acknowledging patient autonomy while maintaining clinical authority. Role-play with a supervisor.

### 6. **Do Not Use Cognitive Restructuring as Primary Intervention for Acute Mania**
- **Action:** Recognize that cognitive restructuring is not effective for grandiose delusions or the core symptoms of mania. Reserve cognitive techniques for maintenance phase and relapse prevention after stabilization.
- **Example:** Instead of "Try thinking about it differently," say: "Right now, your brain is in a state where thoughts are moving very fast and it's hard to slow them down. That's a medical symptom, not something you can just think your way out of. Medication can help with that. Once you're more stable, we can work on managing stress and recognizing early warning signs of mood changes."
- **Practice:** Study the evidence base for CBT in bipolar disorder. Learn that CBT is most effective for maintenance and relapse prevention, not acute mania. Consult with bipolar disorder specialists about appropriate timing of cognitive interventions.

### 7. **Elicit and Respond to Patient Feedback Throughout Session**
- **Action:** Regularly check for understanding, ask about reactions to interventions, and adjust approach based on feedback. This is especially critical when the patient has poor insight.
- **Example:** "Does this make sense so far?" "How are you feeling about what we're discussing?" "Is there anything that doesn't feel right or that you disagree with?" "What would be most helpful for you right now?"
- **Practice:** Practice genuine feedback-seeking (not leading questions). Develop comfort with silence and allowing the patient time to respond. Practice adjusting your approach based on feedback.

### 8. **Involve Family/Support System in Treatment Planning**
- **Action:** With the patient's permission, involve her husband in treatment planning, psychoeducation, and safety planning. Family involvement is critical in bipolar disorder treatment.
- **Example:** "I'd like to meet with you and your husband together to talk about what's happening and how we can work as a team to keep you safe and healthy. Would that be okay?"
- **Practice:** Learn about family-focused treatment for bipolar disorder. Develop skills in family sessions. Understand the role of family in recognizing early warning signs and supporting medication adherence.

### 9. **Provide Psychoeducation About Bipolar Disorder Before Cognitive Work**
- **Action:** Teach the patient about bipolar disorder, the role of medication, sleep's importance in mood regulation, and early warning signs of mood episodes. This builds insight and motivation for treatment.
- **Example:** "Bipolar disorder is a medical condition where the brain's mood regulation system gets out of balance. When it swings high, we call that mania—and that's what I think is happening now. Mania can feel really good and productive, but it also comes with risks like poor judgment and risky decisions. Medication helps keep your mood stable. Sleep is especially important—when sleep gets disrupted, it can trigger or worsen mania. Does that make sense?"
- **Practice:** Develop a psychoeducation handout about bipolar disorder. Practice delivering it in a way that's understandable and non-judgmental. Study the evidence for psychoeducation in bipolar disorder treatment.

### 10. **Conduct Structured Behavioral Assessment and Planning**
- **Action:** Assess current behaviors (sleep, activity level, substance use, financial decisions) and develop a behavioral plan that prioritizes safety and stabilization. Use behavioral monitoring, not just cognitive work.
- **Example:** "Let's track a few things this week: (1) How many hours you sleep each night, (2) Your mood each morning and evening on a scale of 1-10, (3) Any major decisions or purchases you make. We'll look at the patterns together and see if we can understand what's happening."
- **Practice:** Develop a behavioral monitoring form. Practice setting specific, measurable behavioral goals. Learn to use behavioral data to inform treatment planning.
