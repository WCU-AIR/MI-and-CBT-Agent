
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Feedback: Therapist Evaluation

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

No agenda was established at any point during this session. The therapist never collaboratively identified session goals, prioritized problems, or established what would be addressed during the appointment. Given that Nicholas presents with acute alcohol withdrawal (autonomic hyperactivity, tremor, insomnia, nausea, agitation) and severe alcohol use disorder with imminent marital consequences, the absence of agenda-setting represents a critical failure in basic CBT structure.

The session drifts aimlessly through generic reassurance and personal anecdotes without ever establishing: (1) What specific problems will be targeted today, (2) What the realistic goals are for this session, (3) How time will be allocated, or (4) What the patient should expect to accomplish. A proper agenda would have included assessment of withdrawal severity, safety planning, psychoeducation about withdrawal and relapse risk, identification of high-risk situations, and concrete coping strategies—none of which occurred.

The therapist's repeated opening ("Hey there! How's it going today?" and later "Hey there! It's good to see you today. How's everything going with you?") are generic check-ins, not agenda-setting. Effective agenda-setting would sound like: "Nicholas, I know you're dealing with withdrawal symptoms and real concerns about your marriage. Today I'd like to focus on three things: understanding what's triggering your strongest cravings, developing some concrete strategies you can use when anxiety spikes, and making sure you have a safety plan. Does that sound helpful? What feels most urgent to you right now?"

**Summary:**

The therapist completely failed to establish a collaborative agenda, leaving the session without structure, clear goals, or direction. This is a foundational CBT failure that undermines all subsequent work.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made virtually no attempt to elicit or respond to patient feedback throughout the session. Across eight therapist turns, there is not a single instance of the therapist checking whether the patient understood the rationale for suggestions, whether interventions were helpful, or whether the patient felt heard and validated in a meaningful way.

The therapist asks "What's something you enjoy doing that helps you relax, even for a little bit?" and "Have you been able to get outside for some fresh air lately?" but these are information-gathering questions, not feedback elicitation. True feedback elicitation would include: "Does that make sense to you?" "How are you feeling about what we've discussed?" "Is this approach resonating with you, or would you prefer to focus on something else?" "What's your reaction to the suggestion about getting outside—does that feel realistic or overwhelming?"

Most critically, the therapist never checks whether the patient actually understands or agrees with the interventions being suggested. Nicholas expresses significant fear ("I'm scared I might slip up or fail and lose my wife for good"), but the therapist never asks: "What would help you feel more confident that you can get through this?" or "What barriers do you anticipate when you try to implement these strategies?" The patient's stated difficulty with focus ("it's hard to focus"), his fear of failure, and his sense of being overwhelmed are never explored or validated through feedback loops.

The therapist also fails to summarize key points or ask the patient to summarize what he's taking away from the session—a critical feedback mechanism in CBT.

**Summary:**

The therapist demonstrated minimal feedback elicitation, never checking patient understanding, agreement, or satisfaction. This represents a significant gap in collaborative practice and prevents the therapist from knowing whether interventions are landing or being rejected.

### 3. Understanding

The therapist demonstrates surface-level empathy but fundamentally misses the clinical reality of Nicholas's presentation.
