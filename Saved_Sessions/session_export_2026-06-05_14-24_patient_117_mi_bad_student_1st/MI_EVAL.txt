## Summary Assessment

### Strengths
- Maintains topical focus on health behaviors without significant drift
- Communicates directives clearly and unambiguously

**Note:** These are minimal baseline communication skills and do not constitute strengths in the context of Motivational Interviewing practice.

---

### Critical Deficiencies

**This session represents a fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles and demonstrates a confrontational, expert-driven approach that is actively counterproductive to evoking client motivation for change.**

1. **Complete absence of MI spirit:** Session lacks partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation. Characterized by control, judgment, and coercion.

2. **Active harm through confrontation:** Repeated confrontation of patient resistance increases defensiveness and sustain talk. Research demonstrates this makes change less likely, not more.

3. **Invalidation of autonomy:** Patient explicitly identifies core concern—feeling controlled—and psychiatrist responds by intensifying control. This is opposite of therapeutic responsiveness.

4. **Failure to recognize client feedback:** Patient's minimal responses, repeated resistance, and explicit statements about feeling pressured are clear signals current approach is failing. Psychiatrist escalates rather than adapts.

5. **No exploration of patient perspective:** Psychiatrist never asks what patient values, what matters to him, what he's willing to consider, or what barriers he faces. Session entirely clinician-directed.

6. **Pressure and coercion:** Repeated "you have to," "you must," "it's non-negotiable," "no more delays" creates coercive environment suppressing intrinsic motivation.

7. **Missed opportunities:** When patient says "exercise and better sleep and all that, sure," this is opening to explore what he *is* willing to consider. Psychiatrist ignores and continues pressuring about drinking.

---

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change

**This session will likely produce opposite of intended outcome.**

- **Confrontation increases resistance:** Each confrontation entrenches patient further in his position. By session end, patient is more resistant, not less.

- **Controlling behavior suppresses intrinsic motivation:** Patient explicitly states being told what to do doesn't work for him. Psychiatrist's response is to tell him more forcefully. This decreases motivation.

- **Invalidation damages therapeutic alliance:** Patient's autonomy concerns are legitimate and important. Dismissing these damages trust and relationship.

- **Compliance without commitment is unsustainable:** Even if patient complied, compliance without intrinsic motivation is typically short-lived and leads to relapse.

**Likelihood of change:** Very low. Patient will likely leave more resistant to change, less trusting of psychiatrist, and more entrenched in position against being told what to do.

---

### Immediate Recommendations for Clinician

1. **Fundamental shift from expert-driven to collaborative approach:** Abandon "I will tell you what you need to do" stance. Adopt genuine collaborative posture viewing patient as expert on own life.

2. **Validate autonomy concerns rather than dismissing them:** Treat patient's autonomy needs as valuable clinical information. Reflect with genuine understanding and explore what autonomy means to patient.

3. **Use reflective listening to demonstrate understanding:** Reflect content and emotion before offering perspective. This demonstrates respect for patient's viewpoint.

4. **Evoke change talk through open-ended questions:** Replace directives with questions about patient values, what matters to him, what he's noticed, what he wants different.

5. **Recognize and amplify partial agreement:** When patient expresses any openness—even partial—explore rather than move on. Build on these openings.

6. **Use double-sided reflections for ambivalence:** Acknowledge both sides of patient's ambivalence without confrontation. Validate resistance while gently introducing other perspective.

7. **Offer information as collaborative input, not directives:** Share medical information as perspective for patient to consider, not mandate to follow. Ask permission and explore what patient makes of it.

8. **Establish genuine partnership on planning:** Never impose plan. Collaboratively develop with patient input. Ask what he thinks might work, what barriers he anticipates, what he's willing to try.

9. **Respond to resistance with curiosity, not confrontation:** Treat resistance as information to explore. Ask curious questions about what's underneath rather than arguing.

10. **Develop genuine acceptance of client autonomy:** Fundamentally shift belief system. Accept patient has right to make own choices, even if you believe choices are harmful. Your job is to support autonomy while providing information.

---

### Critical Note for Supervision

**This session demonstrates complete absence of Motivational Interviewing practice.** Psychiatrist should not represent this work as MI. This is confrontational, expert-driven approach contraindicated for clients with autonomy concerns and resistance to change.

**Immediate action required:** Intensive MI training including:
- Didactic training on MI principles and spirit
- Observation of skilled MI practitioners
- Supervised practice with feedback
- Personal reflection on beliefs about client autonomy and control
- Potentially personal therapy to explore clinician's own need for control

**Until training completed, psychiatrist should not conduct MI sessions.** Continuing this practice will harm clients and undermine their motivation for change.
