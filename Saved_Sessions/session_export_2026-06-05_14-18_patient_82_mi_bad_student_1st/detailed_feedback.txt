
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and contains multiple instances of explicit judgment and disapproval. The psychiatrist opens with a directive, controlling stance ("I'll be telling you exactly what steps to take") that immediately signals the client is not valued as an equal partner. Throughout the interaction, the psychiatrist repeatedly labels the client's concerns as "excuses" (used 6+ times: "you're just making excuses," "stop making excuses," "hiding behind your social life as an excuse"), which is fundamentally rejecting and shaming language inconsistent with MI spirit.

The psychiatrist demonstrates active disapproval of the client's perspective on social context. Statements like "That's nonsense" (regarding smoking as part of social life) and "you don't get to make it complicated" directly invalidate the client's lived experience and concerns. The repeated use of "you have to" and "no exceptions/no ifs or buts" conveys coercion rather than respect for the client's autonomy or worth. Most critically, the psychiatrist frames the client's hesitation as moral failure ("if you really cared about your health, you would quit") rather than as understandable ambivalence worthy of exploration. There is zero affirmation of the client's strengths, willingness to attend, or partial change talk ("I'm not saying no forever").

The psychiatrist's tone escalates from directive to confrontational, with statements like "This is not negotiable" and repeated demands for immediate commitment. This is antithetical to acceptance. The client explicitly states fear of isolation and loss of belonging—profound human concerns—and the psychiatrist responds with dismissal ("Hanging out with friends who smoke is not a good enough reason") rather than empathic acknowledgment.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance. Judgment, shame-inducing language, invalidation of the client's perspective, and explicit disapproval dominate the session. This creates a hostile therapeutic environment that violates the foundational MI principle of unconditional positive regard.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective from their frame of reference. This session contains almost no reflective listening and demonstrates active failure to understand or validate the client's experience.

The client repeatedly articulates a core concern: his smoking and meth use are intertwined with his identity, belonging, and social connection in the gay party scene. He states: "It's wrapped up in my nights out, my friends, my whole vibe" and "I'm struggling to hear anything beyond demands without feeling judged." **Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no empathic listening. Zero reflections, no validation of the client's emotional experience, active dismissal of his stated concerns, and complete absence of curiosity about his perspective characterize this session. This is a fundamental failure of MI.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI means maintaining strategic focus on a target behavior while remaining collaborative and client-centered. This psychiatrist maintains focus on smoking cessation (the stated target), but does so in a way that is rigid, non-collaborative, and counterproductive.

The psychiatrist does maintain consistent focus on smoking as the target behavior throughout the session—this is the only element preventing a score of 1. However, the *manner* of maintaining direction is antithetical to MI. Rather than strategically guiding the client toward exploration of change, the psychiatrist uses repetitive demands and threats ("you have to quit now," "this is not negotiable," "no more delays"). This is directive imposition, not strategic guidance.

Critically, the psychiatrist fails to recognize or work with the client's stated hierarchy of concerns. Lukas explicitly prioritizes meth use over smoking ("way less intense than the meth stuff," "meth is already a huge thing to deal with"). An MI-consistent approach would acknowledge this prioritization and potentially explore whether addressing meth first might create conditions for addressing smoking. Instead, the psychiatrist insists on immediate smoking cessation regardless of the client's readiness or priorities.

The psychiatrist also fails to clarify the actual target or explore what "quitting smoking" means to this client in context. Does it mean never smoking again? Reducing frequency? Changing social patterns? These questions are never asked. The direction is imposed rather than co-constructed.

Additionally, the session lacks progressive clarification. The psychiatrist repeats the same demand ("quit now") without adapting strategy when the client repeatedly declines. There is no movement toward understanding barriers, exploring ambivalence, or building motivation—hallmarks of strategic direction in MI.

**Summary:**

While the psychiatrist maintains focus on smoking, the approach is rigid, imposed, and non-collaborative. Direction is maintained through coercion rather than strategic guidance, and the psychiatrist fails to work with the client's stated priorities or readiness.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have valuable knowledge. This session demonstrates authoritarian, expert-driven practice with zero genuine collaboration.

The opening statement establishes the power dynamic immediately: "I'll be telling you exactly what steps to take." This explicitly positions the psychiatrist as the sole authority and the client as a passive recipient of instructions. This is the opposite of collaboration.

Throughout the session, the psychiatrist makes unilateral decisions about what the client "must" do, "has to" do, and what is "non-negotiable." The client's input is systematically rejected: "you don't get to make it complicated," "that's nonsense," "no exceptions." These statements shut down the client's contributions rather than incorporating them into shared problem-solving.

When the client attempts to introduce his perspective—that smoking is connected to social identity and that quitting immediately feels impossible—the psychiatrist does not engage with this as valuable information. Instead, it is framed as resistance or excuse-making. A collaborative approach would be: "Help me understand how smoking fits into your social life and what you're worried might happen if you quit." Instead, the psychiatrist says: "you have to break those ties."

There is no joint problem-solving. The psychiatrist does not ask: "What do you think would help?" "What barriers do you see?" "How might we approach this together?" The client is never invited to co-construct solutions. All solutions are imposed.

The language is consistently hierarchical: "you have to," "you must," "you need to," "there's no excuse." Collaborative language would include: "What do you think?" "How do you see this?" "What would work for you?" "Let's explore this together." None of this appears.

Most damaging: when the client expresses willingness to consider change ("I'm not saying no forever either"), the psychiatrist does not build on this collaborative opening. Instead, the psychiatrist intensifies demands, signaling that the client's partial willingness is insufficient and unwelcome.

**Summary:**

## Recommendations

### 1. **Fundamental Shift from Expert to Collaborative Stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Abandon the directive, expert-driven approach entirely. Reframe your role from "telling the client what to do" to "exploring with the client what might work for him."

**Example:** 
- **Instead of:** "You have to quit smoking right now, no exceptions."
- **Use:** "I'm wondering what your thoughts are about smoking. You mentioned it's part of your social life—help me understand how that works for you."

**Practice:** 
- Before each session, remind yourself: "My job is not to convince this client to change. My job is to help him explore his own reasons for change and his own resources for making it happen."
- Record yourself in practice sessions and count how many times you use "you have to," "you must," "you need to." Aim for zero. Replace with "I'm curious," "Tell me more," "What do you think?"
- Practice saying: "This is your decision. I'm here to support whatever you decide, and I'm also here to explore what might be possible."

---

### 2. **Develop Genuine Empathic Listening and Reflective Responses**

**Action:** Learn to reflect back what the client has said before responding with your own perspective. Use reflections to demonstrate understanding of the client's emotional experience and perspective.

**Example:**
- **Client says:** "It's wrapped up in my nights out, my friends, my whole vibe."
- **Instead of:** "That's nonsense."
- **Use:** "So smoking is really connected to your identity and your sense of belonging with your friends. That's important to you."

**Practice:**
- In every client interaction, aim for at least 3-5 reflections per session. Start with simple reflections (repeating back what was said), then progress to complex reflections (capturing emotion and meaning).
- When a client expresses concern, pause and reflect it back before offering any advice or perspective: "It sounds like you're worried that if you quit smoking, you'll lose your place in your friend group. Is that right?"
- Record sessions and count reflections. Aim for a 2:1 ratio of reflections to questions.

---

### 3. **Eliminate Shame-Inducing Language and Judgmental Framing**

**Action:** Remove words like "excuse," "nonsense," "have to," "must," and "should" from your vocabulary. Replace with curious, non-judgmental language.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** "You're just making excuses. If you really cared about your health, you would quit immediately."
- **Use:** "I hear that quitting feels really difficult right now, especially with your friends. Help me understand what would need to be different for you to feel ready."

**Practice:**
- Create a list of your most common judgmental phrases and their MI-consistent alternatives.
- Before sessions, review this list and commit to using alternatives.
- After sessions, review recordings and identify any judgmental language. Rewrite those moments using non-judgmental alternatives.
- Ask a colleague to listen to a recording and flag any shame-inducing language.

---

### 4. **Develop Skill in Evoking Change Talk Through Open Questions**

**Action:** Learn to ask open questions that draw out the client's own reasons for change, rather than lecturing about reasons you've selected.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** "Smoking even occasionally puts you at serious risk for lung disease and cancer. You need to quit completely."
- **Use:** "What concerns you most about your smoking? How do you see it affecting your life?"

**Practice:**
- Learn the "OARS" framework: Open questions, Affirmations, Reflections, Summaries.
- Practice asking open questions that explore:
  - Desire: "What would be good about quitting smoking?"
  - Ability: "How confident are you that you could cut back if you wanted to?"
  - Reasons: "What are your main concerns about smoking?"
  - Need: "How important is it to you to address this?"
  - Commitment: "What would help you feel ready to make a change?"
- Record yourself asking these questions and listen for whether they genuinely invite exploration or are disguised demands.

---

### 5. **Learn to Work with Ambivalence Rather Than Confronting It**

**Action:** Recognize that ambivalence is normal and healthy. Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge both sides of the client's conflict without pushing toward change.

**Example:**
- **Client says:** "I know smoking's bad, but it's part of my social life."
- **Instead of:** "You need to stop minimizing the risks. Smoking is dangerous no matter how much you do it."
- **Use:** "So on one hand, you recognize that smoking isn't great for your health. On the other hand, it feels really connected to your friendships and your social identity. That's a real tension you're sitting with."

**Practice:**
- When a client expresses ambivalence, pause and reflect both sides before offering any perspective.
- Practice the formula: "On one hand... On the other hand..."
- Recognize that reflecting ambivalence without pushing actually *reduces* sustain talk over time, because the client feels understood rather than attacked.

---

### 6. **Respect Client Autonomy and Prioritize Intrinsic Motivation**

**Action:** Explicitly communicate that change is the client's choice. Avoid demands, ultimatums, or "non-negotiable" statements. Build intrinsic motivation by exploring what matters to the client.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** "This is not negotiable. You have to quit smoking right now."
- **Use:** "This is completely your decision. I'm wondering what you think might be possible, and what would help you feel ready."

**Practice:**
- Before each session, remind yourself: "My job is not to make this client change. My job is to help him explore his own reasons for change and support his autonomy in deciding what to do."
- When you feel the urge to demand compliance, pause and ask: "What would happen if I supported his autonomy instead?"
- Practice saying: "You get to decide what's right for you. What do you think?"

---

### 7. **Work with Client Priorities Rather Than Imposing Your Own**

**Action:** When a client presents multiple concerns, explore which feels most important or urgent to them. Build motivation for change on the client's priorities, not yours.

**Example:**
- **Client says:** "Meth is already a huge thing to deal with. The smokes are not the main problem."
- **Instead of:** "Meth or not, the cigarettes are a ticking time bomb. You have to quit now."
- **Use:** "It sounds like meth feels like the bigger concern right now. Tell me more about that. What would it look like to address that first, and then come back to smoking when you're ready?"

**Practice:**
- In sessions, explicitly ask: "What feels most important to you to work on right now?"
- Build your intervention plan around the client's priorities, not yours.
- Recognize that working on the client's priority builds trust and motivation, which often creates conditions for addressing other concerns later.

---

### 8. **Develop Skill in Softening Sustain Talk Without Confrontation**

**Action:** When the client expresses arguments for the status quo, respond with empathic exploration rather than direct confrontation. Use reframing and strategic questioning to soften resistance.

**Example:**
- **Client says:** "I'd lose my place if I stopped smoking."
- **Instead of:** "That's nonsense. You have to break those ties."
- **Use:** "It sounds like your friendships are really important to you, and you're worried about what might happen if you change. I'm curious—do you think real friends would actually abandon you for taking care of your health? Or might there be a way to stay connected while making changes?"

**Practice:**
- When you hear sustain talk, pause and reflect it empathically before responding.
- Use reframing: "So you're saying that smoking helps you feel connected to your friends. I wonder if there might be other ways to stay connected that don't involve smoking?"
- Practice strategic questioning that explores the sustain talk without confronting it: "Tell me more about that. What do you imagine would happen?"
- Recognize that softening sustain talk through empathy and exploration is more effective than confronting it.

---

### 9. **Build Genuine Affirmation and Recognition of Client Strengths**

**Action:** Actively identify and affirm the client's strengths, efforts, and partial progress. This builds self-efficacy and intrinsic motivation.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** "You're just making excuses."
- **Use:** "I appreciate that you're here and willing to think about this. That takes courage. I also notice that you're not saying no forever—you're open to considering change. That's a real strength."

**Practice:**
- In every session, identify at least 2-3 genuine strengths or efforts to affirm.
- When the client shows partial willingness or change talk, explicitly recognize it: "I hear you saying you're not opposed to cutting back. That's important."
- Recognize that affirmation builds the therapeutic relationship and increases intrinsic motivation.
