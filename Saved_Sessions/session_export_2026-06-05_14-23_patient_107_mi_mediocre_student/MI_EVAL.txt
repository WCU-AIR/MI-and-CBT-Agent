### Empathy

### Direction

Psychiatrist decides early that "cutting down slowly" is appropriate goal and repeatedly returns to this recommendation without exploring alignment with patient's priorities or readiness. Direction is clinician-driven rather than client-centered. Fails to explore deeper, more meaningful direction: patient's role as mother and values regarding son's health could evoke intrinsic motivation but remains unexplored.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Profound absence of genuine collaboration throughout session. Psychiatrist consistently adopts expert-driven, hierarchical stance with recommendations presented as solutions to implement, not ideas to jointly explore. Pervasive advice-giving disguised as questions: "Have you thought about trying something like that?" (Turn 4), "Maybe you should try cutting down slowly" (Turn 6), "Maybe you should try to push through even when it's hard" (Turn 8). Performative autonomy statement ("it's your choice") undermined by entire preceding session of directive pressure.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy actively undermined throughout session. Pervasive "should" statements communicate psychiatrist has determined correct course of action and expects compliance: "should try cutting down slowly," "should push through even when it's hard," "should try to set small goals." When patient expresses legitimate barriers ("days already packed," "not sure something else would work"), psychiatrist does not validate her autonomy in deciding feasibility. Instead counters with suggestions, implying patient's assessment of own capacity is incorrect. Most damaging: "push through even when it's hard" directly pressures patient to override own experience and judgment. Uses external leverage (son) to motivate compliance—violation of autonomy support. Phrase "Remember, it's your choice" in Turn 11 is performative and undermined by entire preceding session of directive pressure.

### Partnership

Repeatedly prescribes solutions (exercise, hobbies, deep breathing, one less cigarette daily) without exploring patient's expertise about what works for her. Hierarchical language ("Maybe you should," "It would probably help if") positions psychiatrist as decision-maker, patient as implementer. Pressures patient to override judgment ("push through even when it's hard"). Unilateral goal-setting without collaborative exploration. Performative autonomy ("Remember, it's your choice") undermined by entire session positioning psychiatrist as expert and patient as compliant implementer.

## SUMMARY ASSESSMENT

**Rating: POOR MI PRACTICE**

**Strengths:**
- Clear focus maintained on target behavior (smoking) throughout session
- Warm, inviting opening establishes basic rapport
- Basic reflective listening demonstrates content is heard
- Acknowledgment that cutting down is challenging
- Commitment to follow-up care offered

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Pervasive directiveness:** Dominated by "should" statements and prescriptive advice; tells rather than explores
- **Failure to explore core values:** Patient's identity as mother and son's wellbeing—most powerful intrinsic motivation source—completely missed
- **Minimal empathic depth:** Surface-level reflections fail to capture emotional nuance, fear, or ambivalence
- **Inadequate ambivalence exploration:** Clear ambivalence present but not explored or amplified
- **Failure to evoke change talk:** Multiple opportunities missed to draw out patient's own reasons for change
- **Inadequate sustain talk management:** Pressures patient to override own experience rather than exploring with curiosity
- **Violation of autonomy support:** Repeated pressure to comply; tells patient to "push through" despite her stated difficulty
- **Absence of genuine collaboration:** Expert role adopted; patient's expertise, creativity, and input not invited
- **Performative autonomy:** "It's your choice" undermined by entire preceding session of directive pressure

**Impact on Client Engagement:**
Likely to **decrease** intrinsic motivation and **increase** defensiveness. Patient's commitment is externally motivated compliance ("I can try") rather than genuine motivation. When stress increases (identified primary trigger), patient likely to return to baseline smoking without self-discovered reasons to maintain change. Likelihood of sustained behavioral change is **low**.

**Most Critical Improvements Needed:**
1. Eliminate all "should" statements; replace with open-ended evocative questions
2. Explore patient's core values (motherhood, son's wellbeing) and connect to smoking cessation
3. Develop complex, emotionally attuned reflections capturing fear and ambivalence
4. Establish true collaboration by explicitly inviting patient's ideas and expertise
5. Cultivate and amplify change talk rather than prescribing reasons for change

**Recommendation:** Formal MI training and clinical supervision strongly recommended to develop core MI competencies.
