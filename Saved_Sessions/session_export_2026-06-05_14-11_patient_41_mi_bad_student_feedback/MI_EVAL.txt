### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates a fundamentally judgmental and disapproving stance throughout the session. Language is laden with criticism ("You really have to quit messing around with those pills," "Why didn't you stop sooner"). The psychiatrist frames the client's struggle as a moral failing rather than a complex clinical presentation. Most critically, when the client expresses fear about failing, the psychiatrist responds dismissively: "If you're scared of failing, that's just your mind playing tricks on you." There is no affirmation of the client's three months of progress, genuine motivation, or honest self-reflection. The stance is fundamentally conditional and coercive rather than warm and supportive, with no unconditional positive regard.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal empathic understanding and fails to reflect the client's perspective from his internal frame of reference. When the client articulates core struggles—"knowing I need to stop and actually stopping are two different things"—the psychiatrist does not explore this insight or acknowledge its validity. Instead, the psychiatrist pivots immediately to prescriptive solutions. When the client expresses fear about failing, the psychiatrist invalidates the emotion entirely rather than reflecting it with understanding. There are no complex reflections capturing emotional nuance. The psychiatrist's responses are consistently focused on what the psychiatrist believes should happen, not on understanding why change is difficult for this particular client.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** While the psychiatrist maintains a clear focus on substance use cessation and family responsibility, the manner in which direction is pursued is inconsistent with MI principles. Direction is imposed unilaterally rather than pursued collaboratively. The psychiatrist asks closed questions demanding compliance ("Are you ready to make that commitment today?") rather than open questions exploring the client's own motivations and barriers. Direction lacks strategic progression—the psychiatrist jumps immediately to prescriptive solutions without first understanding the client's resources, values, or readiness. The psychiatrist never asks what has worked in the three months of sobriety or what the client thinks would help most. Direction is also undermined by failure to respond strategically to the client's ambivalence.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration is virtually absent. The psychiatrist consistently uses imperatives positioning himself as the expert with all the answers: "You need to," "You must," "You should," "You have to." When the client offers his own insight—"I think the biggest challenge is myself"—the psychiatrist does not build on this self-awareness but instead responds with more directives. There is no joint problem-solving. The psychiatrist never asks "What do you think?" or "What feels right to you?" When the client expresses a nuanced perspective about fear and readiness, the psychiatrist treats it as resistance to overcome rather than valuable information to explore collaboratively. The power dynamic is entirely one-directional with no genuine power-sharing.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy is actively undermined throughout the session. The psychiatrist uses language that removes choice: "You need to," "You must," "You have to," "There's no room for hesitation or second-guessing." The psychiatrist explicitly denies the client's right to autonomy and self-determination. Most problematically, the psychiatrist uses the client's love for his children as a coercive tool: "If you keep going like this, it's only going to get worse, and your family will suffer." While family motivation is legitimate, using it as a pressure tactic is coercive and undermines autonomy. When the client expresses reasonable caution about fear and failure, the psychiatrist pressures rather than respects: "You have to push past that fear and just commit fully. Failure isn't an option." There is no affirmation of the client's right to choose his own path or recognition that sustainable change requires genuine commitment rather than compliance under pressure.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation is virtually absent despite rich opportunities. The client spontaneously offers change talk ("I don't want to feel that way again," "my family... my kids," "I'm starting to see that change is possible") that should be amplified through reflections and open questions. Instead, the psychiatrist ignores these opportunities entirely. The psychiatrist never asks open questions designed to evoke the client's own reasons for change: "What matters most to you about being present for your kids?" "What have you learned about yourself in these three months?" The psychiatrist's approach is entirely prescriptive, assuming the client hasn't thought about strategies rather than asking what he's already tried and learned. When the client articulates self-awareness—"I think the biggest challenge is myself"—the psychiatrist does not explore this but responds with directives. There is no curiosity about what has worked, no exploration of coping strategies, and no effort to draw out internal motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1

Language reflects this consistently: "You need to," "You must," "You should," "You have to." The psychiatrist never conveys that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client. When the client expresses a nuanced, thoughtful perspective about fear and readiness, the psychiatrist treats it as resistance rather than wisdom about his own process. ### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Despite multiple instances of spontaneous client change talk, the psychiatrist demonstrates no skill in amplifying or reinforcing these statements. The client offers: "I don't want to feel that way again" (desire), "I want to keep it that way" (commitment), "I'm starting to see that change is possible" (ability), and "I'm willing to try making a schedule and committing to it" (commitment). A skilled clinician would amplify these through reflections and follow-up questions. Instead, the psychiatrist ignores these opportunities entirely. When the client says "I'm willing to try," the psychiatrist immediately pivots to pressure and doubt rather than reinforcing the commitment. The psychiatrist fails to evoke change talk through strategic questioning, asking closed questions demanding compliance rather than open questions that would elicit elaboration. The psychiatrist's approach actually undermines change talk by creating an adversarial dynamic where pressure tactics teach the client to suppress concerns rather than explore them openly.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal skill in softening sustain talk. When the client expresses critical ambivalence—"I don't know if I stopped because I knew I had to stop or because… that's where I wanted to stay"—the psychiatrist does not address this through skillful MI techniques like double-sided reflections. Instead, the psychiatrist simply prescribes more rules and willpower. When the client expresses fear about failing, the psychiatrist responds with direct confrontation ("If you're scared of failing, that's just your mind playing tricks on you") rather than validation and exploration. Research shows confrontation increases rather than decreases resistance. The psychiatrist does attempt to redirect focus by emphasizing family responsibility, but this is a pressure tactic rather than skillful softening. The psychiatrist never uses reframing to help the client see concerns differently. When the client says "I'm not sure I've nailed how to deal with all this yet," the psychiatrist could reframe this as important insight but instead responds with more directives.

## Overall Summary

### **Overall Session Score: 1.3/5**

**Fundamental Assessment:** This session demonstrates **poor motivational interviewing practice** that is fundamentally inconsistent with MI principles and spirit. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance characterized by judgment, pressure, and directive advice-giving.

### Strengths:
- Maintains clear focus on substance use cessation and family responsibility
- Demonstrates genuine concern for client wellbeing and family relationships
- Attempts to encourage behavioral change through concrete suggestions

### Critical Areas for Improvement:
- **MI Spirit Violation:** Judgmental, coercive stance antithetical to MI principles
- **Absence of Empathy and Acceptance:** Dismisses emotional experience, invalidates concerns, fails to affirm progress
- **Directive Rather Than Evocative:** Tells rather than asks; prescribes rather than explores
- **Undermines Autonomy:** Uses pressure tactics and coercive language denying client self-determination
- **Fails to Address Ambivalence:** Ignores core ambivalence about whether client chose to stop or was circumstantially forced
- **Confrontational Sustain Talk Handling:** Uses direct confrontation rather than skillful MI techniques
- **No Collaborative Problem-Solving:** Maintains expert-driven stance with no genuine partnership
- **Missed Change Talk Opportunities:** Despite multiple instances of client change talk, fails to amplify or reinforce

### Impact on Client Engagement:
The psychiatrist's approach is likely to **decrease** intrinsic motivation and **increase resistance**. Research demonstrates that confrontational, directive approaches that undermine autonomy actually increase sustain talk and reduce commitment to change. The client enters with genuine motivation and three months of demonstrated progress, but the psychiatrist's judgmental stance and pressure tactics are likely to trigger defensiveness. Coercive use of family responsibility may produce short-term compliance but is unlikely to support sustained, intrinsic motivation.

### Recommendation:
**Immediate action required.** The psychiatrist should engage in formal MI training with practice and feedback. The current approach risks harming the therapeutic relationship, increasing defensiveness, and reducing likelihood of sustained behavior change. With focused training on evocation, empathy, autonomy support, and collaborative problem-solving, this psychiatrist can develop genuine MI competence and provide more effective, evidence-based care.
