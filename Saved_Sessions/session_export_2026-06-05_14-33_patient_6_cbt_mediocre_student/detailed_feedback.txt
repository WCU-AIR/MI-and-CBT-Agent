
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist failed to establish a structured, collaborative agenda at any point in the session. In the opening, the psychiatrist states: "For our session, I thought maybe we could just talk about whatever's been on your mind lately, and see if we can figure out some ways to feel a bit better. Does that sound okay?" This is vague, non-specific, and lacks any prioritization of problems or time allocation. There is no explicit discussion of session goals, target problems, or what Matthew hopes to accomplish in treatment.

Throughout the session, the therapist drifts reactively from topic to topic—business worries, family concerns, sleep problems, alcohol cravings—without ever establishing which problems are most urgent or how session time will be allocated. A proper CBT agenda would have included: (1) explicit identification of the primary treatment target (alcohol use disorder vs. depressive symptoms), (2) prioritization of problems (e.g., "Should we focus first on managing cravings or addressing the thoughts that trigger them?"), (3) time boundaries, and (4) collaborative agreement on what success looks like for this session.

The second session opening is identical—"maybe we can just chat a bit about what's been going on for you recently"—demonstrating no learning or structure across sessions. This is particularly problematic given Matthew's presentation: he has multiple overlapping problems (AUD, alcohol-induced depression, insomnia, occupational anxiety, family strain) that require prioritization. Without agenda-setting, the session becomes unfocused and reactive rather than strategic.

**Summary:**

No meaningful agenda was established in either session. The therapist used vague opening language and allowed the session to drift without prioritization, time management, or collaborative goal-setting. This is a critical foundational failure in CBT structure.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrates basic comprehension of Matthew's explicit statements but misses critical nuances and the underlying clinical picture. When Matthew describes his thoughts ("What if I mess everything up? What if customers start leaving?"), the therapist accurately reflects these concerns. However, the therapist fails to grasp several important clinical realities:

**What was missed:**

1. **The distinction between primary and secondary problems:** Matthew presents with both alcohol use disorder and alcohol-induced depressive disorder. The therapist never clarifies whether the depressive symptoms (fatigue, anhedonia, insomnia, concentration problems) are driving the alcohol use or vice versa. This is clinically crucial because the treatment approach differs significantly. The case formulation indicates depressive symptoms should improve with abstinence, yet the therapist treats them as independent problems requiring cognitive restructuring.

2. **The role of withdrawal and early abstinence:** Matthew is only 3 days sober. The therapist does not acknowledge that his current dysphoria, fatigue, insomnia, and racing thoughts may be partly withdrawal-related or acute depressive symptoms that may resolve with continued abstinence. Instead, the therapist immediately prescribes cognitive coping strategies as if these are stable, chronic thought patterns.

3. **The severity and chronicity of depressive symptoms:** Matthew reports "interval insomnia" (waking at 2 A.M.), fatigue so severe he lacks energy for walks, anhedonia ("haven't really been doing hobbies much"), and concentration problems. These are not mild symptoms. The therapist treats them casually ("maybe try some relaxing activities") without assessing suicide risk, severity, or whether pharmacological intervention might be warranted.

4. **The functional impairment:** Matthew owns a business, is married 18 years, and has maintained employment despite heavy drinking. The therapist does not explore his strengths, coping history (he achieved two 30-day abstinence periods), or what has worked before. The therapist's understanding remains surface-level.

5. **The temporal relationship between worries and drinking:** Matthew states, "usually after work I just want to sit down and unwind, and that's when I'm tempted to drink." This suggests a behavioral pattern (work stress → fatigue → craving for relief) that requires functional analysis, not just thought replacement. The therapist misses this.

**What was done well:**

The therapist does accurately reflect Matthew's core worry ("What if I mess everything up?") and acknowledges the emotional difficulty of changing thought patterns ("it just feels like the doubts are louder than the confidence"). The therapist demonstrates empathy through statements like "Yeah, those worries sound pretty intense."

**Summary:**

The therapist grasps surface-level content but misses critical clinical distinctions (primary vs. secondary problems, withdrawal effects, severity of depression, functional analysis of drinking triggers). Understanding remains adequate for basic rapport but insufficient for effective case conceptualization.

### 7. Guided Discovery
