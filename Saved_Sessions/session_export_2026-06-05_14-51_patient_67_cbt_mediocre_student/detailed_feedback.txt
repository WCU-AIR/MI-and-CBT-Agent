
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

2 = Barely Adequate

6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made minimal effort to establish a structured, collaborative agenda. In the first session, the therapist opened with a vague invitation ("talk a bit about anything that's been bothering you recently") rather than explicitly setting priorities or time-bound goals. While the patient identified fatigue as the primary concern, the therapist never explicitly confirmed this as the session agenda, established specific measurable objectives, or discussed what could realistically be accomplished in the available time.

Most critically, the therapist failed to recognize or address a fundamental clinical reality: **César has documented Obstructive Sleep Apnea Hypopnea (OSAH) with an apnea-hypopnea index of 25 events/hour, oxygen desaturation to 82%, and polysomnography confirmation.** The fatigue, daytime sleepiness, and afternoon energy crashes are medical symptoms of untreated sleep apnea—not primarily psychological phenomena amenable to cognitive restructuring. The therapist's agenda implicitly treated this as a mood/anxiety disorder requiring thought-challenging, completely missing the medical diagnosis that should have been the primary focus.

In the second session, the therapist repeated the same vague opening ("talk a bit about anything that's been bothering you lately") without reviewing what was accomplished in session one, whether homework was completed, or what the current session's specific objectives were. There was no agenda-setting whatsoever—just reactive listening to patient concerns.

**Summary:**

The therapist failed to establish explicit, collaborative, time-bound agendas in either session and, more critically, failed to recognize that the presenting symptoms are primarily medical (sleep apnea) rather than psychological, fundamentally misaligning the treatment approach.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made virtually no attempt to elicit or respond to patient feedback throughout either session. Specific deficiencies include:

1. **No comprehension checks:** The therapist never asked, "Does that make sense?" or "Am I understanding you correctly?" after explaining cognitive concepts. When the therapist suggested thought-challenging ("reminding yourself that you're trying your best"), there was no verification that the patient actually understood the mechanism or found it credible.

2. **No reaction elicitation:** The therapist never asked, "How do you feel about this approach?" or "Does this seem like something that might work for you?" The patient's agreement appears to be compliance rather than genuine buy-in.

3. **No mid-session adjustment:** When the patient revealed that afternoon fatigue is the primary problem and that he "can't keep focus," the therapist did not pause to ask, "Given what you've just told me, do you think the strategies we're discussing will actually address the root cause?" 4. **No end-of-session summary check:** While the therapist summarized the homework at the end of session one ("you'll try taking breaks...remind yourself of positive thoughts...notice your tiredness"), there was no explicit request for feedback: "Does this summary capture what we discussed? 5. **No assessment of session helpfulness:** Neither session ended with, "Was this session helpful for you?" or "What was most useful today?" or "What could I have done differently?"

The therapist operated in a one-directional mode: provide suggestions, receive patient agreement, move forward. This is antithetical to collaborative CBT.

**Summary:**

The therapist demonstrated minimal feedback-seeking behavior, never checking comprehension, adjusting based on patient reactions, or assessing session helpfulness. This represents a critical gap in collaborative practice.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated surface-level understanding of the patient's explicit statements but missed critical clinical information and failed to grasp the underlying medical reality.

**What was understood:**
- The patient experiences fatigue and daytime sleepiness
- These symptoms affect work performance and create worry about job loss
- The patient has negative automatic thoughts about failure and safety

**What was missed or misunderstood:**

The therapist never asked clarifying questions about sleep quality, snoring, witnessed apneas, morning headaches, or nocturia—all red flags for OSAH. The therapist's case formulation treated this as a mood/anxiety disorder rather than recognizing the medical etiology.

2. **Severity of safety concern underestimated:** The patient explicitly stated worry about "nearly nodding off...while driving" and "put myself or others in danger." This is a serious safety issue requiring immediate medical intervention (CPAP therapy, sleep medicine referral), not cognitive restructuring. The therapist's response was to suggest thought-challenging ("reminding yourself that you're managing as well as you can"), which is inadequate for a patient at risk of causing a motor vehicle accident due to untreated sleep apnea.

3. **Fatigue vs. depression distinction missed:** The patient stated, "It's just hard to feel that way when I'm so tired all the time," and "the way I'm thinking just makes me feel stuck." The therapist interpreted this as primarily cognitive/emotional ("those thoughts make me feel tense and more anxious") rather than recognizing that the fatigue is the primary driver and the anxiety is secondary to the exhaustion. 4. **Afternoon pattern significance:** The patient identified that "after lunch, I feel like my energy just drops off" and "afternoons and long meetings seem to be the hardest times." 5. **Lack of diagnostic curiosity:** A competent clinician would have asked: "How many hours are you actually sleeping?" "Do you snore?" "Has anyone noticed you stop breathing?" **Summary:**

The therapist understood the patient's surface complaints but failed to recognize the medical diagnosis of obstructive sleep apnea, instead misformulating the case as a primary anxiety/mood disorder. This represents a fundamental failure of clinical understanding with serious safety implications.
