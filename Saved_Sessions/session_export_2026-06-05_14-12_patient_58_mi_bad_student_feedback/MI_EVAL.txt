# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5
**Comments:** Minimal acceptance demonstrated throughout most of the session. Psychiatrist uses judgmental language ("You really should quit," "You need to stop as soon as possible") and dismisses client's contextualization as "just an excuse" rather than accepting lived reality. Brief shift toward genuine understanding mid-session ("I realize now that just telling you to stop drinking isn't really helping") shows capacity for acceptance, but this is undermined by return to directive language in final exchanges. Unconditional positive regard largely absent.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Severely limited empathy with one strong exception. Psychiatrist demonstrates factual redirection rather than empathic understanding in early exchanges. One complex, accurate reflection captures emotional significance of team belonging and functional role of alcohol: "It sounds like being part of the team and feeling connected is really important to you, and drinking is part of how you bond and relieve stress. That makes a lot of sense." However, this is not sustained. Final exchanges show collapse of empathy—when client expresses fear of isolation, psychiatrist responds prescriptively rather than validating legitimate social concerns. Emotional nuance consistently missed.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3.5
**Comments:** Clear, consistent focus on alcohol reduction maintained throughout session. Target behavior remains explicit and does not drift. However, manner of maintaining direction is problematic—oscillates between collaborative and authoritarian approaches. Early exchanges impose direction unilaterally ("You need to stop as soon as possible"). Middle section shows more collaborative direction ("What do you think might be some ways...?"). Final exchange reverts to imposition ("So here's what you need to do: you have to stop drinking completely"). Inconsistency weakens overall directional effectiveness.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Largely absent with only brief moments of genuine partnership. Opening establishes clear expert-driven, hierarchical dynamic with closed questions and unilateral pronouncements. When client attempts to explain perspective, psychiatrist dismisses as rationalization rather than collaborating. Genuine collaborative moment emerges mid-session ("I want to understand more about your experience"), but is not sustained. Psychiatrist quickly returns to expert role ("So here's what you need to do"). Minimal evidence of shared decision-making or co-construction of solutions throughout session.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5
**Comments:** Severely deficient throughout. Repeated use of controlling language—"have to," "need to," "should," "must"—communicates that change is mandatory obligation, not client choice. Directly contradicts MI core principle of autonomy support. When client expresses ambivalence, psychiatrist does not validate autonomy or explore perspective; instead responds prescriptively. Never affirms client's freedom to choose or emphasizes that change must come from client's own motivation. Message conveyed: "I (expert) have decided what you must do, and you must do it." This controlling approach likely increases resistance.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal and inconsistently applied. Psychiatrist imposes reasons for change rather than evoking client's own motivations. When client spontaneously offers change talk ("I know that it's bad for me"), psychiatrist does not amplify or explore; instead dismisses as "excuse." One significant evocative moment: "What do you think might be some ways to keep that connection and have fun with your teammates without relying on alcohol?" successfully draws out client's own ideas. However, this approach not sustained. Psychiatrist never explores client's own values regarding health or athletic performance; instead assumes these matter and imposes them. Overall pattern is telling rather than asking.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Largely absent. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role throughout most of session, positioning himself as authority who knows what is best. Early statements ("You really should quit," "Why didn't you succeed?") communicate psychiatrist possesses knowledge and authority to direct behavior. When client explains perspective, psychiatrist dismisses as rationalization—not partnership but override of client's understanding of own situation. Brief genuine partnership moment: "I realize now that just telling you to stop drinking isn't really helping. I want to understand more about your experience." However, this is short-lived; psychiatrist quickly reasserts expert role. Minimal evidence that psychiatrist views client as possessing wisdom about own situation.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Poorly executed with repeated missed opportunities. Client spontaneously offers change talk ("I know that it's bad for me," "I want to be in shape for my sport"), but psychiatrist fails to amplify, explore, or reinforce. When client states "I want to be in shape for my sport," psychiatrist responds prescriptively rather than selectively amplifying this change talk. One excellent evocative question ("What do you think might be some ways...?") elicits change talk from client ("Maybe if there was something else the whole team could do together..."), but psychiatrist does not reinforce or build on this. Psychiatrist never asks strategic questions designed to evoke change talk (e.g., "What concerns do you have?" "What would be the benefits?"). Relies on telling rather than strategic questioning.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5
**Comments:** Nearly absent. Client repeatedly expresses legitimate sustain talk rooted in genuine peer dynamics and social consequences ("If I don't join in, it feels like I'm not part of the team," "I'm worried I'd be the odd one out"). Psychiatrist directly confronts rather than skillfully softens: "Drinking with the team is just an excuse. Why didn't you try harder to quit before?" This is confrontational and dismissive, intensifying rather than softening sustain talk. When client expresses concern about isolation, psychiatrist does not explore or validate; instead responds prescriptively. No use of double-sided reflections, reframing, or exploration of ambivalence. Sustain talk is intensified rather than softened, likely increasing resistance to change.

---

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall Score: 2.0/5 – Poor Motivational Interviewing Practice**

### Strengths:
- ✓ One moment of genuine empathic reflection demonstrating capacity for accurate, complex understanding
- ✓ Brief recognition of ineffective approach and willingness to shift strategy mid-session
- ✓ One effective open question that successfully evokes client's own ideas
- ✓ Clear, consistent focus on target behavior (alcohol use)
- ✓ Client remains engaged despite non-MI approach, suggesting baseline rapport exists

### Critical Deficiencies:
- ✗ **Pervasive controlling language** ("have to," "need to," "should," "must") present in nearly every response—directly contradicts MI principles and communicates change as mandatory
- ✗ **Dismissal of client perspective** when client contextualizes behavior; labeled as "excuses" rather than accepted as lived reality
- ✗ **Failure to amplify spontaneous change talk**; repeated missed opportunities to strengthen intrinsic motivation
- ✗ **Confrontation of sustain talk** rather than skillful softening; intensifies resistance rather than reducing it
- ✗ **Oscillation between approaches**; brief collaborative moment undermined by reversion to directive language
- ✗ **Imposition of direction** rather than collaborative exploration; removes client autonomy
- ✗ **Failure to explore ambivalence**; does not help client work through genuine conflict between health goals and peer belonging needs

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change:
**Likely to DECREASE intrinsic motivation and INCREASE resistance.** Controlling language activates psychological reactance. Client's final response ("I'm not sure I can just commit to cutting it out completely right now") reflects this resistance. Session fails to address core issue: client's genuine ambivalence rooted in conflict between health/performance desires and peer belonging needs. Psychiatrist implies false dichotomy (choose health OR belonging) rather than exploring how to maintain connection while reducing risk.

### Primary Recommendation:
**HIGHEST PRIORITY: Eliminate controlling language immediately.** Replace "have to/need to/should/must" with autonomy-supportive language ("I wonder if..." "What do you think about..." "You could consider..."). This single pattern is most damaging element of session and must be addressed before other improvements can be effective.

---

**Recommendation for Training:** Intensive MI training focused on: (1) eliminating controlling language, (2) developing consistent empathic reflection, (3) amplifying change talk systematically, (4) softening sustain talk through exploration rather than confrontation, and (5) understanding collaborative spirit of MI. Role-play practice with feedback, session recording review, and supervision focused on these specific competencies essential.
