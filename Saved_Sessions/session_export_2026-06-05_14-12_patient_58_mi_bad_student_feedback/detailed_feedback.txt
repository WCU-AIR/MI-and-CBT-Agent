
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1.5/5**

**Explanation:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal acceptance throughout most of the session, with only a brief shift toward the end. In the opening exchanges, the clinician uses highly judgmental language and assumes pathology without exploration: "You really should quit because smoking is very harmful," "You need to stop as soon as possible to avoid serious health problems," and "Don't you want to feel better and live longer?" These statements communicate disapproval and position the clinician as the arbiter of what is "right," undermining unconditional positive regard.

When Jamal attempts to contextualize his behavior—"it's not like I'm trying to hurt myself or anything" and "I don't know, like being in a sports team, y-you kind of have to go along with the group sometimes"—the psychiatrist dismisses this as "just an excuse" rather than accepting it as Jamal's lived reality. This invalidation is particularly damaging because it rejects the client's perspective without understanding it first. The statement "Drinking with the team is just an excuse" is directly confrontational and communicates judgment of Jamal's reasoning.

There is a notable shift in the middle of the session when the psychiatrist says, "I realize now that just telling you to stop drinking isn't really helping. I want to understand more about your experience." This represents a genuine attempt at acceptance and represents the only moment where unconditional positive regard emerges. However, this improvement is undermined in the final exchange when the psychiatrist returns to directive language: "So here's what you need to do: you have to stop drinking completely" and "You need to take your health seriously if you want to be a top athlete." The use of "have to" and "need to" reestablishes a judgmental, prescriptive stance that contradicts acceptance.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates poor acceptance overall, characterized by early judgment, dismissal of the client's perspective, and a return to directive language despite a brief moment of genuine understanding. Unconditional positive regard is largely absent.

**Summary:**

Direction is maintained with clear focus on alcohol reduction, but the approach shifts between collaborative and prescriptive, undermining the consistency and effectiveness of strategic guidance. Early and late exchanges show problematic imposition of direction.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Eliminate Controlling Language (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Replace "have to," "need to," "should," and "must" with language that respects autonomy: "I wonder if..." "What do you think about..." "You could consider..." "It's up to you whether..."
- **Example:** Instead of "You have to stop drinking completely," say: "I'm curious—what do you think would happen if you drank less at team events? What would that be like for you?"
- **Practice:** Record yourself and count instances of controlling language. Aim for zero. Practice reframing prescriptive statements into open questions. Role-play with a colleague, having them give you feedback on autonomy-supportive language.

---

### 2. **Amplify and Reinforce Spontaneous Change Talk**
- **Action:** When the client offers change talk, pause and explore it. Ask follow-up questions that deepen and strengthen the client's own motivation.
- **Example:** When Jamal said "I know that it's bad for me," the psychiatrist should have responded: "Tell me more about that. What concerns you most about the drinking?" or "How does that fit with what you want for yourself as an athlete?"
- **Practice:** In supervision, identify all instances of client change talk in recordings. For each instance, write down what you said and what you could have said to amplify it. Practice these amplification responses until they become automatic.

---

### 3. **Validate and Explore Sustain Talk Rather Than Confronting It**
- **Action:** When the client expresses reasons for the status quo, use double-sided reflections and exploration rather than dismissal. Acknowledge the legitimacy of the concern while gently exploring it.
- **Example:** Instead of "Drinking with the team is just an excuse," say: "So you're caught between wanting to protect your health and not wanting to lose your place on the team. That's a real tension. Tell me more about what that's like."
- **Practice:** In role-plays, practice responding to sustain talk with curiosity rather than confrontation. Use phrases like "I hear you saying..." and "That makes sense because..." to validate before exploring.

---

### 4. **Develop Genuine Empathic Reflections Consistently**
- **Action:** Before offering advice or direction, reflect back what you hear the client saying, including the emotional content and the client's perspective. Use complex reflections that capture both the surface content and the underlying meaning.
- **Example:** "It sounds like being part of the team is one of the most important things in your life right now, and drinking is woven into how you all connect and have fun together. And at the same time, you're aware that it might not be the best for your health or your performance. Is that right?"
- **Practice:** In every session, aim for at least 3-5 complex reflections before offering any advice. Record sessions and listen for the depth and accuracy of your reflections. Ask clients for feedback: "Did I understand that correctly?"

---

### 5. **Establish Genuine Partnership by Asking Rather Than Telling**
- **Action:** Shift from a stance of "I know what you need to do" to "I'm curious about your perspective." Ask open questions that invite the client to be the expert on their own situation.
- **Example:** Instead of "So here's what you need to do: you have to stop drinking completely," ask: "What do you think would need to happen for you to feel ready to make a change? What would that look like?"
- **Practice:** Before each session, write down 5-10 open questions you might ask. Practice asking questions rather than making statements. In supervision, have your supervisor count the ratio of questions to statements. Aim for at least 70% questions.

---

### 6. **Explore Ambivalence Rather Than Resolving It Prematurely**
- **Action:** Recognize that ambivalence is normal and healthy. Help the client explore both sides of the conflict without pushing toward one resolution. Use the "decisional balance" approach: explore both reasons for change and reasons for status quo.
- **Example:** "So on one hand, you want to be in top shape for your sport and you know drinking isn't helping that. On the other hand, you're worried that if you stop, you'll lose connection with your teammates. Let's explore both of those. What would it mean to you to be in top shape? And what would it mean to lose that connection?"
- **Practice:** In role-plays, practice sitting with ambivalence without trying to resolve it. Resist the urge to tell the client what to do. Instead, help them explore the conflict more deeply.

---

### 7. **Maintain Collaborative Momentum Throughout the Session**
- **Action:** Once you shift to a collaborative, empathic approach, sustain it. Do not revert to directive language. If you notice yourself slipping back into "have to" or "need to," pause and reframe.
- **Example:** In this session, the psychiatrist had a strong collaborative moment ("I want to understand more about your experience") but then reverted to directive language in the final exchange. Sustaining the collaborative approach would have meant: "Based on what you've shared, it sounds like the key is finding ways to stay connected to your team while also protecting your health. What ideas do you have about how that might work?"
- **Practice:** At the end of each session, reflect on whether you maintained consistency in your approach. If you shifted between collaborative and directive, identify what triggered the shift and practice responding differently in future sessions.

---

### 8. **Explore the Client's Own Values and Priorities**
- **Action:** Rather than assuming what matters to the client (health, athletic performance), ask the client directly what matters to them and why. Build motivation for change based on the client's values, not the clinician's.
- **Example:** "What matters most to you in your life right now? How does drinking fit into that? How does your health fit into that?"
- **Practice:** In every session, ask at least one question about the client's values: "What's important to you?" "What do you want your life to look like?" "What would success look like for you?" Listen carefully to the answers and build your understanding of motivation from there.

---

### 9. **Use Strategic Questioning to Evoke Change Talk**
- **Action:** Ask specific questions designed to elicit the client's own reasons for change, confidence in their ability to change, and commitment to change. These are the key components of change talk.
- **Example:** 
  - Desire: "What would be the benefits of reducing your drinking?"
  - Ability: "If you decided to make a change, how confident would you be that you could do it?"
  - Reasons: "What concerns do you have about continuing to drink?"
  - Need: "How important is it to you to be in top shape for your sport?"
  - Commitment: "What would it take for you to feel ready to try something different?"
- **Practice:** Develop a set of evocative questions for the specific behavior you're addressing. Practice asking these questions in role-plays. In real sessions, use these questions strategically to draw out change talk.

---

### 10. **Develop Self-Awareness About Your Own Agenda**
- **Action:** Recognize that you have an agenda (reducing the client's drinking), but that agenda must not override the client's autonomy or the collaborative relationship. Check yourself: Are you trying to convince the client, or are you trying to understand and support the client's own motivation?
- **Example:** In this session, the psychiatrist's agenda (stop drinking) was so strong that it overrode empathy and collaboration. A more balanced approach would be: "I'm concerned about your drinking because I care about your health and your future. And I also recognize that this is your decision to make. What are your own thoughts about it?"
- **Practice:** In supervision, discuss your own reactions to clients who are ambivalent or resistant. Explore whether your agenda is getting in the way of genuine partnership. Practice letting go of the need to convince and instead focusing on understanding and supporting.
