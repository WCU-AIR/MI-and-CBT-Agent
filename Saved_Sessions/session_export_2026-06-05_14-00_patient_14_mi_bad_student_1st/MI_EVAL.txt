### Acceptance

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Empathy is almost entirely absent. No genuine effort to understand patient's perspective, fears, or internal barriers. When patient expresses feeling "overwhelmed," response is pressure rather than curiosity. Patient's fear of failure ("I'm scared I'll mess up again") is dismissed as excuse rather than explored. ### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains clear focus on smoking cessation (preventing score of 1), but direction is imposed rather than collaboratively established. ### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support actively undermined throughout session. Removal of choice evident in repeated directives: "You have to quit completely today," "You need to commit right now," "You should just throw away your cigarettes today." False binary ultimatums ("Are you going to quit today or not?" repeated multiple times) pressure compliance rather than support autonomous decision-making. Patient's stated readiness repeatedly dismissed: "This isn't about feeling ready—it's about taking action." Psychiatrist tells patient their own sense of readiness is irrelevant. Hesitation pathologized as character flaw rather than respecting patient's right to move at own pace. Patient's suggestion to "start small" rejected as invalid option, removing autonomy. Fundamental violation of autonomy support.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out client's own motivations, values, and reasons for change—entirely absent. Psychiatrist imposes external reasons: "Your life depends on it," "You're risking serious diseases," "Smoking is really bad for your health." Patient offers spontaneous change talk ("I want to quit, I really do," "I do want to live longer and feel better," "I want to do this"), but these precious moments are ignored or dismissed rather than amplified. No reflections or strategic questions explore what motivates patient. Critical evocative questions absent: "What would be good about quitting?" "How would your life be different?" "What matters most to you about your health?" No attempt to evoke confidence or self-efficacy. When patient expresses doubt, response is "You have to push through" rather than exploring strengths or building confidence. Fundamental failure of evocation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership—understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within client—completely absent. Psychiatrist actively assumes sole expert role: "I'll tell you what you need to do," "You have to understand," "You need to commit," "You should just throw away your cigarettes." Patient's self-knowledge dismissed as irrelevant: when patient expresses concern about readiness, psychiatrist responds "This isn't about feeling ready—it's about taking action." Patient's input does not shape session. Repeated ultimatums create power dynamic where psychiatrist holds all authority and patient expected to comply. True partnership would sound like: "You know yourself better than I do. What do you think would work for you?" Instead, psychiatrist communicates: "I know what you need to do, and you need to do it now." Entire session characterized by hierarchy rather than partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** While patient spontaneously offers change talk, psychiatrist makes no effort to cultivate, evoke, or reinforce it. Approach actively suppresses change talk by creating defensive, pressured environment. Patient change talk present: "I want to quit, I really do," "I do want to live longer and feel better," "I want to do this," "Maybe if I try just cutting back a little...it could feel more manageable." Rather than amplifying through reflection and strategic questioning, psychiatrist ignores change talk and continues with pressure. Patient's suggested approach rejected: "No, cutting down isn't good enough." Response to "I want to quit" is ultimatums rather than exploration. No open-ended questions evoke patient's own reasons, ability, or commitment. Repeated dismissals ("you're making excuses," "you're not serious," "fear is just an excuse") create defensive environment that suppresses change talk and increases sustain talk. Fundamental failure to cultivate change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist does not skillfully soften sustain talk; approach actively amplifies it and creates more resistance. Patient sustain talk present: "It's hard and I didn't really stick with it," "I'm scared it'll be the same this time," "I'm worried about those cravings," "I'm scared of failing and feeling worse about myself," "I don't think I can do it," "I need more time to get ready." Rather than using MI techniques to gently redirect this language, psychiatrist confronts directly: told patient to "stop being scared," labeled hesitation as "making excuses," insisted "there's no middle ground," repeated ultimatums. MI-consistent approaches (double-sided reflection, reframing, exploring ambivalence, normalizing) entirely absent. Predictably, patient's sustain talk increases by session end—more doubt, more resistance. Psychiatrist receives score of 2 (not 1) because does not explicitly reinforce sustain talk, but actively amplifies it through confrontation rather than skillfully redirecting. Opposite of softening sustain talk.

## **OVERALL ASSESSMENT**

### **Overall Session Score: 1.1/5**

**Summary:** This session represents a **fundamental failure to implement motivational interviewing principles**. Nearly every core MI element is violated. Acceptance replaced with judgment; empathy with prescription; collaboration with authoritarianism; autonomy support with pressure; evocation with imposition; partnership with hierarchy. 

**Current MI Competency Level:** **Pre-MI.** This is not basic MI competence with room for improvement; this is a confrontational, directive approach inconsistent with MI spirit and research-supported practice for behavior change.

**Clinical Impact:** Patient enters with ambivalence and some motivation ("I want to quit"). By session end, patient is more defensive and doubtful. Psychiatrist has moved patient further away from change, not closer. Research consistently shows confrontational, pressuring approaches backfire and decrease motivation.

**Critical Recommendation:** Seek formal MI training and clinical supervision before continuing this approach. Current methodology likely harms treatment outcomes and decreases likelihood of successful behavior change.
