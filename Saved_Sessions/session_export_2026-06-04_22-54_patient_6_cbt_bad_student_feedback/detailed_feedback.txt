
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made no attempt to establish a collaborative agenda at any point during the session. A proper CBT agenda would have involved: (1) explicitly identifying the session's target problems, (2) prioritizing among multiple concerns (alcohol use disorder, depressive symptoms, sleep disturbance, family conflict), (3) allocating time to specific topics, and (4) establishing measurable goals for the session. Instead, the therapist opened with a vague, open-ended question ("What's been on your mind lately?") and never returned to structure the session around specific, time-limited objectives.

The patient presented multiple interconnected problems: heavy daily alcohol consumption, depressive symptoms (dysphoria, fatigue, anhedonia, insomnia, concentration difficulties), anxiety about his business, relationship strain, and sleep disruption at 2:00 A.M. A competent CBT therapist would have collaboratively prioritized these—for example, "Matthew, I hear several things troubling you: your drinking, the depression, and sleep problems. Given our time today, what feels most urgent to address first?" This never occurred. The session drifted without structure, direction, or explicit goals.

By the end of the session, there is no clear understanding of what was accomplished, what will be targeted next, or how progress will be measured. This represents a fundamental failure of CBT structure.

**Summary:**
No agenda was established. The session lacked structure, prioritization, or explicit goals. This is a critical foundational failure in CBT delivery.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist elicited minimal feedback and did not systematically check for understanding throughout the session. Evidence-based CBT requires regular feedback loops: asking whether the patient understands the therapist's reasoning, whether interventions make sense, whether the patient agrees with the direction, and whether the session is helpful.

The therapist never asked: "Does this make sense to you?" "How are you feeling about what we've discussed?" "Is this approach something you think could work for you?" "What's your reaction to the idea of taking walks or watching movies?" "Do you understand how these strategies connect to your drinking?" The patient's agreement appears assumed rather than verified.

Near the end, the therapist states, "You're doing great just by being here and talking about this," but this is reassurance, not feedback elicitation. True feedback would involve asking the patient to reflect on the session's usefulness: "What's been most helpful so far?" "Is there anything we've discussed that doesn't feel right to you?" "How confident are you that these strategies will actually help with your drinking?"

The therapist also failed to check understanding of the connection between proposed interventions (walks, movies, water, sleep) and the patient's actual presenting problems (alcohol use disorder and alcohol-induced depression). The patient never explicitly confirms understanding of how these coping strategies address his core issues.

**Summary:**
Minimal feedback elicitation; no systematic checking for understanding or agreement. The therapist assumed patient buy-in without verification.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

The therapist demonstrated surface-level empathy but fundamentally misunderstood or failed to engage with the patient's core clinical presentation. ### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness

**Explanation:**

The therapist displayed adequate warmth and genuineness but with significant professional boundary violations and misaligned interpersonal approach for this clinical presentation.
