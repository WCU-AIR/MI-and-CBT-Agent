
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made a single attempt to set an agenda near the end of the session (approximately 70% through the transcript), proposing a time-divided structure: "15 minutes talking about your morning slowness, 15 minutes on your work concentration, and then 10 minutes on some ideas to help with motivation." While this represents an effort to establish structure, it occurs far too late in the session and demonstrates significant deficiencies in CBT practice.

First, the therapist spent the majority of the session (roughly 70% of the interaction) engaging in unstructured, rapport-building conversation before any agenda was established. This violates a fundamental CBT principle: agenda-setting should occur at the beginning of the session to maximize therapeutic efficiency and ensure collaborative goal-setting. By the time the agenda was proposed, substantial time had already been consumed with general discussion, self-disclosure, and non-specific supportive comments.

Second, the proposed agenda lacks specificity regarding treatment goals and desired outcomes. The therapist identified topics to discuss but did not establish concrete, measurable objectives (e.g., "By the end of today's session, we'll identify three specific morning behaviors to modify and create a plan to test one this week"). The agenda reads as a discussion outline rather than a treatment plan. Additionally, the therapist did not prioritize which problem is most functionally impairing or most amenable to intervention—all three areas were given equal weight without clinical rationale.

Third, there is no explicit discussion of how these agenda items connect to Ian's presenting problem (impaired work concentration and functional impairment) or to a broader treatment strategy. The agenda appears disconnected from a coherent case conceptualization.

**Summary:**

The therapist attempted agenda-setting only after 70% of the session had elapsed, and the agenda lacked specificity, prioritization, and connection to treatment goals. This represents minimal adherence to CBT structure.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made virtually no attempt to elicit feedback from the patient throughout the session. Feedback-seeking is a core CBT competency that serves multiple functions: (1) ensuring the patient understands the therapist's conceptualization and recommendations, (2) assessing whether interventions are resonating or feel irrelevant, (3) maintaining collaborative engagement, and (4) identifying misalignment between therapist and patient goals.

Across the entire transcript, the therapist never asked questions such as: "Does that make sense to you?", "How does that land for you?", "Do you think that approach might work for you?", "What are your thoughts on what we've discussed?", or "Is there anything I've said that doesn't fit your experience?" The patient spontaneously offered feedback twice—stating that "thinking positive hasn't really done much for me" and that distraction "only helps for a short while"—but the therapist did not systematically solicit this information.

Most critically, the therapist did not check whether the patient understood or agreed with the recommendations being offered. The therapist suggested multiple interventions (positive thinking, distraction, walking, water intake, stretching, snacks, yoga, cooking) without ever asking whether Ian found these suggestions helpful, realistic, or aligned with his goals. This represents a fundamental departure from collaborative empiricism, a cornerstone of CBT.

At the session's end, the therapist did not summarize key points, ask the patient to articulate his takeaways, or solicit feedback on the session's helpfulness. There is no evidence that the therapist checked whether the patient felt heard, understood, or optimistic about the proposed changes.

**Summary:**

The therapist did not systematically elicit patient feedback at any point in the session. This represents a critical failure in collaborative practice and prevents the therapist from assessing whether interventions are landing effectively.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated partial understanding of Ian's presenting symptoms but missed critical clinical nuances and failed to develop a deeper grasp of his internal experience.

What the therapist understood: The therapist accurately identified that Ian is experiencing low energy, difficulty concentrating, morning slowness, apathy, and reduced motivation. The therapist reflected these symptoms back to the patient ("Sounds like you've been having a rough time with energy and focus") and acknowledged that Ian finds typical self-help suggestions (positive thinking, distraction) ineffective. The therapist also recognized that Ian experiences difficulty initiating activities despite knowing they might help.

What the therapist missed: The therapist failed to understand or explore several critical clinical features that distinguish Ian's presentation:

1. **Anhedonia and apathy as core features**: Ian explicitly stated "more like indifferent" and "even the things I usually enjoy don't seem to lift me much." This is not simple low motivation—it reflects anhedonia (loss of pleasure) and apathy (lack of goal-directed motivation), hallmark features of depression. The therapist treated this as a motivation problem solvable through behavioral activation rather than recognizing it as a symptom requiring targeted intervention.

2. **The distinction between knowing and doing**: Ian stated "I want to feel normal again but I don't know how" and "even getting myself out the door feels like a big effort." This reflects the cognitive-motivational paralysis characteristic of depression, not simple laziness. The therapist's repeated suggestion to "just try" and "push through" demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of depressive inertia.

3. **Morning-specific psychomotor slowing**: Ian reported "especially in the mornings" and "waking up but still feel kind of slowed down or foggy." This pattern suggests possible diurnal mood variation, a feature of depression. The therapist did not explore this temporal pattern or its implications for treatment timing.

4. **The role of recent loss**: The case formulation explicitly notes that Ian's episode "followed the breakdown of his 6-year marriage" and that he has a history of "brief episodes...in the context of personal loss or romantic setbacks." The therapist never explored this precipitant, Ian's emotional response to the divorce, or how grief/loss might be maintaining his current symptoms.

5. **Functional impairment at work**: Ian mentioned "my job needs a lot of mental focus and energy right from the start" and that concentration difficulties are affecting his work. The therapist did not explore the specific nature of these work difficulties, their impact on his career, or how they might be addressed through targeted intervention.

The therapist's empathic responses were generic ("I totally get that," "I hear you") rather than demonstrating specific understanding of Ian's unique experience. The therapist frequently pivoted to personal anecdotes ("When I felt off," "When I was feeling kind of down") rather than deepening exploration of Ian's internal reality.

**Summary:**

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish Agenda at Session Beginning with Clear Structure and Prioritization**
- **Action:** Begin every session by explicitly setting an agenda within the first 5-10 minutes. Collaborate with the patient to identify 2-3 priority problems, allocate time to each, and establish concrete goals for the session.
- **Example:** "Ian, I want to make sure we use our time well today. You mentioned three things: morning slowness, work concentration, and motivation. These are all important. Based on what you've told me, I think morning slowness might be the highest priority because it affects everything else—if we can improve how you feel in the morning, it might help your work concentration and motivation. Does that make sense to you? Should we focus there first, or would you rather start somewhere else?"
- **Practice:** Write out agendas for 5 practice sessions before your next real patient. Include: (1) specific problems to address, (2) time allocation, (3) rationale for prioritization, (4) explicit patient agreement.

### 2. **Systematically Elicit and Integrate Patient Feedback Throughout Session**
- **Action:** After each major point or suggestion, ask for the patient's reaction. Check understanding, assess whether suggestions fit the patient's experience, and adjust based on feedback.
- **Example:** After suggesting behavioral activation, ask: "Does that make sense to you? Do you think that approach might work for you? What concerns do you have about it?" When the patient says "positive thinking hasn't worked," respond with: "That's really important information. It sounds like positive thinking alone isn't enough. Let's understand why. What happens when you try to think positive? What gets in the way?"
- **Practice:** In your next 5 sessions, ask for feedback at least 5 times per session. Use phrases like: "How does that land for you?", "Does that fit your experience?", "What are your thoughts on that?", "Is there anything I've said that doesn't make sense?"

### 3. **Develop a Coherent Case Conceptualization and Treatment Strategy**
- **Action:** Before or early in treatment, develop a clear formulation of what maintains the patient's depression. Identify the key maintaining factors (thoughts, behaviors, emotions, environmental factors) and how they interact. Use this conceptualization to guide all interventions.
- **Example:** "Ian, based on what you've told me, here's how I understand your depression: Your mood is low, which makes everything feel harder and less rewarding. This leads you to avoid activities and stay in bed longer. But avoiding activities actually maintains your low mood because you miss opportunities to experience things that might help you feel better. Additionally, when you do try activities, you expect them not to help (because of your low mood), so you don't notice small improvements. We're going to work on breaking this cycle by gradually increasing your activity level and paying close attention to how your mood changes. Does that make sense?"
- **Practice:** For your next 3 patients, write out a one-paragraph case conceptualization that identifies: (1) the presenting problem, (2) the key maintaining factors, (3) the proposed treatment strategy, (4) how specific interventions address these factors.

### 4. **Eliminate Excessive Therapist Self-Disclosure; Maintain Professional Authority**
- **Action:** Limit personal anecdotes to rare, brief moments when they directly normalize a patient's experience or model a specific coping strategy. Focus on the patient's experience, not the therapist's experience.
- **Example:** Instead of "When I felt off, I found that positive thinking helped," say: "Many people with depression find that positive thinking alone isn't enough. Let's understand what's getting in the way for you and develop a more targeted approach."
- **Practice:** Record yourself in a practice session or review a session recording. Count the number of times you share personal experiences. Aim for zero to one per session. When you do share, ensure it directly serves the patient's treatment.

### 5. **Implement Structured Behavioral Activation with Clear Rationale and Monitoring**
- **Action:** Rather than suggesting random activities, implement behavioral activation systematically: (1) identify avoided activities, (2) create a hierarchy, (3) schedule specific activities with clear objectives, (4) establish monitoring (mood before/after, completion), (5) review results and adjust.
- **Example:** "Ian, we're going to work on gradually increasing your activity level. Research shows that when people with depression increase their activity, even when they don't feel like it, their mood often improves. Let's start small. What's one activity you used to enjoy that you've been avoiding? [Patient: drawing] Okay, let's schedule 15 minutes of drawing for Tuesday and Thursday at 7 PM. Before you draw, rate your energy on a 0-10 scale. After you draw, rate it again. Bring those ratings to our next session. Does that seem doable?"
- **Practice:** For your next patient with depression, implement full behavioral activation: identify 3 avoided activities, create a hierarchy, schedule 2-3 activities for the coming week with specific times and monitoring, and plan to review results at the next session.

### 6. **Use Guided Discovery and Socratic Questioning Rather Than Direct Advice**
- **Action:** Replace advice-giving with skillful questions that help patients reach their own insights. Ask about thoughts, beliefs, evidence, alternatives, and consequences.
- **Example:** Instead of "Try a morning walk," ask: "You mentioned that mornings are hardest. What do you think would happen if you moved your body for 10 minutes right after waking up? What's your prediction? What would we need to do to test that prediction?"
- **Practice:** In your next 5 sessions, aim for at least 10 Socratic questions per session. Use question stems like: "What do you make of that?", "What evidence do you have for that belief?", "What would happen if...?", "How could we test that?", "What else might explain that?"

### 7. **Explore and Address Central Maintaining Factors: Anhedonia, Apathy, and Cognitive-Motivational Paralysis**
- **Action:** Recognize anhedonia and apathy as core depressive symptoms requiring targeted intervention. Explore the patient's experience of these symptoms and how they maintain depression.
- **Example:** "You mentioned that even things you used to enjoy feel flat now. That's a really important symptom of depression called anhedonia. Let's understand it better. When you think about drawing, what goes through your mind? What do you expect will happen? How does that affect whether you try it?"
- **Practice:** In your next patient with depression, explicitly assess for anhedonia and apathy. Ask: "What activities used to bring you pleasure? What's different now? When you try those activities, what's the experience like? What thoughts go through your mind?" Use these answers to guide treatment.

### 8. **Explore the Role of Recent Loss and Grief in Maintaining Symptoms**
- **Action:** Recognize that Ian's depression followed his divorce. Explore his thoughts, feelings, and beliefs about this loss and how they might be maintaining his current symptoms.
- **Example:** "Your depression started after your divorce. That's a significant loss. I'm wondering what that's been like for you. What thoughts go through your mind about the divorce? How do you make sense of it? How might those thoughts be affecting your motivation and energy now?"

### 9. **Assign Specific, Structured Homework with Clear Rationale and Monitoring**
