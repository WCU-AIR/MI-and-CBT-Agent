### Direction

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist operates from a hierarchical, expert-driven stance with minimal genuine collaboration. Unilateral problem definition is evident in diagnostic questions ("Would you say that your eating habits or exercise routines are part of the problem?") that presume the psychiatrist already knows the answer. Prescriptive recommendations are offered without first understanding Sofia's preferences, constraints, or ideas: "Maybe you should try setting small exercise goals," "focus on stress management like relaxation," "you might want to set reminders." These are directives disguised as suggestions. When Sofia expresses worry about maintaining small goals during stress, the psychiatrist does not explore this concern collaboratively but pivots to a confidence scale and prescribes the same solution anyway. Confidence scale is used as a compliance tool to justify the psychiatrist's predetermined plan rather than to understand Sofia's perspective. Sofia never participates in deciding what the plan should be; the psychiatrist decides and Sofia complies. This is expert-driven care, not collaborative MI.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist consistently undermines autonomy through directive language and implicit pressure. Prescriptive language appears throughout: "Maybe you should try setting small exercise goals," "it would probably help if you start," "It's important to keep it simple," "you should try setting a daily reminder." The word "should" signals obligation rather than choice. When Sofia expresses doubt about finding a buddy, the psychiatrist responds "that's okay—you can still do it on your own," which appears supportive but subtly pressures Sofia to proceed with the plan regardless. No explicit affirmation of Sofia's right to choose is provided; phrases like "This is entirely your choice," "What feels right to you?" or "You get to decide" are absent. Session concludes with "Keep at it, and we'll talk again next time," framing the plan as something Sofia must maintain rather than something she has chosen and owns. Confidence scale is used to justify overriding Sofia's hesitation rather than to explore what would increase her confidence.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist relies heavily on telling rather than asking, minimizing genuine evocation. While some open questions are asked early ("Would you say that your eating habits or exercise routines are part of the problem?"), most questions are closed or leading. True evocation questions are absent: "What do you think would need to change for you to feel more energized?" "What matters most to you about your health?" "Tell me about a time when you felt good about your energy—what was different then?" No exploration of Sofia's values or deeper motivations occurs; focus remains on behavioral logistics (reminders, walks, relaxation) rather than meaning and motivation. Sofia's spontaneous change talk ("I know I need to move more," "I want to feel like I'm making some progress") is acknowledged but not strategically amplified or deepened. No exploration of Sofia's strengths or past successes occurs. Advice-giving dominates the session, with the psychiatrist telling Sofia what to do rather than asking what she thinks might help. This installs solutions rather than evoking them.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively assumes the expert role throughout with minimal power-sharing or recognition of Sofia's expertise about her own life. Diagnostic questioning positions the psychiatrist as the one who knows the "right" answer and is testing Sofia's insight rather than genuinely exploring her perspective. All solutions are generated unilaterally by the psychiatrist (reminders, short walks, relaxation, mindfulness) without asking Sofia for her ideas; Sofia is positioned as a passive recipient of expert recommendations. Implicit hierarchy is evident in language: "It's important to keep it simple and realistic" and "You're doing fine taking these first steps" position the psychiatrist as the evaluator of what is important and what constitutes adequate progress. No acknowledgment of Sofia's expertise about her own life is provided. Final summary is the psychiatrist's interpretation of Sofia's experience, not a collaborative co-construction; Sofia is not invited to correct or refine it. Check-in framing ("We'll talk again next time to see how things are going") positions the psychiatrist as the evaluator of Sofia's progress, not as a collaborative partner.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal skill in strategically evoking and reinforcing Sofia's change talk. Sofia's spontaneous change talk is present ("I know I need to move more," "I want something more realistic," "I want to feel like I'm making some progress, even if it's small"), but the psychiatrist does not actively deepen or reinforce it. No strategic evocation of change talk through open questions designed to elicit more: "What makes you want to feel more energized?" "How would your life be different if you had more energy?" "What would you be able to do?" are absent. When Sofia says "I know I need to move more… maybe small steps," the psychiatrist responds with a closed question ("Would you say it's closer to a 5 or an 8?") rather than deepening the change talk. Sofia's strong commitment language ("I want to feel like I'm making some progress, even if it's small") receives only minimal reinforcement ("That's good") without deepening. Confidence scale is used to assess rather than to evoke change talk about what would increase confidence. No exploration of reasons for change occurs; questions like "What are the main reasons you want to address this?" or "What would be the benefits of having more energy?" are absent. Advice-giving crowds out change talk, leaving little space for Sofia to develop and articulate her own motivations.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates mixed performance in handling sustain talk. Direct confrontation is avoided, which is appropriate; the psychiatrist does not argue against Sofia's statements or say "You can do it" when she expresses doubt. However, sustain talk is not skillfully explored or reframed. When Sofia says "I've tried diets before, but they don't stick," the psychiatrist does not explore this past experience to understand what happened or what Sofia learned. No double-sided reflection is used when Sofia expresses both desire for change and fear of failure. When Sofia rates confidence at 5/10 and expresses worry about maintaining change during stress, the psychiatrist responds by prescribing the same plan anyway rather than exploring the doubt. Sofia's statement "I want something more realistic" implicitly contains sustain talk about past approaches being unsustainable, but the psychiatrist does not explore what "realistic" means to Sofia. Minimal validation of Sofia's caution and fear is provided; her hesitation and fear are legitimate protective responses that deserve explicit acknowledgment. Barriers and doubts are acknowledged but not deeply understood or softened through strategic reflection and validation.

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.9/5**

**Clinical Assessment:**

This session demonstrates **poor motivational interviewing practice** and operates primarily from a medical expert model rather than an MI collaborative partnership model. While the psychiatrist avoids overt confrontation and maintains basic session structure, the interaction is characterized by:

- Unilateral problem-definition and solution-generation
- Prescriptive advice-giving without genuine exploration
- Minimal empathic depth and emotional validation
- Failure to evoke intrinsic motivation for change
- Directive language that undermines autonomy support
- Positioning of Sofia as a passive recipient rather than an active collaborator

**Likelihood of Sustained Behavior Change: LOW**

While Sofia appears compliant and agrees to the plan, this session is unlikely to generate lasting intrinsic motivation. Sofia's compliance appears to stem from politeness and desire to please the psychiatrist rather than genuine internal commitment. Her fear of failure remains unaddressed, her stress-related triggers are not explored, and she has not generated or owned the plan. When stress increases (which Sofia has identified as inevitable), she will likely abandon the plan without guilt and return reporting that "it didn't work."

**Most Critical Areas for Improvement:**

1. **Shift from expert-driven to collaborative partnership** — foundational issue underlying most deficiencies
2. **Develop genuine empathic reflections** capturing emotional nuance and validating Sofia's experience
3. **Strategically evoke change talk** through open questions and amplification rather than advice-giving
4. **Explore and reframe sustain talk** (barriers, past failures, doubt) rather than bypassing it
5. **Explicitly support autonomy** through autonomy-supportive language and affirmation of client choice
