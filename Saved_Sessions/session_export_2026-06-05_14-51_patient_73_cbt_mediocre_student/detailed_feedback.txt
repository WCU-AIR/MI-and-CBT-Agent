
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Feedback: Therapist Evaluation

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist failed to establish a structured, collaborative agenda in either session. In Session 1, the opening statement ("just chat about whatever's been on your mind lately") is vague and non-directive, offering no prioritization, time allocation, or explicit session goals. While the patient naturally introduced her primary concern (heart palpitations and panic worry), the therapist never formalized this into a concrete agenda item or checked whether this was the most pressing issue to address that day.

In Session 2, the same pattern repeated: "maybe today we can just talk about whatever's been on your mind lately." This is particularly problematic because the patient had been assigned homework (breathing exercises, thought monitoring) and the session should have begun with explicit review of that work and collaborative goal-setting for the current session. There is no evidence the therapist asked, "What would be most helpful to focus on today?" or "Should we check in on how the breathing practice went, or is there something more urgent?" The therapist allowed the session to drift based entirely on patient initiative rather than establishing mutual priorities.

A proper agenda would have included: (1) review of previous session and homework completion, (2) identification of target problems for today (avoidance behaviors vs. panic symptoms vs. thought patterns), (3) time allocation ("We have 50 minutes; let's spend 15 on homework review, 25 on exposure planning, and 10 on new homework"), and (4) explicit agreement from the patient. None of this occurred.

**Summary:**

The therapist did not establish a structured, collaborative agenda in either session. Sessions lacked prioritization, time management, or explicit goal-setting, relying instead on passive invitation for the patient to direct content.

### 7. Guided Discovery
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist relied heavily on direct suggestion and advice-giving rather than Socratic questioning and guided discovery. In CBT, guided discovery involves asking questions that help the patient reach insights independently, examine evidence, consider alternatives, and draw conclusions. This approach respects the patient's autonomy and leads to more durable change because the patient "owns" the insights.

Instead, the therapist primarily told the patient what to think and do. Examples:

- "Maybe it would help to try thinking about the anxious feelings as your body's way of warning you, but not necessarily meaning something bad is going to happen."
- "You could try reminding yourself of that when you notice the feelings."
- "Maybe when you feel your heart racing, you could try telling yourself, 'This is just anxiety, not danger.'"
- "Maybe when you notice those thoughts, you could try to challenge them by asking yourself, 'Is it really true that I can't handle it?'"
- "Also, maybe it would help to gradually face those situations instead of avoiding them completely."

These are all directive suggestions presented as recommendations. While the therapist occasionally used leading questions ("What do you think?" "Do you think that might help?"), these were designed to elicit agreement rather than genuine discovery.

A Socratic approach would have looked like:

**Therapist:** "When you feel your heart racing, what does that mean to you?"
**Patient:** "It means something's wrong with my heart."
**Therapist:** "And what evidence do you have for that?"
**Patient:** "Well, my heart is racing, and that's a sign of heart problems."
**Therapist:** "I see. And when your heart races, what else could be happening? What else causes hearts to race?"
**Patient:** "I guess... anxiety, exercise, caffeine..."
**Therapist:** "Right. So when you notice your heart racing, how could you figure out which of those is actually happening?"

This approach helps the patient discover alternatives and test them, rather than being told what to think.

The therapist did not ask the patient to examine evidence for catastrophic predictions ("What evidence do you have that you'll crash the car if you have a panic attack?"), consider alternative explanations ("What else could explain your palpitations?"), or test predictions through behavioral experiments ("What would need to happen for you to believe the doctors' reassurance?").

**Summary:**

The therapist relied on direct advice and suggestion rather than Socratic questioning. The patient was told what to think rather than guided to discover insights independently, reducing autonomy and durability of change.

### 6. **Teach and Structure Cognitive Restructuring with Evidence Examination (CRITICAL)**

**Action:** When addressing catastrophic thoughts, systematically examine evidence for and against the thought rather than simply suggesting a reframe.

**Example:**

**Poor (Unsupported Reframe):**
"Maybe it would help to try thinking about the anxious feelings as your body's way of warning you, but not necessarily meaning something bad is going to happen."

**Better (Evidence-Based Restructuring):**
"You have the thought 'My heart racing means I'm having a heart attack.' Let's examine this thought carefully. What evidence do you have that this thought is true?"
[Patient: "My heart is racing, and that's a sign of a heart attack."]
"Okay. And what evidence do you have that this thought might not be true? Have you ever had your heart race without having a heart attack?"
[Patient: "Well, yes, when I exercise or when I'm anxious..."]
"Right. So your heart racing can mean different things. When you had your heart racing in the past without having a heart attack, what was actually causing it?"
[Patient: "Anxiety or exercise."]
"So when you notice your heart racing, how could you figure out which one is happening? What would help you know?"
[Patient: "I guess I could think about what I was doing or feeling right before."]
"Exactly. So a more realistic thought might be: 'My heart is racing, and it could be anxiety, exercise, or caffeine. I can figure out which one by thinking about what I was doing.' How does that thought feel compared to 'I'm having a heart attack'?"

**Practice:**
- Use a thought record (write down: Situation, Automatic Thought, Evidence For, Evidence Against, Alternative Thought, Belief in Alternative)
- Help the patient examine evidence systematically
- Develop alternative thoughts that are realistic, not just positive
- Have the patient rate belief in the original thought before and after the exercise

### 10. **Explain the Anxiety Curve and Prevent Escape Behavior (IMPORTANT)**

**Action:** Before assigning exposure, explain that anxiety will initially increase but will decrease with time if the patient stays in the situation. This prevents the patient from interpreting initial anxiety as a sign to escape.

**Example:**
"When you do exposure—when you face a situation you're avoiding—your anxiety will initially go up. This is normal and expected. Your brain is learning that the situation is not as dangerous as you thought. But if you escape or avoid the situation when anxiety is high, you teach your brain that the situation IS dangerous, and anxiety will increase next time. So the key is to stay in the situation until your anxiety comes down naturally. Usually, if you stay for 20-30 minutes, anxiety will decrease by at least 50%. Does that make sense? This is really important for the exposure to work."

**Practice:**
- Draw the anxiety curve for the patient (anxiety goes up, then gradually decreases over time)
- Explain that escape reinforces avoidance
- Prepare the patient for initial anxiety
- Emphasize the importance of staying in the situation
- Check for understanding: "What will you do if your anxiety goes up during the exposure?"
