
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

0 = Poor
2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

Over the past three months, Ms. Isaac began avoiding leaving her apartment, initially at night and later during the day, due to fears of something bad happening and being unable to summon help. She developed strategies such as having groceries delivered but felt increasingly vulnerable outside her home. At home, she could relax and enjoy leisure activities.

Ms. Isaac reported lifelong nervousness and early separation anxiety, crying during kindergarten drop-offs and seeing a counselor at age 10 during her parents’ divorce. She had long-standing difficulty being alone, maintaining frequent romantic relationships since age 16, and had functioned well socially and professionally until the recent breakup. She denied depressive symptoms beyond transient sadness after the breakup. Her GAD-7 score indicated severe anxiety.

On examination, she appeared anxious but cooperative. Cognition, insight, and judgment were intact. There were no psychotic symptoms, psychomotor changes, or suicidality.

Discussion: Ms. Isaac meets DSM-5 criteria for GAD, characterized by excessive anxiety, difficulty controlling worries, restlessness, fatigue, impaired concentration, and reassurance-seeking. Her recent avoidance of leaving the home suggests early agoraphobic behaviors, though the duration is shorter than the 6-month criterion for DSM-5 agoraphobia. The onset of her symptoms coincides with psychosocial stressors and may be compounded by a lifelong insecure attachment style. Cognitive-behavioral therapy and/or pharmacotherapy (e.g., SSRI or SNRI) are appropriate treatment options.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Expand the Range of CBT Techniques (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action**: Introduce and systematically apply a broader range of evidence-based CBT techniques, including formal thought records, behavioral experiments, relaxation training, worry postponement, and activity scheduling.
- **Example**: 
  - "Let's create a thought record that you can use between sessions. We'll write down the situation, your automatic thought, the evidence for and against it, and an alternative thought. This way, you have a tool to use whenever you notice anxious thoughts."
  - "Let's do a behavioral experiment. Your prediction is that you'll have a panic attack and no one will help. We'll test that prediction by going outside, and you'll write down what actually happens. This will help us see whether your prediction is accurate."
  - "Let me teach you a breathing technique called diaphragmatic breathing. When you feel anxious, this can help calm your nervous system. Let's practice it now so you're comfortable using it during your homework."
- **Practice**: Review CBT manuals (e.g., Anxiety and Phobia Workbook by Arden, Cognitive Therapy of Anxiety Disorders by Clark & Beck) to deepen familiarity with the full range of techniques. Practice applying 2-3 new techniques per week in supervision or role-play.

### 2. **Develop an Explicit Treatment Conceptualization and Plan (HIGH PRIORITY)**
- **Action**: At the beginning of treatment (or early in the next session), articulate a clear conceptualization of the patient's anxiety and an explicit treatment plan with timeline and rationale.
- **Example**: 
  - "Based on what you've shared, here's my understanding of what's maintaining your anxiety: When you think about leaving your apartment, you have scary thoughts like 'I'll have a panic attack and no one will help.' These thoughts trigger anxiety and physical symptoms. To avoid these feelings, you stay home, which provides temporary relief but actually strengthens the anxiety over time. Additionally, seeking reassurance from others temporarily calms you but also reinforces the idea that you need reassurance to be safe. Over the next 8-12 sessions, we'll work on: (1) identifying and challenging these scary thoughts, (2) gradually approaching situations you've been avoiding, (3) reducing reassurance-seeking so you can build confidence in your own ability to manage anxiety, and (4) addressing deeper beliefs about safety and being alone. Does this make sense? Do you have questions?"
- **Practice**: Write out a one-page case conceptualization for each patient, including: presenting problem, maintaining factors, core beliefs, treatment goals, and planned interventions. Review this with supervision.

### 3. **Explore and Address Core Beliefs and Deeper Patterns (HIGH PRIORITY)**
- **Action**: In the next session, explore the patient's core beliefs about safety, vulnerability, and being alone. Use downward arrow technique to identify deeper beliefs.
- **Example**: 
  - "When you imagine being outside alone and something bad happens, what does that mean about you? What does it say about your ability to handle it?"
  - "You mentioned that you've always had difficulty being alone, even as a child. What's the scariest part about being alone for you? What do you worry might happen?"
  - "If you were outside and felt anxious, what's the worst thing that could happen? And if that happened, what would that mean about you?"
- **Practice**: Learn the downward arrow technique and practice identifying core beliefs in supervision. Common core beliefs in GAD include: "I'm not safe," "I'm vulnerable," "I need someone to protect me," "I can't handle uncertainty," "Bad things will happen if I'm not vigilant."

### 4. **Address Reassurance-Seeking as a Maintaining Factor (HIGH PRIORITY)**
- **Action**: Explicitly identify reassurance-seeking as a maintaining factor and develop a plan to reduce it while building the patient's confidence in her own ability to manage anxiety.
- **Example**: 
  - "I've noticed that you seek reassurance from coworkers and your mom when you're anxious. While this feels helpful in the moment, it actually teaches your brain that you need reassurance to be safe, which keeps the anxiety going. As we work together, we'll gradually reduce reassurance-seeking and build your confidence that you can manage anxiety on your own. This might feel uncomfortable at first, but it's an important part of getting better."
  - "When you write down 'People are around' and 'I have my phone,' we want to frame this as evidence-gathering—testing whether your scary thought is true—rather than reassurance-seeking. The difference is that with evidence-gathering, you're building your own confidence; with reassurance-seeking, you're relying on others to calm you down."
- **Practice**: Discuss with supervision how to help patients distinguish between evidence-gathering and reassurance-seeking, and how to gradually reduce reassurance-seeking while maintaining therapeutic support.

### 5. **Provide Psychoeducation About Anxiety and the Treatment Rationale (HIGH PRIORITY)**
- **Action**: In the next session, provide explicit psychoeducation about the anxiety cycle, the function of avoidance, and why the proposed interventions work.
- **Example**: 
  - "Let me explain how anxiety works. When we perceive a threat—real or imagined—our brain triggers the fight-flight-freeze response. Our heart races, we feel tense, and we want to escape. This is a normal survival mechanism. However, when we avoid the situation, our brain learns that the situation is dangerous, and the anxiety gets stronger over time. The way to break this cycle is to gradually approach the situation while using coping strategies. This teaches your brain that the situation is actually safe."
  - "The thoughts you're having—'I'll have a panic attack and no one will help'—feel very true when you're anxious. But when we examine the evidence, we often find that these thoughts are not as accurate as they feel. By challenging these thoughts and testing them through real-world experience, we can reduce the anxiety."
- **Practice**: Develop a brief psychoeducation handout or video that explains the anxiety cycle and the rationale for CBT interventions. Use this with all anxiety patients.

### 6. **Implement Formal Thought Records (MEDIUM PRIORITY)**
- **Action**: Introduce a structured thought record worksheet that the patient can use between sessions to systematically track and challenge anxious thoughts.
- **Example**: 
  - Provide a thought record with columns: Situation | Automatic Thought | Emotion (0-10) | Evidence For | Evidence Against | Alternative Thought | Emotion After (0-10)
