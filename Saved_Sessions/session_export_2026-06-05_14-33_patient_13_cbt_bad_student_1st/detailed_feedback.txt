
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Feedback: Therapist Evaluation

---

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made no attempt to establish a collaborative agenda at any point during either session. A proper CBT agenda would involve: (1) explicitly identifying the session's target problems, (2) prioritizing which issues to address given time constraints, (3) establishing specific, measurable goals for the session, and (4) checking whether the patient agrees with the proposed focus.

Instead, the therapist opened with a vague, unstructured greeting ("Hey there! How's it going today?") and allowed the session to drift reactively. When the patient spontaneously introduced her freezing up in group projects, the therapist never said anything like: "That's really important. Today, I'd like to focus on understanding what happens when you freeze up in class and develop some concrete strategies to help you participate. Does that sound good to you?" No time allocation was discussed. No priorities were established collaboratively. The second session similarly lacked agenda-setting—the therapist simply asked how things were going without establishing what would be worked on that day.

This is a critical failure in CBT structure. Without an agenda, sessions become aimless conversations rather than focused therapeutic work. For a patient with avoidant personality disorder and social anxiety who desperately needs structured exposure and cognitive work, this represents a fundamental departure from evidence-based practice.

**Summary:**

No agenda was established in either session. The therapist did not collaboratively identify target problems, prioritize topics, or establish session goals. This is a foundational CBT failure that undermines all subsequent work.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist never elicited feedback from the patient about the session, her understanding of interventions, or her reactions to the therapist's suggestions. A therapist practicing CBT should regularly check: "Does that make sense?" "How are you feeling about what we discussed?" "Do you think this approach might work for you?" "What are your thoughts on trying this between sessions?"

Throughout both sessions, the therapist made unilateral statements and suggestions without ever circling back to verify understanding or gather the patient's perspective. For example, after suggesting "think positive" multiple times, the therapist never asked: "Does that feel realistic to you?" or "What barriers do you anticipate when trying to think more positively?" After recommending hydration and sleep, no check-in occurred: "Do you think improving sleep might actually help with your anxiety?"

Most critically, at the end of the first session, the therapist did not ask the patient to summarize what she was taking away, did not verify she understood the rationale for any suggestions, and did not solicit her feedback on whether the session was helpful. The second session similarly ended without any feedback elicitation.

This represents complete absence of a core CBT competency. Feedback loops are essential for: (1) ensuring the patient actually understands interventions, (2) identifying obstacles early, (3) maintaining collaborative stance, and (4) adjusting approach based on patient response.

**Summary:**

No feedback was elicited at any point. The therapist never checked patient understanding, solicited reactions to suggestions, or asked for session feedback. This is a complete absence of a fundamental CBT skill.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated surface-level empathy and basic listening but fundamentally missed the clinical picture and the patient's internal experience. The therapist heard that the patient "freezes up" and "worries about sounding dumb" but failed to understand the deeper architecture of her anxiety: the catastrophic predictions, the safety behaviors (avoidance, rehearsal), the perfectionism, the hypersensitivity to perceived criticism, and the pervasive feelings of inadequacy.

When the patient explicitly stated, "It's like I'm stuck in my head" and described racing thoughts about past mistakes, the therapist responded with generic reassurance rather than demonstrating understanding of rumination and anticipatory anxiety. When the patient revealed she avoids situations entirely to escape panic, the therapist did not reflect back understanding of how avoidance maintains anxiety—a core CBT concept.

Instead, the therapist simply encouraged her to try smiling anyway—missing the opportunity to understand *why* she believes people will judge her negatively.

The therapist also failed to understand the patient's sensitivity to perceived criticism (documented in her psychiatric history). When the patient expressed fear of embarrassment and judgment, the therapist responded with personal anecdotes about her own nervousness, which—while well-intentioned—shifted focus away from the patient's specific fears and potentially reinforced the idea that reassurance (rather than exposure and cognitive work) is the solution.

The therapist did not grasp that this patient has lifelong patterns of social inhibition rooted in a critical family environment, not situational nervousness that resolves with positive thinking.

**Summary:**

The therapist demonstrated basic listening but missed critical clinical features: the patient's catastrophic thinking patterns, safety behaviors, perfectionism, and core beliefs about being judged. Understanding remained at surface level without grasping the patient's internal reality or the mechanisms maintaining her anxiety.

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist displayed adequate warmth and genuine concern but with significant interpersonal missteps that undermined the therapeutic relationship. Positive elements included: consistent friendliness, encouragement, and absence of hostility or demeaning language. The therapist was accessible and non-threatening.

### 5. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration was largely absent. True collaboration in CBT involves: (1) shared decision-making about treatment focus and strategies, (2) explicitly inviting the patient's input ("What do you think about trying...?"), (3) treating the patient as an active agent in her own change, and (4) collaborative empiricism—testing beliefs together rather than the therapist prescribing solutions.

Instead, the therapist adopted an expert-advice-giver stance throughout. Examples:

- "Have you ever tried just smiling at someone?" (Prescriptive suggestion)

### 2. **Develop and Implement a Coherent CBT Conceptualization and Treatment Strategy**
- **Action:** Create a written case formulation that explains: (1) How her anxiety is maintained (avoidance prevents learning that situations are safe; catastrophic predictions are unchallenged; perfectionism and rehearsal increase anxiety; critical family background created core beliefs about inadequacy), (2) What specific interventions will target each mechanism, (3) The logical sequence of treatment. Our treatment will focus on: (1) gradually facing social situations so you learn they're safe, (2) examining whether your predictions about people judging you are actually accurate, and (3) reducing the perfectionism that makes you rehearse what you'll say. - **Practice:** Write out a detailed case formulation for this patient. Have a supervisor review it. Practice explaining it to the patient in clear, jargon-free language.

### 3. **Replace Reassurance with Guided Discovery and Socratic Questioning**
- **Action:** When the patient expresses a fear or negative thought, resist the urge to reassure. Instead, ask questions that help her examine the thought: "What makes you think that?" "What evidence do you have?" "Has that actually happened?" "What would you tell a friend who had that worry?" "What's the worst that could realistically happen, and how would you cope?"

- **Practice:** Study Socratic questioning techniques. Practice with a supervisor using role-plays. Record yourself and count how many Socratic questions you ask vs. how many reassuring statements you make. Aim for a ratio of at least 3 questions per reassurance.

### 4. **Implement Cognitive Restructuring Using Thought Records**
- **Action:** Teach the patient to identify automatic thoughts, examine evidence for and against them, and develop more balanced alternative thoughts. Use a structured thought record format: Situation → Automatic Thought → Evidence For → Evidence Against → Alternative Thought → Outcome.
- **Example:** "Let's look at what happened in your group project. You had the thought 'I'll say something stupid.' Let's examine that. What evidence do you have that you'll say something stupid? [Patient: 'I always mess up.'] Okay, let's look at that. Can you think of a time you said something in class that wasn't stupid? [Patient: 'Well, I got an A in chemistry...'] Right. So you're capable of intelligent contributions. What evidence is there against the thought that you'll say something stupid?"
- **Practice:** Learn the thought record format thoroughly. Practice with a supervisor. Assign thought records as homework and review them systematically in sessions.

### 5. **Develop and Implement a Graded Exposure Hierarchy**
- **Action:** Work collaboratively with the patient to identify feared social situations and rank them by difficulty (0-100 SUDS scale). Create a hierarchy from least to most anxiety-provoking. - **Example:** "Let's make a list of social situations that make you anxious, ranked from least to most scary. [Create hierarchy: smiling at someone = 30, asking a question in class = 50, joining a study group = 70, going to a social event = 90]. We'll start with the easier ones and work our way up. For each one, we'll predict what you think will happen, then do it, and see what actually happens. This will help you learn that your predictions aren't accurate."
- **Practice:** Learn exposure therapy principles. Practice creating hierarchies with a supervisor. Practice framing exposures as experiments rather than "challenges."
