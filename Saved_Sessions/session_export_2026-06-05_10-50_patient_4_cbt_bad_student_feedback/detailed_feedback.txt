
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Feedback: Therapist Evaluation

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made no attempt to establish a collaborative agenda at any point in the session. The opening question ("How have things been going since we last talked? Anything on your mind you want to chat about?") is generic and open-ended rather than agenda-setting. While the patient clearly articulated her concerns—anxiety about cancer progression, sleep disturbance, worry about her son's wedding, repetitive reassurance-seeking, and reduced activity—the therapist never explicitly prioritized these issues or established what would be most helpful to address in the session.

A proper agenda would have involved: (1) acknowledging the multiple stressors the patient identified, (2) collaboratively selecting 1-2 priority problems to focus on (e.g., "Your sleep problems and the racing thoughts seem to be causing you the most distress right now. Would it be helpful to focus on those today?"), (3) explaining how much time would be devoted to each area, and (4) checking whether the patient agreed with this plan. Instead, the therapist drifted from topic to topic—water intake, herbal tea, positive thinking, fresh air, window sitting—without any structured framework or explicit prioritization.

The absence of agenda-setting is particularly problematic in this case because the patient has multiple interconnected problems (anxiety, sleep, reassurance-seeking, activity avoidance, catastrophic thinking) that require strategic prioritization. Without an agenda, the session became reactive and unfocused, addressing surface-level suggestions rather than core maintaining factors of her adjustment disorder.

**Summary:**
No collaborative agenda was established. The therapist responded to the patient's concerns with generic suggestions rather than working together to identify and prioritize target problems for the session.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist did not elicit feedback from the patient at any point during the session. There were no explicit questions checking whether the patient understood the therapist's reasoning, whether suggestions felt relevant or feasible, or whether the patient found the session helpful. Phrases like "Does that make sense?" "How does that land for you?" "Do you think that's something you could try?" or "What are your thoughts on that?" were entirely absent.

The therapist made multiple suggestions (positive thinking, drinking water, herbal tea, getting fresh air, sitting by windows, listening to music) without ever asking whether these felt realistic given the patient's medical constraints (hypoxemia, fear of falling, anxiety about being alone, lack of energy). At the end of the session, the therapist simply stated "I'm glad we had this talk today" without asking the patient what she found helpful, what she plans to do, or what questions remained. This represents a complete absence of feedback elicitation, which is a foundational CBT skill. Feedback is essential for ensuring the patient feels heard, understood, and engaged in the therapeutic process—particularly critical for a patient with adjustment disorder who is already experiencing emotional dependency and seeking reassurance.

**Summary:**
No feedback was elicited throughout the session. The therapist did not check understanding, adjust recommendations based on patient barriers, or solicit the patient's perspective on session helpfulness.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated surface-level understanding of the patient's explicit statements but missed critical nuances and the underlying cognitive and behavioral mechanisms maintaining her distress. The therapist accurately reflected that the patient was worried, tired, and struggling with sleep—these explicit concerns were acknowledged. However, the therapist fundamentally misunderstood the *nature* of the patient's anxiety and its function.

The patient explicitly stated: "It feels safer to worry than to let myself believe things might be okay" and "Every time I start to hope, another thought creeps in, like 'What if the cancer has spread?'" These are classic examples of anxiety-driven safety behaviors and catastrophic thinking patterns. The patient also described repetitive reassurance-seeking ("I keep asking the nurses over and over about my results, but it doesn't really make me feel better. It's like I'm stuck in this loop of fear") and activity avoidance driven by fear ("I get nervous moving around because I'm afraid I might get short of breath or fall").

Instead of recognizing these cognitive and behavioral patterns, the therapist responded with generic reassurance ("things often turn out better than we expect") and lifestyle suggestions (water, tea, fresh air). The therapist even validated the reassurance-seeking indirectly by offering reassurance himself ("You're stronger than you think," "tough times don't last, but tough people do"), which reinforces rather than addresses the maintaining cycle.

The therapist also missed the patient's expressed concern about being a burden ("I don't want to trouble anyone with my fears, though—I don't want to be a burden"), which reflects shame and social withdrawal—important features of her adjustment disorder that warrant exploration and intervention.

The therapist's self-disclosure ("When I was stressed about something once...") and repeated personal anecdotes, while intended to be empathic, actually shifted focus away from the patient's unique experience and suggested that simple positive thinking had worked for the therapist—a misunderstanding of the patient's actual problem.

**Summary:**
The therapist grasped explicit content but missed critical cognitive patterns (catastrophic thinking, safety behaviors, reassurance-seeking cycles) and behavioral avoidance maintaining the patient's distress. Understanding remained superficial.

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated moderate warmth and genuine concern but with significant interpersonal limitations that undermined therapeutic effectiveness. Positive elements included: a welcoming tone ("Hi there! Good to see you today"), acknowledgment of the patient's struggle ("Hey, I hear you. That sounds really tough"), and consistent encouragement throughout the session.

However, several interpersonal problems emerged:

**Excessive self-disclosure:** The therapist repeatedly shared personal experiences ("When I was stressed about something once," "I remember once I tried chamomile tea," "I remember one time I was feeling down"). While intended to normalize the patient's experience, this shifts focus from the patient to the therapist and can feel invalidating—as if the therapist is suggesting their own experience is comparable to the patient's life-threatening illness. For a patient already experiencing emotional dependency and seeking reassurance, therapist self-disclosure can blur boundaries and create confusion about whose needs are being addressed.

**Tone inconsistency:** The repeated use of "Hey" and casual language ("Hey, that sounds really nice idea," "Hey, I totally get that") feels somewhat mismatched to the gravity of the patient's situation (hospitalization for recurrent cancer, existential fears about missing her son's wedding). While not hostile or demeaning, the tone occasionally veers toward overly casual for a seriously ill patient in acute distress.
