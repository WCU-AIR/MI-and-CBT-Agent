# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no unconditional positive regard. Uses judgmental language ("you really need to quit," "stop making excuses") and communicates disapproval rather than acceptance. Dismisses client's perspective as invalid and never affirms client's worth or normalizes struggle with peer pressure. Tone is consistently critical and controlling, undermining therapeutic alliance.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Minimal reflective listening used as primary tool. When client expresses core struggle (isolation during sobriety, relationship difficulties), psychiatrist ignores emotional content and pivots to directives. Only superficial reflection attempted late in session, immediately followed by pressure. No exploration of client's values, meaning, or emotional complexity. Fails to capture tension between wanting to avoid DUI and fearing social isolation.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Maintains focus on target behavior (drinking/DUI prevention) but in directive, clinician-driven manner lacking collaboration. Direction is present but imposed rather than collaboratively developed. No progressive exploration of drinking behavior or gradual understanding of connections between peer pressure and consequences. Feels like steering rather than supporting client self-direction.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Demonstrates authoritarian, expert-driven stance with no genuine power-sharing. Unilaterally decides client must: quit completely, cut off friends, join support group, commit immediately. Dismisses client's practical ideas (cab money) with criticism rather than building collaboratively. Uses rhetorical questions demanding compliance ("Yes or no?"). Client's contributions do not meaningfully shape session direction or content.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Actively undermines autonomy through controlling language throughout ("you must," "you have to," "you need to"). Demands immediate commitment with coercive framing. When client explicitly states "I need to do it my way, not just follow orders," psychiatrist invalidates this autonomy need and frames it as excuse-making. Frames consequences as threats to pressure compliance rather than information supporting autonomous choice.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Virtually no evocation demonstrated. Relies on directives and closed, accusatory questions rather than open-ended exploration. Client articulates clear change talk ("if I need my license back, it would be important to stop hanging with them"), but psychiatrist ignores this entirely. Never asks about client's own motivations, values, ideas, or resources. Tells rather than asks throughout entire session.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Assumes sole expert role with hierarchical stance maintained throughout. Positions self as authority on what client needs without acknowledging client's expertise about own life. No shared decision-making; solutions are unilateral. Uses command language ("you have to," "you must") rather than collaborative language. Power imbalance evident in clinician control of agenda, questions, solutions, and pace.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Multiple instances of change talk present but completely ignored. Client states: "if I need my license back, it would be important to stop hanging with them" and "maybe put money aside for a cab"—clear commitment and ability statements. Psychiatrist does not recognize, reflect, or amplify these. Instead responds with criticism ("Why haven't you done that already?"). No strategic questioning to evoke change talk. Pressure tactics suppress change talk and evoke defensive sustain talk instead.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Does not skillfully soften sustain talk; instead directly confronts it, which typically strengthens resistance. When client minimizes drinking ("just beer," "isolated incident"), psychiatrist argues against minimization rather than exploring with curiosity. When client expresses concern about losing friendships, psychiatrist judges ("you have to be stronger than peer pressure") rather than using double-sided reflections. Confrontational approach strengthens sustain talk rather than softening it. Minimal use of strategic MI techniques.

---

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Level:** Pre-MI / Anti-MI

**Session Impact Assessment:** This session demonstrates **fundamental misalignment with Motivational Interviewing principles** across nearly all domains. The psychiatrist employs a directive, confrontational approach antithetical to MI spirit and evidence-based practice.

**Critical Findings:**

- **Directive stance dominates:** Clinician tells rather than explores, creating resistance rather than evoking motivation
- **Acceptance and empathy absent:** Judgmental tone and minimal reflective listening communicate disapproval rather than unconditional positive regard
- **Autonomy undermined:** Controlling language and pressure tactics suppress client's sense of choice and ownership
- **Change talk ignored:** Clear change talk statements missed and not amplified; instead, pressure and directives continue
- **Sustain talk confronted:** Client resistance strengthened through argument rather than softened through strategic MI techniques
- **Partnership absent:** Unilateral decision-making with no genuine collaboration or power-sharing

**Likelihood of Evoking Intrinsic Motivation for Change:** **LOW**

Client will likely leave session feeling pressured, misunderstood, and defensive. Reactance is probable given explicit autonomy need ("I need to do it my way"). Sustain talk is amplified rather than softened. Therapeutic alliance is damaged. Behavior change, if any, will be compliance-based rather than motivation-based and therefore less sustainable.

**Immediate Recommendations:**

1. Seek formal MI training (MINT-certified workshop or online course)
2. Engage in regular clinical supervision with MI-trained supervisor
3. Focus on developing reflective listening as primary response tool
4. Learn to recognize and amplify change talk strategically
5. Shift from directive to collaborative, partnership-based stance
6. Practice explicit autonomy support and acceptance

**Training Priority:** URGENT — This represents significant gap in evidence-based practice requiring immediate intervention.
