# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal genuine acceptance and fails to establish unconditional positive regard. While the opening is warm, the psychiatrist quickly pivots to problem-focused questioning that frames drinking as pathological without first understanding Irina's perspective. No affirmation of her strengths (single mother, full-time worker, student) or validation of her ambivalence. When Irina states drinking isn't a problem, the psychiatrist responds with direct inquiry about consequences rather than accepting her current perspective. Lacks normalization of her struggle and guilt—missed opportunity to validate the difficulty of her situation before problem-solving.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflective listening is present but superficial and emotionally flat. Early reflections are factual paraphrases ("you usually have about two drinks each night, and it helps you unwind") without capturing emotional nuance. When Irina reveals guilt about choosing drinking over time with her son, the psychiatrist's reflection immediately neutralizes the emotion with "but it's your way to relax"—a subtle minimization. Misses opportunities to demonstrate understanding of her internal conflict, exhaustion, and fear of losing her coping mechanism. No complex reflections that deepen insight or acknowledge the painful tension between self-care and parenting.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains general focus on alcohol reduction throughout, which is appropriate. However, direction is pursued through problem-focused questioning rather than collaborative exploration. Early questions presuppose that trouble exists ("Do you think this drinking ever causes any trouble?") rather than asking what concerns Irina. Never clarifies what direction Irina herself wants to move. Notably absent: exploration of Irina's deeper values, hopes, and what matters most to her. While the session does maintain focus and prevents aimlessness, direction is psychiatrist-driven rather than co-created with the client.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Collaboration is minimal throughout. Psychiatrist adopts expert stance—asking questions, providing suggestions, and guiding toward predetermined conclusions—rather than engaging in true joint problem-solving. Unilateral problem identification frames alcohol as "the problem" without exploring Irina's own concerns first. Advice-giving without exploration ("Have you thought about other ways you might relax?") is prescriptive rather than collaborative. Leading questions guide Irina toward psychiatrist's preferred outcome. Strategies are suggested unilaterally (snacks, non-alcoholic drinks) rather than co-developed. Token statement "it's your choice" at end doesn't retroactively create collaboration after psychiatrist-directed session.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** While psychiatrist makes token statement about autonomy ("it's your choice and you're in control"), autonomy support is largely absent. Prescriptive framing ("It would probably help if you had some alternatives, don't you think?") functions as advice disguised as question. Implicit judgment of status quo—phrasing "more comfortable keeping things as they are" subtly frames maintenance as passive while change is framed as important. Assumption of commitment ("You're going to try cutting back") presupposes rather than explores genuine desire. Never explicitly validates Irina's right to maintain current drinking if she chooses. Overall tone subtly pressures toward change without genuinely supporting freedom to choose differently.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out Irina's own motivations, values, and reasons for change—is largely absent. No exploration of what matters most to her or her hopes for herself and her son. Problem-focused rather than resource-focused; doesn't ask about past successes or strengths that have helped her manage challenges. Irina does produce spontaneous change talk ("Maybe I could try one drink," "I want to be better for him"), but psychiatrist doesn't strategically amplify or deepen it. When Irina expresses uncertainty about alternatives, psychiatrist suggests activities rather than asking what she's enjoyed or what would help. Scaling questions (importance, confidence) are used to assess readiness rather than deepen exploration of her own reasons for change.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Partnership—understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside within the client—is largely absent. Psychiatrist assumes expert role and directs session; Irina's contributions are acknowledged but don't substantially shape interaction. Unilateral agenda-setting ("I was thinking we could focus...") centers psychiatrist's perspective. Psychiatrist identifies problems, suggests solutions, and provides strategies; Irina positioned as recipient of expertise rather than co-creator. When Irina expresses barriers, psychiatrist acknowledges but doesn't fundamentally alter approach or ask what she thinks might work. One-drink goal is psychiatrist-suggested and client-accepted, not co-created. Psychiatrist tells Irina what's important rather than asking her to identify her own values.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Some effort to evoke change talk, but approach is inconsistent and lacks strategic depth. Change talk does emerge spontaneously: "Maybe I could try one drink," "I want to be better for him," "I do want to be better at balancing things." However, psychiatrist misses opportunities to deepen it. When Irina says "Maybe I could try one drink," psychiatrist responds supportively but doesn't ask follow-up questions that would strengthen commitment. When Irina says "I want to be better for him," psychiatrist doesn't explore further. Scaling questions (importance, confidence) are evocative but psychiatrist doesn't follow up strategically to ask what would move her higher or what makes her think she could do it. Sustain talk is present but not addressed. Positive: psychiatrist does reinforce commitment at end and affirms effort.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal skill in softening sustain talk. Irina's concerns about losing her only stress relief, fear of increased anxiety, and worry about deprivation are acknowledged but never reframed or explored. Direct questioning that implicitly challenges sustain talk ("Do you think this drinking ever causes any trouble?") can trigger defensiveness rather than soften resistance. Acknowledgment without reframing misses opportunity to validate while exploring alternatives. Problem-solving bypasses ambivalence rather than addressing underlying concerns. Absent: double-sided reflections that acknowledge both sides of her conflict. No values-based reframing that would help Irina see how reducing drinking aligns with what matters most. Minimal use of strategic silence or curiosity to explore sustain talk deeply. Result: Irina's ambivalence remains unresolved and potent.

---

### Overall Session Score: **2.3/5**

## Summary Assessment

**Strengths:**
- Warm, non-threatening opening establishes safe environment
- Maintains general focus on alcohol throughout session
- Accepts negotiated goal (one drink) as reasonable starting point rather than pushing for more
- Elicits some spontaneous change talk from client
- Provides affirmation ("You're doing a good job by thinking about this")
- Schedules follow-up to monitor progress

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Expert-driven stance dominates:** Psychiatrist assumes role of problem-identifier and solution-provider rather than facilitating client's own discovery
- **Minimal genuine empathy:** Reflections are factual rather than emotionally attuned; misses validation of internal conflict and exhaustion
- **Sustain talk never strategically addressed:** Client's concerns about losing coping mechanism remain unresolved
- **Change talk present but not cultivated:** Spontaneous change talk is acknowledged but not deepened through strategic follow-up
- **Intrinsic motivation not evoked:** No exploration of values; psychiatrist tells client what should matter rather than helping her discover it
- **Collaboration minimal:** Client contributions acknowledged but don't substantially shape interaction

**Impact on Client Outcomes:**

Irina leaves with negotiated goal (one drink/night) and moderate confidence (5-6/10), but session didn't build intrinsic motivation, problem-solving skills, or emotional resolution necessary for sustained change. Unresolved ambivalence and externally-imposed goal suggest difficulty maintaining change when stress hits. Likelihood of sustained behavior change: **Moderate to Low**.

**Recommendation:**

Psychiatrist should fundamentally shift from expert stance to facilitator stance, prioritizing: (1) deep empathy through complex reflections, (2) strategic cultivation of change talk, (3) skillful softening of sustain talk through double-sided reflection, (4) collaborative problem-solving, and (5) values clarification to evoke intrinsic motivation. Goal is to help client discover her own reasons for change rather than imposing external solutions.
