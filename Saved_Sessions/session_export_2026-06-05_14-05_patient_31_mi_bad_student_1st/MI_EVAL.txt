### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no evocation present. Psychiatrist uses closed yes/no questions that shut down exploration rather than open it. When client spontaneously offers change talk—"I want to avoid heart problems too," "I'm willing to think about some smaller steps," "Maybe I can start by easing up on junk food"—psychiatrist does not amplify, explore, or reinforce. Instead dismisses proposed steps as "not acceptable" and repeats demands for complete compliance. Tells client what to do rather than asking what client thinks, wants, or believes would work. No open-ended questions designed to evoke motivation: "What concerns you most?" "What would need to happen for you to feel ready?" "What have you tried before?" "What matters most to you?" Client's own resources (walking with dog, desire to avoid heart problems, willingness to consider smaller steps) never explored or built upon. Operates from deficit model rather than evocative model.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Complete opposite of partnership demonstrated. Psychiatrist assumes expert role exclusively and actively suppresses client input. From opening, positions self as authority determining what needs to happen. Tells client what he "needs to change," what he "must" do, what is "not negotiable." No acknowledgment that client has expertise about own life, preferences, constraints, or what might work. When client offers information about life context, psychiatrist dismisses as irrelevant or as excuse-making rather than as valuable information. Never asks "What do you think would work for you?" or "What have you learned from past attempts?" Language consistently hierarchical: "you must," "you have to," "you really need," "This is not negotiable." No inclusive language ("we," "together," "let's explore"). No co-construction of solutions. No genuine sharing of power. When client resists, psychiatrist escalates pressure rather than reconsidering approach.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Fails to evoke, amplify, or reinforce change talk. Client spontaneously produces multiple instances of change talk: "I want to avoid heart problems too" (desire), "I'm willing to think about some smaller steps" (commitment/ability), "Maybe I can start by easing up on junk food" (ability/reasons). In skillful MI, psychiatrist would reflect and amplify these statements. Instead, psychiatrist dismisses proposed steps as "not acceptable" and repeats demands for complete compliance. No open-ended questions designed to evoke change talk: "What would be the benefits?" "What strengths could help you?" "What's one small step you could see yourself taking?" "How confident are you?" No reinforcement of existing change talk. When client says "I'm not saying I don't want to be healthier," psychiatrist does not explore or affirm this desire. When client says "I'm willing to think about some smaller steps," psychiatrist rejects as insufficient rather than building on as foundation for motivation. No strategic use of questioning to evoke change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Uses direct argumentation and confrontation against sustain talk rather than MI-consistent softening techniques. Client produces sustain talk throughout: "I'm not sure it's all that serious," "I don't see how changing everything overnight is gonna fix stuff," "I've done diets before, and they don't stick," "I'm worried about the medicine side effects," "I'm not ready to say yes to your way today." Psychiatrist's response is to argue directly: "You're just making excuses again," "The medicine is important," "You're risking heart disease," "You must start cutting out junk food today." This is confrontation, not softening. Direct argumentation typically strengthens sustain talk through psychological reactance. No MI-consistent softening techniques used: no double-sided reflections acknowledging both concerns and health goals, no reframing of hesitation as understandable caution, no exploration of underlying concerns, no strategic redirection toward change talk. However, psychiatrist does maintain some focus on target behavior (cholesterol management) rather than getting pulled into extended debate, which prevents score of 1/5. Approach is fundamentally flawed and likely to strengthen rather than soften resistance.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.2/5**

**Session Assessment:**

This session represents a **fundamental failure to implement motivational interviewing principles**. The psychiatrist operates from a confrontational, expert-driven, controlling model that directly contradicts MI's core spirit of partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation.

**Critical Deficiencies Across All Domains:**

- **Acceptance (1/5):** Explicit judgment, disapproval, and conditional regard permeate session
- **Empathy (1/5):** Zero reflective listening; dismissal of client concerns as "excuses"
- **Direction (2/5):** Clear focus maintained but through rigid, imposed demands rather than collaboration
- **Collaboration (1/5):** Hierarchical expert stance; systematic rejection of client input
- **Autonomy Support (1/5):** Controlling language; active negation of client choice
- **Evocation (1/5):** Closed questions; dismissal of spontaneous change talk
- **Partnership (1/5):** Exclusive expert role; no genuine power-sharing
- **Cultivating Change Talk (1/5):** Multiple instances of client change talk ignored or dismissed
- **Softening Sustain Talk (2/5):** Confrontational argumentation likely to strengthen resistance

**Current MI Competency Level:**

The psychiatrist is operating at a **pre-MI level**. This is not motivational interviewing practice; it is traditional medical directive counseling with significant deficiencies. The psychiatrist has not yet developed foundational MI skills in reflective listening, collaborative exploration, or autonomy support.

**Impact on Client Engagement and Motivation:**

**Severely Negative.** This session is likely to **decrease** David's intrinsic motivation for change and **increase** his resistance. The repeated use of controlling language, dismissal of concerns, rejection of autonomy, and escalating pressure create an adversarial dynamic that triggers psychological reactance. David explicitly communicates this: "telling me what I 'must' do like that just makes me want to push back." David's final response ("I'm not ready to say yes to your way today") reflects entrenched resistance rather than movement toward change. The psychiatrist has positioned themselves as an adversary to be resisted rather than a partner in David's health journey.

**Most Critical Improvements Needed (Priority Order):**

1. **Adopt genuine collaborative, partnership-based stance** – Replace directive control with collaborative exploration
2. **Develop reflective listening skills** – Use reflections to demonstrate understanding of client perspective
3. **Learn to evoke rather than impose motivation** – Ask open-ended questions to draw out client's own reasons for change
4. **Support client autonomy** – Replace "you must" with "you could"; offer choices; respect client's timeline
5. **Respond to client feedback** – Adjust approach when client communicates what undermines their motivation

**Recommendation for Training:**

This psychiatrist requires **comprehensive MI training**, including:
- Didactic instruction in MI principles and spirit
- Skills practice with feedback (reflective listening, open-ended questions, evocation)
- Supervised clinical application
- Recommended resources: MI Network (motivationalinterviewing.org), "Motivational Interviewing: Helping People Change" (Miller & Rollnick, 2013), formal MI training workshops with certification

**Without significant retraining, this psychiatrist's current approach is likely to harm therapeutic outcomes and client engagement.**
