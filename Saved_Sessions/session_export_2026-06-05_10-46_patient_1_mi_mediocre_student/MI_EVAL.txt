Repeated prescriptive advice appears at least eight times: "Maybe you should think about cutting back," "It would probably be good to start small," "you might want to push yourself a bit," "Maybe you should try setting small goals," "Maybe you should try to reduce it," "you might want to think about some activities," "Maybe you should write down a couple of things." Repeated prescriptive language communicates psychiatrist has determined what Adrian should do, not that Adrian has genuine choice. Adrian produces natural change talk: "I know it's not the smartest to keep doing it with the risks," "I can see it could help," "I don't want to end up worse off health-wise," "I'm willing to see how it goes," "I'll try to pay attention." Scaling questions asked but not used strategically; no follow-up like "What would move that to a 6 or 7?" or "What do you think you'd need to feel more confident?" Weak strategic use of importance/confidence scaling; responses met with more prescriptions rather than evocative follow-up.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.7/5**

**Critical Findings:**

This session represents a **fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles**, reflecting instead a traditional medical expert model. The psychiatrist demonstrates critical deficiencies across nearly all MI domains, with scores of 1/5 in collaboration, autonomy support, and partnership—the core pillars of MI.

**Most Significant Failures:**

1. **Pervasive prescriptive advice-giving** (8+ instances of "should," "might want to," "probably") violates autonomy support and collaboration
2. **Complete failure of partnership** through expert-driven stance and unilateral problem-solving
3. **Minimal evocation** of intrinsic motivation; reliance on external consequences instead
4. **Failure to cultivate change talk** despite Adrian's natural production of it
5. **Insufficient empathy** with surface-level reflections missing emotional nuance and meaning

**Impact on Client Engagement:**

Adrian's low importance rating (5/10), low confidence rating (4/10), and statement "I'm not promising I'll be all in or anything" indicate **compliance rather than genuine commitment**. This session is likely to **undermine intrinsic motivation** and produce poor treatment outcomes. Research demonstrates prescriptive, expert-driven approaches produce worse outcomes than collaborative, evocative approaches.

**Immediate Recommendations:**

- Eliminate all prescriptive language; replace with open-ended evocative questions
- Establish genuine partnership through co-construction of solutions
- Deepen empathy by exploring emotional nuance and meaning
- Evoke intrinsic motivation by exploring Adrian's values and relationships
- Use scaling questions strategically with follow-up evocative questions
- Recognize and amplify change talk through deliberate follow-up
- Normalize ambivalence through double-sided reflections
- Provide explicit affirmations of Adrian's strengths
- Maintain consistent autonomy support throughout

**Conclusion:**

This psychiatrist requires significant training, supervision, and deliberate practice in core MI skills before continuing to conduct MI-based interventions. The current approach is inconsistent with MI principles and risks harming treatment outcomes.
