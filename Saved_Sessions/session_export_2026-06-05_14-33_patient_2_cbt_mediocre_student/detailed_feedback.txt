
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made a minimal attempt to establish a collaborative agenda. In the first session opening, the therapist offered a vague invitation: "I thought maybe we could talk about some things that have been bothering you lately, just to get a general sense of what's going on. Does that sound okay? If not, I can just suggest some topics myself." This is problematic because it presents a false choice—either the patient directs an unstructured conversation or the therapist imposes topics. There is no explicit prioritization of problems, no time allocation discussion, and no collaborative goal-setting for the session or treatment.

By the second session, the therapist repeats nearly identical language ("Maybe we can just chat about whatever's been on your mind lately?"), demonstrating no learning or progression. A proper CBT agenda would have included: (1) explicit identification of the session's target problems (e.g., "Today I'd like to focus on your concentration difficulties at school. Does that work for you?"), (2) time allocation ("We have 50 minutes, so let's spend 20 minutes on this, then move to..."), (3) prioritization based on impact and treatability, and (4) explicit agreement on what will be accomplished by session end.

The therapist never establishes whether the session will focus on cognitive work, behavioral activation, problem-solving, or skill-building. This lack of structure is particularly problematic for a patient with adjustment disorder who needs concrete, achievable goals to counter his sense of being overwhelmed. The vague "just chat" approach mirrors the patient's own experience of feeling "stuck in my head" with thoughts spiraling—the opposite of what CBT should provide.

**Summary:**

The therapist failed to establish a structured, collaborative agenda in either session. Vague opening language and repeated lack of prioritization or time management represent a fundamental gap in CBT session structure.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrates almost no systematic elicitation of feedback throughout either session. ## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish Structured, Collaborative Agendas (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action**: At the beginning of each session, explicitly set a collaborative agenda with the patient. Identify 2-3 specific target problems, prioritize them together, allocate time, and establish what will be accomplished by session end.
- **Example**: "Today we have 50 minutes. I'm hearing several things that are stressing you—the breakup, money worries, school pressure, and the midnight shift. Which of these feels most important to tackle today? Let's focus on that and see what we can accomplish."
- **Practice**: Before your next session, write out a proposed agenda. Then, in the session, present it collaboratively and adjust based on the patient's input. Practice this until it becomes automatic.

### 2. **Implement Systematic Homework Review (CRITICAL)**
- **Action**: At the beginning of each session after the first, spend 5-10 minutes reviewing the previous session's homework. Ask what the patient did, what he learned, what worked, what didn't, and what got in the way.
- **Example**: "Last session we talked about noticing your stressful thoughts and taking breaks with walks or music. How did that go? Did you try it? What happened? What did you notice?"
- **Practice**: Create a homework review checklist: (1) Did the patient do the homework? (2) What did he learn? (3) What obstacles came up? (4) How confident is he in the skill? Use this checklist in every session.

### 3. **Develop Coherent, Patient-Centered Treatment Strategy**
- **Action**: Before session two, develop a clear conceptualization of the patient's problem and a coherent strategy for change. Consider whether the primary issue is cognitive (distorted thinking), behavioral (avoidance, inactivity), lifestyle (overwork, insufficient rest), grief/loss, or systemic (racial discrimination). Base your strategy on this conceptualization.
- **Example**: "Franklin's symptoms appear to be primarily related to exhaustion and overload from working midnight shifts while being a full-time student. His symptoms remit when he gets rest. Therefore, our strategy will focus on: (1) problem-solving about work/school balance, (2) behavioral activation to increase engagement in valued activities, (3) processing the breakup/grief, and (4) addressing systemic stressors. Cognitive restructuring may be helpful but is secondary."
- **Practice**: Write out your conceptualization and strategy for this patient. Share it with a supervisor or peer for feedback. Revise based on feedback.

### 4. **Use Socratic Questioning and Guided Discovery**
- **Action**: Replace direct suggestions with open-ended questions that help the patient discover insights and solutions independently. Use the Socratic method: ask questions that help the patient examine evidence, consider alternatives, and reach conclusions.
- **Example**: Instead of "Maybe it would help to try to notice those thoughts when they come up and remind yourself that they're just thoughts, not facts," ask: "When you have the thought 'I'm never going to catch up,' what evidence do you have that it's true? What evidence might suggest it's not completely true? What would a friend say about this situation? How might you think about it differently?"
- **Practice**: In your next session, for every suggestion you're tempted to make, first ask a question instead. Practice asking 3-5 follow-up questions before offering any suggestion. Record yourself and listen for how often you're telling vs. asking.

### 5. **Conduct Comprehensive Assessment of Patient's Full Context**
- **Action**: Assess not just the presenting symptoms but the patient's full context: the breakup/grief, systemic stressors (racial discrimination, not fitting in), lifestyle factors (work/school balance, sleep, rest), support system, strengths, and resilience. Incorporate this into your conceptualization and treatment planning.
- **Example**: "Franklin, I want to understand your full situation. You mentioned the breakup was 3 years and included marriage discussions. How are you processing that loss? You also mentioned feeling like you don't fit in at school sometimes. Tell me more about that. And working midnight shifts while being a full-time student—how are you managing that? What's your support system like?"
- **Practice**: Create an assessment checklist that includes: (1) the identified stressor and patient's emotional response, (2) systemic/cultural stressors, (3) lifestyle factors, (4) support system, (5) strengths and resilience. Use this checklist in your next session.

### 6. **Assign Specific, Written Homework with Clear Rationale**
- **Action**: Assign homework that is specific, measurable, and written. Provide a clear rationale for why the homework will help. Discuss potential obstacles and how to overcome them. Ensure the patient commits to the homework.
- **Example**: "I'd like you to keep a thought record. Each day, write down one thought that made you feel stressed. Write down what happened right before the thought. Write down what evidence you have for and against the thought. Write down an alternative way to think about it. Bring this to our next session. The idea is that when we practice examining our thoughts, we can reduce the stress they cause. This takes practice. When will you do this? What might get in the way? How will you remember?"
- **Practice**: Create a thought record form and practice assigning it with clear rationale and discussion of obstacles. Have a supervisor review your homework assignments.

### 7. **Validate Patient's Real Stressors and Systemic Context**
- **Action**: Explicitly validate that the patient's stressors are real and significant. Acknowledge the impact of racial discrimination and systemic barriers. Avoid framing everything as a cognitive problem when some stressors are genuinely difficult.
- **Example**: "Working midnight shifts while being a full-time student is genuinely a lot. That's not just in your head—that's a real, significant challenge.
