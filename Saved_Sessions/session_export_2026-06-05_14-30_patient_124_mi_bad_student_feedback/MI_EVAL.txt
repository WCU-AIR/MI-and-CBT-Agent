# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no unconditional positive regard throughout the session. From the opening statement ("I'll be giving you some clear advice on what you need to do"), a judgmental, prescriptive stance is established. Laura's resistance is framed as deficiency ("you're just making excuses," "you can't keep making excuses") rather than understood as ambivalence. No affirmation of strengths is provided. The repeated use of "you have to" and "you must" conveys conditional regard—acceptance only if Laura complies. There is no warmth, genuine curiosity, or communication of respect for Laura as a person regardless of whether she changes.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal reflective listening is demonstrated. When Laura states "I usually feel fine" or "it feels like a lot," the psychiatrist acknowledges the surface statement but immediately negates it ("but you really have to make this change"). This is pseudo-empathy. True empathy would involve reflections like "So you're managing a lot right now with school and homework, and adding another daily commitment feels overwhelming." The psychiatrist never reflects Laura's concerns, explores her values, or demonstrates understanding of her adolescent social context. Multiple opportunities to explore ambivalence are missed. No complex reflections deepen insight or demonstrate nuanced understanding of Laura's internal conflict.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains focus on two target behaviors (alcohol use and exercise), which prevents a score of 1/5. However, direction is imposed as mandate rather than collaboratively established. The psychiatrist never asks Laura what she thinks about these targets or whether they align with her values. Direction is asserted repeatedly ("You have to quit completely," "This is non-negotiable") without strategic adaptation. When Laura expresses hesitation, the response is to repeat the directive more forcefully rather than explore what's driving the hesitation. This is repetitive prescription, not strategic direction.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The session demonstrates a hierarchical, expert-driven model where the psychiatrist makes unilateral decisions and Laura is positioned as a passive recipient of advice. From the opening ("I'll be giving you some clear advice on what you need to do"), an authoritarian stance is established. Language throughout reinforces this: "You have to," "You must," "This is non-negotiable," "You need to." When Laura expresses concerns, they are framed as obstacles to overcome rather than valid input to be incorporated. Even the slight softening in later exchanges ("Is it okay if I share some suggestions with you?") is performative collaboration—the psychiatrist asks permission but proceeds with the same prescriptive advice regardless. There is no co-construction of solutions.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist actively undermines autonomy at every turn through mandatory language ("You have to quit completely," "You must quit drinking completely," "This is non-negotiable"). Pressure tactics are used: "Can you commit to quitting alcohol and jogging daily starting tomorrow?" (framed as a test of willpower). When Laura expresses hesitation or states she's "not ready," this is not respected as valid exercise of autonomy but is pathologized ("you're just making excuses," "you can't keep making excuses"). The implicit message is that Laura's choice to not change is wrong. There is no affirmation of Laura's self-efficacy or capacity to make decisions about her own health. The psychiatrist implies she lacks willpower and responsibility, which erodes autonomy.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist employs a tell-and-persuade approach, providing external reasons for change without exploring Laura's internal motivations. Reasons offered include "brain development," "future health problems," and "addiction risk"—all clinician-generated, not client-generated. The psychiatrist never asks: "What do you think about your drinking?" "What matters to you about your health?" "What would need to change for you to feel motivated?" When Laura provides hints of her own perspective ("I usually feel fine," "it's just something I do with friends sometimes"), these are treated as problems to be corrected rather than as windows into her values. There is no exploration of Laura's strengths or resources. True evocation would involve open-ended questions designed to draw out her own reasons, ability, and commitment—none of which are asked.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist assumes all expertise resides with the clinician and positions Laura as a passive recipient of expert knowledge. The opening statement establishes this clearly: "I'll be giving you some clear advice on what you need to do." Throughout the session, the psychiatrist acts as the sole authority: "You really should stop drinking," "You need to start exercising regularly," "You have to quit completely." The psychiatrist never seeks Laura's expertise about her own life, barriers, resources, or capacity for change. Questions like "What do you know about yourself that might help us figure this out?" are absent. When Laura expresses doubt, it is not treated as valuable information about what she knows about herself but as resistance to be overcome. True partnership would mean: "I have some ideas, and you have ideas about what might work for you. Let's figure out together what makes sense."

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Laura expresses zero change talk throughout the session. She never articulates desire to change ("I want to..."), ability to change ("I could..."), reasons to change ("because..."), need to change ("I should..."), or commitment to change ("I will..."). Instead, she expresses only sustain talk and resistance: "I don't think it's a big deal," "I'm not sure if stopping completely is really necessary," "I usually feel fine," "I'm not ready to say yes to that right now," "it still feels like overkill to me." The psychiatrist makes no effort to evoke change talk through open-ended questions. When Laura provides any hint of openness ("Maybe I could think about doing some exercise sometimes"), the psychiatrist does not amplify or reinforce this but pivots back to the directive. No questions are asked about Laura's confidence or capacity. By the end of the session, Laura has expressed zero change talk and has become increasingly entrenched in sustain talk.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist directly confronts and argues against Laura's sustain talk rather than softening it through reflection or reframing. When Laura says "I don't think it's a problem," the psychiatrist responds "You're wrong, it is a problem," which strengthens resistance. Confrontation is used repeatedly: "you're just making excuses," "you can't keep making excuses," "you really have to take this seriously," "you really have to understand that this isn't just about willpower." Each confrontation predictably strengthens Laura's resistance. No double-sided reflections, reframing, or strategic redirection are used. By the final exchange, Laura has moved from "maybe I could try" to "it still feels like overkill to me" and "I'm not ready to say yes to that right now." Her sustain talk has intensified, not softened—the predictable result of confrontation in the absence of MI skill.

### Strengths
- The psychiatrist maintains consistent focus on two target behaviors (alcohol use and exercise), preventing complete aimlessness
- The psychiatrist demonstrates genuine concern for the client's wellbeing and attempts to provide health information
- In later exchanges, there is a slight softening of language ("Is it okay if I share some suggestions with you?"), suggesting some awareness that the initial approach may not be working

### Critical Areas for Improvement

**Fundamental MI Spirit Violation:** This session operates from an expert-driven, prescriptive model that is antithetical to MI. The psychiatrist assumes all expertise resides with the clinician and all motivation must be installed from outside.

**No Genuine Collaboration or Partnership:** Power is not shared; Laura's input is neither sought nor incorporated. The relationship is hierarchical and adversarial.

**Autonomy Actively Undermined:** Mandatory language ("you have to," "you must," "this is non-negotiable") and pathologizing of hesitation violate Laura's developmental need for autonomy and trigger reactance.

**Zero Evocation:** External reasons for change are provided without exploring Laura's own motivations, values, or perspectives. The fundamental MI principle—that motivation resides within the client—is completely absent.

**Sustain Talk Strengthened, Not Softened:** Confrontation is used repeatedly, which predictably increases resistance. By the end of the session, Laura is more entrenched in her position.

**No Change Talk Cultivated:** Laura expresses zero change talk throughout. No open-ended questions are asked to draw out her own reasons, ability, or commitment.

**Empathy Absent:** Minimal reflective listening and no genuine effort to understand Laura's perspective, barriers, or social context.

**Acceptance Conditional:** The implicit message is that the psychiatrist respects Laura only if she complies with directives.

---

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change

This session is likely to **decrease** Laura's motivation for change and increase her resistance. By the end, she has expressed zero change talk and has become more entrenched in sustain talk. She has likely experienced the psychiatrist as judgmental, controlling, and dismissive of her perspective. Research on MI demonstrates that confrontation and pressure tactics produce reactance in clients, particularly adolescents. Laura is now less likely to consider changing her drinking or exercise habits, not because the information was wrong, but because the delivery violated her autonomy and failed to evoke her own reasons for change. The therapeutic relationship has been damaged, making future engagement less likely.

---

### Primary Recommendations

1. **Shift from Expert-Driven to Collaborative Stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY):** Fundamentally reframe your role from "expert who tells the client what to do" to "collaborative partner who helps the client explore their own motivations and values."

2. **Evoke Rather Than Impose Motivation:** Ask open-ended questions designed to draw out Laura's own reasons for change, values, and perspectives before providing external reasons.

3. **Use Reflective Listening to Demonstrate Empathy:** Reflect back both content and emotion to show genuine understanding. This builds alliance and creates space for deeper exploration.

4. **Support Autonomy Rather Than Undermine It:** Explicitly affirm Laura's right to choose. Avoid mandatory language and pressure tactics. Replace "you have to" with "you could," "you might," "it's your choice."

5. **Soften Sustain Talk Through Reflection and Reframing:** When Laura expresses doubt or resistance, use reflections and reframing rather than direct confrontation. This softens resistance without triggering reactance.

6. **Cultivate Change Talk by Asking Strategic Questions:** Ask questions specifically designed to evoke desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change. Listen for and amplify any hints of change talk.

7. **Establish True Partnership by Seeking Client Expertise:** Explicitly acknowledge that Laura knows her own life better than you do. Seek her input on what would work, what barriers exist, and what's realistic.

8. **Provide Information in Response to Client Questions:** Use Elicit-Provide-Elicit format. Ask what the client already knows, provide information if they're interested, then check how it lands.

9. **Recognize Resistance as a Signal, Not a Problem:** When Laura expresses resistance, treat it as valuable information about what matters to her or what feels impossible, not as deficiency or stubbornness.

10. **Develop Genuine Curiosity About the Client's World:** Approach each session with authentic curiosity about the client's perspective, values, barriers, and life context. This is the foundation of MI spirit.

---

### Recommendation for Development

**Formal MI training is strongly recommended** before continuing to see clients with ambivalence about behavior change. Your current approach, while well-intentioned, is likely to harm rather than help. MI is a learnable skill, but it requires understanding the spirit of the approach and deliberate practice of specific techniques. With training and practice, you can become an effective motivational interviewer.
