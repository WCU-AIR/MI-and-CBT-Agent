# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal acceptance and fails to establish unconditional positive regard. While the opening is warm, the psychiatrist quickly pivots away from the client's stated position and subtly pressures toward change. No explicit affirmation of Marisol's autonomy, independence, or the legitimacy of her perspective. The phrase "But sometimes even small changes can make a difference" contradicts acceptance by implying her current state is inadequate. Lacks genuine validation of the client's self-assessment and right to make her own decisions about her body and health.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Empathy is superficial and largely absent. Psychiatrist makes minimal effort to understand Marisol's actual perspective, values, or the meaning behind her choices. Reflections are factually accurate but emotionally hollow (e.g., "the piercing itself is okay"). No exploration of why autonomy and self-expression matter to her or what "change" means to her. Fails to recognize and reflect Marisol's core value of autonomy. No open-ended questions designed to deepen understanding: "What does health mean to you?" "What matters most to you?" "Tell me more about why the piercing felt important?" These critical omissions represent fundamental failure of empathic exploration.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains a general direction toward health behavior change, but this direction is poorly calibrated to the client's stage of change and lacks strategic clarity. Session begins with appropriate open question but quickly becomes unfocused, addressing multiple potential targets (piercing, exercise, diet, stress, general health changes) without establishing which the client actually wants to work on. Direction is misaligned with Marisol's pre-contemplation stage—psychiatrist attempts action-stage interventions ("try a short walk or healthier snack") rather than strategically working with pre-contemplation through exploration and evocation. Some focus maintained on referral issue but abandoned when client denies problems.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Profound failure of collaboration. Psychiatrist consistently positions herself as the expert with knowledge about what the client "should" do, treating client as passive recipient of advice. Unilateral problem definition: frames piercing as health concern requiring attention without exploring whether Marisol agrees. Prescriptive language throughout: "Maybe you should think about," "it would probably help if you," "you might want to try," "you should still think about." Dismisses client input—when Marisol states "I don't see any reason to change," psychiatrist overrides with "But sometimes even small changes can make a difference." No shared decision-making or co-created goals. Unilateral session summary without checking accuracy or inviting client co-creation.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session actively undermines autonomy support—one of the most critical MI principles. Implicit pressure disguised as choice: "maybe you should think about," "it would probably help if," "you might want to try" are soft forms of directive pressure communicating psychiatrist's belief that client should change. Dismissal of stated autonomy: Marisol explicitly states "I just do what I want" and emphasizes her right to make own decisions; psychiatrist ignores this rather than affirming it. Frames non-change as a problem by repeatedly suggesting client "should" consider changes. No explicit affirmation of choice: never states "This is entirely your decision," "You're in control here," or "I respect your right to choose differently." Subtle coercion in closing: "It would probably help if you just keep doing what feels right for you" implies psychiatrist has preferred direction rather than genuine autonomy support.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out client's own motivations, values, and reasons for change—is almost entirely absent. No exploration of client values: never asks "What matters most to you?" "What do you value?" "What would make you feel good?" No exploration of piercing's meaning: when Marisol says "I just wanted a change," psychiatrist misses rich opportunity to evoke by asking "Tell me more about that—what kind of change were you looking for?" Prescriptive suggestions instead of evocation: tells "maybe eating a bit healthier or moving more" rather than asking "What do you think might help?" No elicitation of client ideas: never asks "What ideas do you have?" "What have you thought about?" No amplification of change talk: Marisol produces minimal change talk ("I guess if it was really easy...maybe"); psychiatrist does not develop this. No exploration of ambivalence or intrinsic motivation. Session characterized by psychiatrist-driven suggestions, not client-driven discovery.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership—understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client—is fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist operates from clear expert stance throughout. Expert-driven agenda: psychiatrist decides what to discuss (health and lifestyle issues), not client. When client identifies piercing as main concern, psychiatrist immediately reframes as potential health problem rather than exploring what client wants to address. Unilateral problem identification: assumes dentist's concerns are relevant frame without exploring whether Marisol agrees. Psychiatrist as authority on motivation: repeatedly suggests what would "help," what client "should" think about, what might "make a difference"—all framed as psychiatric expertise rather than collaborative exploration. No co-construction of goals: session ends without mutually agreed-upon goals or direction. Language of hierarchy throughout: "it would probably help if," "you should think about," "maybe you should try" position psychiatrist as expert advising client. No invitation of client expertise: never asks "You know yourself best—what do you think?" Client's expertise about her own life never solicited or valued.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Change talk—client's own language in favor of change—is virtually absent, and psychiatrist makes almost no effort to evoke or cultivate it. No strategic questions to evoke change talk: never asks questions designed to elicit desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment to change. Minimal client change talk produced: only one weak statement approximating change talk ("I guess if it was really easy...maybe"). No amplification of this one change talk statement: psychiatrist does not develop or explore this opening. No reinforcement of change talk: never reflects back or affirms any change talk produced. Advice-giving instead of evoking: tells "maybe eating a bit healthier or moving more" rather than asking "What do you think might help?" No exploration of values that could support change: never asks about health values, future goals, or what client wants her life to look like. Sustain talk dominates session; psychiatrist does not skillfully redirect it. Session relies on external pressure rather than evoking intrinsic motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk dominates session and psychiatrist handles it poorly. Sustain talk goes largely unaddressed: Marisol produces clear sustain talk throughout ("I don't really think I have any big health issues," "I'm doing okay like this," "I don't see any reason to change," "I'm good for now"); psychiatrist rarely attempts to redirect, often validating it without strategically softening. No double-sided reflections: key MI technique for softening sustain talk is absent. Weak attempts to introduce discrepancy: asks "Do you think there's anything stopping you from making changes if you wanted to?" but does not follow up or develop response. Contradictory responses to sustain talk: when Marisol says "I don't see any reason to change," psychiatrist responds "But maybe if you did want to..." This argues with sustain talk rather than softening it. Premature acceptance of sustain talk: by session end, psychiatrist essentially accepts Marisol's sustain talk and suggests she "just keep doing what feels right for you." No reframing: never attempts to reframe sustain talk in way that opens space for change. One minor strength: psychiatrist avoids direct confrontation or argument with sustain talk, preventing escalation of resistance.

### Overall Session Assessment

**Total MI Competency Score: 1.4/5 (Pre-Competent to Minimally Competent)**

**Summary of Key Findings:**

This session represents a **fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles** across nearly all domains. The psychiatrist operates from a traditional medical expert model rather than an MI collaborative partnership model. Core problems include: expert-driven rather than client-centered approach; advice-giving instead of evocation; pressure disguised as collaboration; absence of genuine partnership; failure to work strategically with pre-contemplation stage; minimal empathic understanding; and failure to evoke intrinsic motivation.

**Impact on Client Engagement and Change:**

This session is **likely to decrease rather than increase** client motivation for change. Subtle pressure triggers psychological reactance, strengthening client's commitment to status quo. Misalignment with pre-contemplation stage creates irrelevance or perceived pressure. Lack of intrinsic motivation evocation means reliance on ineffective external pressure. Damaged therapeutic alliance evidenced by client's polite but disengaged responses. No movement toward contemplation or preparation; client remains in pre-contemplation with no identified change goals.

**Critical Recommendations:**

1. Shift from expert to collaborative partnership (highest priority)
2. Develop genuine empathic understanding of client values and perspective
3. Replace advice-giving with evocative questions
4. Respect and affirm autonomy explicitly
5. Work strategically with pre-contemplation through exploration, not pressure
6. Evoke change talk through strategic questions, not advice
7. Use double-sided reflections to soften sustain talk
8. Establish genuine collaboration through co-created goals and agenda
9. Develop curiosity about client's inner world
10. Avoid premature closure and acceptance of status quo

**Recommendation for Next Steps:**

Psychiatrist should engage in intensive MI training including didactic instruction, observation of skilled practitioners, practice with recorded feedback, and ongoing supervision focused on shifting from expert to collaborative stance.
