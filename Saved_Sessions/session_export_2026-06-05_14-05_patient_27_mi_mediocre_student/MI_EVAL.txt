## Summary of Clinical Feedback

### Strengths
- Maintains clear, consistent focus on smoking and drinking reduction
- Warm, non-hostile opening tone establishes positive rapport
- Demonstrates awareness that client is managing significant life demands
- Offers reasonable, practical suggestions (deep breathing, neighbor support, tracking, gradual reduction)
- Attempts to create structure through session summary and plan development
- Shows commitment to follow-through and ongoing support

### Critical Areas for Improvement

**This session represents a fundamental departure from MI principles and spirit.** The clinician functions as an expert dispensing advice rather than as a collaborative partner evoking intrinsic motivation for change. The pervasive use of directive language ("should," "probably help if," "might want to") undermines client autonomy and positions the clinician as authority rather than partner.

**Most critically, evocation—the core of MI—is virtually absent.** Client offers multiple powerful statements about motivations (wanting to be good example for son), past successes (cutting back on several nights), and own ideas about solutions (exploring neighbor support). Rather than exploring and amplifying these, clinician acknowledges superficially and prescribes what client "should" do. By session end, client has become passive and compliant, agreeing with clinician's plan rather than co-creating it.

**Impact on client engagement and change is concerning.** While client may comply short-term, prescriptive approach does not build intrinsic motivation or self-efficacy. Client's low confidence rating (5-6/10) signals ambivalence that should have been explored, not prescribed around. Research consistently shows client-generated solutions are more sustainable than clinician-prescribed plans.

### Key Recommendations for Development

1. **Replace prescription with evocation** – Shift from "should/probably help if" language to genuine open questions inviting client to generate solutions
2. **Amplify change talk** – Pause and explore client's statements about desire, ability, reasons, and commitment rather than moving to prescription
3. **Use double-sided reflections** – Acknowledge both sides of ambivalence without judgment to soften sustain talk
4. **Explore ambivalence** – When client expresses low confidence, explore what's needed rather than prescribing around it
5. **Co-create plans** – Invite client input at every step; ask "What feels right to you?" rather than presenting clinician-developed plan
6. **Offer genuine affirmations** – Build self-efficacy by recognizing effort, strengths, and past successes
7. **Ask open questions** – Replace closed/leading questions with genuine open questions (aim for 70%+ open questions)
8. **Share power genuinely** – Communicate client is expert on own life; use inclusive language ("we," "let's"); defer to client's wisdom

---

**Supervisor Recommendation:** This clinician demonstrates basic clinical competence but requires focused training in MI principles, particularly in shifting from expert-driven prescription to collaborative evocation. With deliberate practice on the recommendations above—especially replacing directive language with open questions and amplifying change talk—this clinician can develop genuine MI competence. Consider recording and reviewing sessions with focus on these specific areas.
