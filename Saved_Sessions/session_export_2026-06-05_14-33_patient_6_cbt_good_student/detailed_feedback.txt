
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

This document outlines a structured framework for assessing therapist competence in Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT). 
It is used for training, supervision, and evaluation. Each item is scored on a 0–6 scale:

0 = Poor
2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Conduct Comprehensive Functional Analysis of Drinking (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Before the next session, develop a detailed understanding of what drinking does for this patient. In the next session, ask: "When you drink, what specifically does it do for you? How does it help you manage stress, emotions, or situations? What would happen if you didn't drink in those moments?"
- **Example:** "I notice you mentioned needing a drink to calm down and to forget your worries. Let's explore that more. When you feel stressed or worried, what specifically happens when you drink? How does your body feel? Your mind? How long does that relief last? What happens after?"
- **Practice:** Use a functional analysis worksheet to map the antecedents (what triggers drinking), the behavior (drinking), and the consequences (what the patient gets from drinking). This will identify the core maintaining factors and guide behavioral interventions.

### 2. **Expand Cognitive Work to Target Drinking-Specific Beliefs (HIGH PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** In future sessions, systematically identify and challenge the automatic thoughts most directly linked to drinking behavior. Use thought records to help the patient practice cognitive restructuring independently.
- **Example:** "You mentioned thinking 'I need a drink to calm down.' Let's examine that thought. What's the evidence that you need a drink? What happens if you try to calm down without a drink? What other ways might you calm down?"
- **Practice:** Provide the patient with a written thought record template and assign homework to complete one record daily, identifying drinking-related thoughts, examining evidence, and generating alternatives. Review these in session.

### 3. **Integrate Psychoeducation About Alcohol-Induced Depression (HIGH PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Explicitly explain the relationship between alcohol use and depressive symptoms. Help the patient understand that his depression is expected to improve with abstinence, reducing the risk that he will drink to manage mood.
- **Example:** "The depression you're experiencing—the fatigue, low mood, difficulty concentrating—is directly related to your heavy alcohol use. This is actually good news because it means as you reduce drinking and maintain sobriety, these symptoms should improve significantly. This usually happens over 2-4 weeks of abstinence. It's important to know this because sometimes people think they need a drink to feel better, but actually the opposite is true."
- **Practice:** Provide written psychoeducation materials about alcohol-induced depression. Discuss the timeline for symptom improvement. Help the patient distinguish between withdrawal symptoms (which resolve quickly) and depressive symptoms (which resolve more gradually with sustained abstinence).

### 4. **Develop Behavioral Coping Strategies for High-Risk Situations (HIGH PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Identify the specific situations, emotions, and times when the patient is most vulnerable to drinking (e.g., evening stress, being alone). Develop concrete, detailed coping strategies for these situations.
- **Example:** "You mentioned that evening is when you feel restless and the urges hit hardest. Let's develop a specific plan for that time. What time does this typically happen? What could you do instead? Let's be very specific—not just 'take a walk' but 'at 6 PM, I will put on my walking shoes and walk to the park for 20 minutes.' What else could you do? Call a friend? Exercise? Work on a hobby?"
- **Practice:** Create a written "coping card" with specific strategies for high-risk times. Practice these strategies in session through role-play. Assign homework to use at least one strategy daily and record what happens.

### 5. **Implement Behavioral Activation for Depression (MEDIUM PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Assign structured activity scheduling to address depressive symptoms (fatigue, anhedonia, concentration difficulties). Help the patient schedule activities that are likely to be reinforcing and that address his values.
- **Example:** "You mentioned losing interest in activities and feeling fatigued. Let's schedule some activities that you used to enjoy or that might help you feel better. What did you enjoy before your drinking increased? What activities give you a sense of accomplishment or connection?"
- **Practice:** Have the patient complete an activity schedule for the week, including specific times for activities. Assign homework to complete the schedule and rate mood before and after each activity. Review in session.

### 6. **Explore and Address Relapse Risk Factors (MEDIUM PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Explicitly discuss the patient's previous two 30-day abstinence periods. Understand what led to relapse and develop strategies to prevent recurrence.
- **Example:** "You mentioned you've had two 30-day periods of abstinence in the past 4 years. That's great—it shows you can do this. What happened during those times? What was different? What led to you returning to drinking? What could we do differently this time to prevent that?"
- **Practice:** Develop a relapse prevention plan that identifies high-risk situations, warning signs of relapse, and specific coping strategies. Discuss what to do if a lapse occurs (one drink) to prevent full relapse.

### 7. **Assign Structured Thought Records as Homework (MEDIUM PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Provide the patient with a written thought record template and assign daily homework to complete records for drinking urges or depressive thoughts.
- **Example:** "Here's a form that will help you practice what we did today. When you notice an urge to drink or a negative thought, write down: (1) What happened? (2) What did you think? (3) What did you feel? (4) What's the evidence for and against that thought? (5) What's a more balanced thought? Bring these to our next session and we'll review them together."
- **Practice:** Review completed thought records in session. Provide feedback and coaching on the process. Gradually fade the written form as the patient internalizes the skill.

### 8. **Clarify and Articulate Treatment Strategy (MEDIUM PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Develop an explicit, comprehensive treatment strategy and communicate it clearly to the patient. Help him understand how each intervention fits into the larger plan.
- **Example:** "Here's my understanding of what's happening and how we'll address it. You use alcohol to manage stress and difficult emotions, and you've developed some thoughts that make it harder to change—like 'I'm not strong enough' or 'I need a drink to calm down.' We're going to work on both. We'll challenge those thoughts, develop other ways to manage stress, and help your mood improve as you reduce drinking. We'll also plan for high-risk situations so you're prepared. Does this make sense? Do you have questions?"
- **Practice:** Write out the treatment plan and share it with the patient. Revisit and revise as needed based on progress and new information.

### 9. **Assess and Address Family Dynamics (MEDIUM PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Explore the patient's family system and consider involving his wife and/or daughter in treatment. Understand how family dynamics affect drinking and how family can support recovery.
- **Example:** "Your family held an intervention because they care about you and are concerned. That's actually a strength. How is your wife responding to your efforts to change? What does she think would help? Would it be helpful to have her involved in some sessions?"
- **Practice:** Conduct a family session or at minimum discuss with the patient how to communicate with his wife about his treatment goals and how she can support his recovery.

### 10. **Develop Contingency Planning for Lapses (MEDIUM PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Discuss what to do if the patient drinks despite his commitment. Normalize lapses as part of recovery and develop a plan to prevent lapse from becoming relapse.
- **Example:** "If you find yourself having a drink despite your plan, here's what's important: (1) Don't give up. One drink doesn't mean failure. (2) Stop drinking immediately. (3) Call me or go to a support group. (4) We'll review what happened and adjust the plan. Does that make sense?"
- **Practice:** Role-play how the patient would respond to a lapse. Discuss specific people he could call or resources he could access if struggling.

### 11. **Establish Clear Homework Review Process (LOWER PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Explicitly establish that homework will be reviewed at the beginning of each session and that this review is a critical part of treatment.
- **Example:** "At the start of our next session, we'll spend time reviewing how your homework went. We'll look at what worked, what was challenging, and adjust the plan based on what you learn. This is really important because it helps us understand what's working for you and what needs to change."
- **Practice:** Begin each subsequent session with 5-10 minutes of homework review. Provide specific feedback and praise for effort regardless of outcome.
