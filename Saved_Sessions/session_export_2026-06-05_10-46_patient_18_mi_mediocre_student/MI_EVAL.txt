# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal acceptance and unconditional positive regard. Repeatedly implies client lacks willpower ("Do you think maybe you just need to be stronger or more motivated to quit?"), which is fundamentally judgmental and violates MI spirit. Fails to affirm genuine strengths: long-standing desire to quit, awareness of consequences, willingness to engage, and emotional insight. Tone becomes increasingly prescriptive and controlling, communicating conditional rather than unconditional regard. Minimizes legitimate concerns about support groups rather than validating them.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates surface-level empathy through factual paraphrasing rather than emotional attunement. Misses rich emotional content: client's shame about feeling "like a leper," fear of disappointing daughter, ambivalence about family pressure, and sense of being overwhelmed. Responses are largely informational summaries ("So, stress and your responsibilities make it hard to quit") that miss emotional depth. Fails to explore complexity of client's mixed feelings about daughter's support or validate fear about support groups. Reflections are shallow and problem-focused rather than emotionally resonant.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains clear focus on smoking cessation throughout session, which is appropriate. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. Clinician consistently steers toward specific interventions (diary, nicotine replacement, support groups, talking to daughter) without exploring client's own ideas or preferences. By fifth intervention, psychiatrist essentially dictates plan: "Maybe you should set a goal to start the diary this week." Lacks exploration of client's own goals and vision for change. Does not ask what would be helpful first step or what feels most doable.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Session demonstrates minimal collaboration and reflects predominantly expert-driven, hierarchical stance. Psychiatrist consistently positions self as authority who knows what client should do, while client is positioned as compliant recipient of advice. Language reflects unequal power-sharing: "should try," "probably help," "might want to," "would probably help if." True collaboration absent—never asks client what she thinks would work, what her priorities are, or how she wants to approach this. When client expresses hesitation about nicotine replacement, psychiatrist does not explore concerns or ask what would build confidence. Final plan is clinician-created, not co-created.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist frequently undermines client's autonomy and sense of choice through subtle pressure and directive language. Prescriptive language throughout ("should try," "you might want to," "it would probably help if") communicates clinician's agenda rather than supporting self-determination. Minimizes legitimate concerns: when client expresses fear about support groups, psychiatrist responds with "Even though it feels scary, it would probably help if you took those next steps soon," dismissing concern and pressuring action. Closing statement—"Keep at it, and I'm here to support you"—implies client should follow prescribed plan. True autonomy support would explicitly affirm client's right to choose, even if that means not quitting right now.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal evocation and relies on providing information, suggestions, and solutions rather than drawing out client's own motivations and ideas. Rich change talk is not explored or amplified: "I've been wanting to quit for a long time" and "I'm willing to consider those options" are met with problem-solving rather than deeper exploration. When client mentions daughter's frustration, psychiatrist could ask "What does that mean to you?" but instead asks evaluative question. Never asks about client's vision for change, what strengths she possesses, or what she thinks might help. By session end, client's change talk has not been strengthened; final statement reflects compliance ("I'll try my best") rather than genuine ownership of motivation.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist fails to establish genuine partnership throughout session. Clinician assumes expert role possessing knowledge and solutions while client is positioned as passive recipient of advice. Unilateral problem definition: psychiatrist frames problem as client needing "stronger willpower" rather than exploring how client understands her struggle. Solution-focused rather than collaborative—rapidly moves to suggesting interventions without asking what client thinks might help. Power imbalance evident in language ("you should," "you might want to," "it would probably help if"). Psychiatrist essentially decides plan and presents it for acceptance. True partnership would involve shared decision-making, genuine curiosity about client's perspective, and recognition that client is expert on her own life.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal skill in evoking, recognizing, or amplifying change talk. Multiple opportunities missed: when client states "I've been wanting to quit for a long time," psychiatrist could ask "What keeps that alive for you?" but instead asks about methods tried. When client expresses daughter's frustration, psychiatrist could explore "How important is it to you to be the parent she needs?" but instead asks evaluative question. Client's confidence rating of 4-5/10 is not explored through evocation of strengths; instead psychiatrist prescribes interventions. Client's statement "I'm willing to consider those options" is not amplified. Psychiatrist never asks about commitment to change or what would strengthen motivation. Session ends with compliance language ("I'll try my best") rather than strengthened intrinsic motivation.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates adequate but inconsistent skill in handling sustain talk. Does not directly reinforce or confront client's barriers ("It's hard to get through cravings," "Smoking is the only thing that calms me down," "I keep worrying I'll slip back"). Primary strategy is offering solutions (distractions, exercise, meditation, diary) rather than more sophisticated MI techniques. Approach is somewhat surface-level and doesn't deeply address meaning or function of sustain talk. Psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections to acknowledge both sides of ambivalence or explore function of smoking before offering alternatives. Maintains supportive tone and does not shame client, which is positive. Could improve by exploring "What does smoking do for you?" and normalizing concerns rather than quickly moving to solutions.

### **Overall Session Score: 2.2/5**

## Summary Assessment

**MI Competence Level:** Below Average – Significant Deficiencies

**Strengths:**
- Maintains warm, non-confrontational tone throughout
- Does not directly shame or confront client
- Maintains clear focus on smoking cessation as target behavior
- Offers practical, evidence-based suggestions
- Demonstrates willingness to support client
- Allows client to express perspective without interruption

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- **Fundamental MI spirit violation:** Repeatedly implies client lacks willpower; judgmental and inconsistent with acceptance
- **Minimal evocation:** Fails to draw out client's own motivations, ideas, and resources
- **Expert-driven stance:** Positions psychiatrist as authority rather than collaborative partner
- **Prescriptive language:** Uses "should," "probably help," "might want to" throughout, undermining autonomy
- **Shallow empathy:** Paraphrases content but misses emotional depth and nuance of ambivalence and shame
- **Missed change talk amplification:** Rich change talk not explored or strengthened
- **Lack of collaborative planning:** Psychiatrist decides plan and presents it rather than co-creating

**Impact on Client Engagement:**
This session likely **reduces** intrinsic motivation for change despite initial willingness. Client complies with suggestions but compliance is not commitment. Final statement—"I'm still nervous about all of it, but I guess I have to keep trying"—reflects resignation rather than genuine hope. Repeated implications about lacking willpower may increase shame and self-doubt, which are already present.

**Likelihood of Successful Outcome with Current Approach:** LOW

**Recommendation:** Psychiatrist must fundamentally shift from expert-prescriptive stance to collaborative-evocative stance through asking more questions, affirming strengths, exploring motivations deeply, genuinely partnering, and supporting autonomy.
