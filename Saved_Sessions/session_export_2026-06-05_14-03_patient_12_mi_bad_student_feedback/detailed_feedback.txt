
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance or unconditional positive regard throughout this session. From the opening statement—"I want to talk about your health habits and help you make some changes. You really need to quit any unhealthy behaviors you have, okay?"—the clinician establishes a judgmental, prescriptive stance that persists throughout all exchanges. The language is consistently directive and disapproving: "You really need to get serious," "You must stop making excuses," "You have to push yourself," and "This isn't about feeling comfortable—it's about results."

Most critically, when Amira expresses legitimate concerns about overwhelm and sustainability—"I want to start small so I don't burn out"—the psychiatrist responds with dismissal and pressure: "Look, you need to stop hesitating and just push yourself harder." This directly contradicts acceptance. The clinician frames Amira's cautious, thoughtful approach as weakness ("stop making excuses," "stop hesitating") rather than as valid self-knowledge. There is no affirmation of her strengths (high readiness at 8/10, clear motivation, realistic self-assessment, successful avoidance of smoking/drinking). The psychiatrist never normalizes the difficulty of behavior change or acknowledges the legitimacy of her concerns about adjustment to a new environment.

The repeated threat language—"If you don't start now, you'll regret it later" and "If you don't take this seriously, you're just setting yourself up for failure"—communicates conditional regard: acceptance only if the patient complies with the clinician's prescribed approach. This is antithetical to MI spirit.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no genuine acceptance, communicates disapproval of the patient's approach, uses threat-based language, and fails to affirm any of the patient's strengths or valid concerns. Acceptance is entirely absent.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI means maintaining appropriate focus on a specific target behavior while remaining client-centered. This psychiatrist does maintain focus on physical activity as the target behavior—that is consistent. **Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains focus on physical activity but does so in a rigid, imposed manner without responsiveness to the client's input or circumstances. Direction lacks collaboration and flexibility.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Fundamental Reorientation to MI Spirit and Principles**
- **Action:** Complete comprehensive MI training or retraining, with emphasis on the four core MI processes (partnership, compassion, evocation, autonomy support) and the spirit of MI (collaborative, person-centered, non-judgmental).
- **Example:** Before the next session, review the MI definition: "Motivational Interviewing is a collaborative, goal-oriented style of communication with particular attention to the language of change. It is designed to strengthen personal motivation for and commitment to a specific goal by eliciting and exploring the person's own reasons for change within an atmosphere of acceptance and compassion."
- **Practice:** Watch recordings of expert MI practitioners (available through MINT—Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers) and compare their approach to your own. Identify specific moments where they use partnership, evocation, and autonomy support instead of prescription and pressure.

---

### 2. **Replace Prescriptive Language with Collaborative Inquiry**
- **Action:** Eliminate directive language ("You need to," "You must," "You have to") and replace it with open-ended questions and collaborative statements that invite the client's input and expertise.
- **Example:** Instead of: "You have to push yourself to do it regularly. Just walking or light activity isn't enough; you need to do something more intense to really get results."
  
  Say: "You've identified activity as your starting point, and you're at an 8/10 readiness. Tell me more about what kind of movement feels doable for you right now. What would make it feel sustainable?"

- **Practice:** Before each session, write out 3-5 open-ended questions you could ask instead of giving advice. Practice asking these questions in role-play with a colleague or supervisor.

---

### 3. **Develop Reflective Listening Skills**
- **Action:** Learn and practice reflective listening—the cornerstone of MI. Reflections demonstrate that you have heard and understood the client's perspective, which builds trust and collaboration.
- **Example:** When Amira says: "I want to start with something manageable so I don't burn out or quit quickly," reflect back: "You're thinking about what will actually stick for you long-term, and you're worried that jumping in too hard might backfire. That makes sense."
- **Practice:** In every session, aim for a 2:1 ratio of reflections to questions. Record yourself and count your reflections. If you have fewer than 2 reflections per question, you are not listening enough.

---

### 4. **Build on Client Change Talk Rather Than Contradicting It**
- **Action:** When the client expresses motivation for change, amplify and deepen it through strategic questions and reflections. Never contradict or dismiss the client's own reasons for change.
- **Example:** Amira said: "If I'm more active, I think my body confidence would improve too. I definitely want better mood and energy."
  
  Instead of: "Just walking and light activity isn't enough; you need to do something more intense to really get results."
  
  Say: "So improved confidence, better mood, and more energy are really important to you. Tell me more about what that would mean for your life. How would you feel different?"

- **Practice:** In supervision or role-play, have a colleague play the client and express change talk. Practice responding with reflections and deepening questions rather than advice or contradiction.

---

### 5. **Respect and Support Client Autonomy**
- **Action:** Recognize that the client is the expert on their own life, circumstances, and what will work for them. Support their right to choose and emphasize their ownership of the change process.
- **Example:** Instead of: "You can't just be cautious when it comes to your health. If you don't push through the discomfort and intimidation, nothing's going to change."
  
  Say: "You're the expert on what will work for you. You've thought carefully about this, and you know that starting gradually feels more sustainable. Let's figure out what that first step could look like."

- **Practice:** In each session, explicitly affirm the client's autonomy at least once: "This is your decision," "You know what's best for you," "What feels right to you?"

---

### 6. **Explore and Validate Readiness Concerns Rather Than Dismissing Them**
- **Action:** When a client expresses concerns about readiness (like Amira's worry about burnout), treat these as valuable information about what will actually work, not as resistance or excuses.
- **Example:** Instead of: "You have to stop making excuses about feeling stressed or overwhelmed. Everyone feels that way at first, but they still get it done."
  
  Say: "You mentioned that jumping into something too intense might backfire for you. Tell me more about that. What have you learned about yourself from past attempts at change?"

- **Practice:** In role-play, practice responding to readiness concerns with curiosity and validation rather than pressure. Ask: "What would help you feel more confident?" "What would make this feel more manageable?"

---

### 7. **Use Double-Sided Reflections to Honor Ambivalence**
- **Action:** When a client expresses both motivation for change and concerns about it, use double-sided reflections to acknowledge both sides and move toward resolution.
- **Example:** "On one hand, you want to feel stronger and more confident, and you're at an 8/10 readiness. On the other hand, you're concerned that jumping into something too intense might overwhelm you and lead to burnout. Both of those make sense. Let's figure out how to honor both—how to move forward in a way that feels sustainable."
- **Practice:** In supervision, practice writing double-sided reflections for common client ambivalences. Use the formula: "On one hand… On the other hand… Both of those make sense."

### 8. **Eliminate Threat Language and Coercive Tactics**
- **Action:** Remove language designed to pressure or frighten the client into compliance ("If you don't start now, you'll regret it later," "You're just setting yourself up for failure"). This undermines autonomy and typically strengthens resistance.
- **Example:** Instead of: "If you don't take this seriously, you're just setting yourself up for failure."

- **Practice:** Review recordings of your sessions and identify all instances of threat language or pressure. For each one, write an alternative MI-consistent response.

### 9. **Develop Genuine Curiosity About the Client's Perspective**
- **Action:** Approach each client with authentic curiosity about their life, values, concerns, and what will work for them. This is the foundation of empathy and partnership.
- **Example:** When Amira mentioned moving to a new place, the psychiatrist did not explore this. An MI-consistent response: "You mentioned you've recently moved and are still adjusting. Tell me about that. How is that affecting your ability to build new routines?"
- **Practice:** Before each session, remind yourself: "I don't know what will work for this person. I need to ask and listen." In sessions, ask at least 3 questions about the client's life, values, or past experiences before offering any suggestions.
