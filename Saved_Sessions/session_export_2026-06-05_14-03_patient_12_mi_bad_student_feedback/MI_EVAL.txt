### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no unconditional positive regard throughout the session. From the opening statement ("You really need to quit any unhealthy behaviors"), the clinician establishes a judgmental, prescriptive stance. Critical strengths are never affirmed (patient's 8/10 readiness, clear motivation, successful avoidance of smoking/drinking). When the patient expresses legitimate concerns about overwhelm and sustainability, the psychiatrist responds with dismissal and pressure ("stop hesitating," "stop making excuses"). Threat-based language ("If you don't start now, you'll regret it later") communicates conditional regard. Acceptance is entirely absent.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** There are zero reflective listening statements in the entire session. The clinician never reflects back what the patient has said, never checks for understanding, and never demonstrates that the patient's perspective has been heard or considered. When the patient explicitly states concerns about burnout and sustainability, the psychiatrist dismisses them entirely without exploration. Critical contextual information (recent move, adjustment challenges) is acknowledged but not explored. No attempt is made to understand why the patient prefers a gradual approach or what would make activity feel sustainable. Empathy is entirely absent.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains consistent focus on physical activity as the target behavior. However, the direction is imposed rather than collaborative and rigid rather than responsive to the patient's input. The clinician has a predetermined prescription (gym membership, high intensity, three times weekly, immediate start) and simply repeats it with increased pressure when the patient indicates this doesn't fit their needs. When the patient offers her own expertise about what will work for her, the response is dismissal rather than exploration. Direction lacks collaboration and flexibility; it is prescription masquerading as guidance.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist operates from a pure expert-driven, hierarchical stance throughout. Opening statement explicitly rejects partnership: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do." Repeated use of commands ("you need," "you must," "you have to") rather than collaborative language. No questions seek the patient's input on what might work for her. When the patient expresses a different view, the response is contradiction and pressure, not exploration. No joint problem-solving or co-construction of a plan occurs. The clinician treats the patient's perspective as an obstacle to overcome rather than as valuable information to understand and work with. Collaboration is entirely absent.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist actively undermines autonomy throughout the session. Repeated commands and demands ("You have to push yourself," "You must stop making excuses," "You need to commit to this now") leave no room for choice. Threat language ("If you don't start now, you'll regret it later") is designed to coerce compliance. When the patient asserts her own judgment ("I want to start small," "for me, this feels like the best way to set myself up for success"), these statements are dismissed as hesitation and excuses rather than respected as valid self-determination. The psychiatrist frames the patient's autonomy to define what matters to her as weakness ("This isn't about feeling comfortable—it's about results"). Autonomy support is entirely absent; instead, the clinician attempts to control behavior through pressure and coercion.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist makes no effort to evoke the patient's own motivations, values, or resources. The patient has articulated abundant change talk (desire: "I want to feel stronger"; ability: "I'm motivated—8 out of 10 readiness"; reasons: "improved confidence, mood, energy"; commitment: "I'm committed to getting more active"). Rather than building on this rich change talk, the psychiatrist ignores it and substitutes clinician-imposed reasons ("You need to do something more intense to really get results"). No open-ended questions deepen the patient's own motivation. No exploration of past experiences, values, or what would make activity feel sustainable. The clinician imposes external motivation through pressure rather than evoking internal motivation. Evocation is entirely absent.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist actively assumes the expert role and explicitly rejects partnership ("I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do"). Repeated prescriptions are delivered without exploration or collaboration. When the patient offers her own expertise about what will work for her, the response is dismissal. No acknowledgment that the patient knows her own life, circumstances, and change history better than the clinician. No invitation for the patient's input to shape session direction. The clinician's prescriptions dominate while the patient's contributions are ignored or contradicted. Power-sharing is entirely absent. The psychiatrist operates as sole authority on what the patient "needs" and "must" do, treating her as a passive recipient of expert advice rather than as a partner in her own change process.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The patient's stated concerns are minimal and reasonable (adjustment to new environment, worry about burnout, desire for sustainability). These are not true sustain talk but rather expressions of readiness concerns and realistic self-assessment. However, the psychiatrist treats them as resistance and responds with confrontation rather than softening: "You need to stop hesitating," "You can't just be cautious," "You must stop making excuses." ## Overall Summary

### Session Competency Assessment: **1.1/5**

**Critical Findings:**

This session represents a fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles and spirit. The psychiatrist operates from an expert-driven, hierarchical, prescriptive stance that violates nearly every core MI competency. The approach is judgmental, dismissive, controlling, and confrontational—the antithesis of MI.

**Strengths (Limited):**
- Maintains consistent focus on physical activity as target behavior
- Clear, direct communication (though inappropriate for MI context)
- Patient remains engaged due to her strong intrinsic motivation, not clinician skill

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- **No genuine acceptance or unconditional positive regard**
- **Zero reflective listening or empathic understanding**
- **Expert-driven rather than collaborative approach**
- **Active undermining of patient autonomy through commands and threats**
- **Dismissal of patient's own abundant change talk and motivation**
- **Confrontational response to readiness concerns rather than softening**
- **Fundamental misunderstanding of how behavior change actually works**

**Impact on Patient:**

Despite poor MI technique, the patient maintains 8/10 readiness and does not become overtly defensive. This reflects her strong intrinsic motivation and realistic self-assessment—not the quality of the clinician's approach. However, this session is likely to:
- Damage the therapeutic relationship over time
- Trigger psychological reactance if pressure continues
- Undermine sustainable change (patient fears burnout; clinician insists on high intensity)
- Miss opportunity to strengthen intrinsic motivation
- Confirm patient's concern that clinician does not understand or respect her judgment

**Likelihood of Sustained Behavior Change:** Low to moderate. Patient's strong intrinsic motivation may carry her forward despite poor clinician approach, but lack of collaboration and autonomy support significantly reduces likelihood of sustained, self-directed change.

### Recommendations for Improvement

**1. Fundamental Reorientation to MI Spirit**
- Complete comprehensive MI training with emphasis on four core processes: partnership, compassion, evocation, autonomy support
- Review MI definition and core principles before next clinical session
- Watch recordings of expert MI practitioners and compare to own approach

**2. Replace Prescriptive Language with Collaborative Inquiry**
- Eliminate directive language ("You need to," "You must," "You have to")
- Replace with open-ended questions that invite patient input and expertise
- Practice writing 3-5 open-ended questions before each session

**3. Develop Reflective Listening Skills**
- Learn and practice reflective listening as cornerstone of MI
- Aim for 2:1 ratio of reflections to questions in every session
- Record sessions and count reflections; if fewer than 2 per question, increase listening

**4. Build on Patient Change Talk**
- Amplify and deepen patient's own reasons for change through strategic questions and reflections
- Never contradict or dismiss patient's stated motivations
- Use deepening questions: "Tell me more about that," "How would that change your life?"

**5. Respect and Support Patient Autonomy**
- Recognize patient as expert on their own life and what will work for them
- Support right to choose and emphasize ownership of change process
- Explicitly affirm autonomy at least once per session

**6. Explore and Validate Readiness Concerns**
- Treat readiness concerns as valuable information about what will actually work
- Respond with curiosity and validation rather than pressure
- Ask: "What would help you feel more confident?" "What would make this feel more manageable?"

**7. Use Double-Sided Reflections**
- Honor both patient's motivation for change and concerns about it
- Formula: "On one hand… On the other hand… Both of those make sense."
- Move toward resolution that honors both sides

**8. Eliminate Threat Language and Coercive Tactics**
- Remove language designed to pressure or frighten ("If you don't start now, you'll regret it")
- Replace with supportive language: "I'm confident you can do this"
- Review recordings and rewrite all threat language

**9. Develop Genuine Curiosity**
- Approach each patient with authentic curiosity about their life, values, and concerns
- Ask at least 3 questions about patient's life before offering suggestions
- Remind yourself: "I don't know what will work for this person. I need to ask and listen."

**10. Seek Ongoing MI Training and Supervision**
- Engage in regular clinical supervision with MI-trained supervisor
- Participate in MI workshops and training programs
- Use validated MI assessment tools (MITI—Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity) to measure progress
- Commit to at least one MI training or supervision session per month

---

### Final Recommendation

**The psychiatrist should not continue seeing patients for motivational interviewing until completing comprehensive MI training and demonstrating competence through supervised practice and validated assessment tools (MITI coding).** The current approach is likely to harm rather than help patients' long-term motivation and behavior change. While this particular patient's strong intrinsic motivation may buffer against harm, continued use of this approach with other patients poses significant risk to therapeutic relationships and treatment outcomes.
