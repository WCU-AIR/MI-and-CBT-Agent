### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Autonomy support fundamentally undermined throughout. Language is consistently controlling: "You might want to," "It would probably help if," "You should try," "You might want to schedule those into your day." While softer than direct commands, these phrases communicate that psychiatrist knows what Naomi should do. True autonomy support would use: "What feels right to you?" When Naomi expresses doubt ("I don't know if it'll make a big difference"), psychiatrist does not affirm her right to uncertainty or explore what would build confidence. Client positioned as passive recipient of expert advice rather than autonomous agent.

### Evocation

## **SUMMARY OF CLINICAL FINDINGS**

### **Strengths:**
- Basic courtesy and warmth in opening greeting
- Accurate identification and reflection of presenting symptoms
- Recognition that multiple simultaneous changes can feel overwhelming; attempt to break goals into smaller steps
- Willingness to schedule follow-up appointment
- Maintains generally respectful tone throughout

### **Critical Areas for Improvement:**

**1. Fundamental Departure from MI Principles**
This session represents a significant departure from Motivational Interviewing core principles and spirit. The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, directive stance rather than a collaborative, client-centered approach. This violates the fundamental MI principle that expertise and wisdom about change reside within the client.

**2. Severe Collaboration and Partnership Deficits**
The psychiatrist makes unilateral recommendations without genuinely exploring what Naomi thinks might help or what feels doable. The false attribution of clinician-generated ideas to the client ("you're thinking about making one appointment") misrepresents the collaborative process and violates partnership.

**3. Failure to Evoke Client Motivation**
Multiple instances of change talk are present but not amplified or deepened. The psychiatrist prescribes solutions rather than drawing out Naomi's own reasons for change, which may actually weaken intrinsic motivation by framing change as something the clinician recommends rather than something the client genuinely wants.

**4. Undermined Autonomy Support**
Controlling language ("you might want to," "it would probably help if," "you should try") communicates expert authority rather than supporting the client's right to choose. When Naomi expresses doubt or fear, the psychiatrist prescribes reassurance and advice rather than affirming her autonomy.

**5. Superficial Empathy**
Critical moments where Naomi expresses fear, shame, or ambivalence are not deeply explored. When Naomi reveals "I don't want to feel weak or like I can't handle things on my own," this deserves empathic exploration of core shame about dependence, not redirection to therapy options.

**6. Overwhelming Number of Simultaneous Recommendations**
Seven behavioral changes are prescribed without strategic prioritization or exploration of what feels most important or doable to Naomi. Research shows clients are more likely to succeed when they identify one or two priorities themselves rather than being prescribed multiple changes.

### **Impact on Client Engagement and Change:**

This session is likely to **undermine Naomi's intrinsic motivation** for change and may **reinforce her sense of helplessness**. By positioning the psychiatrist as the expert with solutions and Naomi as a passive recipient of advice, the session communicates that change is something that will be done *to* her rather than something she will actively choose and pursue. This is particularly problematic for a client who already expresses doubt about her ability and fear about change.

Naomi leaves the session with a list of things she "should" do rather than with a sense of her own agency and capacity for change. This is likely to reduce her engagement with treatment and her likelihood of sustained behavior change.

## **PRIORITY RECOMMENDATIONS FOR CLINICAL IMPROVEMENT**

### **HIGHEST PRIORITY: Shift from Expert-Driven to Collaborative Stance**
- Fundamentally reframe your role from expert-with-solutions to collaborative partner
- Before prescribing any recommendation, ask the client what they think might help
- Use open-ended questions that invite client input into problem-solving
- **Example:** Instead of "You might want to try finding a therapist," say: "What are your thoughts about what might help? Have you considered therapy, or are there other ideas you've had?"

### **Evoke Rather Than Install Motivation**
- Recognize that Naomi already has motivation for change (desire to stop feeling anxious, hope that things could get better, openness to therapy)
- Your job is to draw this out and amplify it, not to prescribe solutions
- When change talk appears, ask follow-up questions that deepen it
- **Example:** When Naomi says "I wish I could just... stop feeling that way," respond with: "Tell me more about that wish. What would be different if you felt less anxious?"

### **Reduce Prescriptions; Prioritize with Client Input**
- Instead of recommending seven simultaneous behavioral changes, ask the client what feels most important or doable
- Collaboratively identify one or two priorities that the client feels confident about pursuing
- **Example:** "We've talked about several things that might help. Of these, what feels like the most important place to start for you?"

### **Support Autonomy; Avoid Controlling Language**
- Replace prescriptive language with autonomy-supportive language
- Eliminate phrases like "you might want to," "it would probably help if," "you should try"
- Replace with: "What do you think?" "It's up to you." "What feels right to you?" "You're the expert on your own life."

### **Deepen Empathic Reflections; Explore Emotional Nuance**
- Move beyond factual acknowledgment of symptoms to genuine understanding of internal experience
- When client expresses fear, shame, or ambivalence, reflect the emotion and explore its meaning
- Use complex reflections that capture both content and emotion

### **Develop MI-Specific Skills Through Training and Supervision**
- Seek formal MI training, supervision, or consultation to develop core MI competencies
- Consider attending an MI workshop or working with an MI-trained supervisor
- Review session recordings with focus on identifying missed MI opportunities
- Practice specific MI techniques in role-plays with colleagues or supervisors
