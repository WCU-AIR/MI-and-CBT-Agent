Client positioned as recipient of professional wisdom rather than equal partner. ### Autonomy Support

Prescriptive language throughout: "maybe you might want to think about cutting back," "it would probably help if you tried," "maybe it would be a good idea...even if you don't think it's a problem now," "it could help you avoid any issues later on." ### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Essentially absent. Clinician tells rather than asks, prescribes rather than explores, imposes solutions rather than evoking client's own motivation and wisdom. Never asks "What would be reasons for you to think about your drinking?" or "What matters to you about your health?" or "What are your own concerns?" Instead, clinician provides reasons: "It could help you avoid any issues later on." Does not explore client's values about friendship, fun, social connection, or future—resources that could motivate change from within. Perfect evocation opportunities missed: when client says "I don't usually think about setting limits," clinician prescribes rather than asking "What ideas do you have?" Subtle ambivalence ("I guess if drinking got in the way...maybe I'd think about cutting back," "I guess it helps to talk about it") not explored with curiosity. Client's strengths and resources not elicited.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal change talk cultivation. When change talk emerges, clinician fails to amplify or explore it. Client's desire/reason change talk ("I guess if drinking got in the way of something important...maybe I'd think about cutting back") not explored or amplified; clinician moves to prescribing limits instead. Commitment/ability change talk ("I can try setting a small limit just once") emerges only after repeated clinician pressure, not from genuine evocation; clinician does not explore what prompted willingness or strengthen it. Recognition that reflection is valuable ("I guess it helps to talk about it a bit") not explored or deepened. Clinician fails to ask open questions evoking change talk: "What would be good about cutting back?" "How confident are you?" "What matters to you about your health and future?" When change talk does emerge, it is not reflected and amplified. Sustain talk present throughout but not softened; instead, clinician prescribes solutions, which may actually strengthen resistance.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk handled poorly throughout. Twelve instances of sustain talk present ("I don't think it's a problem," "it's not really a big deal," "it's just something we do sometimes," "I don't see it as a big deal," "everyone's doing it...I'd feel left out," "keeping things the same feels better," "I don't usually think about setting limits," "setting limits seems strict," "it might make it harder to enjoy," "I'm not really convinced planning is necessary," "most of my friends drink," "I'm not sure what else we'd do"). Clinician acknowledges concerns but overrides them with "but" statements rather than exploring with curiosity. When client expresses "setting limits seems strict," clinician does not explore what feels strict or why spontaneity matters; instead repeats prescription. No double-sided reflection, curious exploration, or strategic reframing. Clinician argues against sustain talk or repeats prescriptions rather than skillfully softening resistance. Missed opportunities to ask: "Tell me more about what feels strict?" "What's important about keeping things spontaneous?" "Do you think your friends would still want to hang out if you weren't drinking as much?"

### Overall Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.4/5**

**Fundamental Misalignment with MI Principles:**

This session demonstrates **critical deficiency in Motivational Interviewing competence**. The psychiatrist operates from a traditional medical/expert model (clinician identifies problem → prescribes solution → client complies) rather than an MI model (collaborative exploration → evoking client's own motivation → supporting autonomy). While some MI elements are present (reflective listening, maintaining focus), they are used in service of a directive, expert-driven agenda rather than genuine partnership and client-centered exploration.

**Core Deficiencies:**
- Expert-driven rather than client-centered approach
- Prescriptive rather than evocative methodology
- Dismissive of client's clearly stated perspective
- Pressure for change without collaboration or shared goal-setting
- Minimal empathic understanding; reflections are factual, not emotionally attuned
- No genuine autonomy support; subtle pressure applied throughout
- Minimal skill in evoking, amplifying, or exploring change talk
- Poor management of sustain talk; arguments and prescriptions reinforce resistance

**Impact on Client Engagement:**

**Likely negative outcomes:** Reduced intrinsic motivation (change talk emerges as compliance, not genuine motivation); potential psychological reactance (client becomes more entrenched in status quo); missed opportunities for authentic exploration; superficial compliance without follow-through; damaged therapeutic alliance.

**For this specific client:** Anya is lower-risk with no functional impairment. Psychiatrist's approach—pressuring change despite client's clear statement that no problem exists—is likely to reduce trust, trigger defensiveness, decrease intrinsic motivation, and damage the therapeutic relationship.

**Critical Recommendations (Priority Order):**

1. **Shift from Expert to Partner Stance** — Replace directive language with evocative questions; treat client as expert on her own life; share power and decision-making
2. **Develop Genuine Curiosity** — Ask questions because you want to understand, not to persuade; listen without planning next persuasive statement
3. **Stop Prescribing, Start Evoking** — Replace "you should," "it would help," "maybe you might want to" with "I'm curious," "Tell me more," "What do you think?"
4. **Explicitly Affirm Autonomy** — Use language emphasizing choice: "It's completely up to you," "You get to decide," "What feels right to you?"
5. **Explore Ambivalence With Curiosity** — When client shows mixed feelings, explore both sides; use double-sided reflections; trust that exploration evokes change talk
6. **Amplify Change Talk** — When client expresses any movement toward change, reflect it back, explore it, strengthen it
7. **Soften Sustain Talk Skillfully** — Use curiosity and reframing instead of confrontation; ask questions that help client explore her own concerns
8. **Establish Shared Goals Collaboratively** — Genuinely explore what client wants to work on; listen without redirecting; establish goals reflecting client's priorities
9. **Receive Formal MI Training** — Attend 2-3 day MI workshop; engage in regular supervision with MI-trained clinician; record and review sessions
10. **Cultivate Authentic Curiosity** — Before each session, set intention to genuinely understand client's perspective; notice when planning persuasive statements instead of listening

**Current MI Competency:** Below-basic. While some MI elements present, they serve a directive agenda rather than genuine partnership. Session does not embody MI spirit of partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation.

**Likelihood of Evoking Intrinsic Motivation:** Low. Change talk emerges primarily as compliance with pressure, not genuine internal motivation. Research shows externally motivated change is less likely to be sustained. Dismissal of client's perspective and hierarchical stance may trigger reactance, making client more entrenched in status quo.
