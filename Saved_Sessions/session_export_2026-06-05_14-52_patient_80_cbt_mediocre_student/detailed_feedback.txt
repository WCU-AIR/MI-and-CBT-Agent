
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **Conduct comprehensive diagnostic assessment and case conceptualization** (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
- **Action**: Before proceeding with intervention, conduct a thorough assessment of both diagnoses. For separation anxiety, assess: frequency and intensity of catastrophic thoughts, specific triggers, avoidance behaviors, duration of distress, impact on functioning. For tics, assess: specific tics, triggers (anxiety, fatigue, boredom), suppressibility, impact on functioning, social consequences.
- **Example**: "Ethan, let's talk more about the scary thoughts. When you think about a burglar coming to your house, how often does that thought pop into your head? Does it happen every day? Multiple times a day? And when you have that thought, what do you do? Do you call your mom? Do you try to push the thought away? Do you check that doors are locked?"
- **Practice**: Use a structured assessment tool (e.g., SCARED for anxiety, Yale Global Tic Severity Scale for tics) to systematically evaluate symptoms. Document frequency, intensity, triggers, and maintaining factors.

### 2. **Develop an explicit, evidence-based treatment plan with clear prioritization** (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
- **Action**: Based on assessment, develop a written treatment plan that specifies: (1) primary treatment targets (e.g., separation anxiety as primary, tics as secondary), (2) evidence-based interventions for each (exposure for anxiety, HRT/CBIT for tics), (3) sequence of interventions, (4) expected timeline, (5) how progress will be measured.
- **Example**: "Based on what we've learned, here's our plan: First, we'll work on your scary thoughts about your parents and practice being away from them for short periods. We'll start with small steps, like staying in your room for 5 minutes. As you get more comfortable, we'll increase the time. We'll also work on your tics using a technique called habit reversal training, which helps you notice when a tic is about to happen and do something different instead. Does this plan make sense?"
- **Practice**: Review evidence-based treatment protocols for childhood separation anxiety (e.g., Coping Cat program) and tics (e.g., CBIT manual). Ensure your plan aligns with these protocols.

### 3. **Implement exposure therapy for separation anxiety** (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
- **Action**: Develop a graduated exposure hierarchy with the patient and parents. Start with low-anxiety exposures (e.g., staying in another room for 2 minutes) and gradually progress to higher-anxiety exposures (e.g., attending a sleepover). Conduct exposures in session and assign as homework.
- **Example**: "Let's make a list of situations where you're away from your parents, from least scary to most scary. Maybe staying in your room for a few minutes is a 2 out of 10 scary. Going to school without calling your mom might be a 5 out of 10. Attending a sleepover might be a 9 out of 10. We'll start with the easier ones and work our way up. Each time you do it, your brain learns that nothing bad happens, and the scary feeling gets smaller."
- **Practice**: Conduct in-session exposures (e.g., have the patient stay in the waiting room alone for 5 minutes while you observe). Process the experience afterward. Assign homework exposures with clear instructions and parent involvement.

### 4. **Teach systematic cognitive restructuring with evidence examination** (HIGH PRIORITY)
- **Action**: Rather than simply offering alternative thoughts, teach the patient to examine evidence for and against catastrophic thoughts. Use a thought record or similar tool. Help the patient develop realistic, evidence-based thoughts.
- **Example**: "When you think 'A burglar will come to my house and hurt my parents,' let's look at the evidence. Have burglars ever come to your house? How many burglaries happen in your neighborhood per year? What does your house have to protect it (locks, alarm, neighbors)? What's the actual likelihood? What's a more realistic thought that's still honest?"
- **Practice**: Introduce thought records in session. Have the patient practice identifying automatic thoughts, examining evidence, and developing alternative thoughts. Assign thought records as homework.

### 5. **Implement habit reversal training (HRT) or CBIT for tics** (HIGH PRIORITY)
- **Action**: Teach the patient to: (1) increase awareness of tics and their triggers, (2) identify a competing response (an incompatible behavior), (3) practice the competing response when a tic urge arises. For example, if the tic is shoulder shrugging, the competing response might be pressing shoulders down and tensing arm muscles.
- **Example**: "Let's focus on your shoulder shrugging tic. First, I want you to notice when you're about to shrug or when you feel the urge to shrug. What does it feel like right before? Does your shoulder feel tense? Does it happen more when you're worried? Once you notice the urge, instead of shrugging, you can press your shoulders down and tense your arm muscles for a few seconds. This is called a competing response because you can't shrug and do this at the same time."
- **Practice**: Practice competing responses in session. Have the patient identify triggers and practice the competing response when triggers are present. Assign awareness and competing response practice as homework.

### 6. **Assess and address ritualistic behaviors** (HIGH PRIORITY)
- **Action**: Conduct a thorough assessment of the doorway rituals: frequency, triggers, function (anxiety reduction?), impact on daily life. Determine if these meet criteria for OCD. If so, implement exposure and response prevention (ERP). If not, address as anxiety-maintaining behaviors.
- **Example**: "I noticed in your chart that you touch doorjambs in a certain way. Can you tell me about that? How often do you do it? What happens if you don't do it? Does it help you feel less worried? Let's understand this better so we can help you feel less stuck in these patterns."
- **Practice**: If rituals are present, implement ERP: gradually increase exposure to the trigger (e.g., walking through doorways) while resisting the urge to perform the ritual. Process anxiety reduction over time.

### 7. **Assess and address somatic complaints** (MEDIUM PRIORITY)
- **Action**: Inquire about stomachaches and other physical symptoms. Determine if these are anxiety-related or medical. If anxiety-related, address through exposure and cognitive work. Coordinate with school nurse to reduce reinforcement of health-seeking behavior.
- **Example**: "Your mom mentioned you sometimes complain of stomachaches. When do those happen? Do they happen when you're worried about being away from your parents? Let's keep track of when they happen and what you're thinking or feeling at the time."
- **Practice**: Have the patient monitor physical symptoms and their relationship to anxiety. Gradually reduce visits to the school nurse for reassurance-seeking.

### 8. **Establish clear, specific, measurable homework with systematic review** (HIGH PRIORITY)
- **Action**: Assign one or two specific homework tasks per week, not multiple vague tasks. Provide written instructions. In the next session, systematically review: Was it completed? What worked? What didn't? What barriers existed? Adjust based on feedback.
- **Example**: "This week, I want you to practice one thing: staying in your room alone for 5 minutes while your mom is in the kitchen. You can do this once or twice during the week. Write down when you did it and how worried you felt (0-10 scale). We'll talk about it next week."
- **Practice**: Use a homework log or checklist. Have the patient and parents sign off on completion. In session, review and problem-solve barriers.

### 9. **Actively involve parents in treatment** (HIGH PRIORITY)
- **Action**: Schedule a parent session to: (1) explain the diagnoses and treatment plan, (2) teach parents how to support exposure (not reassure excessively), (3) teach parents how to respond to tics (ignore, don't draw attention), (4) coordinate homework between home and therapy.

Instead, we want to help him learn that he can feel worried and still be okay. So when he asks 'Are you safe?' you might say, 'I'm safe, and I know you're worried. Let's practice your deep breathing together.'"
- **Practice**: Provide parents with written guidelines for supporting exposure and managing tics. Schedule regular parent check-ins.

### 10. **Establish a clear session agenda and structure** (MEDIUM PRIORITY)
- **Action**: At the beginning of each session, collaboratively set an agenda: "Last week we worked on X. Today, I'd like to focus on Y. Does that work for you? Is there anything else you want to talk about?" Allocate time explicitly. Follow the agenda.
- **Example**: "Today we have 45 minutes. I want to spend 15 minutes reviewing your homework from last week, 20 minutes practicing the competing response for your tics, and 10 minutes planning homework for this week. Does that sound okay?"
- **Practice**: Use a written agenda. Check in mid-session: "We're doing well on time. Let's make sure we cover the main points."
