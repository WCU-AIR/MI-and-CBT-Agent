# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Profound absence of unconditional positive regard throughout session. Psychiatrist repeatedly uses confrontational language communicating disapproval ("Look, you have to take this seriously," "Stop making excuses," "you really need to stop thinking like that"). Patient's ambivalence is framed as character flaw rather than normal change process. Conditional support masked as acceptance ("I'm here to support you" immediately followed by "you must decide soon"). No genuine warmth, normalization of difficulty, or affirmation of patient's worth independent of health behaviors.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal reflective listening and no genuine effort to understand patient's internal frame of reference. When patient states "There's stuff going on that you don't see," psychiatrist ignores invitation to deeper understanding and pressures further. When patient says "pushing me like this—doesn't help," feedback is not acknowledged or validated. No complex reflections capturing emotional nuance. Classic empathy-killer used: "I understand you feel that way, but..." True empathy would explore why patient feels this way, not dismiss feelings to redirect to agenda.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Clear target maintained (medication adherence, smoking/drinking reduction) but achieved through repeated directives ("You have to," "You must," "You need to") rather than collaborative exploration. Direction imposed through authority and prescription rather than strategic questioning about patient's own goals. One moment of slightly more strategic thinking when suggesting "cutting back a little" as incremental step, but presented as psychiatrist's directive solution, not collaborative exploration. Lacks progressive clarification of patient's own direction and values.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Authoritarian, expert-driven stance throughout. Psychiatrist positions himself as authority who knows best; patient positioned as someone who needs to comply. Opening statement "I'll be giving you some advice on what you need to do" establishes non-partnership dynamic. Repeated directives shut down dialogue rather than invite it. When patient offers resistance ("I'm managing how I see fit"), treated as obstacle to overcome rather than valuable information. Patient's statement "There's stuff going on that you don't see" is direct invitation to collaborative exploration—completely ignored.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimizes choice and emphasizes external pressure and obligation. Repeated use of controlling language: "You really need to," "You have to," "You must," "You can't just keep ignoring it," "Don't let these feelings hold you back." These statements communicate that change is mandatory and patient's preferences don't matter. When patient expresses doubt, psychiatrist responds with pressure rather than affirming right to make own decision. Conditional autonomy offered ("I'm here to support you whenever you're ready") immediately undermined by "you must decide soon." No statements genuinely supporting autonomy like "This is your decision" or "You know yourself best."

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Almost no evocation demonstrated. Psychiatrist repeatedly tells rather than asks. Provides medical facts ("medications and quitting bad habits are proven to improve health") as reasons for change rather than exploring patient's own reasons. Leading question used ("Don't you want to avoid getting sick?") designed to elicit agreement, not genuine evocation of values. Multiple missed opportunities when patient offers hints about perspective ("There's stuff going on that you don't see," "It's complicated"). No questions designed to elicit change talk exploring patient's desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment to change. External reasons imposed rather than internal motivations drawn out.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Expert stance maintained throughout with no conveyance that wisdom about change resides within patient. Opening explicitly establishes non-partnership: "I'll be giving you some advice on what you need to do." Psychiatrist tells patient what he "needs," corrects his thinking, questions his effort ("Why didn't you try harder?"), dismisses concerns as excuses, prescribes solutions. None of these behaviors convey that patient has knowledge about his own situation. When patient states "There's stuff going on that you don't see," appropriate response would be "Tell me more"—instead psychiatrist ignores and continues prescribing. True partnership would involve asking what support patient needs and co-creating solutions.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill demonstrated. Some questions asked that could theoretically evoke change talk ("Don't you want to avoid getting sick?" and "Can you commit to that?") but these are leading questions designed to elicit agreement, not genuine exploration. Critical missed opportunity: when patient offers "Maybe I could try that... but I'm not promising anything. Let's just say I'll think about it," psychiatrist does not reinforce or develop this movement toward change. No strategic questions asked to evoke change talk (benefits of medication, patient strengths, importance of health, what would change). Psychiatrist tells why patient should change rather than asking questions that evoke patient's own change talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Sustain talk handled poorly throughout with direct confrontation and pressure. Patient's sustain talk ("I'm managing how I see fit," "I don't see the point," "It's easier to keep doing what I'm doing," "I'm not convinced this will change much," "It doesn't feel real") met with confrontational responses ("Stop making excuses," "You can't just keep ignoring it," "You have to make serious commitment"). Direct argumentation against client's position strengthens rather than softens sustain talk—predictable MI principle violation. No skillful MI techniques demonstrated (double-sided reflection, reframing, strategic redirection). By session end, patient's entrenchment increased: "Maybe someday, maybe not"—indicating strengthened resistance rather than movement toward change.

### **Overall Session Score: 1.2/5**

## Summary Assessment

**This session represents a fundamental failure to implement Motivational Interviewing principles.** The psychiatrist relies almost exclusively on confrontation, pressure, authority, and external motivation—the exact opposite of MI. Rather than evoking intrinsic motivation, the psychiatrist tells the patient what to do, argues against resistance, criticizes effort, and pressures compliance. This approach is counterproductive: research demonstrates that confrontation and pressure strengthen resistance and reduce likelihood of behavior change.

### Key Deficiencies:
- Authoritarian stance instead of partnership
- Telling instead of asking
- Confrontation instead of collaboration when handling resistance
- Minimal empathy and reflective listening
- Controlling language instead of autonomy support
- Imposition of reasons instead of evocation of patient's own motivations

### Impact on Patient Engagement:
The session likely **decreased rather than increased** patient motivation for change. Patient moved from "Maybe I could try that" to "Maybe someday, maybe not"—indicating increased entrenchment. Psychiatrist's repeated arguments and pressure activated psychological reactance, strengthening patient's sustain talk. Lack of empathy and dismissal of patient's explicit feedback ("pushing me like this—doesn't help") damaged therapeutic alliance.

### Recommendations:
Formal MI training with qualified trainer, supervised practice with recording feedback, study of MI literature, and reflection on personal beliefs about behavior change and client autonomy are essential for this clinician to develop competency in Motivational Interviewing.
