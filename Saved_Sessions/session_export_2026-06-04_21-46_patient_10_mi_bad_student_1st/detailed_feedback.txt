
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a near-complete absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly uses judgmental language that directly contradicts MI principles. From the opening statement—"I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to quit"—the clinician establishes an authoritarian, non-accepting stance. Throughout the session, the psychiatrist labels the patient's legitimate concerns as "excuses" (used 6+ times), calls her "selfish" (twice), and suggests she is "making excuses" rather than engaging with her actual experience.

Most critically, the psychiatrist invalidates Amina's emotional reality and autonomy. When she expresses fear about failure, the response is "you don't get to say it's complicated" and "you don't get to decide when I'm ready"—language that is actively rejecting of her personhood and perspective. The statement "you need to be a better role model" carries implicit criticism and shame. By the final exchange, the psychiatrist declares "This is not a negotiation," which fundamentally rejects the client's right to be treated as a person of worth deserving collaborative respect.

Amina's responses show clear deterioration in engagement. She moves from initial openness ("I want to quit, I really do") to defensive withdrawal ("I can't say yes or no right now"). Her final statement—"shaming me and calling me selfish isn't helping"—is a direct indictment of the psychiatrist's approach. There is zero affirmation of her strengths (her deep love for her son, her partial reduction in smoking, her willingness to attend), and no normalization of the struggle with addiction.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates fundamentally poor acceptance, using shame, judgment, and rejection throughout. This directly violates MI spirit and damages the therapeutic alliance.

---

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective. This session contains virtually no reflective listening and demonstrates a consistent failure to understand or validate Amina's experience. The psychiatrist never reflects back what Amina has shared about her emotional state, her values, or her context.

When Amina says, "Sometimes smoking feels like the only thing keeping me calm," the psychiatrist responds with commands about nicotine replacement rather than exploring this coping mechanism or understanding its function in her life. When she expresses fear ("I'm scared I won't be able to handle everything if I do"), the response is "you don't get to be afraid and just take action"—a direct dismissal of her emotional reality rather than empathic understanding.

The psychiatrist never acknowledges the co-parenting stress Amina mentions multiple times as a genuine contextual factor. Instead, it's treated as an "excuse" or distraction. There is no attempt to understand how the conflict with her son's father affects her capacity to manage change. A single empathic reflection might have been: "It sounds like you're carrying a lot right now—the stress with your son's father, managing work and parenting, and the weight of knowing smoking isn't good for your son. That's a heavy load, and quitting feels like one more thing that could break you."

Instead, the psychiatrist offers zero complex reflections, zero emotional validation, and zero demonstration of understanding Amina's internal world. The repeated phrase "I get that it feels hard" is superficial acknowledgment, not genuine empathy, and is immediately followed by dismissal ("but you have to...").

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no meaningful empathic engagement. Reflective listening is absent, and the client's emotional and contextual reality is consistently invalidated rather than understood.

---

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

While the psychiatrist maintains a clear focus on smoking cessation (which is appropriate), the *method* of maintaining direction is antithetical to MI. Direction in MI means strategically guiding the conversation toward change goals while remaining client-centered and collaborative. This psychiatrist maintains focus through coercion and prescription rather than collaborative exploration.

The stated direction is appropriate: "Today we're going to talk about your smoking habit." However, the execution immediately becomes problematic with "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to quit"—this is directive imposition, not collaborative direction. A MI-consistent approach would be: "I'd like to explore what quitting smoking might look like for you and what might help you get there."

The psychiatrist does maintain topical focus on smoking and nicotine replacement therapy throughout, which prevents complete aimlessness. However, the psychiatrist never explores Amina's own goals, values, or ideas about change. There is no progressive clarification of direction that emerges from the client's own motivation. Instead, direction is imposed: "You will buy those patches today—no ifs, no buts."

The psychiatrist also misses multiple opportunities to redirect toward change in a collaborative way. When Amina mentions she "cut down some," this is change talk that could be explored and amplified. Instead, it's dismissed: "Cutting down isn't enough." When she expresses love for her son and desire to be present for him, these values could guide direction collaboratively. Instead, they're used as weapons: "Your son's health depends on it."

**Summary:**

Direction is maintained through coercion rather than collaboration. While the topic stays focused, the approach violates MI principles by imposing rather than evoking direction.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, with both bringing valuable knowledge. This session demonstrates zero collaboration. The psychiatrist explicitly rejects collaboration: "This is not a negotiation."

From the opening, the power dynamic is unequal and expert-driven. "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do" positions the psychiatrist as the sole authority and the patient as a passive recipient of instructions. The psychiatrist never asks Amina what she thinks might help, what barriers she anticipates, what has worked before, or what her own ideas are about change.

When Amina attempts to share her perspective ("I need to feel like I'm making that choice because I'm ready"), the psychiatrist responds with dismissal: "No, you don't get to decide when I'm ready." This is not collaboration; it's authoritarian control. The statement "you don't get to say it's complicated" actively silences the client's attempt to co-construct understanding.

True collaboration would involve statements like: "What do you think might help you feel ready to try quitting?" or "You mentioned stress with his father makes this harder—what would need to happen for that to feel more manageable?" or "What worked or didn't work the last time you tried?" Instead, the psychiatrist makes unilateral decisions about what "must" happen.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Fundamental Reorientation: From Expert-Driven to Client-Centered**
- **Action:** Completely restructure the opening and approach to position the client as the expert on her own life and change process. Replace "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do" with genuine curiosity about the client's perspective.
- **Example:** "I'd like to understand what brings you in today and what you're hoping we might explore together. You know yourself better than anyone—what do you think might help?"
- **Practice:** Before each session, write down three open-ended questions designed to understand the client's perspective, values, and ideas. Practice asking these without immediately offering advice. Record yourself and listen for how often you use "you have to" vs. "what do you think?"

### 2. **Develop Genuine Empathic Listening and Reflective Responses**
- **Action:** Learn to pause, listen deeply, and reflect back what you hear—especially the emotional content and underlying values—before offering any guidance or information.
- **Example:** When Amina says "Sometimes smoking feels like the only thing keeping me calm," respond with: "It sounds like smoking has become your main way of managing the stress you're under—especially with everything going on with your son's father. That's been really important for you." Then explore: "What else might help you feel calm and in control?"
- **Practice:** In supervision or role-play, practice reflecting back client statements without adding advice. Aim for at least 3-4 reflections before offering any information. Use the formula: "It sounds like... [emotion/value/concern]. Is that right?"

### 3. **Eliminate Shame-Based Language and Judgmental Framing**
- **Action:** Remove words like "excuses," "selfish," "irresponsible," and "making excuses" from your vocabulary. Replace judgmental questions ("Why didn't you just quit?") with curious, non-judgmental ones.
- **Example:** Instead of "Why didn't you just quit the last time you tried? You have to stop making excuses," say: "Last time you tried to quit, what happened? What made it difficult to stick with it?"
- **Practice:** Review recordings of your sessions and identify every instance of judgmental language. For each one, write an alternative MI-consistent response. Practice these alternatives until they become automatic.

### 4. **Respect and Support Client Autonomy**
- **Action:** Recognize that the client has the right to make her own choices, including the choice not to quit smoking right now. Your role is to support her autonomy while exploring her own motivations for change.
- **Example:** "I hear that you're not ready to commit to buying patches today, and I respect that. What would need to happen for you to feel ready? What would help you feel more confident?"
- **Practice:** In every session, explicitly affirm the client's right to choose. Use language like "It's your decision," "You get to decide," "What feels right to you?" Practice tolerating client ambivalence without trying to force resolution.

### 5. **Evoke Rather Than Impose: Ask About Motivation, Values, and Resources**
- **Action:** Shift from telling the client what to do to asking questions that help her discover her own reasons for change. Focus on her values (love for her son), her goals, and her strengths.
- **Example:** "You mentioned how much you love your son and want to be there for him. Tell me more about what that means to you. What would be different in your relationship with him if you weren't smoking?" or "You've already cut down some—how did you manage that? What does that tell you about your ability to change?"
- **Practice:** Develop a set of evocative questions focused on: (1) values, (2) past successes, (3) reasons for change, (4) confidence/ability, (5) commitment. Practice asking these questions and then listening without interrupting or offering advice.

### 6. **Explore and Validate the Context: Co-Parenting Stress and Life Circumstances**
- **Action:** Rather than dismissing the co-parenting conflict as an "excuse," explore it as a genuine contextual factor that affects her capacity to change. Help her problem-solve around this.
- **Example:** "You've mentioned several times that the stress with your son's father makes this harder. Tell me more about how that affects you. What would help you manage that stress differently? Are there ways you and his father could work together on this, or ways you could get support?"
- **Practice:** In sessions, spend time understanding the client's full context—not just the target behavior. Ask about relationships, stressors, support systems, and barriers. Integrate this understanding into your approach rather than treating it as irrelevant.

### 7. **Use Collaborative Problem-Solving Rather Than Prescription**
- **Action:** When the client expresses hesitation or suggests alternatives (like "smaller steps"), treat these as valuable contributions to problem-solving rather than resistance to overcome.
- **Example:** When Amina says "maybe if we can find a way to break this down into smaller steps," respond with: "That's a really good idea. Tell me more about what smaller steps might look like for you. What would feel manageable?"
- **Practice:** In sessions, when you feel the urge to prescribe or direct, pause and ask instead. Practice phrases like: "What do you think might help?" "What feels doable to you?" "How would you want to approach this?"

### 8. **Strengthen Change Talk Through Amplification and Affirmation**
- **Action:** Actively listen for and reinforce the client's own statements about desire, ability, reasons, and commitment to change. Reflect these back and ask questions that deepen them.
- **Example:** When Amina says "I want to quit, I really do," respond with: "That's important to you. Tell me more about why quitting matters so much. What would be different for you and your son?"
- **Practice:** In supervision, review recordings and identify all instances of change talk. For each one, write down how you could have amplified it. Practice these responses until they become automatic.

### 9. **Develop Skill in Softening Sustain Talk Without Confrontation**
- **Action:** When the client expresses reasons against change (sustain talk), use double-sided reflections and strategic reframing rather than confrontation.
- **Example:** When Amina says "I'm scared I'll fail," respond with: "So you want to quit for your son, and you're also worried that if you try and don't succeed, it might feel even worse. That's a real concern. What would help you feel more confident that you could succeed this time?"
- **Practice:** Develop skill with double-sided reflections: "On one hand... and on the other hand..." Practice reframing sustain talk as information about barriers rather than resistance: "It sounds like stress management is a real challenge. Let's talk about how we might address that."

### 10. **Seek Supervision and Training in Motivational Interviewing**
- **Action:** This session demonstrates fundamental misunderstanding of MI principles. Seek formal MI training (e.g., through MINT—Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers) and regular clinical supervision focused on MI skill development.
- **Example:** Participate in MI workshops, practice with role-play partners, record and review your sessions with an MI-trained supervisor, and use validated MI fidelity measures to assess your progress.
- **Practice:** Commit to ongoing learning. MI is a skill that develops over time with deliberate practice and feedback. Set specific goals for skill development and track progress.
