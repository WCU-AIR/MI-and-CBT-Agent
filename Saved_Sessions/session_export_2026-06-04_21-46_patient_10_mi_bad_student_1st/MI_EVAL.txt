# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates near-complete absence of unconditional positive regard. Uses judgmental language throughout ("excuses" used 6+ times, "selfish" twice). Invalidates patient's emotional reality with statements like "you don't get to say it's complicated" and "you don't get to decide when I'm ready." No affirmation of patient's strengths (deep love for son, partial smoking reduction, treatment attendance). Patient explicitly states: "shaming me and calling me selfish isn't helping." Therapeutic alliance deteriorates from initial openness to defensive withdrawal by session end.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no reflective listening or genuine effort to understand patient's perspective. When patient expresses coping mechanism ("smoking feels like the only thing keeping me calm"), psychiatrist responds with commands rather than exploration. Dismisses emotional reality ("you don't get to be afraid") instead of validating. Never acknowledges co-parenting stress as genuine contextual factor. Zero complex reflections, zero emotional validation. Superficial acknowledgments ("I get that it feels hard") immediately followed by dismissal. Fails to demonstrate understanding of patient's internal world or lived experience.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Maintains clear focus on smoking cessation as change goal, preventing session drift. However, direction is imposed through coercion rather than collaboration. Opening statement "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to quit" establishes directive imposition rather than collaborative exploration. Never explores patient's own goals, values, or ideas about change. Misses opportunities to amplify change talk (e.g., "cut down some" dismissed rather than explored). Uses patient's values as weapons rather than collaborative guides. Direction maintained through control, not partnership.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Demonstrates zero genuine collaboration. Explicitly rejects collaboration: "This is not a negotiation." Power dynamic unequal and expert-driven from opening. Never asks patient what she thinks might help, what barriers she anticipates, or what her ideas are about change. When patient attempts to share perspective ("I need to feel like I'm making that choice because I'm ready"), psychiatrist responds with dismissal: "No, you don't get to decide when I'm ready." Patient's suggestion for "smaller steps" never explored. Unilateral decisions imposed throughout. Patient's attempts to collaborate systematically shut down.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Actively undermines autonomy at every turn. Uses controlling language throughout: "you have to," "you must," "you will," "you need to." Explicitly denies client's right to self-determination: "No, you don't get to decide when I'm ready" and "This is not a negotiation." Denies right to express experience: "you don't get to say it's complicated." Uses guilt and moral judgment to coerce compliance rather than support autonomous choice: "you're just being selfish and putting everyone at risk." Patient's explicit request for autonomy ("I need to feel like I'm making the choice for myself") ignored. Pressure increased rather than honored.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Demonstrates almost no evocation. Patient's change talk and values ignored: "I love my son dearly," "I want to be around for him," "I've cut down some," "I want to quit, I really do," "I want to be a better parent" all go unexamined. Never asks open-ended questions to evoke motivation. Uses closed, rhetorical, accusatory questions instead: "Why didn't you just quit the last time?" and "Why haven't you done this already?" Fails to evoke patient's own resources and problem-solving capacity. When patient suggests "smaller steps," this wisdom is dismissed rather than explored. Imposes solutions rather than drawing out patient's own wisdom.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Demonstrates opposite of partnership. Assumes expert role exclusively and actively prevents client from influencing session. Opening statement positions psychiatrist as sole expert, patient as passive recipient. Unilateral decisions made without consulting patient's perspective. Explicit rejection of partnership: "This is not a negotiation," "You don't get to decide when I'm ready," "You don't get to say it's complicated," "No, you don't get to be afraid." Patient's attempts to contribute dismissed without exploration. Patient explicitly requests partnership ("I need your support in helping me get there, not just commands"), which is ignored. By session end, patient has withdrawn from partnership: "I can't say yes or no right now."

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Fails to evoke, amplify, or reinforce change talk. Multiple instances of change talk go unrecognized: desire ("I want to quit, I really do"), ability ("I've cut down some"), reasons ("I love my son dearly"), and need ("smoking is harmful"). Instead of amplifying, psychiatrist dismisses ("Cutting down isn't enough"), ignores, or responds to hesitation with pressure rather than support. Uses shame to force compliance rather than strengthen intrinsic motivation. Creates defensive environment where patient becomes less likely to express change talk. Patient's language shifts from "I want to quit" to "I can't promise" and "I need to find my own way." Pressured, shaming approach actively suppresses patient's own motivation for change.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill in handling sustain talk. Confronts rather than softens patient's arguments for status quo. Patient expresses sustain talk ("stress makes it tough," "I'm scared I'll fail"), and psychiatrist responds with direct confrontation: "Why didn't you just quit? Stop making excuses," "Don't let stress be your excuse," "You don't get to be afraid." This confrontational approach triggers defensiveness and strengthens sustain talk rather than softening it. Patient becomes increasingly entrenched in concerns. No double-sided reflections or strategic reframing attempted. Maintains some focus on change goal (prevents score of 1), but confrontational approach likely increases rather than decreases resistance. Dismisses sustain talk as "excuses" and "nonsense" rather than exploring function.

**Overall Session MI Fidelity Score: 1.1/5**

**Session Embodies MI Spirit:** ☐ Yes     ☒ **No**

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation for Change:** ☐ High     ☐ Moderate     ☒ **Very Low**

---

### Critical Findings

**Strengths:**
- Maintains consistent focus on smoking cessation as target behavior
- Correctly identifies serious health risks for client and child
- Demonstrates persistence with change goal

**Fundamental Deficiencies:**
- Complete absence of acceptance and empathy
- Authoritarian rather than collaborative approach
- Failure to evoke intrinsic motivation
- Confrontational handling of resistance
- Systematic violation of MI spirit (control vs. partnership, judgment vs. compassion)

---

### Clinical Impact

**This session is actively counterproductive.** Patient enters with genuine intrinsic motivation ("I love my son dearly, and I really want the best for him") and willingness to explore change. By session end, patient has withdrawn from engagement ("I can't say yes or no right now") and explicitly stated psychiatrist's approach is harmful ("shaming me and calling me selfish isn't helping").

**Predicted Outcome:** Very low likelihood of genuine quit attempt. If attempted, will be in response to external pressure rather than intrinsic motivation, predicting poor long-term success. More likely: patient disengages from treatment and becomes more entrenched in ambivalence.

**Damage to Therapeutic Alliance:** Significant. Patient has experienced shame, invalidation, and rejection of autonomy. Future engagement will occur with defensiveness and distrust rather than openness.

---

### Immediate Recommendations

1. **Fundamental reorientation:** Shift from expert-driven to client-centered practice
2. **Develop genuine empathic listening:** Practice reflective responses before offering guidance
3. **Eliminate shame-based language:** Remove judgmental framing entirely
4. **Respect and support autonomy:** Recognize client's right to choose
5. **Evoke rather than impose:** Ask questions that help client discover own reasons for change
6. **Explore context:** Validate co-parenting stress as genuine barrier, not excuse
7. **Use collaborative problem-solving:** Treat client suggestions as valuable contributions
8. **Amplify change talk:** Actively reinforce client's own statements about desire and ability
9. **Soften sustain talk:** Use double-sided reflections rather than confrontation
10. **Seek formal MI training:** Participate in MINT-certified training and clinical supervision

---

### Competency Assessment

**Current MI Competency Level:** Pre-MI / Directive Expert-Driven Practice

**Recommendation:** Psychiatrist should not continue seeing clients for behavior change work until MI competency is developed through formal training and supervised practice.
