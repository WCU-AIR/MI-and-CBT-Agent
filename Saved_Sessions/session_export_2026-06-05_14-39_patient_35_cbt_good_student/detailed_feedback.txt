
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

This document outlines a structured framework for assessing therapist competence in Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT). 
It is used for training, supervision, and evaluation. Each item is scored on a 0–6 scale:

0 = Poor
2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Develop a Comprehensive Treatment Plan Addressing All Symptoms and the Medical-Psychiatric Interface**
- **Action:** Before the next session, create a written case formulation that explicitly addresses: (1) How Parkinson's disease contributes to each symptom (fatigue, psychomotor retardation, sleep disturbance), (2) How depression contributes to each symptom (anhedonia, social withdrawal, concentration difficulties), (3) How these interact, (4) Which symptoms are most amenable to behavioral vs. pharmacological vs. medical intervention, (5) Coordination needs with neurology.
- **Example:** "George's fatigue appears to have both neurological (Parkinson's disease) and psychiatric (depression) components. Behavioral activation alone may not address the neurological fatigue, so we should discuss with his neurologist whether medication adjustments might help. Meanwhile, we can work on energy conservation strategies and activity pacing to manage the fatigue he does have."
- **Practice:** Review the case formulation with a supervisor or colleague. Practice articulating the medical-psychiatric interface to patients in clear, non-technical language.

### 2. **Explicitly Address Fatigue with Specific Interventions**
- **Action:** In the next session, prioritize fatigue as a treatment target. Introduce specific fatigue management strategies: (1) Energy conservation (pacing activities, taking breaks), (2) Activity scheduling with built-in rest periods, (3) Sleep hygiene optimization, (4) Discussion of whether medication adjustments might help.
- **Example:** "You mentioned fatigue is a major issue. Let's work on this together. One strategy is to break activities into smaller chunks with rest periods—instead of trying to do a whole activity, you might do 10 minutes, rest 10 minutes, do another 10 minutes. Another is to make sure you're getting good sleep. Let's talk about your sleep and see if there are changes we can make."
- **Practice:** Learn and practice energy conservation and activity pacing techniques. Review literature on fatigue management in Parkinson's disease and depression.

### 3. **Use Written CBT Tools to Support Learning and Retention**
- **Action:** Introduce a simple thought record or worksheet that George can use to track thoughts, evidence, and alternative thoughts. Provide a written copy to take home. For older adults with cognitive deficits, written materials significantly enhance learning.
- **Example:** Create a one-page worksheet with columns: "Situation," "Thought," "Evidence For," "Evidence Against," "Alternative Thought," "What I'll Do." Have George complete this together during session and then use it for homework.
- **Practice:** Develop templates for thought records, behavioral experiments, and activity schedules tailored to older adults. Practice explaining these tools clearly and simply.

### 4. **Frame Homework as Systematic Behavioral Experiments with Data Collection**
- **Action:** Explicitly teach George how to conduct a behavioral experiment. Discuss the hypothesis ("Watching won't help me feel better"), the prediction ("I'll feel the same or worse"), the experiment (watch for 10-15 minutes), and the data collection (notice how you feel before, during, after).
- **Example:** "Let's set up an experiment to test your thought. Your hypothesis is 'Watching won't make me feel any better.' Before you watch, I want you to notice how you're feeling—maybe rate your mood 0-10. Then watch for 10-15 minutes and notice what happens. After, rate your mood again. Write these down so we can look at the data together next time. This will help us see whether your thought is completely true or if there's some relief, even if it's small."
- **Practice:** Practice explaining behavioral experiments to patients. Role-play with colleagues to develop clear, simple language.

### 5. **Develop Contingency Plans for Homework Obstacles**
