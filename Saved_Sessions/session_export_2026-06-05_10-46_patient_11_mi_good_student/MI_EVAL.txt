# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance with repeated affirmations of Amina's strengths ("Your confidence in your abilities shines through clearly," "Your commitment to finding a way forward is truly admirable"). Validates emotional experience and normalizes competing demands. However, could deepen acceptance by more explicitly affirming her caution about childcare resources as wisdom rather than hesitation, and by capturing the specific emotional weight of her unique situation rather than generic validation.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates strong reflective listening with accurate, multi-layered reflections that capture both content and emotion ("the unpredictability of your kids' needs and the lack of reliable childcare really create a tough, uncertain environment that makes stepping into work feel risky and stressful"). Effectively synthesizes concerns into coherent emotional pictures. Misses opportunities to explore deeper emotional significance—particularly the desire for personal identity ("something just for me") and the fear of being let down by resources. Reflections are solid but occasionally remain at surface level rather than deepening into personal meaning.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintains clear, strategic focus on employment barriers and childcare solutions with logical session progression: validate accomplishment → explore barriers → evoke motivation → assess importance → identify past strengths → introduce resources → develop concrete next steps. Direction is appropriate and intentional. Could be strengthened by more explicitly connecting action steps (making a list, calling places) back to the original goal of attending the next job interview, which would enhance directional coherence and help client see the connection between small steps and meaningful change.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates genuine collaborative stance with consistent invitations for Amina's input ("What do you think would need to change?" "Would it be okay if I shared some information?" "Is there a particular type of childcare support you're most interested in learning about?"). Seeks permission and invites preferences about information-sharing. However, could strengthen collaboration by first exploring Amina's existing knowledge and past attempts before offering expert information ("What have you already tried? What resources are you aware of?"). This would honor her expertise and prevent positioning psychiatrist as sole source of solutions.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently respects Amina's right to choose with permission-seeking language and affirmation of her thoughtful approach ("Taking the time to explore options carefully shows your thoughtful approach and commitment"). Avoids prescriptive language and frames next steps as her choice. Could be more explicit by positioning all options—including pausing or reconsidering—as genuinely available. The phrase "maybe with some support" subtly implies support is expected rather than purely optional. Could strengthen by explicitly stating: "What feels right to you? Some find support helpful, but what would work best for you?"

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates basic evocation through open questions about past strengths ("have there been times when you managed to balance competing demands?") and motivations ("what do you feel most hopeful about?"). However, significantly misses opportunities to deeply explore Amina's own reasons for change, values, and creative solutions. When Amina expresses confidence at "10," psychiatrist affirms but doesn't ask "What specifically makes you so sure?" When she mentions wanting "something just for me," psychiatrist doesn't explore this deeper motivation. Most critically, introduces childcare resources before fully evoking Amina's own ideas about solutions. Should ask: "What ideas do you have about handling unpredictability?" before offering expert suggestions. This would honor her resourcefulness and increase intrinsic motivation.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates partial partnership with some collaborative moments but frequently assumes expert role that subtly undermines true power-sharing. While psychiatrist asks permission and invites input, overall structure positions psychiatrist as knowledgeable guide and Amina as recipient of guidance. When introducing resources, frames psychiatrist as expert source ("There are a few local programs...") rather than inviting co-exploration. Doesn't consistently invite Amina to recognize and articulate her own strengths—presents them as psychiatrist's observations rather than Amina's self-recognition. True partnership would involve: "You mentioned managing doctor's appointments and school runs. What does that tell you about your own abilities?" This allows Amina to own her strengths rather than receive them as assessment.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates basic cultivation of change talk through open questions that elicit strong statements (confidence at "10," importance at "10," "I really want to find a way through it"). However, fails to strategically amplify or deepen this language. When Amina states confidence at "10," psychiatrist affirms but doesn't ask follow-up questions that would strengthen ability talk. When she expresses commitment ("I have to find a way"), psychiatrist doesn't reflect back and reinforce this powerful statement. Misses critical opportunity to ask: "What would it mean for you to have that childcare in place? How would that change things?" This would elicit change talk about benefits of solving the problem. Cultivation is present but significantly below level needed for skillful MI practice. Should strategically use follow-up questions to deepen commitment, ability, and reasons for change.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates generally skillful handling of barriers and sustain talk through validation, reframing, and double-sided reflections. Effectively softens concerns by: (1) validating as legitimate rather than dismissing ("Taking the time to explore options carefully shows your thoughtful approach"), (2) reframing barriers as solvable ("having access to emergency or drop-in childcare would really ease some of the unpredictability"), (3) using double-sided reflections that acknowledge both barrier and capability. When Amina expresses caution about childcare resources, psychiatrist validates without pressure. Could be more sophisticated by explicitly reframing caution as strength: "That same careful thinking is going to help you find the right solution." This would soften sustain talk while strengthening ability talk and client confidence.

---

### **Overall MI Competency Assessment**

**Session Score: 3.7/5 – Adequate MI Competence with Notable Room for Improvement**

**Summary:**

This session demonstrates adequate MI competence with a warm, accepting foundation and solid reflective listening. The psychiatrist establishes clear direction and appropriate collaboration on the surface. However, the session falls short of skillful MI practice in critical areas: **insufficient evocation of client motivation and resources, minimal strategic amplification of change talk, and partial rather than full partnership**. Most significantly, the psychiatrist introduces expert information and resources relatively early without fully exploring Amina's own ideas, past attempts, and creative solutions. This reduces intrinsic motivation and positions the psychiatrist as the expert problem-solver rather than Amina as the expert on her own life.

**Strengths:**
- ✓ Strong acceptance and warmth with consistent affirmations
- ✓ Solid reflective listening with accurate, multi-layered reflections
- ✓ Clear direction and logical session progression
- ✓ Genuine collaboration on surface level with permission-seeking
- ✓ Effective softening of sustain talk through validation and reframing
- ✓ Appropriate autonomy support with choice-affirming language

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- ✗ Significantly increase evocation of client's own motivations, ideas, and resources before offering expert information
- ✗ Strategically amplify change talk through follow-up questions that deepen commitment, ability, and reasons for change
- ✗ Strengthen true partnership by explicitly inviting client to recognize and articulate own expertise and strengths
- ✗ Deepen reflections to explore emotional meaning and personal values rather than remaining at surface level
- ✗ Reduce expert positioning and increase collaborative problem-solving throughout session

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation for Change: Moderate**

Amina leaves with concrete next steps and affirmed importance/confidence. However, because the psychiatrist didn't fully evoke her own ideas and solutions, her sense of ownership and intrinsic motivation may be somewhat limited. She may follow through because the psychiatrist suggested it and she's cooperative, but may not feel as deeply invested as if she had generated solutions herself. With recommended improvements—particularly increased evocation and strategic amplification of change talk—this psychiatrist could significantly enhance client motivation and likelihood of sustained change.
