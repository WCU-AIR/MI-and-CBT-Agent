# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal acceptance demonstrated in early exchanges with confrontational language ("Look, you really need to stop making excuses"). Significant lapses in unconditional positive regard. Notable improvement beginning in fourth exchange with validating statements ("Thanks for sharing how you're feeling about quitting"). Early judgment and dismissal of client's perspective represents critical MI violation, though psychiatrist demonstrates capacity for recovery and improved acceptance in later exchanges.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Largely absent in first three exchanges with minimal reflective listening and failure to understand client's perspective. Closed, judgmental questions ("Why haven't you tried quitting yet if you know the risks?") demonstrate lack of empathic exploration. Turning point in fourth exchange with basic reflections ("It sounds like you're considering making a change but want to do it in a way that feels manageable for you"). Later reflections show improvement but lack depth and complexity expected of skilled MI practice. Missed opportunities to explore emotional nuance and ambivalence regarding stress management and peer dynamics.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Problematic and inconsistent with MI principles in first three exchanges. Psychiatrist imposes specific direction (immediate cessation) without exploring client's goals or readiness. Prescriptive statements ("you need to stop right now before it ruins your lungs") violate collaborative principle. Direction improves from fourth exchange onward with exploratory questions ("Can you tell me more about what makes quitting when basketball season starts a good time for you?"). Early directiveness represents significant MI violation; later exchanges show appropriate collaborative goal-setting without imposing solutions.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Severely compromised in first three exchanges with expert, authoritarian stance ("You have to take responsibility here"). Psychiatrist tells rather than asks, prescribes rather than explores. Closed questions assume answers rather than invite genuine inquiry. Client's defensive response ("I'm not just making excuses") indicates collaboration has been damaged. Significant improvement from fourth exchange with validating language and genuine invitations for input ("What do you think you could start doing now?"). Later exchanges demonstrate adequate collaboration but lack explicit affirmation of client's expertise and full partnership equality.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Critically deficient throughout first three exchanges—one of the most serious MI violations in this session. Multiple instances of pressure and prescription that remove client choice: "you need to stop right now," "You have to take responsibility," "Are you going to quit today, or are you going to keep putting your health and future at risk?" Client's reassertion of autonomy ("I'm gonna do it on my terms so it sticks") indicates defensive response to pressure. Substantial improvement from fourth exchange with questions affirming agency ("What do you think you could start doing now?"). Early violations represent critical deficiency risking reactance and undermining intrinsic motivation.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Largely absent in first three exchanges; psychiatrist tells why client should change rather than asking client to explore own reasons. First genuine evocative question appears in fourth exchange ("Can you tell me more about what makes quitting when basketball season starts a good time for you?"), which successfully elicits rich change talk about basketball performance and focus. Subsequent questions effectively draw out client's ideas about preparation steps and trigger identification. However, missed opportunities for deeper value-based evocation regarding identity as athlete and core values. Client's intrinsic motivation exists independent of psychiatrist's early efforts.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Severely compromised in first three exchanges with expert-driven stance positioning client as problem to be fixed ("you really need to stop making excuses," "You have to take responsibility here"). Language diminishes client's perspective and implies irresponsibility. Client's defensive response indicates partnership has been damaged. Improvement from fourth exchange with validating language ("Thanks for sharing how you're feeling") and collaborative questions. Later exchanges show adequate partnership but could strengthen through explicit acknowledgment of client's expertise. Early violations represent critical breach of MI principles undermining collaborative spirit.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Change talk present but inconsistently cultivated. Client generates substantial change talk independently ("I was thinking to not use when basketball season starts," "I play better when I'm not high") even when psychiatrist not skillfully evoking it. Critical missed opportunity in early exchanges to recognize and amplify emerging commitment. From fourth exchange onward, psychiatrist begins cultivating change talk more effectively through evocative questions. Client readily articulates own reasons for change when asked. However, psychiatrist misses opportunities to explicitly reinforce and amplify change talk. Strategic questioning about ability, need, and commitment could be employed more consistently.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk handled poorly in early exchanges through direct confrontation ("Vaping is harmful, and you should quit right now"), which strengthens rather than softens client's arguments for status quo. Client elaborates sustain talk in response to pressure, indicating confrontational approach has backfired. From fourth exchange onward, improved handling through evocative questioning and collaborative planning. Psychiatrist could employ more sophisticated techniques such as double-sided reflections to validate sustain talk while maintaining focus on change goals. By final exchanges, client's sustain talk diminishes as focus shifts to change talk and concrete planning, suggesting later approach has been effective.

---

### Overall Session Assessment

**Current MI Competency Level:** Basic to Intermediate (Inconsistent Application)

**Session Strengths:**
- Demonstrates capacity for self-correction and adaptation mid-session
- Later evocative questioning effectively elicits client's own ideas and motivations
- Client remains engaged despite early MI violations, suggesting underlying therapeutic alliance
- Collaborative goal-setting aligns with client's timeline and preferences
- Successfully identifies barriers (stress, peer influence) and explores alternative coping strategies

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- Early confrontation and directiveness violate core MI principles and risk triggering reactance
- Minimal acceptance and empathy in first half of session
- Failure to recognize and amplify early change talk represents missed opportunity to strengthen intrinsic motivation
- Inconsistent autonomy support throughout session
- Limited use of sophisticated MI techniques (double-sided reflections, reframing, strategic amplification)
- Shallow exploration of client's core values and identity as athlete

**Impact on Client Engagement and Change:**
Client's sustained engagement suggests intrinsic motivation tied to basketball has carried the session despite early missteps. However, early confrontation likely reduced depth of commitment and triggered defensive motivation ("I'm gonna do it on my terms so it sticks"). Later collaborative work more likely to support sustained change, but session would have been significantly more effective with consistent MI practice throughout.

**Likelihood of Evoking Sustained Client Motivation:** Moderate. Client has identified concrete preparation steps and enlisted peer support, which are positive indicators. However, early violations have likely compromised full intrinsic motivation. With improved later approach, reasonable likelihood of sustained change exists, but optimal outcomes would require consistent MI practice from session start.

---

### Recommendations for Clinician Development

**Priority 1:** Eliminate confrontational, prescriptive language from session start. Replace with open exploratory questions that invite client to articulate own concerns and motivations.

**Priority 2:** Implement consistent empathic reflective listening from beginning of session. Reflect both content and emotion; use complex reflections demonstrating deep understanding.

**Priority 3:** Recognize and amplify early change talk immediately through reflection and follow-up questions to strengthen emerging motivation.

**Priority 4:** Maintain consistent autonomy support throughout session using language that affirms client's right to choose and emphasizes agency.

**Priority 5:** Seek supervision with MI-trained clinician. Record and review sessions to identify specific moments of MI deviation and practice alternative responses.
