## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths
- Maintains consistent focus on specific health behaviors requiring attention
- Identifies concrete, measurable targets for change (smoking, exercise, credit card reduction, gambling)
- Demonstrates persistence and genuine concern for patient outcomes

### Critical Areas for Improvement

**1. Fundamental Departure from MI Principles**
This session violates core MI principles. The psychiatrist operates from a directive, expert-authority model that positions the patient as deficient rather than as a capable person struggling with ambivalence. This approach directly undermines intrinsic motivation for change.

**2. Complete Absence of Evocation**
The most critical deficiency is the failure to evoke the patient's own motivations and values. The patient explicitly states "I wanna travel, find someone special, maybe have a family someday—so I guess that kinda means I've gotta start making changes," yet the psychiatrist never explores or strategically links these values to behavior change. This represents a fundamental missed opportunity.

**3. Failure to Validate Ambivalence**
When the patient expresses legitimate concerns (fear of isolation, feeling overwhelmed, worry about losing identity), these are dismissed rather than validated. The psychiatrist responds to hesitation with pressure ("you just have to push through the fear") rather than exploration and support. This creates defensiveness rather than openness.

**4. Elimination of Autonomy and Partnership**
The patient is never invited to co-create solutions or determine implementation strategies. All decisions are made unilaterally by the psychiatrist. This removes the patient's sense of ownership and control, which are essential for sustained behavior change.

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change

**First Session Outcome:** Patient offers tentative compliance ("I'll cut up the cards and try to avoid those places"), but this appears to be compliance with authority rather than genuine commitment to personal values.

**Second Session Outcome:** Patient has returned to defensive minimization ("The pokies feel like just a bit of cash, a bit of fun, so I tend to downplay how bad it really is"). Change talk has diminished, sustain talk has increased, and the patient's tone has shifted from tentative commitment to defensiveness.

**Likelihood of Sustained Change:** Low. Research demonstrates that externally imposed change (driven by pressure and authority) is less likely to be sustained than internally motivated change (driven by personal values). The psychiatrist's approach has likely activated psychological reactance—the natural human tendency to resist when autonomy is threatened.

## Recommendations for Improvement

### Priority 1: Shift from Expert-Authority to Collaborative Partnership
Replace prescriptive language ("you need to," "you have to") with exploratory language ("I'm curious about," "Tell me more about," "What do you think"). Fundamentally reframe your role from expert-who-knows-what-the-patient-needs to collaborative partner-who-helps-the-patient-discover-their-own-solutions.

### Priority 2: Develop Genuine Evocation Skills
Learn to identify and strategically amplify the patient's own motivations, values, and reasons for change. When the patient mentions a value (travel, family, meaningful relationships), ask follow-up questions that deepen this and explicitly link it to current behavior. This is the core of MI.

### Priority 3: Validate Ambivalence Rather Than Pressuring
When the patient expresses hesitation, fear, or ambivalence, respond with validation and exploration rather than pressure. Ambivalence is normal and expected. Your job is to help the patient explore both sides and move toward change at their own pace.

### Priority 4: Use Reflective Listening to Demonstrate Genuine Empathy
Develop the skill of accurately reflecting back what the patient is saying, particularly the emotional content. This demonstrates that you understand and care about the patient's perspective, not just the behavior you want to change.

### Priority 5: Ask Open-Ended Questions to Evoke Change Talk
Replace closed questions ("Are you planning to exercise?") and rhetorical/accusatory questions ("Why haven't you quit before?") with genuine open-ended questions designed to elicit the patient's own reasons for change. Aim for questions beginning with "What," "How," "Tell me about," "Help me understand."

### Priority 6: Support Autonomy by Offering Choice and Collaborative Problem-Solving
Instead of telling the patient what to do, ask the patient what they think might work. Invite the patient to problem-solve barriers collaboratively. Use phrases like: "What do you think?" "What feels right to you?" "What barriers do you anticipate, and how might we work around them?"

### Priority 7: Soften Sustain Talk Through Double-Sided Reflections
When the patient expresses reasons for not changing, use double-sided reflections that acknowledge both the function of the current behavior and the patient's goals for change. Avoid confrontation, which typically strengthens sustain talk rather than softening it.

### Priority 8: Affirm and Build on Patient's Own Ideas
When the patient offers ideas about change or expresses commitment, affirm these and build on them rather than criticizing them as insufficient. This strengthens intrinsic motivation. Never criticize the patient's ideas as insufficient or inadequate.

### Priority 9: Attend Formal MI Training
This session demonstrates significant departures from MI principles. Consider attending a formal Motivational Interviewing training workshop (2-3 days) or seeking ongoing MI supervision with an MI-trained supervisor. MI is a learnable skill requiring deliberate practice and feedback.

### Priority 10: Shift Your Conceptualization of Your Role
You are not the expert who knows what the patient needs. You are a facilitator who helps the patient discover their own reasons for change and their own solutions. The patient is the expert on their own life. Assume the patient is doing the best they can with the resources they have and that they possess wisdom about their own life that you don't have access to.

## Clinical Implications

**Current Trajectory:** Without significant changes in approach, the likelihood of sustained behavior change is low. The patient's second-session responses indicate that the directive approach has activated defensiveness and reduced intrinsic motivation.

**Potential with MI-Consistent Approach:** If the psychiatrist shifts to genuine MI principles—particularly evocation, validation of ambivalence, and autonomy support—the patient's own stated values (travel, family, meaningful relationships) could become powerful motivators for change. The patient has already demonstrated insight ("I guess that kinda means I've gotta start making changes") that could be strategically amplified through MI techniques.

**Most Critical Next Step:** In the next session, focus on genuine evocation. Ask the patient about her dreams (Egypt, family, meaningful relationships) and explore how current behaviors affect those dreams. Let the patient draw her own connections between values and behavior change. This shift alone could significantly improve outcomes.
