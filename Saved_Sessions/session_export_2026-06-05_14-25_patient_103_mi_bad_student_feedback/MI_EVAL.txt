# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no unconditional positive regard or nonjudgmental stance. Language consistently conveys disapproval and moral condemnation ("You're wrong to think it's not a big deal," "You have to stop making excuses"). Milan's perspective is labeled as incorrect rather than explored with curiosity. No affirmation of Milan's strengths, autonomy, or worth is provided. The repeated use of authoritarian language creates a punitive rather than supportive tone, fundamentally inconsistent with MI's spirit of acceptance.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal reflective listening and frequent dismissal of Milan's emotional reality. When Milan explicitly states feeling unheard ("It feels like everyone's just telling me what to do without listening"), the psychiatrist responds with pseudo-reflection followed by immediate dismissal. When Milan shares vulnerable disclosure about social pressure and fear of exclusion, the psychiatrist responds with surface-level advice rather than exploring the emotional core. No complex reflections capture Milan's ambivalence, fear of losing friends, or developmental struggle. Empathy is largely absent throughout the session.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains focus on alcohol use but in an imposed, rigid manner rather than strategically guiding Milan toward his own motivation. The target is overly rigid (complete abstinence) without exploring Milan's own goals or values. When Milan resists, the psychiatrist doubles down rather than adjusting strategy. One open question is asked ("Can you tell me about a recent time when you felt that pressure to drink?"), showing brief strategic thinking, but this is immediately undermined by advice-giving. The session lacks a coherent strategic plan beyond forcing compliance with the clinician's predetermined solution.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist operates from a rigid expert-driven, authoritarian stance throughout. Language is consistently unilateral ("You have to," "You must," "You need to"). When Milan requests collaboration ("Can't we talk about why I drink at all before just saying I have to stop?"), the psychiatrist acknowledges superficially but continues the same directive pattern. True collaboration—asking Milan what he thinks, seeking his input on goals, co-constructing solutions—never occurs. The power dynamic is entirely one-directional: clinician as expert with answers, Milan as compliant recipient. This is the antithesis of collaboration.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist actively undermines autonomy throughout. Repeated "you have to," "you must," and "you need to" language communicates that Milan has no choice. When Milan states his own assessment of what is realistic, the psychiatrist dismisses it: "You have to have the willpower to make this change... You need to commit to quitting now; there's no time for 'thinking it over.'" Milan's right to choose is not respected, and his ownership of the change process is actively discouraged. No affirmation of Milan's freedom to decide or support for his autonomy is provided.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no evocation. External reasons for change (health risks, future problems) are imposed rather than Milan's own motivations being drawn out. No questions explore what Milan thinks, values, or what might motivate him. When Milan generates change talk ("I guess I'm not exactly proud of the way I've been drinking"), the psychiatrist immediately pivots to directive language rather than amplifying this self-generated motivation. The one open question asked is not followed by evocative exploration. Milan's own wisdom is never sought or valued.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist actively assumes the expert role for the entire interaction, positioning himself as the authority who knows what Milan needs. Language reflects hierarchical, non-partnership stance throughout ("You have to," "You must," "You need to"). When Milan explicitly requests partnership, the psychiatrist briefly engages but then reverts to the expert role. No acknowledgment that Milan understands his own situation better than the clinician, no genuine collaborative exploration, and no treatment of Milan's ideas as having equal weight. Partnership is entirely absent.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist imposes change talk rather than evoking it. When Milan generates potential change talk ("I'm not exactly proud of the way I've been drinking" and "Maybe I could try backing off a little"), the psychiatrist fails to amplify or explore it. Instead, the clinician dismisses Milan's own change language: "No, no, you're not getting it... There's no room for maybe." The psychiatrist actively discourages Milan's own change language while imposing external change talk through directive statements. This approach is ineffective and triggers defensiveness rather than motivation.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist directly confronts sustain talk rather than skillfully softening it, which predictably strengthens Milan's resistance. When Milan expresses reasons for not changing ("I really don't think it's that bad," "I'm not ready to quit completely"), the psychiatrist responds with direct confrontation ("You're wrong," "You have to stop making excuses"). No double-sided reflections, reframing, or strategic redirection is used. By the end of the session, Milan's sustain talk has intensified, indicating that the confrontational approach has backfired and increased defensiveness.

## Overall Session Summary

### Strengths
- Maintains consistent focus on the target behavior (alcohol use) throughout the session
- Demonstrates genuine concern for Milan's wellbeing, though expressed counterproductively
- One open-ended question is asked, showing brief awareness that understanding Milan's perspective might be valuable
- One attempt to acknowledge Milan's emotional experience ("I understand stopping immediately feels hard")

### Critical Areas for Improvement
- **Fundamental MI spirit violation:** Entire session characterized by directive, authoritarian stance directly contradicting MI's core principles
- **Acceptance and empathy absent:** Judges Milan's perspective as "wrong" and fails to validate emotional experience
- **Autonomy actively undermined:** Repeated "you have to" language removes Milan's sense of choice and agency
- **Evocation replaced with imposition:** External reasons for change imposed rather than Milan's own motivations drawn out
- **Sustain talk directly confronted:** Rather than skillfully softening resistance, psychiatrist argues with Milan, strengthening defensiveness
- **Collaboration nonexistent:** Operates as expert authority rather than collaborative partner
- **Change talk not cultivated:** When Milan generates own change language, it is dismissed or contradicted rather than amplified

---

## Clinical Impact

This session significantly undermines Milan's motivation for change and damages the therapeutic relationship. Rather than increasing intrinsic motivation, the directive approach triggers reactance—Milan becomes more defensive, more entrenched in his position, and less likely to engage authentically in future sessions. Milan explicitly states, "Telling me there's no time for thinking it over just makes me want to push back more," indicating the psychiatrist's approach is counterproductive. By session end, Milan has moved away from change, not toward it. He is less likely to return for future sessions, less likely to consider changing his drinking behavior, and more likely to dismiss the psychiatrist's concerns as judgmental and controlling. An adversarial dynamic has been created rather than a collaborative one, which is particularly damaging with an ambivalent adolescent sensitive to perceived control.

---

## Key Recommendations

1. **Shift from expert-driven to client-centered stance:** Replace all "you have to/must/need to" language with open-ended questions and reflective listening.

2. **Develop genuine empathy and acceptance:** Actively work to understand Milan's need for peer connection and fear of social exclusion. Communicate unconditional positive regard even when disagreeing with his choices.

3. **Amplify Milan's own change talk:** When Milan generates change language, immediately pause and explore it with curiosity rather than moving to advice.

4. **Stop confronting sustain talk:** Use double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic redirection instead of arguing with Milan's reasons for not changing.

5. **Explore Milan's values:** Ask what matters most to him (school, friendships, independence, health) and explore how his drinking aligns with those values.

6. **Support autonomy explicitly:** Affirm Milan's right to make his own decisions, even if they differ from your recommendation. This paradoxically increases likelihood of change.

7. **Ask Milan's perspective first:** Consistently ask his opinion and concerns before offering information or advice.

8. **Develop complex reflective listening:** Move beyond surface reflections to capture emotional nuance, ambivalence, and deeper meaning.

9. **Examine your own agenda:** Recognize when frustration arises and reconnect with MI principles rather than pushing harder.

10. **Reframe your goal:** Shift from "getting Milan to quit" to "understanding Milan and evoking his own motivation for change."
