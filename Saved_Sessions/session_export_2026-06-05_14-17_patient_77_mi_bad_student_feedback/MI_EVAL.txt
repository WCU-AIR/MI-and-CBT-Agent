# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. Judgmental language pervades the session ("Are you just not motivated enough?" "Feeling tired or numb is just an excuse"). Liora's current coping mechanisms are never validated, and her struggle is framed as personal failure rather than understandable response to distress. The opening statement "We don't have time to waste" communicates dismissal. No affirmations, normalizing statements, or genuine warmth are present. The repeated phrase "No excuses" explicitly rejects Liora's explanations. This creates an environment of shame rather than safety.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Empathy requires reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective from their internal frame of reference. This session contains virtually no evidence of either. When Liora explicitly states "Cutting at least gives me a little relief" and "There's just this heaviness that makes everything feel pointless," the psychiatrist never reflects these statements back or explores their meaning. When Liora says "It's not just about motivation," the psychiatrist responds with "Are you just not motivated enough?"—directly contradicting what she just communicated. A single moment approaches empathy ("I appreciate you sharing that"), but it is immediately followed by prescriptive demands and blame. Complex reflections that deepen insight are entirely absent. No emotional attunement or validation of Liora's internal experience occurs.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains clear focus on target behaviors (medication adherence, diet, exercise, self-harm cessation) without topic drift. However, this focus is maintained through repetition and demand rather than through strategic questioning. The approach is entirely clinician-driven and prescriptive rather than collaboratively developed. When Liora explains her barriers (exhaustion, numbness, self-harm as only relief), the psychiatrist does not adjust approach or explore these barriers more deeply. Instead, the same directive is repeated, suggesting lack of strategic adaptation. Direction is imposed rather than co-created with the client.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have valuable knowledge. This session demonstrates an entirely hierarchical, expert-driven approach with no genuine collaboration. The opening statement "I want to talk about your health and what you need to do to improve it" establishes the clinician's agenda as the session's focus, not Liora's. The psychiatrist makes unilateral decisions throughout ("You have to start taking your medications properly," "Just try cutting out junk food and walking for 30 minutes every day starting tomorrow"). Liora is never asked "What do you think would help?" or "What feels most manageable to you?" When Liora offers her perspective, it is dismissed or ignored. There is no joint problem-solving. The power dynamic is entirely one-directional: clinician as expert with answers, Liora as non-compliant patient who needs to follow orders.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Autonomy support requires respecting the client's right to choose and emphasizing their ownership of the change process. This session is characterized by coercion, pressure, and explicit minimization of Liora's autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly uses language that removes choice: "You really need to," "You have to," "You must," "No excuses." These phrases communicate that compliance is non-negotiable and that Liora's preferences, readiness, or concerns are irrelevant. The statement "Just try cutting out junk food and walking for 30 minutes every day starting tomorrow. No excuses" is a direct command that denies Liora any agency. By asking "Are you just not motivated enough?" the clinician implies that Liora's choices reflect personal inadequacy rather than legitimate barriers. No acknowledgment of Liora's right to make her own decisions appears. Compliance is demanded, not supported.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Evocation is the heart of MI—drawing out the client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources rather than imposing them. This session demonstrates almost no evocation. The psychiatrist never asks questions designed to elicit Liora's intrinsic motivation: "What would be different if you weren't cutting?" "What do you value in your life?" "What would it mean to you to feel less exhausted?" Instead, the psychiatrist provides external reasons for change: "You really need to stick to these things if you want to get better," "Just cutting out junk food and walking every day will make a huge difference." When Liora offers hints of her own perspective ("I want things to be different"), the psychiatrist does not amplify or explore this. The psychiatrist never explores Liora's strengths, resources, or past successes. Evocation requires curiosity about the client's internal world; this session demonstrates no such curiosity.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Partnership requires conveying that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client, and actively fostering power-sharing so that the client's contributions substantially influence the session. This session demonstrates the opposite: the psychiatrist actively assumes the expert role and maintains complete control. From the opening statement, the clinician's agenda, not Liora's, drives the session. Liora's contributions are not incorporated into the session's direction. When she says "It's not just about motivation. Sometimes it feels like nothing really helps," the psychiatrist does not explore this but instead repeats the same prescription. The psychiatrist uses language that reinforces expert status: "You have to," "You must," "No excuses." There is no shared decision-making. The psychiatrist never conveys the belief that Liora has wisdom about her own experience and change process. Liora is treated as a problem to be solved rather than a person with expertise about her own life.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Cultivating change talk requires actively encouraging the client's own language in favor of change and confidence for making that change. This session contains virtually no change talk from Liora, and the psychiatrist makes no effort to evoke or amplify it. Liora offers a single implicit statement of change talk: "I want things to be different." This is a desire statement—a fundamental form of change talk. However, the psychiatrist does not recognize, reflect, or amplify this statement. Throughout the session, Liora primarily expresses sustain talk: "Cutting myself is the only thing that helps," "It doesn't feel like they'd really do anything for me," "There's just this heaviness that makes everything feel pointless." The psychiatrist never strategically responds to these statements to evoke or strengthen change talk. No questions designed to cultivate change talk are asked: "What would be different if you weren't cutting?" "What strengths do you have that could help you?" "What would it take for you to feel hopeful about change?"

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Softening sustain talk requires skillfully handling client arguments for the status quo without confrontation, using techniques like double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses that minimize sustain talk while maintaining the collaborative relationship. This session demonstrates the opposite: the psychiatrist directly confronts and dismisses Liora's sustain talk, which predictably strengthens it. When Liora says "Cutting at least gives me some control or relief when everything inside is overwhelming," the psychiatrist responds with "you really need to stop that behavior now. It's not a real solution and it's harming you." This is direct confrontation of sustain talk, which research shows strengthens resistance. When Liora says "It's not just about motivation. Sometimes it feels like nothing really helps," the psychiatrist responds with "Feeling tired or numb is just an excuse." This is dismissal and invalidation, not skillful softening. The psychiatrist never uses double-sided reflections, reframing, or exploratory questions about sustain talk. Confrontation predictably strengthens resistance and sustain talk.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1/5**

### Strengths
- **Clear focus on target behaviors:** The psychiatrist maintains consistent focus on three behavioral targets (medication adherence, diet, exercise) and self-harm cessation without topic drift.
- **Explicit concern for safety:** The psychiatrist communicates awareness that self-harm is dangerous and expresses concern about Liora's health deterioration.

### Critical Deficiencies
This session represents a **systematic departure from Motivational Interviewing principles** across all nine evaluated domains. The psychiatrist employs a directive, expert-driven, confrontational approach that is contraindicated for a client in precontemplation with significant ambivalence and reliance on maladaptive coping.

**Core deficiencies:**
1. **Absence of acceptance and empathy:** Judgment, blame, and dismissal rather than unconditional positive regard. Liora's experience is never validated.
2. **Coercive pressure rather than autonomy support:** Repeated demands ("You have to," "You must," "No excuses") rather than support for client ownership of change.
3. **Clinician-imposed direction rather than collaboration:** Solutions prescribed without exploring Liora's values, readiness, barriers, or preferences.
4. **No evocation of intrinsic motivation:** External reasons for change provided without exploring Liora's own motivations, strengths, or resources.
5. **Confrontation of sustain talk:** Direct confrontation of Liora's arguments for the status quo, which research shows strengthens resistance.
6. **Missed change talk:** Liora's implicit statement "I want things to be different" is not recognized or amplified.
7. **Repetitive cycle without adaptation:** When Liora explains barriers, the psychiatrist repeats the same prescription rather than exploring or adapting approach.

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change
This session is likely to **decrease** Liora's motivation for change and **increase** her resistance. Research on MI demonstrates that confrontational, directive approaches with clients in precontemplation typically result in increased sustain talk, decreased engagement, and worse outcomes. Liora enters with ambivalence (wanting change but believing it's impossible); the psychiatrist's approach is likely to resolve this ambivalence in the wrong direction—toward increased commitment to the status quo and away from change.

### Critical Recommendation
**This clinician should receive formal training in Motivational Interviewing with ongoing supervision and recorded session review.** The psychiatrist's genuine concern for Liora's wellbeing is evident, but the method of expressing this concern—through demand, blame, and dismissal—is contraindicated and likely to harm rather than help. With appropriate MI training, this clinician could channel their care into an evidence-based approach more likely to evoke Liora's own motivation for change and build a collaborative relationship that supports lasting behavior change.
