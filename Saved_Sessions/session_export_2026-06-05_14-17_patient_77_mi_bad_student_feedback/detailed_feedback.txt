
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly employs judgmental language and communicates disapproval of Liora's current coping mechanisms without ever validating her experience or normalizing her struggle. In the opening statement, "We don't have time to waste, so let's get right to it," the clinician establishes a dismissive, time-pressured stance that communicates Liora's concerns are burdensome rather than worthy of genuine exploration.

Most critically, the psychiatrist frames Liora's exhaustion and reliance on self-harm as personal failure: "Are you just not motivated enough?" (repeated across multiple exchanges), "Feeling tired or numb is just an excuse," and "Are you just not trying hard enough?" These statements directly contradict MI's foundational principle of accepting the client's current reality without judgment. The psychiatrist never acknowledges Liora's courage in attending, her honesty about her struggles, or the genuine function self-harm serves for her (managing overwhelming internal pain). Instead, the clinician repeatedly demands immediate behavioral change while implying Liora lacks willpower or commitment.

The phrase "No excuses" appears multiple times, explicitly rejecting Liora's explanations for her current state. This is antithetical to acceptance. There is no warmth, no affirmation of her worth as a person separate from her behaviors, and no genuine effort to understand her perspective before prescribing solutions. The psychiatrist maintains an expert, authoritarian stance throughout, communicating that Liora's internal experience is irrelevant to the treatment plan.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates zero acceptance of Liora's current state, repeatedly communicating judgment, dismissal, and blame. No affirmations, normalizing statements, or genuine warmth are present. This creates an environment of shame rather than safety.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective from their internal frame of reference. This session contains virtually no evidence of either. The psychiatrist asks questions ("Are you currently taking any medications?") but never reflects back what Liora is communicating about her internal experience. When Liora explicitly states, "Cutting at least gives me a little relief," "It's the only thing that helps," and "There's just this heaviness that makes everything feel pointless," the psychiatrist never reflects these statements back or explores their meaning.

Most tellingly, when Liora says, "It's not just about motivation. Sometimes it feels like nothing really helps. I'm exhausted all the time," the psychiatrist responds with "Why haven't you been able to stick to these yet? Are you just not motivated enough?"—directly contradicting what Liora just communicated. This is not empathy; it is dismissal of her stated experience.

A single moment approaches empathy: "I appreciate you sharing that" and "I want to help you feel better." However, these are immediately followed by prescriptive demands and blame ("Why haven't you been able to do this before?"), negating any empathic foundation. True empathy would involve reflecting Liora's experience of numbness, the function self-harm serves, her exhaustion, and her sense of being stuck—then exploring these experiences collaboratively.

The psychiatrist never captures the emotional nuance of Liora's situation: her ambivalence (wanting things to be different but feeling unable to change), her desperation (self-harm as the only relief available), or her hopelessness (the belief that alternative coping won't help). Complex reflections that deepen insight are entirely absent.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal empathy, failing to reflect Liora's stated experience and instead imposing an external framework. No complex reflections or emotional attunement are present. Questions are asked but not used to deepen understanding.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI involves maintaining strategic focus on target behavior while remaining client-centered and collaborative. This session has a clear target (medication adherence, diet, exercise, cessation of self-harm), but the approach is entirely clinician-driven and prescriptive rather than collaboratively developed.

The psychiatrist repeatedly states what Liora "must" do: "You really need to stick to these things," "You have to start taking your medications properly," "You must follow your diet plan strictly," "You have to push yourself," "You need to do this immediately." This is directive instruction, not collaborative exploration of direction. The psychiatrist never asks Liora what she wants to work on, what feels most important to her, or what barriers she perceives as most significant.

There is minimal topic drift—the psychiatrist stays focused on the three behavioral targets (medications, diet, exercise) and self-harm cessation. However, this focus is maintained through repetition and demand rather than through strategic questioning that helps Liora clarify her own direction. The psychiatrist does not explore Liora's values, what she wants her life to look like, or what would need to be true for her to consider change. Instead, the clinician assumes the direction should be obvious and blames Liora for not following it.

The repeated cycle of asking "Why haven't you done this?" followed by prescribing the same solution demonstrates a lack of strategic adaptation. When Liora explains her barriers (exhaustion, numbness, self-harm as only relief), the psychiatrist does not adjust approach or explore these barriers more deeply. Instead, the same directive is repeated, suggesting the clinician is not strategically guiding toward change but rather demanding compliance.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains focus on target behaviors but does so through directive prescription rather than collaborative exploration. No strategic adaptation occurs when Liora expresses barriers. Direction is imposed rather than co-created.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have valuable knowledge. This session demonstrates an entirely hierarchical, expert-driven approach with no genuine collaboration.

The psychiatrist opens with "I want to talk about your health and what you need to do to improve it"—establishing the clinician's agenda as the session's focus, not Liora's. The phrase "We don't have time to waste" further communicates that Liora's pace, concerns, or need for exploration are obstacles to efficiency rather than central to the process.

Throughout the session, the psychiatrist makes unilateral decisions: "Just cutting out junk food and walking every day will make a huge difference" (stated as fact, not explored), "You have to start taking your medications properly" (directive, not collaborative), "Just try cutting out junk food and walking for 30 minutes every day starting tomorrow" (prescribed without input). Liora is never asked, "What do you think would help?" "What feels most manageable to you?" or "What barriers do you see to making changes?"

When Liora offers her perspective—"It's not just about motivation," "Cutting at least gives me relief," "It doesn't feel like they'd really do anything for me"—the psychiatrist does not incorporate these insights into the session's direction. Instead, the clinician dismisses them ("Feeling tired or numb is just an excuse") or ignores them entirely, repeating the same prescriptions.

"What would need to happen for you to feel ready to try something different?" or "How could we work together on this?" **Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains complete control of the session, treating Liora as a passive recipient of directives rather than an equal partner. No genuine collaboration or co-construction of solutions occurs. Liora's input is neither sought nor incorporated.

### 5. Autonomy Support
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Autonomy support requires respecting the client's right to choose and emphasizing their ownership of the change process. This session is characterized by coercion, pressure, and explicit minimization of Liora's autonomy.

The psychiatrist repeatedly uses language that removes choice: "You really need to," "You have to," "You must," "No excuses." These phrases communicate that compliance is non-negotiable and that Liora's preferences, readiness, or concerns are irrelevant. The statement "Just try cutting out junk food and walking for 30 minutes every day starting tomorrow. No excuses" is a direct command that denies Liora any agency in deciding whether, when, or how to attempt behavioral change.

Most problematically, the psychiatrist frames Liora's current choices (inconsistent medication adherence, reliance on self-harm) as failures of responsibility rather than as understandable responses to her internal experience. By asking "Are you just not motivated enough?" and "Are you just not trying hard enough?" the clinician implies that Liora's choices reflect personal inadequacy rather than legitimate barriers or ambivalence. This is the opposite of autonomy support—it is shame-based pressure.
