
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

4. Can the patient apply learnings independently?

Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS)
This document outlines a structured framework for assessing therapist competence in Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT). 
It is used for training, supervision, and evaluation. Each item is scored on a 0–6 scale:

0 = Poor
2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Explicitly Address Safety, Accountability, and the Seriousness of Intimate Partner Abuse**
- **Action:** In the next session, begin by directly discussing the impact of Lucas's behavior on his wife and children. Use clear, non-judgmental language that establishes accountability without shame: "Your wife is afraid of you, and that fear is understandable given what has happened. Part of my role is to help you change this behavior so your family feels safe. That's going to require real commitment and honesty from you."
- **Example:** "I want to be clear: breaking objects, yelling, and losing control around your family is serious. It's affecting your wife's sense of safety and your children's wellbeing. We need to make sure that as we work together, your family is safe. If you ever feel like you might hurt someone, we need to have a plan for that."
- **Practice:** Review literature on intimate partner abuse and therapist responsibility. Consider whether additional safety planning, couples work, or referral to specialized anger management programs is indicated. Consult with supervisor about ethical obligations.

### 2. **Conduct a Thorough Functional Analysis of Anger**
- **Action:** In the next session, systematically explore what anger achieves for Lucas. Ask: "What happens when you get angry? What do people do? What do you get out of it?" Explore whether anger helps him feel heard, assert control, avoid responsibility, or achieve other goals. Understanding the function is critical for developing effective interventions.
- **Example:** "You mentioned that anger feels like the only way to make yourself heard. Let's explore that more. When you get angry, what specifically happens? Does your wife listen more? Do you feel more in control? What would happen if you didn't get angry—would your needs go unmet?"
- **Practice:** Use a functional analysis worksheet. For each recent outburst, identify: trigger, thoughts, feelings, physical sensations, behavior, and consequences (both immediate and longer-term). Identify patterns in what anger achieves.

### 3. **Develop Deeper Cognitive Work Around Core Beliefs**
- **Action:** Move beyond surface automatic thoughts to explore underlying beliefs about perfectionism, control, fairness, and self-worth. Use downward arrow technique: "If your wife doesn't have dinner ready, what does that mean? What does it say about her? What does it say about you?"
- **Example:** "You said you feel like you have to do everything. What would it mean if you didn't do everything? What would that say about you as a person? As a provider? As a husband?"
- **Practice:** Use a thought record that includes automatic thought, evidence for/against, core belief, and alternative belief. Have Lucas practice identifying his thoughts in low-stakes situations first, then gradually in higher-stakes situations.

### 4. **Teach Explicit, Detailed Coping Techniques**
- **Action:** Do not assume Lucas knows how to breathe deeply or relax. Teach specific techniques with explicit instructions. Consider: diaphragmatic breathing (4-4-4 counts), progressive muscle relaxation, grounding techniques (5-4-3-2-1 sensory awareness), or time-out procedures.
- **Example:** "Let's practice together. Breathe in slowly through your nose for a count of 4, hold for 4, then exhale through your mouth for 4. Feel your belly expand as you breathe in. Let's do this together five times right now."
- **Practice:** Have Lucas demonstrate the technique in session. Provide a written handout with step-by-step instructions. Assign daily practice (not just when angry) to build automaticity.

### 5. **Implement Behavioral Experiments to Test Thoughts**
- **Action:** Design homework as true experiments, not just practice. For example: "This week, when you notice your wife is busy, try the breathing and reminder technique. Notice what happens to your anger level. Also notice: does your wife actually ignore you? Does she actually not contribute? What evidence do you observe?" This turns homework into data-gathering.
- **Example:** "Let's set up an experiment. Next time you come home and things are chaotic, use your breathing and reminder. Then, write down: (1) What did I notice about my wife's contributions? (2) Did she actually ignore me? (3) What happened to my anger level? (4) What did I do instead of yelling?"
- **Practice:** Use a simple behavioral experiment worksheet. Have Lucas predict what will happen, then observe what actually happens. Review results in next session.

### 6. **Address the Belief That Anger Is Necessary to Be Heard**
- **Action:** This is a critical belief maintaining the behavior. Explore alternatives: assertiveness training, direct communication, problem-solving. Help Lucas develop skills to express needs without anger.
- **Example:** "You feel like anger is the only way to be heard. Let's think about other ways. What if you said to your wife, calmly but firmly: 'I'm feeling overwhelmed. I need us to talk about how we're dividing household responsibilities.' Would that work? What would make you feel heard without anger?"
- **Practice:** Role-play assertive communication. Teach Lucas to use "I" statements, express needs clearly, and listen to his wife's perspective. Practice in session, then assign homework to use these skills.

### 7. **Develop a Comprehensive Anger Management Plan Beyond In-the-Moment Coping**
- **Action:** Anger management requires more than breathing. Develop a multi-level plan: (1) reduce overall irritability through lifestyle factors (sleep, exercise, stress management), (2) identify and avoid triggers when possible, (3) address underlying beliefs and vulnerabilities, (4) develop in-the-moment coping, (5) address consequences and repair relationships.
- **Example:** "Let's think about this comprehensively. First, are you getting enough sleep? Exercise? These affect irritability. Second, what situations trigger you most? Can we plan for those? Third, we're working on your thoughts and coping skills. Fourth, after an outburst, what can you do to repair things with your wife?"
- **Practice:** Create a written anger management plan that Lucas can reference. Include warning signs, coping strategies, and post-outburst repair steps.

### 8. **Provide Written Materials and Structured Worksheets**
- **Action:** Do not rely on Lucas's memory or verbal instructions. Provide written thought records, breathing instructions, warning signs checklists, and homework worksheets. This is particularly important for patients with potential cognitive rigidity or high stress.
- **Example:** Provide a "Anger Warning Signs" card listing physical sensations, thoughts, and behaviors that precede outbursts. Provide a "Coping Strategies" card with specific techniques. Provide a thought record template.
- **Practice:** Create a patient workbook or handout packet. Review materials in session to ensure understanding.

### 9. **Establish Clear Homework Review and Troubleshooting Process**
- **Action:** At the end of each session, explicitly state: "Next session, we'll review how the breathing and reminders went. We'll look at what worked, what didn't, and adjust the plan if needed." In the next session, begin by reviewing homework in detail before moving to new material.
- **Example:** "How did the practice go this week? Did you remember to do it? What happened when you used the breathing? Did it help? What got in the way? Let's problem-solve together."
- **Practice:** Develop a homework review protocol. Always begin sessions with homework review. If homework wasn't completed, explore barriers non-judgmentally and problem-solve.

### 10. **Consider Referral to Specialized Anger Management or Couples Therapy**
- **Action:** While individual CBT is appropriate, consider whether Lucas would benefit from specialized anger management groups or couples therapy. Given the intimate partner abuse context, couples therapy must be carefully considered (some argue it's contraindicated in abuse situations). Consult with supervisor.
- **Example:** "I want to make sure you get the best help possible. There are specialized anger management programs that might be helpful. There's also the option of working with your wife together to rebuild trust and communication. Let's discuss what might work best for you."
- **Practice:** Research evidence-based anger management programs in your area. Understand the literature on couples therapy in abuse contexts. Consult with supervisor about appropriate referrals.
