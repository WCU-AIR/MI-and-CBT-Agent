# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains neutral, professional demeanor but fails to communicate genuine warmth, affirmation, or unconditional positive regard. Minimal validation of client's positive efforts (marathon training). No explicit affirmation of autonomy or worth. Supportive language appears only at session end, after directive approach has already been established. Tone remains transactional rather than warm and collaborative.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflective listening is surface-level and factual rather than capturing emotional nuance or deeper meaning. Psychiatrist misses opportunities to reflect client's core values (e.g., balance, social connection, stress relief). Reflections are brief and immediately followed by questions or advice, preventing genuine connection. Complex reflections that integrate ambivalence are entirely absent. Perspective-taking remains basic and transactional.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** General focus on dietary improvement is maintained, but direction is muddled and reactive rather than strategically guided. Session begins with open exploration but quickly narrows to diet without clear rationale or exploration of client's priorities. When client indicates low importance (4-5/10), psychiatrist does not explore discrepancy or pivot to client's actual concerns. No coherent strategic thread connecting exploration to change planning. Direction lacks clarity about why diet matters or connection to client's stated goals.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session is fundamentally expert-driven rather than partnership-based. Psychiatrist repeatedly uses prescriptive language: "Do you think you should maybe try meal prepping?", "It would probably help if you planned ahead," "Maybe you should just try small changes," "I'd suggest you try that." When client expresses low importance and moderate confidence, psychiatrist does not explore client's ideas or co-construct solutions. Instead, psychiatrist imposes plan unilaterally. Client's response reflects compliance, not genuine collaboration or ownership. No evidence of seeking client input on barriers, preferences, or priorities.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is nearly absent. Language is consistently prescriptive and frames change as obligation rather than choice. When client expresses low importance (4-5/10), psychiatrist ignores this signal and prescribes changes anyway, directly undermining client choice and intrinsic motivation. No language emphasizing that change is client's decision or that client is expert on own life. Only mention of support ("I'm here to support you") comes at end and frames support as helping client comply with psychiatrist's plan, not as respecting autonomy. This directly contradicts MI spirit.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out client's own motivations and values—is almost entirely absent. Psychiatrist relies on telling rather than asking, providing reasons for change (better energy, improved performance) rather than evoking client's reasons. Leading questions suggest answers rather than genuinely evoking perspective. When client rates importance at 4-5/10, psychiatrist does not ask "What would need to happen for this to move from a 4 to a 6?" or explore what the rating means. No strategic questioning to elicit desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment. Session characterized by clinician-provided reasons, not client-generated motivation.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership is fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist consistently assumes expert role, positioning self as authority with knowledge about what client should do. Power dynamic is unequal throughout. Psychiatrist asks questions, listens briefly, then prescribes solutions—classic expert-driven model. Language is rarely inclusive ("we," "together," "let's figure out") and is instead clinician-centered ("I'd suggest," "I'm here to support"). Client contributions do not substantially influence session direction; psychiatrist's recommendations remain unchanged regardless of client's stated priorities. Opposite of true partnership where client wisdom would shape direction.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Change talk is neither evoked nor cultivated. Psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to amplify limited change talk client produces ("I could probably do better," "I'm willing to try a bit of that," "I'll try some healthier snacks"). When client expresses willingness, psychiatrist immediately prescribes specific actions rather than exploring motivation. No open-ended questions designed to elicit change talk: "What would be the benefits?", "How might this help?", "What's one thing you could imagine trying?" Client's low importance rating (4-5/10) is never explored to understand what it represents or what might increase it. Fundamental failure to cultivate change talk—core MI technique.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk is present ("I don't have major health problems," "it's fine for now," "not a huge problem," "I'm still getting through my runs," "going out is part of life") but not skillfully addressed. Psychiatrist sometimes reinforces sustain talk by validating minimization without exploring discrepancy. When client says diet isn't holding him back, psychiatrist responds "your diet isn't really a big problem"—validating rather than gently exploring. Double-sided reflections and strategic reframing are absent. Psychiatrist asks importance/confidence scales (appropriate) but does not use responses to explore or reframe sustain talk. Overall sustain talk management is weak.

---

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall Score: 1.4/5 – Poor MI Practice**

### Critical Deficiencies:
- **Expert-driven, prescriptive stance** rather than collaborative and evocative
- **Absence of genuine empathy and acceptance** with minimal affirmation
- **No autonomy support**—client's low importance ignored; changes prescribed regardless
- **Failure to evoke change talk**—psychiatrist tells rather than asks
- **Non-collaborative problem-solving**—plan imposed rather than co-constructed
- **Sustain talk not addressed skillfully**—minimization sometimes validated
- **Missing actual presenting concern**—referral was for elevated alcohol use (18 drinks/week), never addressed; significant clinical oversight

### Impact on Client Engagement:
Client's compliance ("Yeah, I can give that a shot") reflects external pressure, not genuine commitment. Statement "I'm not promising miracles or anything" indicates low confidence and minimal buy-in. Without intrinsic motivation, autonomy support, and genuine partnership, sustained behavior change is unlikely.

### Immediate Priorities for Improvement:
1. Shift from expert-driven to collaborative stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
2. Develop genuine empathy through deeper, complex reflections
3. Explore and amplify change talk strategically
4. Address sustain talk with double-sided reflections, not validation
5. Support autonomy explicitly and respect low importance ratings
6. Clarify actual presenting concern (alcohol use) and explore discrepancies
7. Use importance/confidence scales as exploration tools, not endpoints
8. Establish genuine partnership through inclusive language and shared decision-making
9. Address actual referral concern in future sessions
10. Develop comfort with ambivalence rather than pushing for change

---

**Recommendation:** Psychiatrist requires focused training and practice in core MI competencies, particularly collaborative stance, autonomy support, and strategic evocation of change talk. Recorded practice sessions with feedback are strongly recommended.
