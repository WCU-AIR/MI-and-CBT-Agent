### Empathy

Basic content reflections offered ("it sounds like the meth use has caused some issues") but emotionally shallow—captures facts but misses emotional core of ambivalence and fear of losing identity/belonging. When client articulates profound concern about social exclusion, psychiatrist responds with suggestions rather than reflecting emotional weight. Fails to explore deeper meaning behind statements. Complex reflections demonstrating understanding of client's core fears entirely absent. ### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Maintains topical focus on meth use throughout session, preventing aimless wandering. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. Psychiatrist presupposes "cutting down" as target without first establishing this as client's goal. Quickly narrows focus and suggests specific strategies (acting classes, community groups, gradual reduction) before adequately understanding client's own goals and values. Administers confidence ruler prematurely given lack of genuine collaborative goal-setting. Direction is psychiatrist-driven rather than emerging from client's own exploration of ambivalence.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal genuine partnership. Psychiatrist adopts expert-driven stance throughout, offering prescriptive suggestions ("you should try," "it would probably help if," "maybe you should") followed by questions seeking agreement rather than co-exploration. When client expresses legitimate concerns (fear of losing friends), psychiatrist does not explore collaboratively but moves forward with plan as if concern addressed. Psychiatrist never asks what client thinks would work, what he has tried, or what his ideas are for change. Session reads as psychiatrist telling client what to do, with client passively agreeing. Power-sharing and co-construction of solutions absent.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Frequent use of directive language undermines client's perception of choice and ownership. Prescriptive framing throughout ("should try," "would probably help if," "don't forget") creates subtle pressure. Questions like "What do you think?" often follow directives, feeling like seeking agreement rather than genuinely inviting autonomous choice. Psychiatrist never explicitly affirms client's right to choose differently or proceed at own pace without judgment. Late-session statements ("it's okay to take it one step at a time") are autonomy-supportive but insufficient to counterbalance earlier directive stance. Implicit pressure to comply with psychiatrist's agenda evident.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal evocation; heavy reliance on advice-giving and problem-solving instead. When client presents change talk ("I want to sort out my money, my health, and my career"), psychiatrist does not amplify or deepen—moves immediately to solution-generation. Open questions asked are exploratory rather than evocative. True evocation absent: no questions drawing out client's own reasons for change, no exploration of what matters most beyond party scene, no investigation of passion for acting as intrinsic motivation. Fails to explore client's strengths and resources. When client expresses ambivalence, psychiatrist provides plan rather than evoking client's own ideas about what would work.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist consistently assumes expert role, positioning self as authority on what client should do. Frames self as problem-solver and architect of change ("I'll help you put together a simple plan," "I can help you come up with a plan"). While supportive language present, underlying dynamic is expert-driven. True partnership would involve recognizing client's expertise about own life and asking what he thinks might work. Instead, psychiatrist tells client what to try. Psychiatrist frames reassurance as expert guidance rather than genuine recognition that client is expert on own change process. Client's final statement ("I appreciate you being understanding") reflects gratitude for non-judgment, not genuine partnership in change process.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Significant deficiency in strategically evoking and reinforcing client's own change language. Client offers change talk ("I want to sort out my money, my health, and my career," "I do think it's important—maybe a 7," "focusing on acting again might help me feel better") but psychiatrist fails to amplify or deepen any statements. When client rates importance as 7, psychiatrist does not explore ("What makes it a 7?") or strengthen motivation. Misses opportunities to evoke change talk through strategic open questions. Generic encouragement ("Keep at it," "You've got this") provided but not strategic amplification of client's own change language. No questions designed to elicit desire talk, ability talk, or commitment talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill in handling client's arguments for status quo without confrontation. Significant sustain talk presented ("It's not out of control," "It's just part of the scene," "nothing really feels the same," "I'm scared of what I'd be giving up," "I'm worried about losing friends") but psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or reframing. When client expresses fear about social exclusion, psychiatrist acknowledges briefly but moves forward without exploring concern deeply. Fails to use strategic questioning to soften sustain talk. Directive approach may inadvertently strengthen sustain talk by creating pressure and defensiveness. By session end, client is compliant but not genuinely motivated—agreeing to please psychiatrist rather than because ambivalence resolved.

## **Overall Session Score: 2.1/5**

### **Summary:**

This session demonstrates **below-average MI competence** with significant deficiencies in core MI principles. While the psychiatrist maintains a non-judgmental tone and provides supportive language, the session is fundamentally **expert-driven and advice-focused** rather than client-centered and evocative.

**Critical Deficiency:** The psychiatrist fails to help the client explore and resolve his core ambivalence. The client presents a profound conflict: he values career, health, and financial stability, yet fears that reducing meth use will result in social exclusion and loss of identity. Rather than helping him work through this tension, the psychiatrist prescribes solutions (acting classes, gradual reduction, talking to friends) that feel imposed rather than owned.

**Impact on Engagement and Change:** While the client appears compliant by session end, his compliance reflects agreement to please the psychiatrist rather than genuine ownership of the change process. His confidence is low (4-5/10), and his ambivalence remains unresolved. Likelihood of follow-through is low; risk of relapse is high. The client has not developed intrinsic motivation for change or resolved core fears about losing identity and belonging.

**Key Strengths:**
- Non-judgmental, non-confrontational tone
- Consistent topical focus
- Supportive language and encouragement
- Recognition that change is a process
- Willingness to provide concrete support

**Key Areas for Improvement:**
- Excessive advice-giving undermines autonomy and evocation
- Minimal reflective listening; emotional depth absent
- Failure to explore ambivalence before problem-solving
- Missed opportunities to amplify change talk
- Inadequate handling of sustain talk and fears about loss
- Expert-driven stance rather than genuine partnership
- Premature goal-setting before ambivalence explored
- Insufficient autonomy support throughout session

**Recommendation:** Psychiatrist must fundamentally shift from expert-driven, advice-focused approach to client-centered, evocative approach. Develop genuine empathic understanding of client's ambivalence, ask rather than tell, amplify change talk, validate concerns about loss, foster genuine partnership, and support autonomy. These changes will help client develop authentic motivation for change grounded in own values and goals rather than external pressure.
