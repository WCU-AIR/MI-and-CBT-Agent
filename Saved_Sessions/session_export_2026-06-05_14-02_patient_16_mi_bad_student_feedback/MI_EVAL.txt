# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard throughout the session. Opening with "I'm here to tell you exactly what you need to do" establishes an authoritarian, non-accepting stance. Explicit judgment and disapproval are evident in statements such as "You really need to take your health seriously," "Stop making excuses," and "you have to stop hesitating." The patient's perspective is repeatedly dismissed without acknowledgment of his worth or struggle. When the patient expresses uncertainty about the alcohol-blood pressure connection, the psychiatrist responds with dismissal rather than validation: "Your drinking is definitely contributing to your blood pressure, whether you believe it or not." Positive behaviors (quitting smoking years ago, willingness to consider exercise) are never affirmed. The repeated use of "you have to," "you must," and "you really need to" creates a coercive atmosphere fundamentally incompatible with MI spirit. Zero acceptance demonstrated.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective. This session contains virtually no reflective listening. When the patient states "I just haven't really made it a routine. No real reason why, just haven't gotten around to it," the psychiatrist responds with judgment: "You really need to stop making excuses"—a direct judgment rather than understanding. When the patient expresses that "Walking for 30 minutes every day sounds like a lot," the psychiatrist does not reflect this concern or explore overwhelm; instead, he dismisses it: "There's no room for 'eventually.'" The psychiatrist never attempts to understand why the patient minimizes the alcohol connection, never explores his beliefs about causation, and never demonstrates curiosity about his perspective. There are zero complex reflections, zero attempts to capture emotional nuance, and zero genuine curiosity about the patient's internal world. No empathy demonstrated.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** While the psychiatrist maintains clear focus on two target behaviors (exercise and alcohol reduction), this focus is pursued through coercion rather than collaborative exploration. The psychiatrist specifies exact prescriptions ("walking 30 minutes a day") without understanding the patient's current activity level, schedule, preferences, or barriers. There is no progressive clarification of direction through dialogue—only repeated assertion of what "must" happen. Critically, the psychiatrist never helps the patient clarify his own goals or values. There is no exploration of what health means to the patient or what would motivate him specifically. The direction is entirely imposed from above. While the focus is clear, it lacks the collaborative element essential to MI. The psychiatrist also fails to notice that the patient's resistance is increasing throughout the session and continues with greater pressure rather than adjusting strategy. Minimal direction demonstrated; approach is prescriptive rather than exploratory.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring "between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful." This session demonstrates zero collaboration. The psychiatrist explicitly positions himself as the sole expert and authority: "I'm here to tell you exactly what you need to do to improve" (stated twice). Throughout the session, the psychiatrist makes unilateral decisions about what is necessary and what the patient "must" do. When the patient expresses doubt about the alcohol-blood pressure connection, the psychiatrist does not explore this or invite collaborative problem-solving; instead, he asserts authority: "Your drinking is definitely contributing to your blood pressure, whether you believe it or not." The psychiatrist never asks the patient for his ideas about what might work, never solicits input on barriers, and never invites collaborative problem-solving. Questions are used only to gather information or pressure compliance, not to co-construct understanding. The power dynamic is entirely hierarchical. No collaboration demonstrated.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support requires respecting the client's right to choose and emphasizing client ownership of the change process. This session is fundamentally coercive and directly undermines autonomy at every turn. The psychiatrist uses language that eliminates choice: "you have to," "you must," "you really need to," "there's no room for," and "this is not optional." When the patient appropriately asserts autonomy—"I'm not ready to do right now," "I need some more time to think about it," "I'm not going to say yes just because you're pushing me"—the psychiatrist responds with increased pressure rather than respecting his choice. The psychiatrist explicitly states: "No, you really have to promise. This is not optional." This is a direct attack on autonomy. The psychiatrist also uses fear-based pressure ("If you don't start now, your health will get worse") as a coercive tool to override autonomy. True autonomy support would involve acknowledging the patient's right to make his own decisions. Instead, the psychiatrist communicates that autonomy is not respected and compliance is mandatory. Zero autonomy support demonstrated.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation requires drawing out the client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources through asking rather than telling. This session contains zero evocation. The psychiatrist tells the patient what he needs to do rather than asking what he thinks might help. The psychiatrist tells him that alcohol is contributing to his blood pressure rather than exploring the patient's beliefs and inviting him to examine the connection himself. When the patient expresses ambivalence ("I'm not sure cutting down my drinking is really going to change my blood pressure"), this is a perfect opportunity for evocation. Instead, the psychiatrist responds with assertion: "Your drinking is definitely contributing to your blood pressure, whether you believe it or not." This shuts down exploration rather than opening it. The psychiatrist never asks evocative questions such as: "What do you think might be contributing to your high blood pressure?" "What would need to happen for you to feel ready?" "What matters most to you about your health?" The session is entirely advice-giving and telling, with no attempt to draw out the patient's own motivations, reasoning, or resources. Zero evocation demonstrated.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership requires conveying that "expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client" and actively fostering power-sharing so that the client's contributions substantially influence the session. This session demonstrates the complete opposite: the psychiatrist assumes the expert role for the entire interaction, and the patient's contributions have zero influence on the session's direction. The opening statement—"I'm here to tell you exactly what you need to do to improve"—explicitly rejects partnership and establishes a hierarchical expert-patient relationship. The psychiatrist makes all decisions unilaterally: what behaviors must change, how much exercise is required, what the timeline is, and what the consequences will be if the patient does not comply. When the patient offers his perspective ("I don't think the alcohol has much to do with it," "I'm not convinced that my drinking is really causing a problem"), these contributions are dismissed rather than incorporated. By the end of the session, the patient explicitly states: "I'm not going to say yes just because you're pushing me"—a clear indication that partnership has failed and that the patient feels pressured rather than supported. Zero partnership demonstrated.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Cultivating change talk requires actively encouraging the client's own language in favor of change and confidence for making that change. This session contains minimal change talk from the patient, and the psychiatrist does nothing to evoke or amplify it. The patient offers a few soft statements that could be considered change talk: "I do want to stay healthy," "I guess I could try exercising more," and "Maybe I can think about walking more." These are weak but present. However, the psychiatrist does not recognize, amplify, or build upon these statements. When the patient says "I do want to stay healthy," the psychiatrist responds by focusing on barriers rather than amplifying the motivation. When the patient says "Maybe I can think about walking more," the psychiatrist does not explore or encourage this; instead, he pressures for immediate commitment. The psychiatrist's approach actually suppresses change talk by using coercion, dismissal, and pressure, creating an environment where the patient becomes more defensive and entrenched in sustain talk. The psychiatrist never uses open questions to evoke change talk. Zero skill in cultivating change talk demonstrated.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Softening sustain talk requires skillfully handling client arguments for the status quo without confrontation, using techniques like double-sided reflections and reframing. This session demonstrates the opposite: the psychiatrist directly confronts and attacks sustain talk, which intensifies it. The patient offers several sustain talk statements: "I just haven't really made it a routine," "I don't think the alcohol has much to do with it," "I'm not ready to do right now," "I need some more time to think about it," and "I'm not going to say yes just because you're pushing me." These are normal expressions of ambivalence and resistance to change. The psychiatrist's response is consistently confrontational: "Stop making excuses," "No, you really have to promise," "There's no room for 'eventually,'" and "you have to stop hesitating." These responses directly attack the patient's resistance, which is a fundamental MI error. Confrontation increases defensiveness and entrenches sustain talk rather than softening it. By the end of the session, the patient's sustain talk has become stronger and more explicit: "I'm not going to say yes just because you're pushing me"—a direct statement that the confrontational approach has failed and backfired. Zero skill in softening sustain talk demonstrated.

## OVERALL SESSION ASSESSMENT

### **Overall MI Competency Score: 1.0/5**

---

### **Summary of Findings**

#### **Strengths:**
- ☐ The psychiatrist maintains consistent focus on two specific health behaviors (exercise and alcohol reduction)
- ☐ The psychiatrist clearly communicates concern about the patient's health
- ☐ The psychiatrist provides accurate medical information about hypertension risks

#### **Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- ☒ **Fundamental MI spirit violations:** The entire session is built on coercion, judgment, and expert authority rather than partnership and evocation
- ☒ **Zero acceptance or empathy:** The patient's perspective is consistently dismissed without validation or understanding
- ☒ **Counterproductive approach:** The psychiatrist's pressure increases patient resistance throughout the session, moving the patient from mild ambivalence to explicit refusal
- ☒ **No autonomy support:** The patient's right to choose is attacked directly ("This is not optional")
- ☒ **No evocation:** The psychiatrist tells rather than asks, imposing motivation from outside rather than drawing it out from within
- ☒ **Suppression of change talk:** Minimal change talk is offered, and what little exists is not amplified or built upon
- ☒ **Intensification of sustain talk:** The confrontational approach directly strengthens the patient's resistance and arguments against change
- ☒ **Repeated ineffective strategy:** Despite clear evidence that pressure is not working, the psychiatrist continues with greater intensity rather than adjusting approach

---

### **Impact on Client Engagement and Change**

This session is likely to **DECREASE** the patient's motivation for change and damage the therapeutic relationship. The patient's explicit statement—"I'm not going to say yes just because you're pushing me"—indicates that he feels pressured rather than supported and is unlikely to follow through with recommendations. Research consistently demonstrates that confrontational, coercive approaches to behavior change are ineffective and often backfire, particularly with ambivalent clients.

---

### **Recommendations for Improvement**

**Priority 1: Fundamental Reorientation from Expert Authority to Partnership**
- Replace prescriptive directives with collaborative exploration
- Begin sessions with explicit collaborative stance: "I'd like to work together to understand what might help you"
- Invite patient input on all decisions regarding behavior change

**Priority 2: Develop Genuine Acceptance and Empathy Through Reflective Listening**
- Make reflective listening your primary response to patient statements
- Reflect before offering advice or information
- Communicate understanding and respect for the patient's perspective

**Priority 3: Evoke Rather Than Impose**
- Replace directive statements with open questions designed to elicit patient motivation
- Ask: "What do you think might be contributing to your high blood pressure?" "What would need to happen for you to feel ready?"
- Draw out the patient's own reasons for change rather than imposing motivation from outside

**Priority 4: Recognize and Amplify Change Talk**
- Listen carefully for any statement suggesting movement toward change
- Pause and explore change talk further rather than moving on
- Use reflections and follow-up questions to strengthen this language

**Priority 5: Support Autonomy**
- Replace obligatory language ("you must," "you have to") with autonomy-supportive language ("your choice," "what feels right to you?")
- Explicitly affirm the patient's right to choose
- Acknowledge that change is difficult and the patient gets to decide

**Priority 6: Soften Sustain Talk Through Reflection, Not Confrontation**
- When the patient expresses resistance, reflect it with empathy rather than attacking it
- Use double-sided reflections that acknowledge both sides of ambivalence
- Gently explore resistance rather than confronting it

**Priority 7: Seek Formal MI Training and Supervision**
- This session demonstrates fundamental misalignment with MI principles
- Commit to at least 20 hours of formal MI training
- Engage in monthly supervision with an MI-trained clinician
- Record and review sessions to identify deviations from MI principles

---

### **Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation for Change**

**VERY LOW.** This session has likely decreased the patient's motivation and damaged the therapeutic relationship. If the psychiatrist continues with this approach, the patient is likely to disengage from treatment, not follow through with recommendations, and avoid future appointments. If the psychiatrist shifts to an MI-consistent approach, the patient is much more likely to feel understood, explore his own ambivalence, develop his own reasons for change, and follow through with decisions he has made autonomously.

**The choice of approach matters profoundly for patient outcomes.**
