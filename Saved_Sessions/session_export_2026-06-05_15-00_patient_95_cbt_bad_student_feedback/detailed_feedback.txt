
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## CRITICAL PRELIMINARY NOTE

This session represents **significant departures from evidence-based CBT practice** and falls substantially short of the standard of care for a 13-year-old with Specific Learning Disorder and Generalized Anxiety Disorder. The therapist engaged in extensive self-disclosure, provided reassurance without cognitive restructuring, failed to establish collaborative agenda, did not apply any systematic CBT techniques, and missed critical opportunities for behavioral intervention. This evaluation reflects serious clinical deficiencies that require immediate remediation.

## 1. Agenda

### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

No agenda was established at any point in this session. The therapist opened with a general, unstructured question ("How have you been feeling lately?") without collaboratively identifying session priorities, target problems, or time allocation. Daphne presented multiple significant concerns—academic difficulties in mathematics, anxiety about mistakes and disappointing others, sleep disturbance, peer relationship difficulties, and argumentative interactions around homework—yet the therapist never prioritized these or established what would be addressed in the session.

Throughout the session, the conversation drifted without direction. The therapist responded reactively to whatever Daphne mentioned (sleep, water intake, music, routines) rather than guiding the session toward specific, measurable goals. There was no explicit statement of "Here's what we'll work on today" or "Of these concerns, which feels most urgent to address first?" The second session opening ("Hey, it's really nice to see you again. How have you been doing since we last talked?") repeated this error—no agenda was set despite this being a follow-up session where progress review and continued planning should occur.

For a 13-year-old with anxiety and learning difficulties, agenda-setting is particularly critical because it provides structure, reduces anxiety through predictability, and ensures the limited session time targets the most impactful problems. The absence of agenda meant the session lacked coherent direction and failed to model the structured problem-solving approach central to CBT.

**Summary:**

No collaborative agenda was established in either session opening. The therapist failed to identify, prioritize, or structure work around Daphne's presenting problems, resulting in an aimless, reactive session without clear therapeutic direction.

## 2. Feedback

### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made minimal attempt to elicit or respond to feedback about the session itself. There were no explicit questions checking whether Daphne understood the therapist's reasoning, whether interventions were helpful, or what she thought about the session direction. Phrases like "Does that make sense?" or "How does that land for you?" or "What do you think about trying this?" were absent.

There was no attempt to summarize main points at the end of either session, no check-in about whether Daphne felt heard or whether the session was helpful, and no solicitation of her input on what would be most useful to focus on next time.

This is particularly problematic with adolescents, who are developmentally more likely to disengage if they don't feel their input matters. Daphne explicitly stated "It doesn't always feel that way" in response to reassurance and "it's hard to hold onto that feeling" regarding positive thinking—clear signals that the therapist's approach wasn't resonating—yet the therapist did not pause to check in about this or adjust based on her feedback.

**Summary:**

The therapist did not systematically elicit feedback about the session, patient understanding, or therapeutic helpfulness. No adjustments were made based on Daphne's implicit signals that reassurance-based interventions were insufficient.

## 3. Understanding

### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated surface-level understanding of Daphne's explicit statements but missed critical nuances and the deeper structure of her anxiety. The therapist accurately reflected that Daphne worried about school, felt scared at night, and had difficulty with math. However, several significant gaps emerged:

**What was missed:**

1. **Core cognitive distortion:** Daphne repeatedly expressed a perfectionistic belief system ("if I mess up, people will be disappointed in me, and then it's like I'm not good enough"; "if I make mistakes, then people will think I'm not smart"). The therapist never identified this as a central, modifiable thought pattern driving her anxiety and avoidance.

2. **Anxiety-avoidance cycle:** Daphne stated "when it gets hard, I just want to get it over with as fast as possible" and "the harder I try, the more upset I get, and then I just want to stop." This describes classic anxiety-driven avoidance and safety-seeking behavior. The therapist did not recognize this pattern or explore how avoidance maintains anxiety.

3. **Sleep maintenance behaviors:** Daphne disclosed needing a nightlight and parental reassurance to fall asleep—classic safety behaviors that maintain anxiety. The therapist normalized this ("I used to be scared of the dark too") without recognizing these as maintaining factors in her GAD.

4. **Functional impairment:** Daphne described arguing with her mother during homework, feeling exhausted, wanting to "shut down," and experiencing pervasive worry that interferes with concentration. The therapist treated these as separate issues rather than recognizing them as interconnected symptoms of anxiety disorder.

5. **Learning disorder interaction:** Daphne has a specific learning disorder in mathematics combined with anxiety. The therapist did not distinguish between skill deficits (which require tutoring/academic intervention) and anxiety-driven avoidance/perfectionism (which CBT can address). This distinction is critical for appropriate case conceptualization.

The therapist's repeated self-disclosure ("I remember when I was in school..."; "I used to be scared of the dark too"; "When I was feeling down...") shifted focus away from understanding Daphne's internal experience and toward the therapist's own history. While intended to normalize, this actually communicated that the therapist's experience was the reference point rather than Daphne's unique presentation.

**Summary:**

The therapist grasped explicit content but failed to identify core cognitive patterns, anxiety-maintenance cycles, or the functional impact of symptoms. Self-disclosure replaced deeper empathic understanding of Daphne's specific difficulties.

### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated adequate warmth and genuine concern but with significant interpersonal missteps that undermined therapeutic effectiveness. Positive elements included:
