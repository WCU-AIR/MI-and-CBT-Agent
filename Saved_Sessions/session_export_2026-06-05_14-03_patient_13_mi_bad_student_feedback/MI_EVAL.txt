### Collaboration

**Comments:** Minimal collaboration throughout, particularly in opening phase. Opening statement ("I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do") explicitly positions psychiatrist as sole expert and client as passive recipient. ### Autonomy Support

Yes or no?") minimize client choice and frame change as mandatory. Statement "you have to stop making excuses" dismisses client's legitimate concerns and undermines autonomy to hold own perspective. Later exchanges show minimal improvement with occasional invitations for choice ("Does that sound like something you're willing to try?"). Single genuine autonomy support moment occurs when psychiatrist affirms client's own idea about support group attendance. Overall, psychiatrist rarely emphasizes that change is client's decision to make.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Severe deficiencies in core MI technique. First three exchanges tell rather than ask; psychiatrist imposes reasons for change ("you really need to quit using right now," "It's just a matter of willpower") rather than evoking client's own motivations. Question "Why didn't you stop sooner?" is accusatory, not evocative. Beginning in fourth exchange, evocation improves with open questions ("What kind of help do you think would make a difference?"). However, psychiatrist fails to strategically amplify client's change talk ("I really shouldn't have done it," "maybe it's worth a try"). No systematic exploration of client's values, reasons for change, or strengths. Missed opportunities to deepen emerging motivation through follow-up questions.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Partnership severely compromised throughout. Opening explicitly positions psychiatrist as sole expert ("I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do") and client as passive recipient. Dismissal of client perspective ("you have to stop making excuses") communicates that psychiatrist's expertise supersedes client's lived experience. Middle exchanges show partial partnership with questions positioning client as expert on own needs ("What kind of help do you think would make a difference?"). ### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Virtually no skill demonstrated in this critical MI technique. Client offers clear change talk in first response ("I really shouldn't have done it") and psychiatrist completely ignores it, instead focusing on sustain talk and imposing external pressure. Throughout first three exchanges, whenever client expresses openness to change, psychiatrist responds with confrontation rather than reflection and amplification. Middle exchanges show some improvement with questions that could evoke change talk, but psychiatrist fails to strategically deepen responses. When client says "maybe I could try just showing up to one support group meeting," psychiatrist affirms but does not strengthen through follow-up questions. Client's tentative change talk ("I guess I can try," "maybe it's worth a try") acknowledged but not amplified. No systematic effort to evoke or strengthen change language throughout session.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Poor skill in handling sustain talk, particularly in early exchanges. Client's primary sustain talk ("I just feel helpless about this whole deal," "No one wants to help me") represents legitimate barriers deserving empathic exploration, not dismissal. Second exchange responds to sustain talk with direct confrontation ("feeling helpless isn't going to get you anywhere") rather than softening. Third exchange uses false dichotomy ("Do you want to keep risking your life, or are you ready to take control?") that amplifies rather than softens resistance. Beginning in fourth exchange, psychiatrist demonstrates better skill with empathic reflection ("It sounds like you've been carrying a heavy burden on your own") that validates without reinforcing hopelessness. Later exchanges show skillful redirection through exploration rather than confrontation. However, psychiatrist misses opportunities for sophisticated double-sided reflections that would acknowledge sustain talk while highlighting change talk.

## **Overall Session Score: 1.8/5**

### **Summary Assessment**

This session demonstrates a **fundamental failure to embody the spirit of Motivational Interviewing**, particularly in the critical opening phase. The psychiatrist begins with an explicitly authoritarian, expert-driven stance that is directly inconsistent with MI principles and likely damages the therapeutic relationship with a client whose core presenting concern is feeling unsupported and alone.

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Confrontational, judgmental opening that violates unconditional positive regard
- Dismissal of client's legitimate barriers as "excuses"
- Failure to systematically evoke and amplify change talk
- Minimal autonomy support and genuine partnership
- Inconsistent application of MI principles with reversion to authoritarian stance in final exchange

**Strengths:**
- Shift toward empathy and validation in middle exchanges
- Recognition of client-generated ideas in later portion
- Some open-ended questions demonstrating movement toward evocation
- Demonstrated capacity to recognize confrontation is ineffective

**Clinical Impact:**
While Amira expresses tentative agreement to attend a support group, this appears to be compliance rather than genuine intrinsic motivation. Her underlying hopelessness and isolation remain largely unaddressed. Early confrontation and inconsistent approach have likely reduced therapeutic alliance and client confidence in psychiatrist's ability to support change. Without more skillful evocation of client's own reasons for change and genuine partnership, sustained behavior change is unlikely.

**Current MI Competency Level:** Trainee-level practitioner requiring significant additional training and supervision to develop true MI competence.
