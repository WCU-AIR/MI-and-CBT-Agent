# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance or unconditional positive regard. From the opening statement ("I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to quit"), a judgmental, authoritarian stance is established. The patient's emotional experience is explicitly dismissed ("You're wasting time focusing on your feelings"), and character is labeled pejoratively ("You need to stop being stubborn"). No affirmation of strengths, normalization of struggle, or genuine warmth is evident. The approach is fundamentally judgmental and actively undermines acceptance, directly contradicting MI principles.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Empathy and reflective listening are virtually absent. The patient communicates frustration with repeated lectures, distress from medication side effects, and fatigue from pressure, yet the psychiatrist responds with demands rather than exploration. No complex reflections capture emotional nuance. The statement "You're wasting time focusing on your feelings" explicitly rejects empathic engagement. The psychiatrist never acknowledges the validity of the patient's frustration or explores barriers with genuine curiosity. This represents a fundamental failure in MI competence and damages the therapeutic alliance.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** While the psychiatrist maintains consistent focus on smoking cessation as the target behavior, the manner of pursuit is entirely inconsistent with MI principles. Direction is achieved through repeated demands ("You have to quit," "You need to stop"), threats ("If you don't quit now, you'll only make things worse"), and pressure tactics ("This is not optional if you want proper treatment"). True MI-consistent direction would involve collaborative exploration of goals, asking permission, and respecting when the patient indicates lack of readiness. The approach is coercive rather than collaborative.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration is entirely absent. The opening statement ("I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to quit") establishes unilateral expert authority. The psychiatrist never asks for the patient's ideas, seeks input on treatment options, or acknowledges the patient's valuable knowledge about his own experience with medications. Commanding language ("you have to," "you need to," "listen up") positions the clinician as authority and the patient as someone who must comply. When the patient attempts to redirect toward dental treatment, the psychiatrist overrides this preference rather than exploring it collaboratively. This is fundamentally incompatible with MI.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy is systematically undermined through coercion, pressure, and removal of choice. Statements like "You have to quit if you want your teeth fixed properly" and "This is not optional if you want proper treatment" frame quitting as mandatory, not a choice. The repeated question "Are you going to quit today or not?" presents a false binary that pressures immediate commitment. The psychiatrist never acknowledges the patient's right to choose or respects his stated preference to focus on dental treatment. When Seamus repeatedly states he is "not ready," the psychiatrist responds with increased pressure rather than respecting this boundary. This approach is antithetical to MI and likely increases resistance.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation is virtually nonexistent. The psychiatrist relies entirely on telling and lecturing rather than drawing out the patient's own motivations, values, and resources. Statements like "Your smoking is the problem here" and "Smoking is destroying your health and your teeth" are unilateral pronouncements delivered in commanding tones. The patient does offer change talk ("I'm serious about my health," "I guess I got to really want to") that could have been evoked and amplified, but these statements are completely ignored. Evocative questions that invite exploration are absent; instead, accusatory questions ("Why haven't you tried harder to quit?") are used. This represents a fundamental failure in MI technique.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership is completely absent. The psychiatrist positions himself as the sole expert with all the answers and explicitly rejects the patient's expertise about his own experience. The statement "You're wasting time focusing on your feelings" dismisses the client's expertise about his own experience. The psychiatrist never acknowledges that Seamus knows his medication experience better than anyone or invites him to share ideas about what might work. When the patient mentions medication side effects, a partnership-oriented clinician would explore this as valuable information; instead, it is dismissed as "making excuses." The commanding tone and expert-driven stance establish hierarchy rather than partnership, which is fundamentally incompatible with MI.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Change talk is not cultivated; instead, it is ignored and suppressed. The patient offers statements that could indicate openness to change ("I'm serious about my health," "I guess I got to really want to"), but these are completely overlooked. The psychiatrist's pressuring approach and lectures do nothing to strengthen these statements and likely cause the patient to retreat into sustain talk. Effective cultivation would involve asking open questions about reasons for change, reflecting and amplifying statements favoring change, and exploring values. None of this occurs. The psychiatrist's confrontational approach actively suppresses change talk and invites defensiveness instead.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Sustain talk is not softened; it is directly confronted, which predictably strengthens resistance. The patient's sustain talk ("I just want to get my teeth fixed," "I tried the pills and they didn't work," "I'm not ready to quit today") is met with dismissal ("Stop making excuses"), argument ("You're wasting time focusing on your feelings"), and pressure ("Are you going to quit today or not?"). This direct confrontation triggers psychological reactance and increases defensiveness. Skillful softening would involve double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses that validate ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist argues against the patient's resistance, which is the opposite of MI technique and predictably increases entrenchment.

---

### **Overall MI Competency Assessment**

**Overall Session Score: 1.1/5**

**Summary:** This session represents a **fundamental failure to implement Motivational Interviewing principles**. The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no MI competence across all nine domains. The approach is characterized by authoritarian expert stance, systematic dismissal of the patient's perspective, direct confrontation of resistance, removal of autonomy, coercive pressure tactics, and complete absence of evocation or collaborative exploration.

**Current Competency Level:** Pre-MI. This is not basic MI competence with room for improvement—this is a complete absence of MI-consistent practice.

**Impact on Client Engagement:** This session is likely to **decrease** motivation for change and **increase** resistance. The patient leaves more defensive, frustrated, and less likely to engage in smoking cessation efforts. The therapeutic alliance is damaged, and the patient explicitly asks to leave.

**Critical Recommendations:**
1. Fundamental philosophical shift from expert-driven to collaborative partnership
2. Development of genuine empathic listening skills
3. Elimination of confrontational language about sustain talk
4. Respect for patient autonomy and readiness
5. **Formal MI training required** – This level of deficiency requires structured training, not feedback alone

**Recommendation for Practice:** This psychiatrist should not continue providing smoking cessation counseling until formal MI training is completed and competence is demonstrated through supervised practice and recorded session review.
