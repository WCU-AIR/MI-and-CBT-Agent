
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **URGENT: Recognize and Communicate Medical Severity**
- **Action:** In the next session, explicitly state the medical diagnosis and urgency. Say: "Valerie, I want to be direct with you. Your body is showing signs of severe malnutrition—your weight is dangerously low, you've missed periods, and your symptoms (headaches, fatigue, poor concentration) are your body telling you it needs fuel. That's why you're tired, why you have headaches, why you can't concentrate. To get better, we need to gradually increase your eating to at least 2,000-2,500 calories per day, with medical monitoring."
- **Practice:** Role-play delivering this message with a supervisor. Practice maintaining compassion while being medically clear. This is not punitive; it's life-saving.

### 2. **Establish Explicit Treatment Goals and Strategy**
- **Action:** Collaboratively establish clear, measurable treatment goals: (1) weight restoration to healthy BMI (18.5-24.9), (2) normalized eating patterns (3 meals + 2-3 snacks daily), (3) resolution of medical complications (return of menses, resolution of headaches/fatigue), (4) reduced excessive activity. Create a written treatment plan with specific targets and timelines.
- **Example:** "Our goal is to get your weight to at least 110 pounds over the next 3-4 months, with medical monitoring every 2 weeks. We'll work together to gradually increase your eating. Here's what we'll focus on: [written plan]. Does this make sense? What are your concerns?"
- **Practice:** Develop a template for eating disorder treatment plans. Practice presenting this to patients and families. Consult with a medical provider to establish appropriate weight restoration targets and monitoring protocols.

### 3. **Conduct Comprehensive Eating Disorder Assessment**
- **Action:** Systematically assess: (1) beliefs about eating, weight, and body ("What thoughts go through your mind when you consider eating more? What would happen if you gained weight?"), (2) specific barriers to eating (nausea, bloating, lack of appetite, cultural/religious restrictions, financial limitations), (3) compensatory behaviors (excessive activity, food rituals), (4) medical history (amenorrhea duration, other complications), (5) family dynamics and cultural context, (6) motivation for change.
- **Example:** "I want to understand what makes eating difficult for you. You mentioned feeling bloated and nauseous after eating. Can you describe that in detail? When does it happen? How long does it last? What do you do about it? Have you tried anything to manage it?"
- **Practice:** Use a structured eating disorder assessment tool (e.g., EDE-Q, SCOFF). Practice asking about sensitive topics (body image, compensatory behaviors) with compassion and directness.

### 4. **Address Postprandial Discomfort Systematically**
- **Action:** Postprandial bloating and nausea are major barriers to eating and must be addressed. Develop a plan: (1) educate about refeeding syndrome and normal GI adaptation, (2) use graded exposure to eating with systematic desensitization, (3) teach symptom management (pacing, positioning, distraction), (4) monitor for medical complications, (5) consider medical consultation if symptoms are severe.
- **Example:** "Your stomach is not used to food right now because you've been eating very little. When you start eating more, your stomach may feel uncomfortable at first. This is normal and will improve as your body adapts. Let's start with small amounts and gradually increase. We can also try eating slowly, sitting upright, and taking a short walk after eating to help with digestion."
- **Practice:** Learn about refeeding syndrome and GI adaptation in malnutrition. Practice graded exposure protocols. Develop a symptom management handout for patients.

### 5. **Implement Structured Meal Planning**
- **Action:** Do not suggest vague "small snacks." Create a specific, written meal plan with: (1) target calories (gradually increased), (2) specific foods, (3) meal times, (4) portion sizes, (5) strategies for managing discomfort. Involve a registered dietitian if possible.
- **Example:** "Here's your meal plan for this week: [specific meals and snacks with times and portions]. Monday breakfast: 1 cup oatmeal with banana and milk (300 calories). Mid-morning snack: yogurt and granola (150 calories). Lunch: rice and beans (400 calories)..." Provide written copy.
- **Practice:** Collaborate with a dietitian to develop meal plans for eating disorder patients. Learn about nutritional rehabilitation protocols. Practice explaining meal plans to patients and troubleshooting barriers.

### 6. **Use Behavioral Experiments, Not Just Cognitive Restructuring**
- **Action:** For anorexia nervosa, behavioral change precedes cognitive change. Design behavioral experiments to test beliefs and build tolerance: (1) "Let's try eating a small meal and observe what happens. You predict you'll feel very bloated. Let's see what actually happens." (2) "Let's gradually increase your activity level and see if your energy improves." (3) "Let's track your mood and energy on days you eat more vs. less."
- **Example:** "This week, I'd like you to try eating a full breakfast on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. Before each meal, write down what you predict will happen (e.g., 'I'll feel very bloated'). After eating, rate your actual bloating on a 0-10 scale. Let's see if your predictions match reality."
- **Practice:** Design behavioral experiments for common eating disorder beliefs. Practice explaining the rationale clearly. Learn to troubleshoot when experiments don't go as planned.
