
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly uses judgmental language that directly contradicts MI spirit. In the second exchange, the clinician states: "Look, you really need to stop making excuses" and "you're just making excuses and not taking responsibility"—language that labels the client's ambivalence as moral failure rather than a normal part of change readiness. The psychiatrist frames Anika's hesitation as weakness ("You have to be strong") and suggests she is "letting down your team," which is shame-inducing rather than affirming.

Most critically, the psychiatrist never acknowledges Anika's legitimate concerns, her partial commitment to change, or her strengths (she identifies basketball as a value, recognizes performance impacts, rates confidence at 7/10, and articulates a concrete goal). Instead, the clinician dismisses these as "excuses" and "procrastination." There is no warmth, no normalization of ambivalence as a natural part of adolescent decision-making, and no affirmation of her autonomy or worth as a person. The repeated ultimatums ("Are you ready to quit today or not?") communicate conditional regard—acceptance only if she complies immediately.

The psychiatrist's opening statement—"I'm here to help you make the right choices"—frames the clinician as arbiter of "right" choices, positioning Anika as needing correction rather than as a capable person with inherent wisdom about her own life.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal acceptance, using judgmental language, shame induction, and conditional regard. No affirmation of client strengths or autonomy is present. This directly undermines MI spirit and damages therapeutic alliance.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective. This psychiatrist demonstrates almost none. When Anika articulates her stress triggers ("especially when I'm stressed or feeling down. That's when I really want to vape"), the clinician does not reflect this or explore it. Instead, the response pivots immediately to confrontation: "You really need to stop making excuses."

Instead, the clinician repeatedly insists: "Waiting until the season starts is just procrastinating" and "There's no point in waiting or making plans." This is not empathy—it is dismissal of the client's lived experience and reasoning. True empathy would involve reflecting: "You're worried that if you quit cold turkey without a plan for stress, you might slip up and feel worse about yourself. **Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal empathic listening, fails to reflect client statements, dismisses her reasoning, and never explores her perspective or emotional experience. No complex reflections are present.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI means maintaining strategic focus on the target behavior (vaping reduction/cessation) while remaining client-centered. This psychiatrist does maintain focus on vaping—the session does not wander. However, the direction is imposed rather than collaborative, and it is rigid rather than strategic.

The psychiatrist's direction is: "Quit today, throw away the vape pen, commit now." This is a single, inflexible target with no flexibility, no exploration of Anika's readiness, and no strategic sequencing. In contrast, Anika has already articulated a more nuanced direction: reduce vaping during basketball season, develop stress-management alternatives, and build confidence through a structured plan.

A strategically directed MI session would acknowledge Anika's stated goal (quit for basketball season) as a legitimate direction and explore how to strengthen it, remove barriers, and build commitment over time. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly rejects her direction and imposes an alternative without collaborative exploration.

The psychiatrist does not ask clarifying questions about what would help Anika succeed, what barriers exist, or how to build her confidence. There is no strategic sequencing—no movement from exploring ambivalence to strengthening commitment to planning concrete steps. Instead, there is repetitive confrontation about the same demand.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains focus on vaping but imposes a rigid, non-collaborative direction without strategic exploration of readiness or barriers. No flexibility or client-centered sequencing is evident.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Adopt MI Spirit: Shift from Expert to Partner**
- **Action:** Fundamentally reframe your role from "expert who tells clients what to do" to "collaborative partner who helps clients discover their own reasons for change."
- **Example:** Instead of "You have to quit now," say: "You've mentioned wanting to play better and not get suspended. Tell me more about what's most important to you right now. How do those goals fit together?"
- **Practice:** Before each session, remind yourself: "My job is to evoke, not impose. The client's wisdom about their own life is more powerful than my advice." Record yourself in practice sessions and count how many times you tell vs. ask. Aim for 70% questions, 30% statements.

---

### 2. **Cultivate Genuine Empathy Through Reflective Listening**
- **Action:** When the client speaks, pause and reflect back what you hear—both the content and the emotion—before responding with your own perspective.
- **Example:** When Anika says "I'd feel better if I had a plan on how to handle stress without vaping before I just quit cold turkey," reflect: "So you're thinking that if you had a solid plan for managing stress first, you'd feel more confident about quitting. You want to set yourself up to succeed, not fail. That makes sense."
- **Practice:** Use the formula: "What I hear you saying is [content] and it sounds like you're feeling [emotion]. Is that right?" Practice this in every session until it becomes natural. This demonstrates genuine understanding and builds alliance.

---

### 3. **Amplify Change Talk Instead of Confronting It**
- **Action:** When the client expresses motivation for change, lean into it. Ask questions that deepen and strengthen it. Never contradict or dismiss it.
- **Example:** When Anika says "I play better when I'm not high," respond: "That's really important. Tell me more about what you notice when you're not high. How does your game change? What does that tell you about yourself?"
- **Practice:** Identify all instances of change talk in your sessions (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment). For each one, write down a follow-up question that would deepen it. Practice asking these questions until they feel natural.

---

### 4. **Support Autonomy: Respect Client Timeline and Offer Choice**
- **Action:** Stop demanding immediate change. Instead, respect the client's readiness and explore what would help them succeed on their own timeline.
- **Example:** Instead of "You have to quit today," say: "You've mentioned thinking about quitting when basketball season starts. That's a concrete goal. What would help you stick with that plan? What might get in the way?"
- **Practice:** In every session, explicitly affirm client choice: "This is your decision to make. I can share information, but ultimately, you get to decide what's right for you and when you're ready." Notice how this shifts the dynamic from confrontation to collaboration.

---

### 5. **Replace Shame-Based Language with Acceptance and Affirmation**
- **Action:** Eliminate judgmental language ("making excuses," "not taking responsibility," "you have to be strong"). Replace it with affirmation of the client's strengths and autonomy.
- **Example:** Instead of "You're just making excuses," say: "I can hear that you're thinking carefully about this. You want to set yourself up to succeed. That shows real wisdom about yourself."
- **Practice:** Before sessions, identify shame-based phrases you commonly use. Write down MI-consistent alternatives. Practice saying them aloud until they feel natural. Ask colleagues to give you feedback on your language.

---

### 6. **Use Strategic Questioning to Evoke Client Motivation**
- **Action:** Ask open-ended questions designed to draw out the client's own reasons for change, not to impose your reasons.
- **Example:** "What matters most to you about basketball?" "What would it feel like to play at your best?" "How do you see vaping fitting into your goals?" "What do you think would help you succeed?"
- **Practice:** Develop a repertoire of evocative questions. Practice asking them in role-plays until they feel natural. Focus on genuine curiosity—you're not trying to trick the client into agreeing with you; you're genuinely interested in their perspective.

---

### 7. **Handle Sustain Talk with Double-Sided Reflections and Reframing**
- **Action:** When the client expresses reasons not to change, acknowledge both sides of their ambivalence. Reframe the underlying need in a way that supports change.
- **Example:** When Anika says "It helps me relax and have fun with my friends," respond: "So relaxing and connecting with friends is really important to you. Those are good things. I'm curious—are there other ways you could relax and have fun with your friends that wouldn't risk your basketball?"
- **Practice:** Practice double-sided reflections: "Part of you [sustain talk], and part of you [change talk]. Both of those are real." This normalizes ambivalence and gently shifts focus toward change.

---

### 8. **Collaborate on Goal-Setting Rather Than Imposing It**
- **Action:** When the client articulates a goal (like Anika's plan to quit for basketball season), explore it collaboratively rather than rejecting it.
- **Example:** "You've mentioned wanting to quit when basketball season starts. That's a clear goal. Let's think about that together. What would help you stick with that? What might get in the way? How could we prepare you to succeed?"
- **Practice:** In sessions, when a client proposes a goal, pause and ask: "What do you think would help you succeed with that?" and "What barriers might come up?" This positions you as a collaborative problem-solver, not an authority figure.

### 9. **Provide Information in a Collaborative, Non-Confrontational Way**
- **Action:** Share health information, but do so as a partner offering perspective, not as an expert imposing truth. Ask permission and invite the client's reaction.
- **Example:** "I want to share some information about vaping that might be helpful. Is that okay? [Wait for permission.] Research shows that vaping can affect lung function and athletic performance. I'm curious—does that match what you've noticed in your own experience?"
- **Practice:** Before giving information, ask: "Would it be helpful if I shared what I know about this?" After sharing, ask: "What do you make of that?" This invites the client to integrate information in their own way rather than passively receiving it.
