### Acceptance

Uses judgmental language ("making excuses," "not taking responsibility") that labels client ambivalence as moral failure. No affirmation of client strengths (7/10 confidence, concrete basketball goal, identified performance impact). Shame-inducing statements ("letting down your team") and conditional regard communicated through repeated ultimatums. Opens with "I'm here to help you make the right choices," positioning clinician as arbiter of correctness rather than affirming client autonomy and worth.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1

When client articulates stress triggers ("especially when I'm stressed or feeling down"), clinician pivots to confrontation rather than exploring. Client's sophisticated reasoning about timing and stress-management planning is never reflected or validated. Misses client's ambivalence entirely—fails to acknowledge both social connection value and athletic performance concerns. No complex reflections present. Dismisses rather than demonstrates understanding of client's lived experience and reasoning.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Maintains focus on target behavior (vaping) but direction is imposed and rigid rather than collaborative and strategic. Single inflexible target: "quit today, throw away vape pen, commit now." No exploration of readiness or strategic sequencing. Client has articulated more nuanced direction (quit during basketball season, develop stress-management alternatives, build confidence through structured plan), which is rejected without collaborative exploration. ### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Demonstrates authoritarian, expert-driven stance throughout. Opening statement establishes clinician as arbiter of "right choices." Language is consistently directive ("You really should," "You have to," "You need to") rather than invitational. When client offers perspective about needing stress-management plan, clinician states "There's no point in waiting or making plans"—explicitly rejecting client input. Client attempts collaboration multiple times ("Can we talk about that?" "What do you think?") but receives no genuine engagement. No shared decision-making, no "we," no co-construction of solutions. Unilateral power and control maintained throughout.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1

Frames non-compliance as moral failure ("making excuses," "not taking responsibility"). Threat-based statements ("risk losing your spot on the team," "damaging your health permanently") are scare tactics designed to coerce compliance. Client explicitly asserts autonomy ("I'm not gonna just throw it away today") and clinician escalates pressure rather than respecting this boundary. No language supporting client choice or ownership of change process.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Demonstrates minimal evocation; instead imposes reasons for change and dismisses client's own reasons. Client spontaneously generates substantial change talk ("I play better when I'm not high," "I want to succeed," "I'm about a seven out of ten confident," "I'm serious about quitting") but clinician ignores all of it. No strategic amplification of motivation. When client articulates own resources ("I'd feel better if I had a plan on how to handle stress"), clinician dismisses them: "There's no point in waiting or making plans." Imposes external reasons ("Your coach will notice," "You risk losing your spot") rather than evoking internal motivation. Almost no genuine curiosity or questioning designed to elicit client wisdom.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Assumes expert role and denies client's expertise about her own life. Opening statement positions psychiatrist as arbiter of "right choices." Repeated directives assume clinician knows what is best without collaborative exploration. When client articulates sophisticated self-knowledge (stress triggers, need for plan, importance of timing), clinician dismisses as "excuses" and "procrastination." Client's legitimate insight that she plays better when not high is never built upon. Fails to acknowledge client's self-knowledge about her own functioning. Rejects client's repeated attempts to engage in partnership. Maintains unilateral authority throughout; no genuine power-sharing occurs.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Fails to cultivate, amplify, or reinforce substantial change talk spontaneously generated by client. Client expresses: desire ("I want to play well," "I want to succeed"), ability ("I'm about a seven out of ten confident," "I'm pretty sure I can stop for a month"), and commitment ("I'm serious about quitting," "I'm willing to try stopping when basketball season begins"). Clinician ignores all change talk and pivots to confrontation. Actually weakens change talk through contradiction—when client states 7/10 confidence, clinician responds "Waiting until the season starts is just procrastinating," undermining stated confidence. No reflections deepen change talk. No questions explore client's own motivation. Clinician imposes external reasons instead of amplifying internal ones.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Attempts to address sustain talk through information-giving about vaping's harms ("just as bad as smoking," "damaging your lungs and performance"), which is legitimate MI technique. However, approach is confrontational rather than collaborative. Uses judgment ("You really should quit," "You need to take this seriously") that triggers defensiveness rather than openness. Sustain talk actually increases across session—client becomes more entrenched in her position that quitting today is unrealistic. No double-sided reflections acknowledging both sides of ambivalence. No reframing of underlying needs. No strategic redirection exploring how basketball season focus could support quitting. Confrontation hardens rather than softens sustain talk. By session end, client's sustain talk has solidified; she reaffirms her own timeline rather than adopting clinician's demand.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.1/5**

**Session Embodies MI Spirit:** ☐ Yes     ☒ No

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation for Change:** Very Low – Session likely to **decrease intrinsic motivation** and **increase psychological reactance**

---

### Key Observations

**Strengths:**
- Maintains focus on target behavior (vaping) throughout session
- Provides factually accurate information about vaping's health risks
- Appropriately identifies real risks to client's health and athletic participation

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Pervasive judgment and shame:** Labels client as irresponsible, weak, self-sabotaging
- **Coercion and threat:** Uses ultimatums and fear-based messaging to control behavior
- **Dismissal of client expertise:** Rejects client's sophisticated self-knowledge as "excuses"
- **Failure to evoke:** Ignores substantial change talk; imposes external reasons instead
- **Rejection of collaboration:** Maintains unilateral control; rejects client's attempts at partnership
- **Escalating confrontation:** Repeats same demand with increasing intensity when client resists

---

### Impact on Therapeutic Relationship and Client Engagement

**Predicted outcomes:**
- Client unlikely to feel heard, understood, or respected
- Increased defensiveness and psychological reactance
- Client more entrenched in her timeline (basketball season) rather than adopting clinician's demand (today)
- Decreased likelihood of honest disclosure in future sessions
- Potential disengagement from treatment

**Paradoxical effect:** Clinician's pressure may actually **delay change.** Client has already articulated concrete goal and demonstrated confidence (7/10). Skilled MI would strengthen this commitment; instead, clinician attacks it, potentially causing client to defend her timeline more rigidly.

---

### Primary Recommendations for Improvement

1. **Shift from expert to partner:** Reframe role from "telling what to do" to "collaboratively exploring"
2. **Develop genuine empathy:** Practice reflective listening; pause to understand before responding
3. **Amplify change talk:** Lean into client motivation; ask questions that deepen it; never contradict it
4. **Support autonomy:** Respect client's timeline and choices; offer information, not demands
5. **Adopt MI spirit:** Recognize client's intrinsic motivation is more powerful than external pressure
6. **Seek formal MI training:** Attend MINT-certified workshop or evidence-based MI training program
7. **Replace shame-based language:** Eliminate judgmental phrases; practice affirmation and acceptance
8. **Use strategic questioning:** Develop evocative questions designed to draw out client's own reasons for change

---

**Clinician should fundamentally restructure approach from confrontation to collaboration, from imposing to evoking, and from expert authority to genuine partnership to effectively engage this client and support sustainable behavior change.**
