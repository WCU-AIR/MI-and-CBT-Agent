# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance throughout. Validates client's perspective early ("vaping is more of a social and relaxing activity") without confrontation. Frequently affirms client strengths ("That's a pretty insightful observation," "I really appreciate you sharing that"). Normalizes ambivalence without labeling client as irresponsible. Minor opportunity: could more explicitly affirm autonomy regarding scope of change (permanent cessation vs. seasonal break).

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates solid reflective listening with accurate, multi-dimensional reflections early in session. Captures functional role of vaping ("intertwined with your social life and a way to unwind") and emotional nuance of ambivalence ("tension between what vaping gives you and what you want to achieve in sports"). Later reflections become somewhat more straightforward. Misses opportunity to deepen exploration when client says "Sometimes I don't even realize why I want to vape until I actually say it out loud"—could have explored underlying emotions more fully. Surface-level response to client's nervousness about talking to Sarah.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintains appropriate and strategic focus on target behavior (vaping during basketball season) throughout session. Opening question establishes client-centered direction while allowing client to set agenda. Progressively guides conversation from general ambivalence to concrete planning (identifying Sarah as support, establishing check-ins, crafting conversation starter). Subtle shift toward prescriptive direction when suggesting specific language for talking to Sarah. Session focuses primarily on one-month basketball season goal without exploring longer-term direction beyond the season.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates strong collaborative stance with frequent joint problem-solving. Opening establishes shared agenda-setting rather than expert-driven approach. Uses collaborative language throughout ("What do you think it would be like...?" "How about we think together...?"). Incorporates client's ideas and allows them to shape session direction. Builds on client's identification of Sarah as support rather than introducing alternative strategies. One minor instance: suggests specific language for talking to Sarah before fully exploring what client might generate independently, though client does adapt this to her own style.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently affirms client's right to choose and emphasizes ownership of change process. Opening statement ("I want to make sure this time is really about what matters most to you") establishes respect for autonomy. Validates both options when client expresses ambivalence about quitting "for good or just take a break during the season." Repeatedly emphasizes client's agency and capability ("shows your ability to take control and make changes when you set your mind to it"). Minor opportunities: avoid prescriptive suggestions regarding language for Sarah conversation; explicitly acknowledge client's right to not change or continue vaping after basketball season.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates moderate evocation of client's own motivations and resources but lacks strategic amplification of change talk. Early evocative questions invite exploration ("What do you notice about how vaping fits into your life...?"). However, when client spontaneously generates change talk ("I play better when I'm not high," "I'm pretty sure I can stop for a month"), psychiatrist acknowledges but doesn't consistently deepen or amplify. Misses opportunities to explore values and past successes more thoroughly. Does not systematically evoke four types of change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need). Focus remains somewhat surface-level on practical planning rather than values exploration.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates strong partnership throughout most of session, conveying that expertise and wisdom about change reside within client. Opening positions client as expert on her own priorities. Consistently uses inclusive language and shares power ("How about we think together...?" "What do you think would help you most?"). Validates client's insights and decision-making. Respects client's expertise by building on suggestions rather than overriding them (affirms casual approach to talking with Sarah). One minor instance where partnership becomes slightly unbalanced: suggests specific language before fully exploring what client might generate. Does not explicitly invite client's input on overall approach or ask "How would you like me to help you?"

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates moderate cultivation of change talk but with significant missed opportunities for strategic amplification and reinforcement. Client generates substantial change talk throughout ("I play better when I'm not high," "I was thinking to not use when basketball season starts," "I'm pretty sure I can stop for a month," "I can do that again this time too"). Psychiatrist acknowledges this change talk but doesn't consistently amplify or reinforce through follow-up questions. When client identifies past success ("I actually felt better playing"), psychiatrist could have asked deeper questions about what enabled that success. Does not systematically evoke different types of change talk. Reinforces confidence when client rates herself 7/10 and 8/10, but this represents only one dimension of change talk cultivation.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates adequate but inconsistent skill in softening sustain talk. Early sustain talk is addressed skillfully through double-sided reflection ("It sounds like vaping is more of a social and relaxing activity...What do you notice about how vaping fits into your life...?"). However, when client continues to emphasize benefits ("vaping with friends is fun, and it helps with stress"), psychiatrist validates without strategically redirecting. Misses opportunity to ask: "How might you get that stress relief and fun through other activities with Sarah?" When client expresses ambivalence about quitting "for good or just take a break," psychiatrist validates but doesn't use strategic reframing to emphasize change side. Later sustain talk is not consistently addressed through reframing and strategic questioning.

---

## Overall Session Summary

**Current Competency Level:** Competent, client-centered practice with good foundational MI skills (3.7/5)

**Key Strengths:**
- ✓ Strong opening and collaborative stance from beginning
- ✓ Consistent acceptance and genuine warmth throughout
- ✓ Solid reflective listening with accurate, emotionally attuned reflections
- ✓ Effective use of double-sided reflections acknowledging both benefits and costs
- ✓ Practical planning and concrete goal-setting
- ✓ Autonomy support and client confidence building (7/10 → 8/10)

**Primary Areas for Development:**
- ⚠ Insufficient amplification of change talk—acknowledge but don't deepen client motivation
- ⚠ Limited strategic evocation—don't systematically elicit desire, ability, reasons, and need
- ⚠ Occasional prescriptive moments—suggest solutions before fully evoking client ideas
- ⚠ Inconsistent softening of sustain talk—validate without strategic redirection
- ⚠ Narrow focus on short-term goals—limited exploration of long-term change beyond basketball season
- ⚠ Limited values clarification—miss opportunities to connect change to intrinsic motivation

**Recommendation:** With focused practice on change talk amplification, strategic evocation, and values exploration, this clinician has clear potential to advance from competent to advanced MI practice.
