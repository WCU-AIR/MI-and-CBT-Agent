# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Inconsistent acceptance demonstrated throughout session. Initial warmth present ("It's good to see you today"), but middle exchanges (2-5) contain significant lapses with directive, judgmental language ("You really need to focus... You must start making better choices"). Repeated use of "you must," "you have to," and "you really should" conveys conditional regard contingent on compliance. Improved acceptance emerges in final exchanges with validation of client perspective ("I appreciate you sharing your perspective"). Avoids labeling client as "addict" showing some restraint. Critical failure: extended middle section undermines therapeutic alliance through disapproving tone and pressure tactics.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal empathic reflection in critical middle exchanges. When client expresses core concerns about peer belonging and feasibility of abstinence, clinician responds with pressure rather than reflection, showing no effort to understand client's internal frame of reference. Significant missed opportunity in exchange 3 when client offers spontaneous change talk ("I don't like feeling sick... I get into trouble") but clinician dismisses it by pivoting to directive pressure. Turning point in exchange 6 with genuine reflection: "It sounds like belonging and social connections are really important to you, and that quitting cold turkey feels overwhelming." This demonstrates clinician can provide accurate, complex reflection but arrives too late. Final exchanges show improved empathy with validation of client's plan. Damage to alliance substantial due to early empathy failures.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clear focus on target behavior (alcohol use) maintained throughout session without topic drift. However, manner of maintaining direction is problematic in exchanges 2-5, where direction pursued through directive pressure rather than collaborative exploration. Clinician repeatedly insists on abstinence as only acceptable goal ("Cutting back isn't enough... You have to quit completely"), which is directive rather than client-centered. Critical shift in exchange 6 where direction becomes collaborative: "How about we work together to come up with a plan that feels manageable for you?" Final exchanges continue collaborative approach with strategic questioning ("What's one thing you feel confident trying this week?"). Demonstrates that direction can be maintained without coercion, but required five exchanges to learn this approach.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal collaboration for most of session with clinician adopting expert-driven, hierarchical stance. Exchange 2 explicitly positions psychiatrist as authority: "Trust me, you'll feel a lot better if you just do it." Exchanges 3-5 continue pattern of rejecting client's proposals without exploration—when client proposes cutting back, clinician overrides suggestion with expert pronouncements ("You have to quit completely"). No exploration of why client thinks cutting back might work or incorporation of client's reasoning into plan. Stark contrast emerges in exchange 6 with language shift: "work together," "feels manageable for you," "What do you think?" Signal genuine collaboration. Exchanges 7-8 maintain collaborative stance with validation of client's own ideas rather than imposing expert solutions. Session demonstrates clinician learned during interaction but lacked this skill initially.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually absent throughout most of session with controlling language that minimizes choice and emphasizes obligation. Consistent prescriptive pattern: "You really need... You must... Trust me... you have to take this seriously... You have to quit completely—no exceptions... You need to have the willpower... You have to be strong and committed... If you don't, things will only get worse... It's time to make that choice." Language communicates psychiatrist has decided what client must do; client's role is compliance, not choice. When client proposes cutting back, clinician explicitly rejects autonomous choice: "You have to quit completely." Repeated questions "Are you ready to make that change now?" frame change as psychiatrist-decided necessity, not client choice. Only moment of autonomy support appears in exchange 6: "feels manageable for you" acknowledges client's right to define feasibility. Client's final statement—"Thanks for not making me feel like I have to just quit instantly"—reveals experience of earlier exchanges as controlling and coercive. Single late moment cannot offset extensive autonomy-undermining language.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal evocation for most of session with clinician providing reasons for change and prescribing solutions rather than drawing out client's own motivations. Exchange 2 demonstrates pattern: when client says he doesn't think much about health, clinician tells him what to do rather than asking questions to evoke his own concerns. Exchanges 3-5 continue pattern of telling why client should quit without asking questions to evoke client's own recognition of risks or values. Critical missed opportunity in exchange 3 when client spontaneously offers change talk: "I don't like feeling sick or throwing up... I get into trouble more than I should." Excellent material for evocation, but clinician doesn't explore further; instead pivots to telling client he must quit completely, essentially dismissing change talk offered. Turning point in exchange 6 with evocative questions: "How about we look at what's working and what's not?... What do you think might make it easier?... What's one thing you feel confident trying this week?" When asked, client immediately generates rich material and solutions, demonstrating evocative questions work and were needed much earlier.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Largely absent in first half of session with clinician assuming expert role and positioning client as someone needing to be told what to do. Exchange 2 explicitly claims expert authority: "Trust me, you'll feel a lot better if you just do it." Positions clinician as authority who knows best with implicit message that client's judgment is insufficient. Exchanges 3-5 continue pattern: clinician repeatedly overrides client's contributions without exploring reasoning. When client proposes cutting back, psychiatrist says "cutting back is just not enough... You have to quit completely" without incorporating client's perspective into plan. Language throughout reflects hierarchical rather than partnership-based interaction: "You really need," "You must," "You have to," "Trust me" all position psychiatrist as authority making decisions for client, not with client. Shift to partnership begins in exchange 6: "work together," "feels manageable for you," "What do you think?" Exchanges 7-8 maintain partnership with validation of client's plan rather than imposing expert solutions. Extensive early exchanges without partnership have already damaged therapeutic relationship; client had to repeatedly defend position because clinician wasn't treating him as partner.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill demonstrated with clinician actually undermining change talk when it spontaneously emerges. Exchange 3 contains excellent change talk: "I don't like feeling sick or throwing up... I get into trouble more than I should... Maybe I'd like not feeling so crappy the next day." Direct acknowledgment of negative consequences and desire for change. However, clinician doesn't amplify or reinforce this change talk; instead pivots to directive pressure, essentially dismissing client's own motivation. Skilled MI response would have been: "So the morning-after sickness really bothers you. Tell me more about that. What else concerns you?" This would cultivate and strengthen change talk. Exchanges 4-5 show similar missed opportunities: when client says "I'm not saying I never think about quitting," this change talk could be explored and amplified, but clinician responds with pressure instead. Turning point in exchange 6 with evocative question: "What's one thing you feel confident trying this week?" This invites client to articulate own commitment and confidence. Exchange 7 shows strong change talk: "I'd like to not feel sick or get in trouble all the time... That sounds like something I could manage." Clinician reinforces: "That sounds like a solid plan to me... Remember, it's all about making choices that help you feel better." However, reinforcement somewhat weak; more skilled response would further cultivate change talk through follow-up questions.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually absent with clinician using confrontational approaches that amplify rather than soften client arguments for status quo. Exchange 3 contains sustain talk: "Drinking with my friends is how we have a good time, so it's a trade-off." Skilled response would use double-sided reflection acknowledging ambivalence: "So on one hand, drinking is how you connect with friends and have fun, and on the other hand, you're tired of feeling sick and getting in trouble. Both matter to you." Instead, clinician responds with directive pressure: "You really should just stop drinking altogether. Cutting back isn't enough." This directly confronts sustain talk without acknowledging validity, which typically strengthens rather than softens it. Exchange 4 contains legitimate sustain talk about feasibility and peer relationships: "It's just social... I don't know if quitting completely would work... I've seen people try and just end up going back or feeling left out." Rather than softening, clinician responds with more pressure: "You're risking your health, your future, and your relationships... You need to have the willpower to say no." Confrontational approach teaches client to argue more strongly for drinking. Exchange 5 offers core concern: "It's about feeling like I belong and not wanting to be the odd one out." Skilled response would acknowledge: "So belonging is really important to you, and you're worried quitting would put that at risk. That makes sense." Instead, clinician responds with pressure. Turning point in exchange 6 with some skill: "It sounds like belonging and social connections are really important to you, and that quitting cold turkey feels overwhelming." This reflection acknowledges sustain talk without confronting it. Clinician then explores alternatives: "How about we work together to come up with a plan that feels manageable for you?" More skillful approach, but appears only after five exchanges of confrontation that amplified sustain talk.

## Overall Session Assessment

**Overall Score: 1.9/5**

### Summary of Strengths:
- Clear focus on target behavior (alcohol use) maintained throughout without topic drift
- Significant and genuine shift toward collaborative, client-centered practice by exchange 6
- Demonstrates openness to learning and responsiveness to client feedback
- Validates client's own solutions rather than imposing expert recommendations in final exchanges
- Realistic, client-generated goal-setting increases likelihood of follow-through

### Summary of Areas for Improvement:
- **Extensive directive pressure** in exchanges 2-5 with repeated "you must," "you have to," "you really should" violates MI collaborative spirit and triggers defensive sustain talk
- **Failure to evoke spontaneous change talk** when client offers it in exchange 3; clinician dismisses rather than explores client's own motivation
- **Confrontation of sustain talk** rather than softening; directly confronting concerns about peer relationships and feasibility strengthens rather than weakens sustain talk
- **Minimal empathic reflection** particularly regarding client's legitimate concerns about social isolation and difficulty of cold-turkey abstinence
- **Autonomy undermining** through controlling language and dismissal of client's proposed alternatives
- **Missed partnership opportunities** with clinician assuming expert role rather than positioning client as partner with valuable self-knowledge
- **Late-stage skill demonstration** with MI skills appearing only in exchange 6 when they should have been present from beginning

### Critical Recommendations:
1. Eliminate directive language; replace with collaborative, choice-affirming framing
2. Develop skill in evoking and amplifying spontaneous change talk (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
3. Learn to soften sustain talk through acknowledgment and reframing rather than confrontation
4. Strengthen empathic reflection, particularly around core client concerns
5. Establish true partnership by valuing client expertise about their own life
6. Use autonomy-supportive language consistently throughout sessions
7. Implement mid-session check-ins to assess alliance and adjust approach
8. Seek supervision and record sessions for fidelity review using MITI scale
