
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. The psychiatrist's opening statement—"We need to get straight to the point" followed by "You really should quit those habits because they're harmful"—immediately establishes a judgmental, prescriptive stance that violates the foundational MI principle of acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly uses language that conveys disapproval: "You really need to quit completely," "Why didn't you stop already if you know it's harmful?", "Why are you making excuses instead of just doing what's right?", and "No, listen, you don't get to decide that."

Most critically, the psychiatrist explicitly denies Margaret's autonomy and personhood by stating "No, listen, you don't get to decide that. Cutting down is just making excuses." This is not merely judgmental—it is dehumanizing and directly contradicts MI's core principle that clients possess inherent worth and dignity. The psychiatrist frames Margaret's reasonable hesitation as moral failure ("making excuses," "not taking this seriously") rather than as understandable ambivalence. There is no warmth, no affirmation of her strengths (her willingness to engage, her social connections, her self-awareness about retirement changes), and no genuine support for her as a person struggling with a complex behavior.

The only moment where acceptance appears is in the later exchange ("Thanks for sharing that. I understand that change can feel overwhelming..."), but this represents a dramatic and unexplained shift in approach rather than consistent demonstration of acceptance throughout the session. This single moment cannot redeem the pervasive judgmental stance that dominated the majority of the interaction.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal acceptance, relying instead on judgment, prescription, and explicit denial of the client's autonomy. This fundamentally undermines the MI spirit and creates a confrontational dynamic that is likely to increase client defensiveness and sustain talk.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening, genuine effort to understand the client's perspective, and capturing emotional nuance. This session contains virtually none of these elements. The psychiatrist asks closed, directive questions ("Are you currently smoking or drinking alcohol?", "Are you ready to commit to quitting?", "Are you going to stop drinking entirely starting today?") that demand compliance rather than explore Margaret's experience.

**Summary:**

### 5. Autonomy Support

## Recommendations

### 1. **Adopt a Genuine Partnership Stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Begin every session by explicitly positioning the client as the expert on their own life. Use language that conveys genuine curiosity about the client's perspective rather than judgment about their choices.
- **Example:** Instead of "You really should quit those habits because they're harmful," say: "I'm interested in understanding your perspective on your drinking. What's your sense of how it's affecting you? What concerns, if any, do you have about it?"
- **Practice:** Before your next session, write down three opening statements that convey genuine curiosity rather than judgment. Practice saying them aloud until they feel natural. Record yourself and listen for tone—are you genuinely curious or subtly judgmental?

### 2. **Eliminate Controlling Language and Explicit Autonomy Violations**
- **Action:** Remove phrases like "You have to," "You must," "You need to," "You should," and "You don't get to decide." Replace them with language that respects choice: "You might consider," "Some people find it helpful to," "What do you think about," "What feels doable to you?"
- **Example:** Instead of "No, listen, you don't get to decide that. Cutting down is just making excuses," say: "I hear that cutting down feels more realistic to you right now. Help me understand what makes that feel more doable than quitting completely. What would be the benefits of cutting down for you?"
- **Practice:** Identify your most common controlling phrases. Create a list of autonomy-supportive alternatives. Practice using these alternatives in role-plays until they become automatic.

### 3. **Develop Genuine Empathic Listening and Reflection Skills**
- **Action:** When a client shares information, pause and reflect back what you heard, focusing on emotion and meaning rather than just facts. Use complex reflections that deepen insight.
- **Example:** When Margaret says "I enjoy going to the pub with friends. It's part of my social life," reflect: "So the pub isn't just about the drinking—it's about connection and belonging. That's important to you, especially now that you're retired and have more time." This shows you understand the deeper meaning.
- **Practice:** In your next session, aim for at least 5 reflections before you offer any advice or information. Record the session and count your reflections. Aim to increase this number in subsequent sessions.

### 4. **Learn to Evoke Rather Than Install Motivation for Change**
- **Action:** Ask open-ended questions that draw out the client's own reasons for change, rather than telling them why they should change. Focus on the client's values, goals, and concerns.
- **Example:** Instead of "You really should quit because it's harmful," ask: "What concerns do you have about your drinking? What would be different in your life if you reduced your drinking? What matters most to you about your health?"
- **Practice:** Develop a set of evocative questions for common presenting problems. Practice asking these questions in role-plays. Notice how clients respond differently when you ask rather than tell.

### 5. **Amplify and Reinforce Change Talk**
- **Action:** When clients produce change talk (statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment to change), reflect it back with emphasis and explore it further. This strengthens the client's own motivation.
- **Example:** When Margaret says "I guess I can cut down some," respond: "You're thinking you could reduce your drinking. What would that look like for you? What would be the benefits of cutting down?" This amplifies her change talk rather than dismissing it.
- **Practice:** In your next session, identify every instance of change talk. Write it down. Reflect it back to the client. Notice how this affects the client's subsequent statements.

### 6. **Develop Skillful Responses to Sustain Talk**
- **Action:** When clients express reasons for not changing, use techniques like double-sided reflection, reframing, and strategic redirection rather than direct confrontation.
- **Example:** When Margaret says "Seven drinks a week doesn't seem like that much," instead of arguing ("That's too much"), use a double-sided reflection: "On one hand, you see seven drinks as moderate compared to others. On the other hand, your doctor and your son have expressed concern, and you had that fall. What do you make of that discrepancy?"
- **Practice:** Develop responses to common sustain talk statements. Practice using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic redirection in role-plays.

### 7. **Respect Client Autonomy Even When You Disagree**
- **Action:** Acknowledge that clients have the right to make choices you disagree with. Express your concerns without trying to control their decisions.
- **Example:** Instead of "You have to quit completely," say: "I'm concerned about your health based on what I know about alcohol and falls. I'd like to work with you to find a plan that feels manageable to you. What are you willing to try?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, explicitly acknowledge the client's right to choose at least once. Notice how this affects the therapeutic relationship.

### 8. **Develop Awareness of Your Own Agenda and Biases**
- **Action:** Recognize that your strong belief that Margaret should quit completely is your agenda, not necessarily hers. Be curious about why you feel so strongly about this. Consider whether your agenda is serving the client or serving your own need to be "right."
- **Example:** Before your next session, ask yourself: "What am I hoping will happen in this session? Am I hoping the client will agree with me, or am I genuinely curious about their perspective? If they don't do what I think they should, how will I feel?"
- **Practice:** Seek supervision or consultation with an MI trainer. Discuss your reactions to clients who don't follow your recommendations. Explore whether your need to be right is interfering with your ability to be genuinely collaborative.

### 9. **Receive Formal MI Training**
- **Action:** Enroll in a comprehensive Motivational Interviewing training program. This session demonstrates fundamental misunderstandings of MI principles that require structured, supervised learning.
- **Example:** Look for training programs that include: (1) didactic instruction on MI principles, (2) video demonstrations of skillful MI, (3) practice with feedback, (4) review of your own recorded sessions with an MI trainer.
- **Practice:** After completing training, seek ongoing supervision. Record your sessions and review them with an MI trainer. Aim to achieve proficiency ratings of 4-5 on the MITI (Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity) scale.

### 10. **Develop Self-Compassion and Humility**
- **Action:** Recognize that this session reflects a common pattern in healthcare—the expert trying to convince the patient to do what's "right." This pattern is understandable but ineffective. Develop compassion for yourself as you learn a different approach.
- **Example:** Instead of judging yourself for this session, recognize: "I was trying to help, but my approach was counterproductive. I'm learning a better way. This is a normal part of professional development."
- **Practice:** After each session, reflect on what went well and what you'd do differently. Celebrate small improvements. Seek feedback from colleagues and supervisors. Remember that developing MI competence is a process that takes time and practice.
