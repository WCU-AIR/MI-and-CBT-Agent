# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Profound absence of unconditional positive regard. Psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses client concerns as "excuses" (6+ instances) and employs coercive, controlling language ("You don't get to say no here," "it's mandatory"). No affirmation of client strengths, willingness to attend, or courage in facing change. Conditional regard communicated—acceptance only if client complies immediately. Adversarial positioning undermines therapeutic relationship and violates MI's foundational principle of unconditional positive regard.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no reflective listening demonstrated. Client's identity-level concerns about smoking ("It's been a part of me for so long") are dismissed rather than explored. Psychiatrist fails to understand why client fears weight gain, withdrawal, or time constraints. No reflections capture emotional nuance. Client change talk ("That made me rethink everything") is ignored rather than explored. Critical missed opportunity when client states need to "do it in a way that I feel ready for"—this insight about her change process is overridden rather than validated. Fundamental failure to understand client's internal frame of reference.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Session maintains focus on smoking cessation and exercise as target behaviors, preventing score of 1/5. However, direction pursued through coercion and ultimatums rather than collaborative exploration. Psychiatrist imposes specific intervention (nicotine patches) and demands immediate implementation without exploring ambivalence, barriers, or client readiness. Rigid approach—when client suggests "cutting back first," psychiatrist dismisses this: "Cutting back is just prolonging the problem." No flexibility or exploration of alternative pathways. Strategic focus undermined by power struggle over compliance rather than collaborative problem-solving.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Entirely hierarchical, expert-driven stance with no genuine collaboration. Psychiatrist positions self as sole authority; client positioned as someone who must comply. Explicit rejection of client input: "You don't get to say no here," "No, you don't get to negotiate here," "I'm telling you—start the patches today." Rosa's repeated attempts to introduce her perspective are rejected or overridden. No genuine power-sharing. True collaboration would involve asking for client input, exploring her ideas, and co-constructing plan. Instead, session becomes power struggle where psychiatrist's agenda is imposed despite client's clear, repeated communication that she is not ready.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Systematically undermines autonomy through coercion, threats, and explicit removal of choice. Direct violations include: "You have to quit smoking immediately," "You don't get to say no here," "Starting the patches today isn't optional—it's mandatory," "If you don't do this now, you're just choosing to harm yourself." Never affirms client's freedom to choose. Threat frames non-compliance as self-harm, removing genuine choice and violating autonomy support. Escalating demands when client expresses need for time to prepare demonstrates active undermining of client's perception of control over change process.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Almost no evocation demonstrated. Client's change talk statements ("I know I've got to stop," "That made me rethink everything") are ignored or overshadowed by demands for immediate action. No exploration of what prompted health concerns or what client's own reasons for change might be. External reasons imposed ("Your health will seriously decline") rather than internal motivations evoked. Client's resources and strengths never affirmed—attendance, health awareness, willingness to keep talking, and insight into own change process all overlooked. No curiosity about client's values or what smoking provides in daily life. Fundamental failure to draw out intrinsic motivation.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Demonstrates opposite of partnership—complete assumption of expert authority with active suppression of client input. Psychiatrist claims unilateral expertise: "Listen, you need to stop worrying," "You have to stop overthinking this," "I'm telling you—start the patches today." Each time Rosa contributes her perspective, input is rejected or overridden. No genuine power-sharing. True partnership would acknowledge that Rosa knows her own life, fears, and capacity for change better than psychiatrist; would seek her input; would treat her suggestions seriously. Instead, session becomes power struggle where psychiatrist's agenda is imposed despite Rosa's repeated, clear communication about her needs and readiness.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal change talk from client and no effort to evoke or amplify it. Rosa's change talk statements ("I know I've got to stop," "That made me rethink everything," "I want to be healthy," "I'm willing to keep working with you") receive no reflections, deepening questions, or affirmations. Psychiatrist moves immediately to demands and threats, which suppresses further change talk. Client becomes defensive and focuses on explaining barriers rather than articulating reasons for change. No cultivation of confidence language. Psychiatrist dismisses doubt ("you have to stop being scared and just do it") rather than exploring it and building confidence collaboratively. Critical failure to amplify client's own motivation for change.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Attempts to address sustain talk through confrontation and dismissal rather than skillful softening. Responses include: "Those are just excuses people use," "you need to stop worrying," "Withdrawal and weight gain are minor issues." This approach actually strengthens sustain talk—client becomes more defensive and articulates concerns more firmly. By session end, client states firmly: "I'm not ready to start the patches today," stronger sustain talk than initial ambivalence. No double-sided reflections, reframing, or validation employed. Scores 2/5 rather than 1/5 because sustain talk is addressed (albeit counterproductively); complete absence would warrant 1/5. Confrontational approach increases resistance rather than softening it.

## Overall Session Assessment

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.1/5**

### Critical Findings:

**Strengths (Minimal):**
- Maintains consistent focus on smoking cessation as target behavior
- Recognizes medical urgency of smoking cessation

**Severe Deficiencies:**
- Authoritarian stance replacing collaboration throughout session
- Coercive language and explicit threats ("If you don't do this now, you're just choosing to harm yourself")
- Dismissal of legitimate client concerns as "excuses" (6+ instances)
- Complete absence of reflective listening
- Change talk ignored and suppressed rather than evoked
- Client autonomy actively undermined
- Resistance strengthened rather than softened

### Impact on Client Engagement:

**Negative trajectory:** Client enters session in contemplation (ambivalent but open); leaves more firmly entrenched in resistance. Psychiatrist has transformed ambivalence into defensiveness. Rosa is now **less likely** to quit smoking, less likely to return for follow-up care, and less likely to trust medical advice in future.

### Clinical Recommendation:

**This psychiatrist is not competent to deliver motivational interviewing.** The session demonstrates fundamental gap between stated MI approach and evidence-based MI practice. Immediate, intensive training and supervision required before continuing MI-based interventions. Current approach violates MI principles and causes harm by increasing resistance and decreasing intrinsic motivation for change.

**Most Critical Improvements Needed:**
1. Shift from directive/authoritarian to collaborative/partnership stance
2. Develop reflective listening as primary clinical tool
3. Eliminate all coercive language and threats
4. Learn to evoke rather than impose motivation
5. Respect and actively support client autonomy
