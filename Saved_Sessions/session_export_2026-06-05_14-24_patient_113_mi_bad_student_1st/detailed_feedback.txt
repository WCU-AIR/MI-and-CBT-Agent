
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

 # DETAILED CLINICAL EVALUATION
## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and contains multiple instances of explicit judgment and disapproval. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses Rosa's concerns as "excuses" (appearing 6+ times throughout the session: "you can't just keep making excuses," "Those are just excuses people use," "you're just making excuses to avoid taking real action," "you're just making excuses again"). This language is fundamentally antithetical to MI spirit and communicates to the client that her fears and ambivalence are not legitimate concerns worthy of understanding.

The psychiatrist also employs coercive, controlling language that strips away dignity: "You don't get to say no here," "No more arguing," "it's mandatory," "you have to," repeated 20+ times throughout the session. This authoritarian stance communicates conditional regard—acceptance only if Rosa complies immediately. When Rosa expresses fear about withdrawal and weight gain, the psychiatrist responds with dismissal ("Those are just excuses," "stop worrying," "stop being scared") rather than validation. There is no affirmation of Rosa's strengths, her willingness to attend the session, her insight into her own ambivalence, or her courage in facing difficult change. The psychiatrist never normalizes her struggle or acknowledges that ambivalence about major life changes is universal and understandable.

The repeated demand for immediate commitment to patches ("start the patches today—no ifs, ands, or buts") combined with threats ("If you don't do this now, you're just choosing to harm yourself") creates an adversarial rather than supportive relationship. Rosa is positioned as the problem to be fixed through force, not as a person with inherent worth deserving respect.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance. Judgment, dismissal, and coercion replace unconditional positive regard throughout. This approach directly contradicts MI's foundational principle and will likely increase client defensiveness and resistance.

---

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective from their internal frame of reference. This session contains almost no reflective listening and demonstrates minimal effort to understand Rosa's experience. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interprets her statements as resistance or avoidance rather than exploring their meaning.

When Rosa says, "I just can't imagine not going through life without smoking. It's been a part of me for so long," the psychiatrist does not reflect this identity-level concern or explore what smoking means to her. Instead, the response is dismissive: "you need to stop thinking it's complicated—it's really not." When Rosa articulates legitimate fears about withdrawal ("Withdrawal sounds awful"), the psychiatrist invalidates rather than explores: "you need to stop worrying about withdrawal and weight gain. Those are just excuses."

The psychiatrist never attempts to understand *why* Rosa fears weight gain (financial burden of new wardrobe, self-image concerns, compounded stress during withdrawal), *why* withdrawal feels so threatening, or what smoking provides in her daily life. There are no reflections that capture emotional nuance. Phrases like "It sounds like you're worried about managing multiple changes at once" or "You're concerned that quitting cold turkey might set you up to fail" are entirely absent.

The psychiatrist also fails to recognize Rosa's change talk. When she says, "I know I've got to stop" and "That made me rethink everything," these statements are not affirmed or explored—they are overshadowed by demands for immediate action. The psychiatrist misses the opportunity to understand what prompted this rethinking and to build on it collaboratively.

Most critically, when Rosa repeatedly expresses that being forced makes her "want to shut down" and that she needs to "do it in a way that I feel ready for," the psychiatrist does not reflect or explore this crucial insight about her own change process. Instead, the demands intensify, demonstrating a fundamental failure to understand the client's perspective.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no empathic listening. No reflections are offered, client concerns are dismissed rather than explored, and the psychiatrist shows no genuine curiosity about Rosa's internal experience or motivations. This represents a critical failure in MI practice.

---

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI means maintaining strategic focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation) while remaining client-centered and collaborative. This session does maintain focus on smoking and exercise as target behaviors, which prevents it from scoring 1/5. However, the direction is pursued through coercion and ultimatums rather than collaborative exploration, which fundamentally undermines effective MI practice.

The psychiatrist clearly identifies the goal (quit smoking, start exercising) and repeatedly returns to it. However, the approach is prescriptive rather than exploratory. Instead of asking, "What would need to happen for you to feel ready to quit?" or "What do you see as the biggest barrier right now?" the psychiatrist imposes a specific intervention (nicotine patches) and demands immediate implementation.

The psychiatrist also fails to maintain focus strategically. Rather than exploring Rosa's ambivalence to understand and resolve it, the psychiatrist attempts to override it through repetition and pressure. A more strategically directed approach would involve: (1) exploring what smoking provides Rosa, (2) understanding her specific fears about withdrawal and weight gain, (3) identifying her values and how smoking conflicts with them, (4) collaboratively identifying barriers and solutions. Instead, the session becomes a power struggle over whether she will start patches today.

The direction is also rigid—there is no flexibility or exploration of alternative pathways to change. When Rosa suggests "cutting back a bit before trying the patches," the psychiatrist dismisses this: "Cutting back is just prolonging the problem." This closes off exploration of what might work for this particular client and demonstrates that the psychiatrist's agenda (immediate patches) takes precedence over understanding Rosa's readiness and preferences.

**Summary:**

While the session maintains focus on smoking cessation, the direction is pursued through coercion rather than collaborative exploration. The psychiatrist imposes a specific intervention without understanding Rosa's readiness, barriers, or preferences, which undermines strategic MI practice.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have valuable knowledge. This session demonstrates an entirely hierarchical, expert-driven stance with no genuine collaboration. The psychiatrist positions himself as the authority who knows what is best and the client as someone who must comply.

Evidence of failed collaboration includes:
- "You have to quit smoking immediately" (directive, not collaborative)
- "You can't just keep making excuses" (judgment, not partnership)
- "You don't get to say no here" (explicit rejection of client input)
- "No, you don't get to negotiate here" (dismissal of client's ideas)

## Recommendations

### 1. **Fundamental Shift from Directive to Collaborative Stance** (HIGHEST PRIORITY)

**Action:** Adopt a genuine partnership approach where the client is treated as the expert on their own life. Replace all directive language ("you have to," "you must," "you need to") with collaborative language ("let's explore," "what do you think," "how can I support you").

**Example:** 
- **Instead of:** "You have to quit smoking immediately and start working out regularly. You can't just keep making excuses."
- **Use:** "I can see that smoking is important to you and that quitting feels complicated. I'm wondering what would need to happen for you to feel ready to make a change. What are your thoughts?"

**Practice:** 
- Record yourself in practice sessions and count how many times you use directive language vs. collaborative language. Aim for 80%+ collaborative language.
- Role-play with a colleague where they play a resistant client and you practice replacing "you have to" with "what do you think about," "tell me more about," "how would that work for you."
- Review MI training materials on the spirit of MI, particularly the emphasis on partnership and autonomy support.

---

### 2. **Develop Reflective Listening Skills—Begin with Simple Reflections**

**Action:** Implement reflective listening as the primary response to client statements. For every client statement, offer at least one reflection before asking another question or providing information.

**Example:**
- **Client:** "I just can't imagine not going through life without smoking. It's been a part of me for so long."
- **Instead of:** "You need to stop thinking it's complicated—it's really not."
- **Use:** "Smoking has been such a central part of your identity for so long that imagining life without it feels almost impossible. That makes sense."

**Practice:**
- Start with simple reflections that capture the content and emotion of what the client says.
- Progress to complex reflections that add depth or meaning.
- Use the "reflection sandwich": Listen → Reflect → Ask a follow-up question.
- Practice with a colleague or supervisor until reflections feel natural.

---

### 3. **Stop Dismissing Client Concerns—Validate and Explore Instead**

**Action:** When a client expresses a concern (weight gain, withdrawal, difficulty finding time), respond with validation and curiosity rather than dismissal. Acknowledge that their concern is real and understandable, then explore it.

**Example:**
- **Client:** "Many people have told me, if I quit smoking, I'm going to gain weight."
- **Instead of:** "Those are just excuses people use to avoid quitting."
- **Use:** "Weight gain is a real concern for you. That makes sense—many people do experience that. Tell me more about what worries you about gaining weight. What would that mean for you?"

**Practice:**
- When you feel the urge to dismiss a concern, pause and ask yourself: "What is the client really worried about here? What does this concern represent?" This often reveals deeper values or fears worth exploring.
- Practice validating statements: "That makes sense," "I understand why that would worry you," "That's a legitimate concern."
- Explore the concern with genuine curiosity rather than trying to convince the client it's not real.

---

### 4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk—Don't Impose Reasons for Change**

**Action:** When the client expresses desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment to change, pause and explore it. Ask questions that deepen and amplify their own reasons for change rather than imposing external reasons.

**Example:**
- **Client:** "I know I've got to stop. That made me rethink everything."
- **Instead of:** "So you need to start the patches today."
- **Use:** "Tell me more about what made you rethink everything. What specifically did the doctor say that shifted your thinking? What are you most concerned about preventing?"

**Practice:**
- Listen for change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment, taking steps).
- When you hear it, reflect it back and ask an open question to deepen it.
- Use the OARS technique: Open questions, Affirmations, Reflections, Summaries.
- Track how much change talk vs. sustain talk emerges in your sessions. Aim for more change talk.

---

### 5. **Respect Autonomy—Offer Choice and Support Client Decision-Making**

**Action:** Eliminate coercive language entirely. Replace demands with genuine offers of choice. Support the client's right to decide, even if you disagree with their decision.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** "You don't get to say no here. Starting the patches today isn't optional—it's mandatory."
- **Use:** "I want to support you in whatever way works best for you. Some people find patches helpful, others prefer gum or medication, and some prefer to quit cold turkey. What feels right to you? What have you heard from others that resonates with you?"

**Practice:**
- Before each session, remind yourself: "This is the client's decision. My job is to support them in making a decision that works for them, not to force compliance."
- Practice offering genuine choices: "What would you like to focus on today?" "How would you like to approach this?" "What feels doable to you?"
- When a client says no, respond with curiosity rather than escalation: "I hear that you're not ready. What would need to happen for you to feel ready?"

---

### 6. **Address Ambivalence Through Exploration, Not Pressure**

**Action:** Recognize that ambivalence is normal and healthy. Rather than trying to override it through pressure, explore it collaboratively to help the client resolve it themselves.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** "No, you don't get to negotiate here. Cutting back is just prolonging the problem. You have to quit completely now."
- **Use:** "I hear that you're torn—part of you knows you need to quit, and part of you is worried about what that will mean. Both of those things are real. Let's talk about what's pulling you in each direction. What would make quitting feel more doable?"

**Practice:**
- Use the "decisional balance" technique: explore both sides of the ambivalence without judgment.
- Ask: "What are the good things about smoking for you?" and "What concerns do you have about smoking?" This helps the client articulate their own reasons for change.
- Use double-sided reflections: "On one hand... on the other hand..."
- Trust that exploring ambivalence leads to resolution better than pressure does.

---

### 7. **Build Confidence and Self-Efficacy—Don't Undermine It**

**Action:** When a client expresses doubt or fear, respond by exploring their strengths and past successes rather than dismissing their concerns or demanding they "just do it."

**Example:**
- **Client:** "I'm scared about withdrawal. I'm not sure I can handle it."
- **Instead of:** "You have to stop being scared and just do it."
- **Use:** "Withdrawal is real and it's understandable to be concerned. Let's talk about what might help. Have you successfully made other difficult changes in your life? What helped you get through those? What strengths do you have that could help you through this?"

**Practice:**
- When a client expresses doubt, ask about past successes: "Tell me about a time you've overcome a challenge. What did you do? What helped?"
- Affirm their strengths: "You've shown real courage in coming here and being honest about your struggles."
- Explore what support they need: "What would help you feel more confident? Who could support you? What resources might help?"

### 8. **Eliminate Threats and Coercive Language Entirely**

**Action:** Remove all language that frames compliance as the only acceptable choice or non-compliance as self-harm. Replace threats with genuine support and respect for client autonomy.

**Example:**

- **Use:** "I care about your health and I want to support you in making changes that work for you. I'm here to help, and I respect that you need to make this decision in your own time."

**Practice:**
- Review recordings of your sessions and identify any threatening or coercive language.
- Replace each instance with supportive, respectful language.
- Remind yourself: "My job is not to force compliance. My job is to support the client in making their own decision."
