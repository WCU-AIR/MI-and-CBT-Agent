
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda

**Explanation:**

For a patient with schizoaffective disorder presenting with multiple concurrent symptoms (depressed mood, anhedonia, suicidal ideation, auditory hallucinations, persecutory delusions), a structured agenda is clinically essential. The therapist should have explicitly prioritized: (1) safety assessment regarding suicidal ideation, (2) psychotic symptoms and their relationship to mood, (3) behavioral activation, and (4) reality-testing of specific thoughts. No time boundaries are set, no priorities negotiated, and no explicit goals established for either session.

**Summary:**

Agenda-setting was essentially absent. The therapist used open-ended, permissive language rather than collaborative structure, failing to establish clear session goals, prioritize problems, or create a roadmap for treatment.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Conduct Comprehensive Safety Assessment (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** In the next session, conduct a detailed suicide risk assessment including: frequency and duration of suicidal thoughts, presence of intent and plan, access to means, protective factors, and previous attempts. Establish a written safety plan with crisis contacts, coping strategies, and means restriction.
- **Example:** "I want to check in about the thought you mentioned—that people would be better off without you around. How often do you have that thought? Do you ever think about how you might hurt yourself? Do you have a plan? What would help keep you safe if these thoughts get stronger?"
- **Practice:** Review suicide risk assessment protocols, practice asking direct questions about intent and plan, and develop a template for safety planning that you can use with all patients presenting with suicidal ideation.

### 2. **Establish Structured, Collaborative Agenda with Clear Prioritization**
- **Action:** At the beginning of the next session, explicitly set a collaborative agenda. Ask the patient which issues are most pressing, negotiate priorities together, and establish time allocation. Prioritize: (1) safety, (2) psychotic symptoms and reality-testing, (3) depressive symptoms and behavioral activation, (4) social reengagement.
- **Example:** "Today I want to make sure we focus on what's most important to you. You mentioned feeling down, hearing voices, and having some scary thoughts. Which of these is bothering you the most right now? What would be most helpful to work on today? We have about 45 minutes, so let's figure out how to use that time best."
- **Practice:** Before each session, write down the patient's presenting problems and explicitly prioritize them based on clinical urgency and the patient's preferences. Practice stating the agenda clearly and checking for agreement.

### 3. **Distinguish Between Depressive and Psychotic Cognitions and Apply Appropriate Interventions**
- **Action:** Develop a conceptualization that distinguishes between depressive thoughts (which may respond to cognitive restructuring) and psychotic delusions (which require reality-testing, delusion-focused CBT, or acceptance-based approaches). Apply different interventions to each.
- **Example:** "I notice you have two different kinds of thoughts. One is the depressive thought 'I'm a burden,' which comes when you're feeling down. The other is the thought that you did terrible things when you were younger, which the voices confirm. These might need different approaches. For the depressive thought, we can look at evidence. For the voices and the memories, we might need to test whether those memories are actually accurate or if they're something your mind is creating. What do you think?"
- **Practice:** Study delusion-focused CBT and voice-focused CBT. Learn to conduct reality-testing experiments. Practice distinguishing between different types of cognitions and selecting appropriate interventions for each.

### 4. **Implement Structured Thought Records with Clear Rationale**
- **Action:** Instead of vague "thought-monitoring," provide the patient with a structured thought record that includes: situation, automatic thought, emotion, evidence for/against the thought, alternative thought, and outcome. Explain the rationale: "By writing down your thoughts and examining the evidence, we can see whether they're actually true or if depression is distorting how you see things."
- **Example:** Provide a one-page thought record template. Walk through an example together: "When you felt down yesterday, you thought 'I'm a burden.' Let's write that down. What emotion did you feel? What evidence do you have that you're a burden? What evidence do you have against that? What might someone who cares about you say? What's a more balanced thought?"
- **Practice:** Create a library of thought record templates tailored to different types of thoughts (depressive, anxious, psychotic). Practice walking patients through the process step-by-step and reviewing completed records in session.

### 5. **Conduct Functional Analysis of Behavioral Activation**
- **Action:** Rather than suggesting generic activities, conduct a functional analysis. Ask the patient: What activities did you enjoy before? What activities are consistent with your values? What small steps could you take? What barriers prevent engagement? Develop a graded behavioral activation plan.
- **Example:** "I know motivation is low right now, which makes sense when you're depressed. But sometimes doing things—even small things—can help improve mood. Let's think about what you used to enjoy or what matters to you. You mentioned drawing in high school. What did you like about that? What would it take to do a little drawing this week? Could you start with just 5 minutes? What would make that easier?"
- **Practice:** Learn to conduct functional analysis. Practice identifying valued activities and breaking them into small, achievable steps. Develop a graded approach to behavioral activation that accounts for low motivation and amotivation.

### 6. **Implement Collaborative Empiricism and Guided Discovery**
- **Action:** Replace directive suggestions with Socratic questioning. Help the patient reach insights independently by asking questions that guide discovery: "What evidence do you have? What would someone else say? What would need to happen to test this? What have you learned from past experiences?"
- **Example:** Instead of "Maybe you're not really a burden," ask: "You think you're a burden to people. What evidence do you have for that? What evidence do you have against it? If I asked someone who cares about you, what would they say? What would need to happen for you to believe you're not a burden?"
- **Practice:** Study Socratic questioning techniques. Practice asking open-ended questions and resisting the urge to provide answers. Record yourself and review for instances where you directed rather than discovered.

### 7. **Systematically Elicit and Respond to Feedback**
- **Action:** At multiple points in each session, ask for feedback: "How does this make sense to you? Is this approach helpful? What would be more useful? How are you feeling about our work together?" Adjust based on feedback.
- **Example:** "I want to make sure I'm helping in the way that's most useful for you. How are you feeling about what we talked about today? Did it make sense? Is there anything you'd like to do differently?"
- **Practice:** Develop a habit of asking for feedback at the beginning, middle, and end of each session. Practice responding non-defensively to critical feedback and adjusting your approach accordingly.

### 8. **Develop Clear Rationale for All Interventions**
- **Action:** Before suggesting any intervention, explain the rationale clearly. Help the patient understand the mechanism of change: "Why might this help? How does this connect to your symptoms? What's the theory behind this?"
- **Example:** "The reason I'm suggesting we monitor your thoughts is that sometimes when we're depressed, our thoughts become automatic and we believe them without questioning. By writing them down and examining them, we can see whether they're actually true or if depression is distorting how we see things. Does that make sense?"
- **Practice:** For each intervention you use, write out the rationale in plain language. Practice explaining it to patients in a way that makes sense and builds buy-in.

### 9. **Assign Specific, Structured Homework with Clear Rationale and Behavioral Experiment Framing**
- **Action:** Instead of vague homework, assign specific tasks framed as behavioral experiments with a hypothesis to test. Provide written instructions, verify understanding, and establish a plan for review.
- **Example:** "This week, I'd like you to try an experiment. When you notice the thought 'I'm a burden,' I want you to: (1) Write down the situation, (2) Write down the thought, (3) Use the phrase 'That's just a thought, not a fact,' (4) Notice what happens to the thought and your mood, (5) Write down what you observed. We'll review this together next session to see if this strategy is helpful. Does this make sense? Can you walk me through what you'll do?"
- **Practice:** Create a homework assignment template that includes: the specific task, the rationale, written instructions, and a plan for review. Practice verifying understanding before the patient leaves the session.

### 10. **Review Prior Homework at the Beginning of Each Session**
- **Action:** Make homework review a standard part of session structure. Ask: "How did the homework go? What did you try? What worked? What didn't work? What barriers came up? What did you learn?"
- **Example:** "Last week we talked about noticing your thoughts and using that phrase. How did that go? Did you try it? What happened when you did? What made it hard to do?"
- **Practice:** Add homework review to your session opening checklist. Practice asking follow-up questions to understand what worked and what didn't, and adjust interventions based on the patient's experience.

### 11. **Develop Competence in CBT for Psychotic Symptoms**
- **Action:** Pursue specialized training in delusion-focused CBT and voice-focused CBT. Learn evidence-based approaches for reality-testing, coping strategy enhancement, and acceptance-based work with psychotic symptoms.
- **Example:** Study protocols like "Cognitive Therapy for Psychosis" (Fowler, Garety, & Kuipers) and "Working with Voices and Visions" (Romme & Escher). Practice applying these techniques with supervision.
- **Practice:** Attend workshops or training programs on CBT for psychosis. Seek supervision from an expert in this area. Practice with multiple patients and get feedback on your application of these techniques.

### 12. **Establish Clear Treatment Conceptualization and Strategy**
- **Action:** Develop a comprehensive case conceptualization that integrates the patient's history, current symptoms, maintaining factors, and treatment targets. Establish a clear strategy for change that progresses logically toward symptom reduction.
- **Example:** "John's schizoaffective disorder involves both psychotic symptoms (delusions, hallucinations) and depressive symptoms (low mood, anhedonia, suicidal ideation). The depression and isolation maintain the psychotic symptoms, and the psychotic symptoms worsen the depression. Our strategy will be: (1) Ensure safety, (2) Reality-test the delusions, (3) Develop coping strategies for voices, (4) Activate behavior to improve mood, (5) Rebuild social connection. This should help break the cycle."
- **Practice:** For each patient, write out a one-page case conceptualization and treatment strategy. Review this at the beginning of each session to ensure you're making progress toward clear goals.
