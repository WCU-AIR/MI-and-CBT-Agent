
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 5. Collaboration
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration is severely compromised in this session. While the therapist uses collaborative language ("Maybe we can start by talking about," "Maybe you could try"), the actual decision-making is therapist-driven, not collaborative.

The therapist identifies the problem (thoughts causing avoidance), proposes the intervention (cognitive reframing + behavioral activation + breathing exercises), and suggests specific homework (putting on shoes, stepping outside, breathing exercises) with minimal genuine input from the patient. The patient is positioned as a recipient of suggestions rather than an active partner in treatment planning.

True collaboration in CBT involves:
1. **Shared problem formulation**: The therapist should ask, "How do you understand what's happening? What do you think is driving these feelings?" Instead, the therapist immediately proposes a cognitive model.
2. **Collaborative hypothesis development**: Rather than suggesting "your thoughts are making you feel stuck," the therapist could ask, "What do you think is the connection between what you're thinking and how you're feeling?"
3. **Shared decision-making about interventions**: The therapist should present options and ask which resonates with the patient, rather than prescribing specific techniques.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish Comprehensive Assessment and Case Formulation (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Conduct a systematic assessment of all depressive symptoms and their relationship to Parkinson's disease. Develop an explicit, written case formulation that integrates medical and psychological factors. Present this formulation to the patient collaboratively.

**Example:**
"Based on what you've told me and what I know about Parkinson's disease, here's how I understand what's happening: Your Parkinson's disease is causing real physical fatigue and slowing, which is making it harder to do activities. At the same time, you're having thoughts like 'I'm a burden' and 'I won't enjoy it anyway,' which makes you less likely to try. When you avoid activities, you feel more isolated and your mood gets worse. Also, your sleep problems might be making the fatigue worse during the day. Does this match your understanding? What would you add or change?"

**Practice:**
- Use a structured assessment tool (e.g., PHQ-9 for depression, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index for sleep)
- Create a timeline showing when symptoms started relative to Parkinson's diagnosis
- Map the relationships between medical symptoms, thoughts, behaviors, and mood
- Write out the formulation and review it with the patient

### 2. **Implement Collaborative Agenda-Setting and Treatment Planning**

**Action:** At the beginning of the next session, collaboratively establish a clear agenda with prioritized target problems, explicit treatment goals, and a structured treatment plan.

**Example:**

**Practice:**
- Write the agenda on a whiteboard or paper that both therapist and patient can see
- Explicitly state time allocation ("We have 50 minutes today; I'd like to spend 20 minutes on X, 20 on Y, and 10 on Z")
