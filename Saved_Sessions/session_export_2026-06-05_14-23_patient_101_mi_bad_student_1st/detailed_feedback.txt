
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance—the foundational pillars of MI acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly communicates disapproval, dismissal, and conditional regard throughout the interaction.

From the opening statement—"I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to improve"—the clinician establishes an authoritarian, judgmental framework that directly contradicts MI spirit. When the patient discloses stress and fatigue as barriers to exercise, the psychiatrist responds: "Look, you have to stop making excuses. Everyone is busy, but you have to prioritize your health." This is explicitly judgmental language that pathologizes normal human experience (tiredness, stress) as moral failure ("excuses"). The patient's lived experience is invalidated rather than normalized.

Most damaging is the repeated dismissal of the patient's autonomy and perspective: "No, you really need to take this seriously," "No, you really have to see it my way. This isn't about feelings; it's about facts." These statements communicate that the patient's internal experience is irrelevant and that disagreement equals moral deficiency. The psychiatrist never affirms the patient's strengths (willingness to engage, openness to discussion, desire for health), never normalizes the difficulty of behavior change, and never communicates genuine warmth. Instead, there is consistent coldness, pressure, and implicit judgment that the patient is failing or being unreasonable.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates zero acceptance. Judgmental language, dismissal of the patient's perspective, conditional regard based on compliance, and absence of affirmation create an environment antithetical to MI principles and harmful to therapeutic alliance.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective from their internal frame of reference. This session contains virtually no empathic responding and instead demonstrates repeated empathic failures.

The patient explicitly states: "It's just... sometimes school and everything gets kind of overwhelming, you know? And when I have free time, I just want to chill, not push myself too much... it's more like I'm tired or stressed." This is a clear disclosure of internal struggle. The appropriate empathic response would reflect this emotional reality: "It sounds like you're juggling a lot right now—school is weighing on you, and when you get a break, your body and mind need rest. That makes sense." Instead, the psychiatrist responds: "Look, you have to stop making excuses. Everyone is busy, but you have to prioritize your health." This is not empathy; it is dismissal disguised as advice.

An empathic response would acknowledge the genuine difficulty: "It sounds like committing to three times a week feels overwhelming right now, and you're worried about setting yourself up to fail." Instead: "No, you really have to see it my way. This isn't about feelings; it's about facts." The psychiatrist explicitly rejects the patient's emotional reality as irrelevant.

The patient's final statement—"the way you're pushing makes me feel kind of trapped, like you're not getting why it's hard for me"—is a metacommunication about the therapeutic relationship itself. Rather than reflecting this feedback with genuine curiosity ("Help me understand what 'trapped' feels like for you"), the psychiatrist continues the same pressuring stance.

There are zero complex reflections, zero attempts to capture emotional nuance, and zero evidence of perspective-taking. The psychiatrist operates entirely from their own frame of reference.

**Summary:**

Empathy is completely absent. The psychiatrist dismisses emotional content, rejects the patient's internal experience as invalid, and demonstrates no reflective listening or genuine effort to understand the patient's perspective.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI means maintaining strategic focus on target behavior while remaining collaborative and client-centered. This session does maintain focus on exercise behavior (the target), but does so in a way that is rigid, non-strategic, and counterproductive to actual behavior change.

The psychiatrist consistently returns to the same directive: "You need to exercise three times a week, starting immediately." This is repeated verbatim or near-verbatim across multiple exchanges without adaptation, despite clear evidence that this approach is not working. The patient repeatedly expresses ambivalence, hesitation, and resistance ("I'm not ready," "it feels too much," "I can't promise"), yet the psychiatrist responds with intensified pressure rather than strategic exploration.

Effective direction in MI would involve: (1) exploring the patient's own reasons for change, (2) understanding barriers from the patient's perspective, (3) collaboratively identifying realistic steps, and (4) allowing the patient to set the pace. None of this occurs. Instead, the psychiatrist uses direction as a tool for coercion: "You must start exercising three times a week immediately... Instead, the psychiatrist rejected this collaborative opportunity and doubled down on the same demand.

**Summary:**

Direction is maintained but executed poorly. The psychiatrist focuses on exercise but uses rigid, coercive tactics rather than strategic exploration. The approach is unlikely to produce sustained behavior change.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Foundational Shift: Adopt MI Spirit and Stance**
- **Action:** Fundamentally reframe your role from "expert who tells patients what to do" to "collaborative partner who helps patients explore their own motivations for change."
- **Example:** Instead of "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to improve," begin with: "I'd like to understand what brings you in today and what your thoughts are about your health. What matters most to you?"
- **Practice:** Before your next session, spend 15 minutes reflecting on this question: "What would it look like if I trusted that this patient has wisdom about their own life and their own change process?" Practice this mindset shift in supervision or role-play.

---

### 2. **Develop Genuine Empathic Listening and Reflective Responses**
- **Action:** When a patient discloses barriers or struggles, pause and reflect back what you hear with genuine curiosity and validation, rather than dismissing it as "excuses."
- **Example:** When the patient said "sometimes school and everything gets kind of overwhelming... when I have free time, I just want to chill," respond with: "It sounds like you're carrying a lot right now—school is demanding, and when you get a break, your mind and body need rest. That makes complete sense. Tell me more about what's been overwhelming."
- **Practice:** Record yourself in sessions and count how many times you use reflective listening vs. advice-giving. Aim for a 2:1 ratio of reflections to directives. Practice complex reflections that capture emotional content and meaning, not just facts.

---

### 3. **Ask Open-Ended Questions to Evoke the Patient's Own Motivation**
- **Action:** Replace prescriptive statements with genuine questions designed to draw out the patient's own reasons for change, values, and goals.
- **Example:** Instead of "You need to exercise three times a week," ask: "What would be good about being more active?" "What does health mean to you?" "If you were more active, what would be different in your life?" "What's one small step you could see yourself taking?"
- **Practice:** Develop a list of 10 open-ended evocative questions and practice using them in role-play. Focus on questions that explore desire, ability, reasons, and commitment to change.

---

### 4. **Recognize and Amplify Change Talk**
- **Action:** Listen carefully for any statements the patient makes about wanting to change, believing they can change, or reasons for change. When you hear change talk, reflect it back and explore it further.
- **Example:** When the patient said "I do want to stay healthy, yeah," respond with: "You do care about your health—that's important. Tell me more about what being healthy means to you and why it matters."
- **Practice:** In supervision, review session recordings and identify all instances of change talk. For each one, write down what you said in response. Did you amplify it, ignore it, or undermine it? Practice responding to change talk with reflections and follow-up questions.

---

### 5. **Support Autonomy and Collaborative Decision-Making**
- **Action:** Explicitly communicate that the patient has choice and that you are partners in exploring options together. Use language like "we," "let's," "what do you think," "what feels doable to you?"
- **Example:** Instead of "You need to commit to three times a week," say: "Let's think about what might work for you. What feels realistic given everything you have going on? What's one small step you could see yourself trying?"
- **Practice:** In role-play, practice responding to patient hesitation with curiosity rather than pressure. When a patient says "I'm not sure I can do that," practice responding with: "That makes sense—it feels like a lot right now. What would make it feel more doable?" rather than "You have to do it anyway."

---

### 6. **Soften Sustain Talk with Double-Sided Reflections and Reframing**
- **Action:** When a patient expresses reasons for not changing, acknowledge both sides of their ambivalence without confrontation. Use reflections that validate their concerns while gently pointing toward change.
- **Example:** When the patient expressed difficulty with commitment, instead of "Stop making excuses," use: "So on one hand, you care about your health and want to stay active. On the other hand, school is really overwhelming right now and you need time to rest. Both of those things are true. I'm wondering—what would need to happen for you to feel like you could add more activity without it feeling like too much?"
- **Practice:** In role-play, practice responding to sustain talk with reflections that validate the patient's experience while exploring possibilities for change. Avoid confrontation and pressure.

---

### 7. **Respond to Patient Feedback About the Relationship**
- **Action:** When a patient provides direct feedback about the therapeutic relationship (as this patient did: "the way you're pushing makes me feel kind of trapped"), pause and genuinely listen. Use this as an opportunity to repair and adjust.
- **Example:** "I hear you saying that my pushing is making you feel trapped. That's really important feedback. I want to work with you, not against you. Let's slow down and talk about what would feel more helpful to you."
- **Practice:** In supervision, discuss how to respond when patients give you feedback that your approach isn't working. Practice staying curious and collaborative rather than defensive.

---

### 8. **Eliminate Fear-Based Coercion and Prescriptive Language**
- **Action:** Remove language like "you have to," "you must," "you need to," "you really should," and fear-based statements like "if you don't, you'll suffer serious health problems."
- **Example:** Instead of "If you don't exercise, you're risking serious health issues," explore: "What are your thoughts about how activity affects your health? What concerns do you have?"
- **Practice:** Review your session recordings and count how many times you use prescriptive language. Set a goal to reduce it by 80%. Replace it with collaborative language and open-ended questions.
