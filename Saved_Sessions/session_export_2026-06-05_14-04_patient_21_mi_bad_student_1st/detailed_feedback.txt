
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and contains multiple instances of explicit judgment and disapproval. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels Brandon's behavior as problematic without exploring his perspective, uses pejorative language ("weak-willed," "lying to yourself," "fooling yourself"), and communicates clear disapproval of his choices. Most critically, the psychiatrist questions Brandon's character and motivation ("Are you just weak-willed?" "Are you just not trying hard enough?"), which directly violates the MI principle of accepting the client's inherent worth regardless of their current behavior.

The psychiatrist's opening statement—"I'm here to talk about your drinking habits, and I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to fix this"—immediately establishes a judgmental, expert-driven stance rather than collaborative exploration. Throughout the session, phrases like "you're seriously underestimating," "you're not thinking straight," "you're just trying to justify," and "stop feeling sorry for yourself" communicate contempt and disapproval. These statements are antithetical to MI's core principle of acceptance. The psychiatrist never affirms Brandon's willingness to engage, his honesty about his struggles, or his openness to conversation. There is no warmth, no normalization of ambivalence, and no genuine support for Brandon as a person.

Brandon's responses show increasing defensiveness and withdrawal ("it feels like you're not really hearing me," "calling me weak or a liar just makes me shut down a bit"), which is a direct consequence of the psychiatrist's judgmental stance. A client experiencing acceptance would show increasing openness; instead, Brandon becomes more entrenched in his position.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance. Repeated use of judgment, character attacks, and disapproval creates a hostile therapeutic environment that violates fundamental MI principles and actively damages the therapeutic alliance.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective. This psychiatrist demonstrates minimal empathy throughout the session. There are no reflective statements that capture Brandon's emotional experience, his ambivalence, or his underlying concerns. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses Brandon's stated experience ("that's just an excuse," "you're not thinking straight," "you're just trying to justify").

Brandon explicitly articulates his perspective multiple times: drinking helps him cope with stress, it's part of his social connection, he's not ready to commit to change immediately, and he wants to understand what cutting back would look like. The psychiatrist never reflects any of this back to Brandon in a way that demonstrates understanding. A single empathic reflection might sound like: "So drinking has become an important way for you to unwind after work and stay connected with friends, and the idea of giving that up without knowing what you'd do instead feels overwhelming right now." Instead, the psychiatrist responds with confrontation and demands.

The psychiatrist also fails to recognize or validate Brandon's change talk. Brandon acknowledges that drinking "has caused some problems," that he's "thought about stopping or cutting back," and that he's "open to talking about how I might start cutting back." These are genuine motivational statements that should be explored and amplified. Instead, they are ignored or contradicted. The psychiatrist never asks clarifying questions about Brandon's concerns, his values, or what matters to him—all essential components of empathic understanding in MI.

The psychiatrist's repeated questions ("Why haven't you tried quitting before?") are asked in an accusatory tone rather than genuine curiosity, which further demonstrates lack of empathy. True empathy would involve wondering aloud about Brandon's experience: "I'm curious what's made it hard to cut back so far" or "What do you think would need to be different for you to feel ready?"

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no genuine empathic understanding. Reflective listening is entirely absent, Brandon's perspective is repeatedly dismissed, and change talk is ignored rather than explored.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI involves maintaining strategic focus on the target behavior while remaining client-centered. This psychiatrist does maintain focus on alcohol consumption—the session never wanders from the topic. However, the direction is imposed rather than collaborative, and it lacks strategic sophistication.

The psychiatrist's direction is one-dimensional: "quit drinking now" or "cut down immediately." There is no exploration of Brandon's goals, values, or what meaningful change might look like for him. A strategically directed MI session would clarify: What does Brandon want for his life? What role does drinking play in that vision? What would be a realistic first step? Instead, the psychiatrist simply repeats the same directive without adaptation.

The psychiatrist does ask some relevant questions ("Do you drink every day?" "Have you tried quitting before?" "Did you use any aids?"), which shows minimal attempt at direction-setting. However, these questions are asked in a prosecutorial manner rather than collaborative exploration. The psychiatrist never asks about Brandon's own goals or what he might want to change, which would be essential for client-centered direction.

Additionally, the psychiatrist fails to adjust direction based on Brandon's responses. Brandon repeatedly states he's "not ready" to quit today but is "open to talking about cutting back slowly." A strategically directed MI session would pivot toward exploring what "cutting back" might mean, what barriers exist, and what small steps Brandon might consider. Instead, the psychiatrist simply repeats the demand to quit immediately, demonstrating inflexible rather than strategic direction.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains focus on alcohol but in an imposed, non-collaborative manner. Direction lacks strategic sophistication and fails to adapt to Brandon's stated readiness level or goals.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners. This session demonstrates an authoritarian, expert-driven approach that is fundamentally anti-collaborative. The psychiatrist's opening statement—"I'm here to tell you exactly what you need to do to fix this"—immediately establishes a hierarchical, non-collaborative stance.

Throughout the session, the psychiatrist uses commanding language: "you have to quit," "you need to stop," "you must take this seriously," "you have to commit." These are directives, not invitations for partnership. True collaboration would involve language like "What do you think about..." or "How might we work together on..." or "What would feel manageable to you?"

The psychiatrist never seeks Brandon's input on the process. Brandon explicitly requests collaboration ("Maybe we can talk about what cutting back would actually look like instead of just saying I have to stop right now"), but the psychiatrist ignores this request and continues issuing directives. This is a critical missed opportunity to establish partnership.

There is no shared decision-making. The psychiatrist decides what Brandon needs (to quit or cut down immediately) and attempts to impose this decision. Brandon's perspective—that he's not ready for immediate change but is open to gradual reduction—is dismissed rather than incorporated into the treatment plan.

The power dynamic is entirely one-sided. The psychiatrist positions himself as the expert who knows what's best, while Brandon is positioned as a resistant, unmotivated patient who needs to be convinced or coerced. In true collaboration, both parties would contribute expertise: the psychiatrist brings clinical knowledge about health risks, and Brandon brings knowledge about his own life, values, and what might be feasible for him.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates zero collaboration. An authoritarian, expert-driven stance dominates throughout, with no genuine partnership or shared decision-making.

### 5. Autonomy Support
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Autonomy support requires respecting the client's right to choose and emphasizing their ownership of the change process. ## Recommendations

### 1. **Fundamentally Shift from Confrontation to Collaboration**
- **Action:** Adopt a collaborative stance that treats Brandon as an equal partner with expertise about his own life. - **Example:** Instead of "You have to quit now," say: "I'm concerned about the health risks of daily drinking. At the same time, I know you're the expert on your own life. What's your sense of what might need to change?"

### 2. **Establish Genuine Acceptance and Unconditional Positive Regard**
- **Action:** Communicate respect for Brandon as a person, separate from his behavior. Eliminate all judgmental language and character attacks. Normalize his ambivalence and struggle as universal human experiences.

- **Action:** Use reflective listening to demonstrate that you understand Brandon's perspective. Reflect not just content but emotion and meaning. Use complex reflections that deepen insight.

- **Action:** Ask open questions that invite Brandon to explore his own reasons for change, rather than telling him why he should change. Use evocative questions strategically.
- **Example:** Instead of "You need to understand daily drinking will ruin your health," ask: "What concerns do you have about your drinking?" or "If you continued at this level, what do you think might happen?" or "What would be different in your life if you cut back?"

### 8. **Explore Ambivalence Rather Than Trying to Resolve It**
- **Action:** Recognize that Brandon holds both desire to change and reasons to stay the same. Rather than attacking one side, explore both sides with genuine curiosity.

- **Practice:** Practice exploring ambivalence using the "develop discrepancy" technique from MI. Ask questions that help the client articulate both sides without you arguing for one side.

- **Practice:** Practice asking open questions about goals: "What would you like to change?", "What would feel manageable?", "What's a realistic first step?", "How would you know if things were getting better?"

### 10. **Develop Self-Awareness About Your Own Reactions**

- **Practice:** Seek supervision or consultation to explore your reactions to resistant clients. Consider your own beliefs about change, responsibility, and how people should behave. Develop compassion for the difficulty of change.
