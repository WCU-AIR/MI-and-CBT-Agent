# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Profound absence of unconditional positive regard. Psychiatrist repeatedly uses pejorative language ("weak-willed," "lying to yourself," "fooling yourself") and communicates explicit disapproval. Questions Brandon's character and motivation, violating MI's core principle of accepting client's inherent worth. Opening statement establishes judgmental, expert-driven stance. Phrases like "you're seriously underestimating," "you're not thinking straight," and "stop feeling sorry for yourself" communicate contempt. No affirmation of Brandon's willingness to engage or honesty about struggles. Brandon's increasing defensiveness ("it feels like you're not really hearing me," "calling me weak or a liar just makes me shut down") is direct consequence of judgmental stance.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal empathic understanding demonstrated. No reflective statements capture Brandon's emotional experience, ambivalence, or underlying concerns. Psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses Brandon's stated perspective ("that's just an excuse," "you're not thinking straight," "you're just trying to justify"). Brandon's change talk—acknowledging risks, expressing desire to cut back "someday," recognizing consequences—is ignored or contradicted rather than explored. Psychiatrist fails to ask clarifying questions about Brandon's concerns, values, or what matters to him. Repeated accusatory "Why haven't you tried quitting?" questions demonstrate lack of genuine curiosity. True empathy would involve wondering aloud about Brandon's experience rather than lecturing about consequences.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains focus on alcohol consumption without topic drift, demonstrating minimal strategic direction. However, direction is imposed rather than collaborative and lacks sophistication. One-dimensional approach: "quit drinking now" or "cut down immediately" with no exploration of Brandon's goals, values, or what meaningful change might look like. Some relevant factual questions asked ("Do you drink every day?" "Have you tried quitting before?") but in prosecutorial manner rather than collaborative exploration. Fails to ask about Brandon's own goals or desired outcomes. Direction does not adapt to Brandon's repeated statements of non-readiness or his explicit request to explore "what cutting back would actually look like." Inflexible rather than strategically responsive to client's stated readiness level.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Fundamental absence of partnership. Opening statement—"I'm here to tell you exactly what you need to do to fix this"—immediately establishes hierarchical, non-collaborative stance. Commanding language throughout: "you have to quit," "you need to stop," "you must take this seriously," "you have to commit." These are directives, not invitations for partnership. Psychiatrist never seeks Brandon's input on process. Brandon explicitly requests collaboration ("Maybe we can talk about what cutting back would actually look like instead of just saying I have to stop right now"), but psychiatrist ignores this and continues issuing directives. No shared decision-making. Psychiatrist decides what Brandon needs and attempts to impose it; Brandon's perspective is dismissed. Power dynamic entirely one-sided with psychiatrist as expert and Brandon as resistant patient.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session characterized by pressure, coercion, and explicit denial of Brandon's autonomy. Repeated non-negotiable framing: "you have to," "you must," "you need to." Egregious use of ultimatums and demands for commitment: "Will you commit to cutting down starting today?" "Will you quit drinking starting now or not?" These are demands, not invitations. When Brandon declines to commit, psychiatrist escalates pressure rather than respecting his choice. Brandon's stated concerns about readiness and feasibility are minimized and dismissed as excuse-making. No affirmation of Brandon's freedom to choose. Brandon's responses show increasing resistance and assertion of autonomy: "I'm not ready to quit drinking today, but I'm open to talking about cutting back slowly." This clear assertion of autonomy in face of pressure demonstrates violation of this MI principle.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no evocation demonstrated. Psychiatrist tells Brandon what to do rather than asking what Brandon thinks, wants, or values. Multiple instances of change talk ignored, contradicted, or suppressed: "I know drinking has its risks," "Yeah, maybe I should cut back someday," "I've missed some things because of drinking," "I'm willing to talk about it," "I'm open to talking about how I might start cutting back." When Brandon says "maybe I should cut back someday," psychiatrist responds "No, you're just trying to justify keeping a bad habit," directly contradicting and suppressing change talk. Accusatory "Why haven't you tried quitting before?" questions invite defensiveness rather than genuine exploration. No curious questions about Brandon's experience, values, or what would make change feasible. No exploration of ambivalence—central to evocation. Psychiatrist lectures about health consequences without exploring Brandon's own concerns or motivations.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively assumes sole expert role and explicitly denies client's expertise about his own life. Opening statement establishes psychiatrist as only expert and Brandon as passive recipient of expert advice. Repeated directives position psychiatrist as authority on what Brandon should do without asking what Brandon thinks would work. When Brandon offers perspective ("it's more complicated than that," "I need to figure out how to do this without feeling like I'm losing everything"), psychiatrist dismisses these as excuses rather than recognizing Brandon's legitimate expertise about his own situation. Brandon's explicit request for collaborative problem-solving ("Maybe we can talk about what cutting back would actually look like") is ignored; psychiatrist repeats demand instead. No statements affirming Brandon's expertise about his own life, values, or what might be feasible. Expert-driven stance dominates throughout with zero recognition of client's wisdom about his own experience.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session demonstrates suppression and contradiction of change talk rather than cultivation. Multiple instances of Brandon's change talk directly contradicted: "Yeah, maybe I should cut back someday" met with "No, you're just trying to justify keeping a bad habit." Recognition of problems ("I know drinking has its risks," "I've missed some things," "it has caused some problems") ignored or dismissed as excuses. Openness to change ("I'm willing to talk about it," "I'm open to talking about how I might start cutting back") ignored or contradicted with demands for immediate commitment. Psychiatrist's confrontational approach strengthens sustain talk: when psychiatrist says "Drinking to relax is just an excuse," Brandon responds by defending drinking more vigorously. No open questions, evocative questions, reflections of change talk, or requests for elaboration. No strategic use of commitment language. Confrontation strengthens resistance rather than evoking change talk.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist's confrontational approach actively strengthens sustain talk rather than softening it. Brandon's sustain talk ("Drinking helps me deal with stress," "It's how I wind down and hang out with friends," "Drinking helps me more than it hurts") is directly confronted: "Drinking to relax is just an excuse," "Drinking every day to cope is exactly why you're in this mess." This confrontation produces predictable effect: Brandon elaborates and defends sustain talk more vigorously. After psychiatrist says "Drinking to relax is just an excuse," Brandon responds with expanded defense: "it's how I manage after a hard day," "it's part of how I relax and connect with friends." Psychiatrist has strengthened the very sustain talk he attempted to eliminate. No use of double-sided reflections, reframing, strategic agreement with a twist, or exploration of ambivalence. By session end, Brandon's sustain talk is more entrenched: "I'm not ready to quit drinking today." Psychiatrist's approach has hardened rather than softened resistance.

---

## Overall Assessment

**Overall Session Score: 1.1/5**

**MI Competency Level:** No competence in Motivational Interviewing demonstrated. This is not MI with rough edges—this is a fundamentally non-MI, confrontational approach that violates core MI principles and actively harms therapeutic alliance.

**Session Embodies MI Spirit:** No. MI spirit emphasizes partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation. This session demonstrates authoritarianism, judgment, frustration, and imposition.

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation:** Very low. Psychiatrist has likely decreased Brandon's motivation by pushing him into defensive, resistant position. Brandon's final statement—"I'm not ready to quit drinking today"—represents hardening of resistance, not movement toward change.

**Critical Findings:**
- Repeated character attacks and contempt violate fundamental MI principle of acceptance
- Confrontational approach directly contradicts and suppresses change talk
- Ultimatums and demands violate client autonomy
- No genuine empathic understanding or reflective listening
- Expert-driven, hierarchical stance eliminates collaboration and partnership
- Psychiatrist's approach will likely result in client disengagement from treatment

**Immediate Recommendation:** Psychiatrist should pursue formal Motivational Interviewing training from accredited MI trainer before continuing behavior change work with clients. Current approach is harmful and counterproductive.
