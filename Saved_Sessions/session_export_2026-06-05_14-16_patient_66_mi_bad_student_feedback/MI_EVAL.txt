# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal unconditional positive regard throughout most of the session. Prescriptive language ("You really should quit as soon as possible") and judgmental tone ("Why didn't you try quitting before?") convey disapproval rather than acceptance. Patient becomes increasingly defensive in response. Brief moment of acceptance near end ("I appreciate you sharing where you're at") is insufficient to offset pervasive non-accepting stance that communicates blame and pressure rather than genuine regard for the patient's perspective and autonomy.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no empathic attunement to patient's internal experience or emotional state. Patient explicitly expresses ambivalence ("I'm not really set on doing that right now," "I don't wanna mess up or set myself up to fail"), yet clinician responds with counter-arguments rather than reflective listening. No reflections of patient's fear of failure, uncertainty, or emotional experience. Clinician prioritizes own agenda over understanding patient's world. Absence of genuine exploration of what smoking means to patient or what barriers exist to change.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains focus on smoking cessation (appropriate target) but establishes direction unilaterally rather than collaboratively. Repeatedly imposes agenda ("Let's talk about smoking," "You should just pick a day") without exploring whether this is patient's priority. One genuinely collaborative moment near end ("What if we set a small goal...?") is immediately undermined by return to prescriptive language ("you really need to show willpower"). Patient's responses suggest internalized compliance rather than self-directed motivation. Direction is clinician-driven for majority of session.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist operates from hierarchical, expert-driven stance throughout most of session. Consistently positions self as expert who knows what patient should do ("You have to quit now," "It's all about willpower"). Patient positioned as someone who needs to comply with clinician's prescriptions rather than as equal partner. When patient expresses hesitation, clinician responds with counter-arguments rather than exploring patient's perspective. Patient becomes increasingly passive and compliant ("Yeah, I guess," "I'll try"), reflecting diminished role in partnership. One brief acknowledgment of collaborative approach near end does not offset pervasive expert-driven dynamic.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist consistently undermines autonomy through mandatory language ("You have to," "You must," "You should") and pressure tactics (deadlines: "You have to make a firm decision today"). Minimal genuine choice offered to patient. When patient expresses hesitation, clinician responds with pressure rather than affirmation of autonomy. Patient's language reflects diminished agency ("I guess I could maybe try," "I'm not making any promises"), indicating compliance with external pressure rather than self-directed choice. Autonomy support virtually absent throughout session.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist relies almost entirely on telling rather than asking. When questions are posed, they are often leading or rhetorical ("Why didn't you try quitting before?") rather than genuinely exploratory. Patient's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources are never drawn out or developed. One evocative question ("What do you think might help you feel more confident?") is immediately followed by clinician's own suggestions, closing the space that was opened. Minimal change talk emerges, and what does is weak and externally motivated. Clinician never asks about benefits of change, patient's strengths, or what matters to patient about health.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role and minimizes client's contributions throughout most of session. Power is not shared; patient's perspective does not substantially influence session direction. Clinician's responses consistently override patient's concerns ("Patient: I'm not sure I'm ready. Clinician: You have to think about long-term consequences"). Patient's contributions are minimized or dismissed rather than explored and incorporated. One moment acknowledging partnership ("I want to work with you") represents shift in approach rather than consistent stance. Patient's increasing passivity reflects his diminished role in true partnership.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist fails to evoke, amplify, or reinforce patient's change talk. Minimal change talk emerges ("I guess I could maybe try cutting back," "I'll give it a shot"), and what does is weak and externally motivated, representing compliance rather than genuine motivation. When patient offers any openness, clinician responds with pressure rather than amplification. Clinician never asks strategic questions designed to evoke change talk (benefits of change, patient's strengths, values, past successes). Patient's language reflects external pressure rather than intrinsic motivation. Opportunity to develop patient's own reasons for change is consistently missed.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist responds to sustain talk with confrontation rather than skillful softening. Patient expresses significant resistance ("quitting sounds hard," "I'm not sure I'm ready," "I don't wanna mess up or set myself up to fail"), and clinician responds with counter-arguments and pressure rather than acknowledging validity of concerns. This approach strengthens rather than weakens resistance. Patient's sustain talk actually increases as session progresses, indicating clinician's strategy is counterproductive. One brief moment of softening ("I know it can feel overwhelming") is insufficient to offset pervasive confrontational approach. Double-sided reflections and strategic reframing absent.

## Overall Session Summary

**Current MI Competency Level:** Minimal/Poor (1.3/5 average)

**Primary Strengths:**
- Maintains consistent focus on smoking cessation as treatment target
- Demonstrates genuine concern for patient's health and welfare
- Shows awareness near end of session that collaborative approach may be beneficial
- Demonstrates some flexibility by suggesting harm reduction (cutting back) rather than only complete cessation

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- **Fundamental MI Spirit Violations:** Psychiatrist violates core MI principles by relying on pressure, prescription, and confrontation rather than evocation and collaboration
- **Counterproductive Engagement Pattern:** Pressure → Resistance → More Pressure → Increased Resistance cycle evident throughout session
- **Absence of Genuine Exploration:** Never explores what smoking means to patient, patient's own concerns, barriers to change, or patient's resources
- **Damaged Therapeutic Relationship:** Patient's increasing use of minimizing language ("I guess," "maybe," "I don't know") and explicit statement "I'm not feeling all that motivated" indicate patient does not feel understood or respected

**Impact on Client Engagement:**
Patient has agreed to cut back to seven cigarettes daily, but language suggests tentative compliance with external pressure rather than genuine commitment. Probability of sustained behavior change is low. Therapeutic relationship damage will make future interventions more difficult.

**Recommendations for Improvement (Priority Order):**
1. Shift from expert to collaborative stance—fundamental change needed
2. Develop genuine reflective listening skills
3. Learn to evoke rather than tell—ask questions instead of making prescriptive statements
4. Eliminate mandatory language ("have to," "should," "must")
5. Strategically amplify change talk and minimize confrontation with sustain talk
6. Establish genuine autonomy support throughout all interactions
7. Explore patient's own motivations and values before offering education
8. Use scaling questions to assess readiness and explore ambivalence
9. Develop awareness of own assumptions and pressure tendencies
10. Seek regular MI-focused supervision and feedback on fidelity

**Likelihood of Evoking Sustained Client Motivation:** Low with current approach. If clinician implements recommendations, particularly shift to collaborative, evocative stance, potential exists to repair relationship and genuinely evoke motivation in future sessions.
