## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths

This session demonstrates one genuine strength: the psychiatrist maintains clear focus on the target behavior (alcohol reduction) and expresses authentic concern for the client's health and wellbeing. The psychiatrist also demonstrates awareness that MI principles exist, as evidenced by one collaborative exchange ("Maybe we can explore some smaller steps together that feel manageable for you?"). This suggests the clinician has some foundational knowledge of MI, even if inconsistently applied.

### Areas for Improvement

This session represents a fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles and spirit. The psychiatrist employs a confrontational, authoritarian approach characterized by prescription, pressure, and urgency rather than partnership, evocation, and autonomy support. Most critically, the psychiatrist repeatedly overrides the client's clearly stated readiness level, self-assessment, and identified strategies with demands for immediate abstinence. When the client provides rich change talk ("I really don't want to go down that path," "If I drink less, I'll be more reliable with using birth control," "I'm willing to try to drink less"), this is not explored or amplified. Instead, the psychiatrist responds with generic warnings and prescriptions. The session also fails to address the client's secondary concern about contraceptive reliability, despite her explicitly linking it to alcohol use. Most problematically, the psychiatrist cycles through the same confrontational pattern across multiple exchanges, suggesting this is not a momentary lapse but rather a fundamental misalignment with MI principles. The one moment of genuine collaboration is immediately abandoned, indicating inconsistent application rather than genuine MI orientation.

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change

This approach is likely to be counterproductive. The client enters the session with clear motivation for change (importance rating of 8/10), insight into the connection between drinking and contraceptive reliability, and identification of a concrete protective factor (non-drinking friends). She also demonstrates appropriate self-awareness about her readiness level and what feels manageable. The psychiatrist's confrontational response—overriding her self-assessment, dismissing her identified strategies, and demanding immediate abstinence—is likely to increase defensiveness and entrenchment. Research on MI demonstrates that confrontation typically increases sustain talk and decreases change talk, reducing the likelihood of behavior change. By the end of this session, the client has not been asked to explore her own reasons for change, has not been affirmed for her insights or motivation, and has been pressured to commit to a goal she has clearly stated she is not ready for. This is likely to result in decreased engagement, reduced intrinsic motivation, and lower likelihood of follow-through on any change goals.

## Recommendations for Improvement

### 1. **Abandon Confrontation and Adopt Collaborative Stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
Replace prescriptive language ("You need to," "You must," "You should") with collaborative language respecting client autonomy. Example: Instead of "You need to stop drinking completely, right? Why haven't you quit already?" try "I'm hearing that cutting back feels important to you, and that quitting cold turkey feels overwhelming right now. Help me understand what makes that feel so difficult."

### 2. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk Systematically**
When clients offer statements of desire, reason, ability, or commitment to change, pause and explore these using reflective listening. Amplify and deepen change talk rather than moving past it.

### 3. **Respect and Build on Client-Identified Strategies and Resources**
When clients identify concrete strategies or protective factors (like non-drinking friends), affirm these, explore them further, and help develop them into actionable plans rather than overriding with your own prescriptions.

### 4. **Use Open-Ended Questions to Explore Readiness and Capacity**
Replace closed, leading questions with genuine open-ended exploration of the client's readiness, capacity, and self-assessment. Example: Instead of "Are you ready to quit drinking right now?" try "Where do you see yourself right now in terms of making changes with your drinking?"

### 5. **Develop Complex, Double-Sided Reflections to Address Ambivalence**
When clients express ambivalence, use reflective listening that acknowledges both sides. This validates the client's experience and often naturally softens sustain talk without confrontation.

### 6. **Explore the Full Picture: Address Secondary Concerns and Connections**
When clients identify multiple concerns or connections between behaviors (like drinking and contraceptive reliability), explore these fully rather than focusing narrowly on one issue.

### 7. **Affirm Client Insight and Self-Awareness**
Regularly acknowledge and affirm the client's own insights, self-awareness, and appropriate self-assessment. This builds self-efficacy and reinforces the client's role as expert on their own life.

### 8. **Assess and Respect Readiness; Collaborate on Goal-Setting**
Explicitly assess the client's readiness for different levels of change. Rather than prescribing a goal, collaborate with the client to set goals that feel realistic and achievable to them.

### 9. **Receive Formal Training in Motivational Interviewing Principles and Practice**
Engage in certified MI training (2-3 days minimum), including didactic instruction, skill practice, and feedback. Join an MI consultation group for ongoing skill development. Record and review sessions regularly with a supervisor skilled in MI.

### 10. **Reflect on Your Own Beliefs About Change and Client Autonomy**
Examine your own beliefs about how change happens and the role of client autonomy. Confrontation and pressure often reflect clinician beliefs that clients need to be convinced or corrected rather than supported in their own change process.
