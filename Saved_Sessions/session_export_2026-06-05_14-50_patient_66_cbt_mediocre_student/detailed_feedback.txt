
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made no attempt to establish a structured, collaborative agenda for the session. The opening statement—"I thought we could just talk a bit about whatever's been bothering you lately, maybe figure out some ways to feel better. Does that sound okay? We can just kind of go with the flow"—explicitly abandons agenda-setting in favor of an unstructured, drift-based approach. This is fundamentally incompatible with CBT practice.

In the first session, the therapist never clarified session goals, prioritized problems, or established time boundaries. The patient presented a clear, complex issue (OCPD-driven controlling behavior in a relationship), but the therapist did not collaboratively define what success would look like, what specific behavioral targets to address, or how to sequence interventions. By the second session (which appears to be a repeat of the first), the therapist again opens with identical language: "So, maybe we can just chat about whatever's been bothering you lately and see if we can find some ways to feel better." This represents a complete failure to establish continuity or build on prior work.

The repeated opening across multiple sessions suggests this is the therapist's default approach rather than an oversight. In CBT, agenda-setting is not optional—it structures the entire session, ensures efficient use of time, and communicates professionalism and intentionality to the patient. Without it, sessions become reactive and unfocused, particularly problematic for a patient with OCPD who requires clear structure and explicit goals.

**Summary:**

No agenda was established in any session. The therapist explicitly rejected structure in favor of "going with the flow," which violates core CBT principles and leaves sessions directionless.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist elicited minimal feedback and never systematically checked for patient understanding, satisfaction, or reactions to interventions. Across all sessions, there is only one instance where the therapist asks for feedback: "How does that sound?" This occurs after homework assignment but represents a perfunctory check-in rather than genuine feedback elicitation.

- "What's your reaction to this approach?"

This is the only explicit feedback solicitation, and it occurs at session end rather than throughout. There is no mid-session feedback checking, no verification that the patient understood the cognitive restructuring attempts, and no assessment of whether the proposed behavioral experiments felt realistic or acceptable.

For a patient with OCPD—who is perfectionistic, controlling, and prone to rigidity—systematic feedback elicitation is especially critical. It allows the therapist to identify resistance, clarify misunderstandings, and collaboratively adjust interventions. The absence of this creates a risk that the patient will dismiss interventions as unrealistic or will comply superficially without genuine engagement.

**Summary:**

Feedback was elicited only once, at session end, in a perfunctory manner. No mid-session checking for understanding or reactions occurred. This represents inadequate adherence to a core CBT principle.
