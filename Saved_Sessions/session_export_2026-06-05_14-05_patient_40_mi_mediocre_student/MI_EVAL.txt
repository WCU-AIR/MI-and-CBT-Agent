# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal acceptance and unconditional positive regard demonstrated. While opening is warm, clinician quickly pivots to problem-focused questioning that frames client's limitations as deficits rather than understandable responses. Fails to validate emotional experiences of discouragement and fear. When patient expresses worry about "feeling useless," clinician responds with prescriptive advice rather than empathic validation. Tone is clinical and solution-focused rather than warm and supportive.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Surface-level reflective listening present but emotionally flat. When patient describes beautiful experience but inability to participate fully, clinician reflects only the positive while glossing over emotional weight of limitation. Misses profound statement about fear-weakness cycle. When patient expresses shame about feeling "useless," clinician responds with reassurance rather than exploring what failure means. Does not capture existential frustration or identity concerns underlying patient's struggle.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clear focus on increasing physical activity maintained throughout session, which is appropriate given referral. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. Clinician moves to recommendations before fully understanding patient's perspective, values, or intrinsic motivations. Prescriptive language ("Maybe you should...") used repeatedly. Incomplete assessment of referral question—session focuses almost entirely on activity with minimal attention to medical adherence component.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Expert stance maintained throughout with client in subordinate role. Repeated use of directive language: "It would probably help if," "Maybe you should," "It would probably help if you wrote them down." Pseudo-collaboration present—decisions already made, client asked to consent rather than co-create. When patient offers ideas (small goals like stretching), clinician acknowledges but incorporates into clinician-designed framework without asking how patient wants to structure plan. Power not genuinely shared.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Prescriptive language throughout minimizes autonomy. No explicit affirmation of patient's right to choose or emphasis on ownership of change process. Phrases like "Maybe you should start," "It would probably help if you just took it slow" frame clinician's recommendations as logical course of action. When patient expresses hesitation ("I can't promise I'll stick with it all week"), clinician does not explore or affirm right to move at own pace. Closing statement positions clinician as authority figure monitoring compliance rather than patient as autonomous agent.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually absent. Clinician relies heavily on advice-giving and problem-solving rather than drawing out patient's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources. Initial change talk ("I've been thinking about trying to be more active") met with diagnostic questioning rather than evocative exploration. When patient articulates ambivalence, clinician does not explore what makes activity "good" for him or what he values. Critical questions never asked: "What would being more active mean to you?" "What do you value about your health?" "What would it take to do that again?" Patient's own idea about small goals acknowledged but not explored further.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Clinician actively assumes expert role throughout. Language consistently positions psychiatrist as authority: "It would probably help if," "Maybe you should," "That's a good idea" (clinician evaluates), "Just remember" (clinician instructs). When patient offers ideas, clinician incorporates them into clinician-designed framework without asking how patient wants to structure approach. No genuine co-construction of plan. Clinician does not acknowledge that patient knows his body and situation best. By session end, plan is clinician's plan; patient's role is to comply.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Not strategically cultivated. Initial change talk ("I've been thinking about trying to be more active," "It was very, very beautiful") not amplified or explored. When patient says "I do think being more active would be good for me," clinician does not ask "What makes you think that?" or "Tell me more about why that's important." When patient generates own idea about small goals, clinician says "That's a good idea" but does not reinforce further or ask "What else might you be able to do?" Session ends with compliance language ("I'll do my best to try those small steps") rather than genuine motivation language.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk acknowledged but not skillfully softened. Clinician relies on reassurance and advice-giving rather than exploring underlying fears or reframing experiences. When patient describes fear-weakness cycle, clinician does not ask "Tell me more about how that works" or "What have you learned about managing that?" When patient expresses protective pessimism ("it feels kind of pointless"), clinician offers solution rather than exploring learned helplessness. When patient worries about "feeling like a failure," clinician responds with reassurance rather than reframing what failure means. Opportunities to deepen understanding and shift perspective missed.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.8/5 – Poor MI Competence**

### Key Strengths
- Clear, consistent focus on target behavior (physical activity)
- Respectful, non-judgmental tone throughout
- Acknowledgment of patient's ideas when offered
- Professional demeanor and appropriate opening

### Critical Deficiencies
- **Absence of evocation:** Clinician prescribes solutions rather than evoking patient's own motivations and resources
- **Expert-driven rather than collaborative:** Repeated use of "should" language positions clinician as authority
- **Minimal empathic depth:** Surface-level reflections miss emotional nuance and underlying fears
- **Failure to explore ambivalence:** Patient's mixed feelings addressed with reassurance rather than curiosity
- **Inadequate change talk cultivation:** Initial motivation statements not amplified or explored
- **Incomplete referral assessment:** Medical adherence component largely unaddressed

### Impact on Patient Engagement and Change
**Low likelihood of sustained behavior change.** While patient may attempt prescribed plan short-term, language suggests compliance rather than genuine commitment ("I'll do my best," "I can't promise I'll stick with it all week"). Clinician has not helped patient resolve ambivalence, connect activity to personal values, or address core fears (shame, fear of failure, protective pessimism). Research demonstrates that directive approaches increase reactance and reduce sustained behavior change.

### Most Critical Recommendations
1. **Shift from expert-driven to evocative stance** – Ask questions rather than prescribe solutions
2. **Develop deep empathic reflections** – Capture emotional nuance and validate internal experience
3. **Explore and honor ambivalence** – Help patient resolve mixed feelings through reflection, not reassurance
4. **Explicitly affirm autonomy** – Replace "should" language with invitations for patient input
5. **Strategically cultivate change talk** – Amplify and explore patient's own motivation statements

---

**Recommendation for Clinician:** This session demonstrates supportive counseling rather than Motivational Interviewing. Fundamental reframing of clinical approach is needed to embody MI spirit of partnership, evocation, and client-centered change. Recommend MI training or supervision focused on shifting from expert stance to facilitator of self-discovery.
