
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **Immediately Implement Structured CBT Conceptualization and Treatment Planning**
- **Action:** Before the next session, develop a clear CBT conceptualization of the patient's difficulties. Identify maintaining factors (e.g., selective attention to threat, avoidance preventing disconfirmation, magical thinking providing sense of control). Develop a specific, evidence-based treatment plan with clear goals and interventions.
- **Example:** "Henry's paranoid ideation appears to be maintained by selective attention to threat cues (he notices classmates avoiding him but not those who interact normally) and confirmation bias (he interprets neutral events as evidence of plotting). His magical thinking may serve to create a sense of control in an unpredictable world. Our strategy will be: (1) behavioral experiments to test paranoid beliefs, (2) thought records to examine magical thinking, (3) gradual behavioral activation to address social withdrawal, (4) attention training to reduce hypervigilance."
- **Practice:** Consult CBT manuals for paranoid ideation and schizotypal personality disorder (e.g., Kingdon & Turkington's "Cognitive Therapy of Schizophrenia"). Review case conceptualization frameworks. Practice articulating conceptualizations aloud until they become automatic.

### 2. **Replace Generic Advice with Guided Discovery and Socratic Questioning**
- **Action:** Stop offering direct advice. Instead, use Socratic questions to help the patient examine his beliefs and reach his own conclusions. This is more effective for paranoid ideation and builds collaborative alliance.
- **Example:** Instead of "Try to think positive," ask: "You mentioned feeling watched by classmates. What specific evidence have you noticed that they're watching you? What would need to be true for you to feel confident they're not watching you? Are there other ways to interpret what you're observing?"
- **Practice:** Role-play Socratic questioning with a supervisor. Record yourself and review for instances where you lapsed into advice-giving. Practice asking open-ended questions that invite exploration rather than closed questions that invite yes/no answers.

### 3. **Implement Behavioral Experiments to Test Paranoid Beliefs**
- **Action:** Design collaborative behavioral experiments to test the patient's paranoid beliefs. This is a core CBT technique for paranoid ideation and provides concrete evidence that can shift beliefs more effectively than verbal persuasion.
- **Example:** "You believe classmates are avoiding you and plotting against you. Would you be willing to design an experiment to test this? For example, we could track: (1) How many times classmates initiate interaction with you, (2) How many times you initiate interaction with them, (3) What they say when you interact. We'll collect data for one week and review it together. This will help us see whether your belief is supported by evidence."
- **Practice:** Review CBT manuals on behavioral experiments. Practice designing experiments that are specific, testable, and collaborative. Discuss with supervisor how to present experiments in a way that doesn't feel confrontational to a paranoid patient.

### 4. **Introduce Thought Records to Examine Magical Thinking**
- **Action:** Teach the patient to use thought records to track situations, thoughts, feelings, and behaviors related to magical thinking. This helps the patient develop awareness of his thought patterns and examine the evidence for his beliefs.
- **Example:** "I'd like to teach you a tool called a thought record. When you have a thought like 'my thoughts might cause things to happen,' we'll write down: (1) What happened? (2) What did you think? (3) What evidence supports that thought? (4) What evidence contradicts it? (5) What's a more balanced way to think about it? Let's start with one example from this week."
- **Practice:** Create sample thought records for paranoid ideation and magical thinking. Practice teaching thought records to a colleague or supervisor. Ensure you can explain the rationale clearly and answer questions about how it works.

- **Action:** Significantly reduce personal anecdotes. While brief, relevant self-disclosure can build rapport, the current frequency (6+ instances) shifts focus from the patient to the therapist. For a paranoid patient, clear professional boundaries are reassuring.
- **Example:** Instead of "I remember when I got into gardening for a bit—it was relaxing," say: "Many people find that engaging in meaningful activities can provide some relief. For you, it sounds like studying biology is meaningful. Let's talk about how we can build on that."
