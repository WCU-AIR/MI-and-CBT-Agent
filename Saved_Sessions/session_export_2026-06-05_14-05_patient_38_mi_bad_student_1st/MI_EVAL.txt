# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Complete absence of unconditional positive regard. Psychiatrist demonstrates explicit judgment and disapproval throughout session. Opening directive ("I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do") signals client perspective is irrelevant. Language communicates contempt: "No, you don't get to say no," "You have to decide now," "Cutting down is just making excuses." Patient's coping mechanisms dismissed without validation. No affirmation of strengths, no normalization of struggle, no genuine warmth. Psychiatrist treats patient as adversary rather than person worthy of respect. Patient's response—"Pushing me harder just makes me want to dig in and hold on to smoking"—indicates acceptance has been violated and defensive reactance triggered.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Zero genuine effort to understand patient's perspective. Reflective listening entirely absent; arguments and directives replace empathic exploration. When patient states stress is significant factor in smoking, psychiatrist dismisses: "Stress is not a good reason to keep smoking." Patient's fear about quitting ("worried about how much harder things would feel") met with pressure rather than exploration. No complex reflections capturing emotional nuance. No attempt to understand patient's worldview, past experiences, or current life context. Psychiatrist focused entirely on imposing predetermined solution rather than understanding patient's ambivalence or competing priorities.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains clear focus on smoking cessation (appropriate), but method is fundamentally incompatible with MI. Focus is imposed rather than collaboratively established. Never explores whether smoking cessation is patient's goal or what patient's own reasons for change might be. Repeated demands for quit date within one week represent attempt to force direction rather than guide it. Statements like "You must do it immediately" and "You have to decide now" reflect directive control, not collaborative guidance. Patient's final request—"Can we talk about how I might get ready for that step instead?"—indicates patient seeking collaborative direction that psychiatrist has failed to provide. Direction exists but delivered in way that violates MI principles and increases resistance.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no collaboration present. Psychiatrist explicitly rejects collaboration from opening: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to quit." Consistently overrides patient's input. When patient references research about cutting down, psychiatrist dismisses without discussion: "No, no, no. You have to quit completely. Cutting down is just making excuses." When patient expresses readiness concerns, psychiatrist responds with demands rather than exploration. Most damaging: "No, you don't get to say no"—antithesis of collaboration, communicating patient's voice and autonomy are irrelevant. No joint problem-solving, no shared decision-making, no co-construction of solutions. Psychiatrist is sole authority imposing solutions on passive recipient.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Explicit violations of autonomy at multiple critical points. Psychiatrist directly attacks client choice: "No, you don't get to say no," "You have to decide now." When patient expresses ambivalence about readiness, psychiatrist responds with pressure: "No, you can't wait around feeling overwhelmed. You have to be serious about this now." Frames quitting as non-negotiable without exploring whether patient agrees or what approach patient believes might work. No acknowledgment that patient has right to make own choices about health. Patient's response—"I don't want to feel like I'm being pressured or rushed"—directly indicates autonomy violation. Patient explicitly states: "If I'm forced to decide right now, I'm not sure I can commit." Textbook example of how violating autonomy support generates reactance and resistance.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist tells repeatedly and never genuinely evokes. Questions asked are rhetorical and accusatory rather than evocative: "Why haven't you quit yet if you know it's bad?" "Why haven't you made quitting your number one priority?" These are accusations disguised as questions, not genuine inquiries seeking understanding. True evocation would involve: "What do you think about smoking in your life?" "What concerns do you have about quitting?" "What would need to happen for you to feel ready?" Instead, psychiatrist provides answers and imposes reasons for change. Patient offers spontaneous change talk ("Wow, I didn't realize it was so dangerous, not just for some people but for me personally too"), but psychiatrist does not build on or explore this. Immediately pivots to pressure. Never explores patient's own reasons for changing, values regarding health, past attempts and learning, or strengths and resources.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist assumes exclusive expert role and actively suppresses client input. Opening statement establishes this: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to quit." Positions self as sole authority throughout: "You have to quit completely," "You need to stop making excuses," "You must do it immediately." When patient offers information ("I was reading that sometimes cutting down can still help"), psychiatrist dismisses without discussion. Does not acknowledge that patient knows their own life, stress levels, capacity, and readiness better than anyone else. Patient explicitly states this need: "I'm the one who has to live with this choice every day" and "I need some space to come to this decision myself." Psychiatrist ignores these clear statements. True partnership would involve: "You know your life better than I do. What do you think might work for you?" Instead, psychiatrist maintains exclusive expert authority and actively suppresses power-sharing.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Change talk not cultivated; it is suppressed. Patient offers one instance of genuine change talk: "Wow, I didn't realize it was so dangerous, not just for some people but for me personally too." Skilled MI practitioner would immediately amplify this with exploration. Instead, psychiatrist pivots to pressure: "Still, picking a quit date feels kind of overwhelming... No, you can't wait around feeling overwhelmed." Does not explore, amplify, or reinforce change talk. Confrontational approach generates defensive sustain talk instead: "I'm not sure if quitting is really such a priority," "I don't feel like it's as risky for me personally," "I'm not ready to set a date," and most tellingly, "Pushing me harder just makes me want to dig in and hold on to smoking even more." Psychiatrist never asks open questions that would evoke change talk. Tells and demands instead, which suppresses change talk and amplifies sustain talk.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Sustain talk amplified rather than softened through direct confrontation. Patient expresses legitimate sustain talk: "I'm not sure if quitting is really such a priority," "I don't feel like it's as risky for me personally," "I have a lot on my plate," "I'm not ready to set a date." Rather than softening with empathy and strategic reflection, psychiatrist argues directly: "You really should be smoking less," "Stress is not a good reason to keep smoking," "You can't just rely on luck or past illnesses," "No, you can't wait around feeling overwhelmed." Each is direct confrontation of patient's stated concern. Skilled MI practitioner would use double-sided reflection acknowledging both sides without arguing. Instead, psychiatrist argues, causing patient to defend sustain talk more strongly. Patient's final response demonstrates entrenchment: "I don't want to feel like I'm being pressured or rushed... Demanding I pick a quit date right now just isn't working for me." Patient's statement—"Pushing me harder just makes me want to dig in and hold on to smoking even more"—is direct indicator that psychiatrist's approach has backfired, amplifying sustain talk rather than softening it.

Patient's explicit statement—"Pushing me harder just makes me want to dig in and hold on to smoking even more"—is direct evidence that approach is counterproductive. Patient came with some openness (contemplation stage) and expressed genuine change talk; psychiatrist's approach has likely moved patient backward and strengthened commitment to status quo.

Psychiatrist requires formal MI training with supervised practice and feedback before continuing MI-based interventions. Current approach is not only ineffective—it is actively harmful to therapeutic relationship and patient outcomes.
