# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal acceptance and frequently adopts a judgmental, directive stance. While opening greeting is warm, the session quickly shifts to prescriptive mode with repeated "should" language ("Maybe you should just try to be more mindful," "It would probably help if you set some reminders"). This communicates conditional regard rather than unconditional positive regard. No affirmation of Adrian's strengths (managing infection despite discomfort, attempting medication adherence) or normalization of change difficulty. Dismisses Adrian's low readiness scores (3/10, 4/10) rather than accepting his current state.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates surface-level listening but lacks genuine empathic depth. Reflections are factual and brief ("Okay, so the meth use on weekends might be making the pain worse sometimes"). Critical emotional windows are missed—when Adrian states meth "keeps me from getting bored or stuck in my head," psychiatrist does not explore what "stuck in my head" means or the underlying emotional need. No exploration of Adrian's shame, fear about drug contamination, or ambivalence about change. Emotional nuance is consistently ignored in favor of behavioral focus.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains general focus on infection and medication adherence but direction is muddled and inconsistently pursued. Initial opening is vague ("I usually like to focus on things like diet, exercise, or any habits you want to improve"). While psychiatrist attempts to link meth use to infection healing, this direction is abandoned after Adrian expresses low readiness. Final direction drifts toward vague goals ("be more mindful," "small steps") without clear behavioral targets. No exploration of Adrian's values, vision of health, or how meth use fits into life goals. Direction lacks strategic clarity and progressive refinement.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Significant deficiency. Psychiatrist demonstrates authoritarian, expert-driven stance throughout with minimal genuine collaboration. Repeatedly imposes solutions disguised as suggestions: "Maybe you should just try to be more mindful," "It would probably help if you set some reminders," "Maybe you should just try to keep it simple." When Adrian explicitly rejects alarms ("Nah, I haven't really done alarms or anything"), psychiatrist dismisses feedback and reasserts authority rather than asking "What would work better for you?" No invitation for Adrian's ideas or problem-solving. Final summary tells Adrian what he "wants" rather than asking and co-creating plan together. Fundamentally inconsistent with MI spirit.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively undermines Adrian's autonomy and sense of choice. Pervasive use of "should" language communicates that Adrian *ought* to comply, creating pressure and removing sense of choice. When Adrian provides own readiness assessment (3/10 importance, 4/10 confidence), psychiatrist overrides it: "Since the importance and confidence are kind of low, maybe you should just try to be more mindful." This communicates Adrian's assessment doesn't matter. No explicit affirmation of Adrian's right to choose differently. When Adrian states "It's not that easy to just stop something I do for fun," psychiatrist does not validate this as legitimate choice but instead pivots to scaling questions. Final exchange subtly pressures Adrian to comply while appearing to accept his position.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal evocation and relies on telling Adrian why he should change. When Adrian mentions meth "keeps me from getting bored or stuck in my head," psychiatrist does not evoke Adrian's own understanding with curiosity. When Adrian expresses ambivalence ("Yeah, maybe it could help if I cut back a bit, but I don't know if quitting or cutting down is a big deal"), psychiatrist does not evoke Adrian's thinking about pros and cons but instead jumps to scaling questions. After Adrian answers scaling questions with low scores, psychiatrist concludes with prescription rather than evoking Adrian's thinking about why scores are low or what would need to change. Minimal evocation of Adrian's values, strengths, or resources. No questions about what matters most, what Adrian is good at, or when he successfully made changes before.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role and demonstrates no genuine partnership. From opening, psychiatrist frames session as psychiatrist's agenda ("I usually like to focus on things like diet, exercise") rather than asking Adrian what he wants to focus on. Adrian clarifies concern is infection, but psychiatrist does not fully honor this—attempts to broaden focus to "habits you want to improve," which is psychiatrist's framework, not Adrian's. Repeatedly tells Adrian what to do rather than co-creating solutions. Fails to invite Adrian's expertise about his own life. Questions are closed and leading ("Do you think stopping or cutting down on the meth would help your infection heal faster?") rather than open and exploratory. Final summary exemplifies absence of partnership—psychiatrist tells Adrian what he "wants" rather than asking and co-creating plan. This is unilateral expert pronouncement, not partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist fails to strategically evoke or amplify Adrian's change talk. Adrian produces some spontaneous change talk ("Yeah, maybe it could help if I cut back a bit," "Yeah, I get that it might be good to change," "I'll see if I can do better"), but psychiatrist does not amplify or reinforce. When Adrian says "Yeah, maybe it could help if I cut back a bit," psychiatrist does not follow up with "Tell me more about that" but instead pivots to Adrian's sustain talk. When Adrian says "Yeah, I get that it might be good to change," psychiatrist does not reinforce or explore but concludes session with vague encouragement. Psychiatrist fails to use strategic questions to evoke change talk (e.g., "What concerns you most?" "What would be different if you cut back?" "How would your health improve?"). No use of reflections to amplify change talk or double-sided reflections that emphasize change side of ambivalence.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal skill in softening sustain talk and sometimes inadvertently reinforces it. Adrian produces significant sustain talk (minimization: "I only do it on weekends, snort it mostly, so it's not like I'm doing crazy amounts every day"; justification: "It's something to do, keeps me from getting bored"; resistance: "It's not that easy to just stop something I do for fun"). When Adrian minimizes use, psychiatrist does not skillfully redirect but asks direct question that could be perceived as confrontational: "Do you think stopping or cutting down on the meth would help your infection heal faster?" When Adrian says "It's not that easy to just stop something I do for fun," psychiatrist does not acknowledge legitimacy or explore but pivots to scaling questions, implicitly suggesting Adrian should reconsider. When Adrian expresses low confidence, psychiatrist does not explore or build self-efficacy but responds with "Since the importance and confidence are kind of low, maybe you should just try to be more mindful." Misses opportunities to use double-sided reflections that acknowledge both sustain talk and change talk without confrontation.

### Overall Session Assessment

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.6/5**

**Summary:** This session demonstrates **significant deficiencies in Motivational Interviewing practice** and does not embody MI spirit. Psychiatrist operates from expert-authority model rather than collaborative partnership. Fundamental gaps include: (1) pervasive prescriptive language ("should," "maybe you should") communicating judgment and pressure; (2) dismissal of Adrian's readiness assessment (3/10, 4/10) with imposed solutions; (3) minimal empathic depth and failure to explore emotional world; (4) failure to evoke Adrian's own motivation; (5) inadequate handling of sustain talk; (6) vague, non-specific direction; (7) absence of genuine partnership. While psychiatrist demonstrates basic courtesy and attempts to link meth use to health outcomes, these minimal strengths are vastly outweighed by fundamental MI competence gaps.

**Current Competency Level:** Pre-MI or non-MI level. Session resembles traditional advice-giving rather than Motivational Interviewing. Psychiatrist tells Adrian what to do rather than asking what Adrian thinks. This approach is likely to **decrease Adrian's intrinsic motivation for change** and may increase resistance.

**Critical Recommendations:**
1. **Shift from expert-authority to genuine partnership** (highest priority)
2. Develop genuine empathic depth through complex reflections
3. Evoke Adrian's own motivation rather than imposing it
4. Strategically amplify change talk and soften sustain talk
5. Honor Adrian's readiness level and autonomy
6. Eliminate prescriptive language; replace "should" with curiosity
7. Establish clear, collaborative goals and direction
8. Develop skill in handling ambivalence without confrontation
9. Strengthen understanding of MI spirit and principles through training/supervision

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation:** **Unlikely.** Prescriptive, expert-driven approach may strengthen Adrian's resistance and defensiveness. Adrian needs to feel heard, understood, and respected as expert on his own life. This session does not provide that experience.
