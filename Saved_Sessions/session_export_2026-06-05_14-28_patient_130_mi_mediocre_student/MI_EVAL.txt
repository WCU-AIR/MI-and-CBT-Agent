# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal acceptance and fails to establish unconditional positive regard. While opening is warm, the approach quickly becomes problem-focused and agenda-driven. No affirmations of Tyler's worth or normalization of adolescent struggle occur. The statement "Since you're low on the importance scale, maybe you should just try to keep things like they are for now" is patronizing and dismissive, communicating that Tyler's stated low motivation is acceptable only if he complies with the hidden agenda. Tyler's legitimate concerns about friendships and coping with home stress are treated as obstacles rather than understandable human needs. Surface-level politeness masks a subtly judgmental, agenda-driven stance.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflective listening is present but superficial and used instrumentally rather than to demonstrate genuine understanding. The reflection "Sounds like your friends are pretty important to you, and you don't want to cause any problems by saying no" captures content but misses emotional depth. When Tyler reveals that substance use helps him "relax and forget about all the stuff at home," the psychiatrist fails to acknowledge this emotional pain and coping mechanism. No exploration of Tyler's fear of peer rejection, his need for autonomy, or his use of substances as a response to family stress occurs. Emotional nuance and deeper meaning are consistently missed. Empathy is inadequate for building therapeutic alliance.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains clear focus on substance use reduction, which is appropriate for the presenting concern. However, direction is pursued through leading questions and subtle pressure rather than collaborative exploration. Questions like "Do you think that maybe stopping or cutting down could help you feel better?" presuppose the psychiatrist's agenda rather than evoking Tyler's own observations. The contradictory statement in the fourth exchange—"maybe you should just try to keep things like they are for now. But it would probably help if you think about some ways to avoid trouble"—is confusing and undermines clarity. Direction is present but pursued in ways that undermine MI principles and client autonomy.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** True collaboration is fundamentally absent. The psychiatrist maintains an expert, directive stance throughout, with Tyler positioned as a passive recipient of the psychiatrist's agenda. Leading questions impose the psychiatrist's framework rather than inviting Tyler to define the problem. Prescriptive advice is disguised as questions ("Maybe you should think about how it affects you"). The psychiatrist tells Tyler what he "should" do rather than asking "What do you think might help?" or "What ideas do you have?" Tyler's ideas, wisdom, and expertise about his own life are never solicited or valued. The psychiatrist is the expert; Tyler is the problem to be solved. This is fundamentally inconsistent with MI spirit.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is actively undermined throughout the session. The psychiatrist uses subtle pressure, leading questions, and prescriptive advice rather than genuinely supporting Tyler's right to choose. Contradictory messaging ("maybe you should just try to keep things like they are for now. But it would probably help if...") implies Tyler should change to "avoid trouble"—pressure disguised as acceptance. Phrases like "it would probably help if you think about some ways to avoid trouble" and "Do you think you could try to cut back a little, even just to keep the peace at home?" frame change as something Tyler "should" do to manage his mother's reaction, not as something he chooses for himself. No explicit affirmation of Tyler's freedom to choose occurs. The implicit message is compliance, not authentic choice.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out the client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources—is almost entirely absent. The psychiatrist consistently tells rather than asks, prescribes rather than explores, and imposes rather than evokes. Critical missed opportunities include: (1) Tyler's statement that substance use helps him "relax and forget about all the stuff at home" is never explored for underlying stress or alternative coping strategies; (2) Tyler's fear of peer rejection is never explored; (3) Tyler's relationship with his mother and her concerns are never examined; (4) Tyler's values, goals, and aspirations are never accessed. Leading questions presuppose the psychiatrist's agenda rather than inviting Tyler to articulate his own perspective. When Tyler does express minimal change talk ("I guess I could try"), the psychiatrist fails to amplify or explore it further. Tyler's own wisdom and resources are never accessed.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership—the collaborative stance that expertise and wisdom about change reside within the client—is fundamentally absent. The psychiatrist assumes the expert role throughout, with Tyler positioned as a passive recipient of the psychiatrist's knowledge and direction. At no point does the psychiatrist communicate "You know your life better than I do. What do you think would work?" or "Let's figure this out together." Instead, the implicit message is "I'm the expert. Here's what you should do." The power dynamic is entirely unequal: the psychiatrist controls the agenda, asks the questions, proposes solutions, and evaluates progress. Tyler is passive, reactive, and compliant. The opening ("I thought we could talk a bit about any health habits you might want to work on") sounds collaborative but the psychiatrist has already decided the topic and direction. Tyler's input is not genuinely solicited.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk is minimal and poorly cultivated. When Tyler expresses weak change talk ("I guess I could try to hang out without using all the time"), the psychiatrist fails to amplify it. Instead, the psychiatrist immediately adds caveats ("But you don't want it to affect your friendships"), reinforcing ambivalence rather than strengthening change talk. The psychiatrist frames Tyler's minimal commitment as externally motivated ("to keep things smoother at home") rather than helping him discover his own reasons for change. Critical missed opportunities to evoke change talk include: (1) exploring what Tyler values about managing stress differently; (2) exploring Tyler's ability to navigate peer pressure; (3) exploring whether Tyler recognizes any validity in his mother's concerns; (4) connecting change to Tyler's values and aspirations. Leading questions presuppose the psychiatrist's agenda rather than evoking Tyler's own change talk. Strategic evocation is absent.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk emerges throughout the session ("I don't really wanna be here," "I don't see what the big deal is," "I'm fine the way things are," "I'm not really thinking about changing"), and the psychiatrist's handling is inconsistent and often counterproductive. The psychiatrist accurately reflects sustain talk but fails to soften it through reframing, double-sided reflections, or exploration of ambivalence. When Tyler says "I don't see what the big deal is," the psychiatrist never asks "Help me understand. What would need to happen for you to see this as a problem?" Leading questions often reinforce sustain talk rather than gently shifting focus. The psychiatrist never uses reframing to acknowledge Tyler's strengths (e.g., "You value your friendships and you're loyal—that's a strength. The question is whether you can be loyal AND take care of yourself"). Strategic responses to soften sustain talk are absent; instead, the psychiatrist's approach creates confusion and reinforces resistance.

### Key Findings

**Strengths:**
- Basic reflective listening with accurate content reflection
- Maintained non-confrontational tone throughout
- Consistent focus on presenting concern (substance use)
- Acceptance of minimal commitment without excessive pushing

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Absence of genuine partnership:** Expert-directive stance undermines MI principles
- **Failed evocation:** Tells rather than asks; prescribes rather than explores
- **Subtle pressure and manipulation:** Leading questions and prescriptive language create compliance, not authentic motivation
- **Inadequate empathy:** Superficial reflections miss emotional depth and meaning
- **Poor sustain talk management:** Reinforces resistance rather than exploring ambivalence
- **Minimal change talk cultivation:** Fails to amplify or explore Tyler's own language about change

**Impact on Client:**
Tyler's importance rating remains 1-2. His final statement ("I'm not really making big promises or anything, but I'll think about it") indicates compliance rather than authentic motivation. The session is unlikely to increase intrinsic motivation for change. Tyler's own wisdom, values, fears, and resources remain unexplored and unaccessed.

**Recommendation:**
This psychiatrist requires significant formal training and ongoing supervision in MI to develop competence. The core deficiency is the absence of genuine evocation—the heart of MI. Fundamental reframing of the clinician's role from expert to collaborative partner is essential for effective practice.
