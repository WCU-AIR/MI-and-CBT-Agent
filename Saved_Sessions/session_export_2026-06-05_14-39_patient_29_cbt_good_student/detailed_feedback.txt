
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Reassess and reconceptualize the case (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Conduct a thorough functional analysis of Kyle's antisocial behavior. Specifically assess: (a) What triggers his urge to steal/break rules? (b) What does he get out of it (thrill, autonomy, material gain, status)? (c) What maintains the behavior? (d) What is his level of callousness and lack of remorse? (e) How do ADHD symptoms contribute?
- **Example:** Ask Kyle directly: "When you broke into the store, what were you thinking? What did you want? What did it feel like? What happened after? Would you do it again?" Listen for evidence of instrumental planning, lack of concern for consequences, or thrill-seeking.
- **Practice:** Review the case conceptualization with a supervisor. Ensure you understand conduct disorder with limited prosocial emotions and how it differs from other presentations. Read: Frick & Nimon (2012) on callous-unemotional traits in youth.

### 2. **Shift treatment focus from cognitions to behavior and consequences**
- **Action:** Develop a behavioral contingency management plan for the inpatient setting. Establish clear consequences for rule-breaking and reinforcement for prosocial behavior. Coordinate with nursing staff and Kyle's family.
- **Example:** "Kyle, here's how it works in the hospital: If you follow the rules and treat others respectfully, you earn points toward privileges (extra time outside, phone calls, etc.). If you break rules or are disrespectful, you lose points. This isn't punishment—it's how we help you learn that your choices have consequences."
- **Practice:** Learn contingency management techniques. Role-play implementing consequences with a supervisor. Practice maintaining consistency and not becoming frustrated when Kyle tests limits.

### 3. **Directly address callousness and lack of remorse**
- **Action:** In future sessions, explicitly explore Kyle's lack of concern for others and consequences. Use values clarification to help him identify what he actually cares about and how his behavior aligns (or doesn't) with those values.
- **Example:** "Kyle, I notice you don't seem worried about the store owner or the people you hurt. Is that true? What would it take for you to care? What do you actually care about—your freedom, having money, not being bored, having friends?"
- **Practice:** Develop skill in values clarification work. Practice asking about Kyle's goals and dreams without judgment. Learn to connect values to behavior change.

### 4. **Incorporate impulse control and problem-solving training**
- **Action:** Teach Kyle concrete strategies for pausing before acting, considering alternatives, and planning ahead. Use the "STOP" technique: Stop, Think about consequences, Options, Plan.
- **Example:** "Before you take something or break a rule, use STOP: Stop and take a breath. Think—what will happen if I do this? Options—what else could I do? Plan—what's my best move?" Practice this with real scenarios from Kyle's life.
- **Practice:** Learn problem-solving training techniques. Practice teaching these skills to youth with ADHD. Role-play with Kyle using scenarios from his history.

### 5. **Involve family in treatment planning**
- **Action:** Schedule a family session with Kyle's mother and stepfather. Assess their understanding of conduct disorder, their ability to implement consequences, and their own stress/resources. Develop a coordinated plan for home.
- **Example:** "Mrs. Harper, Kyle's behavior is driven by a combination of not thinking about consequences and not caring about how his actions affect others. At home, we need to be consistent about consequences. If he steals, there's a consequence. If he's respectful, there's a reward. Can you do that?"
- **Practice:** Develop family therapy skills. Learn to assess parental capacity and stress. Practice teaching parents about conduct disorder and evidence-based parenting strategies.

### 6. **Establish clear behavioral goals and measurement**
- **Action:** Work with Kyle to establish specific, measurable behavioral goals (not just cognitive goals). Examples: "No rule violations for 3 days," "Complete homework 4 days per week," "Use problem-solving steps before acting impulsively."
- **Example:** "Kyle, our goal isn't just for you to feel better or think differently. Our goal is for you to make better choices. That means no stealing, no fighting, and following the rules. Let's track how many days you do that."
- **Practice:** Learn to set SMART goals with youth. Practice measuring behavior objectively. Learn to give feedback based on data.

### 7. **Revise homework to be specific, concrete, and well-prepared**
- **Action:** For the next session, assign homework that is specific, concrete, and directly tied to behavioral change. Provide a structured form. Role-play the homework. Establish a clear review plan.
- **Example:** "Kyle, this week, I want you to do one thing your mom or stepdad asks you to do without arguing. It can be anything—take out the trash, do homework, whatever.
