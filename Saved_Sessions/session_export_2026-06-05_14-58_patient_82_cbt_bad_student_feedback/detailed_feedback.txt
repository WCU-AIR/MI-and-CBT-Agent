
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made no attempt to establish a collaborative agenda at any point during this session. The opening question—"How have you been feeling lately? Anything on your mind you want to chat about?"—is an open-ended inquiry rather than agenda-setting. A proper CBT agenda would involve: (1) explicitly reviewing the patient's presenting problems, (2) collaboratively prioritizing which issues to address in this session, (3) establishing time allocation for each topic, and (4) gaining explicit agreement on session goals.

The patient spontaneously identified his primary concern (restless legs sensations, sleep disruption, daytime fatigue) and the therapist never formalized this into a structured agenda. There is no evidence the therapist and patient agreed on specific, measurable session objectives. The conversation drifts without clear direction—the therapist introduces tangential topics (personal anecdotes about hiking, water intake, fresh air) without connecting them to a coherent treatment plan. A proper agenda would have established: "Today we're going to focus on understanding your leg sensations better and developing specific strategies to manage them during dialysis. We'll also discuss how sleep disruption connects to your daytime fatigue. Does that sound like a good use of our time?"

This represents a fundamental failure in CBT structure. Without agenda-setting, sessions lack direction, accountability, and measurable progress.

**Summary:**

No collaborative agenda was established. The session lacked structure, prioritization, or explicit agreement on goals—critical foundational elements of CBT.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist never elicited feedback from the patient about the session, his understanding of recommendations, or his reactions to the therapist's suggestions. This is a complete absence of a core CBT competency. Evidence-based feedback elicitation includes: (1) asking whether the patient understood the therapist's reasoning, (2) checking whether suggestions feel realistic or helpful, (3) soliciting reactions to interventions, (4) summarizing key points and asking for patient input, and (5) assessing satisfaction with the session direction.

The therapist made multiple suggestions (think positive, drink water, move legs, make sleeping area cozy, get fresh air, focus on happy memories) without once asking: "Does this make sense to you?" "Do you think this is something you could actually do?" "How realistic does this feel given your dialysis schedule?" "What are your thoughts on what we discussed today?" The patient's responses suggest some skepticism or difficulty implementing suggestions (e.g., "the itching can be pretty persistent," "the relief doesn't last long," "it's hard to ignore that feeling"), yet the therapist never explored these barriers or adjusted based on patient feedback.

In the second session, the identical pattern repeats—no feedback elicitation whatsoever. This represents a critical failure in collaborative practice and prevents the therapist from understanding whether interventions are landing, whether the patient feels heard, or whether adjustments are needed.

**Summary:**

Zero instances of feedback elicitation across both sessions. The therapist never checked understanding, solicited reactions, or adjusted based on patient input—a fundamental CBT failure.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrates surface-level understanding of the patient's explicit complaints but misses critical clinical nuances and fails to grasp the patient's internal experience. The therapist accurately reflects that the patient has leg sensations, sleep problems, and finds joy with grandchildren. However, significant gaps emerge:

1. **Medical complexity**: The patient explicitly states he must limit fluids due to dialysis ("I have to be careful about how much fluid I take in"). The therapist repeatedly recommends drinking water without acknowledging this constraint. This suggests the therapist either didn't understand or didn't retain this critical information. A therapist with genuine understanding would say: "I hear you need to be careful with fluids because of dialysis. Let's think about what hydration strategies might work within those limits."

2. **The nature of RLS**: The patient describes classic RLS symptoms (sensations worse during sedentary periods, relieved by movement, worse at night, distressing). The therapist never demonstrates understanding that this is a neurological condition with specific triggers and patterns. Instead, he treats it as a general sleep/mood problem amenable to positive thinking.

The therapist doesn't explore this—he simply repeats the suggestion without understanding the patient's lived experience of its limitations.

4. **Cultural and contextual factors**: The patient is a 63-year-old Chinese immigrant, former pharmacist, now on dialysis. His identity has shifted from professional to dependent. The therapist shows no curiosity about how this affects his mood, self-worth, or coping. No exploration of cultural factors, family dynamics, or the psychological impact of chronic illness.

5. **The distinction between depression (in remission) and RLS**: The case notes indicate depression is in remission and the primary current problem is RLS. The therapist conflates these, treating RLS symptoms as if they're primarily psychological/mood-related rather than neurological.

The therapist's repeated personal anecdotes ("When I was younger," "I remember when I had a tough time," "I find that helps me") further demonstrate lack of understanding—he's centering his own experience rather than deeply understanding the patient's unique situation.
