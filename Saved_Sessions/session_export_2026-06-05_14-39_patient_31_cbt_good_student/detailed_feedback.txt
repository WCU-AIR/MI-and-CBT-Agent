
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## CRITICAL PRELIMINARY NOTE

**This evaluation reveals a fundamental and dangerous clinical error: The therapist is conducting CBT for a patient with acute delirium—a medical emergency requiring immediate psychiatric/medical intervention, not psychotherapy.**

Wesley Brown presents with:
- Acute onset (2 weeks) fluctuating consciousness and attention
- Fever (39.9°C), tachycardia (115 bpm), hypertension (172/74)
- Anticholinergic toxidrome (mydriasis, dry mucosa, urinary retention, depressed reflexes)
- Medication-induced delirium (cyclobenzaprine + benzodiazepines + opioids)
- Dangerous behavior (running with knives, displaying guns)
- MMSE 16/30 (severe cognitive impairment)

**CBT is contraindicated.** Delirium requires:
1. Medical stabilization (medication review, treatment of underlying cause)
2. Environmental management (orientation cues, reduced stimulation)
3. Possible pharmacological intervention (antipsychotics for severe agitation)
4. NOT cognitive restructuring of psychotic symptoms

The therapist's approach—treating paranoid ideation and perceptual disturbances as modifiable cognitions—fundamentally misunderstands delirium pathophysiology. This patient cannot engage in collaborative empiricism due to fluctuating consciousness and impaired attention. **Proceeding with CBT delays necessary medical intervention and risks patient safety.**

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made an appropriate opening attempt at agenda-setting by asking "Are there one or two main things you'd like to focus on during our session?" and requesting a mood rating (0-10). This demonstrates basic awareness of CBT structure. However, the agenda-setting was fundamentally inadequate for this clinical presentation.

**What was done:** The therapist elicited the patient's stated concerns (confusion, paranoia, feeling watched) and obtained a subjective mood rating (7/10). The therapist then proposed focusing on "one recent situation where these feelings and thoughts came up strongly"—the kitchen incident—and the patient agreed.

**What was critically missing:**

1. **No medical/psychiatric triage:** The therapist did not assess whether this patient is medically stable for psychotherapy. With fever, tachycardia, hypertension, acute confusion, and dangerous recent behavior (running with knives, displaying guns to neighbors), medical clearance and stabilization should precede any psychotherapy.

2. **No assessment of capacity for CBT:** The therapist did not evaluate whether the patient can sustain attention, follow complex reasoning, or engage in collaborative empiricism—all prerequisites for CBT. MMSE 16/30 indicates severe cognitive impairment incompatible with standard CBT.

3. **No prioritization of safety:** The agenda did not address the patient's recent dangerous behavior or current safety planning. This is a critical omission given the presentation.

4. **No acknowledgment of delirium symptoms:** The therapist treated fluctuating consciousness, perceptual disturbances, and paranoia as psychological phenomena suitable for cognitive work, rather than recognizing these as delirium symptoms requiring medical intervention.

5. **No time-limited, realistic goals:** The agenda implied that cognitive restructuring could resolve the patient's symptoms within a session, which is unrealistic for delirium.

**Why this matters:** Inappropriate agenda-setting in this context delays necessary medical care and creates false hope that psychotherapy will address a medical emergency. It also exposes the patient to continued risk if dangerous behavior is not immediately addressed through medical stabilization and environmental management.

**Summary:**
The therapist demonstrated basic agenda-setting technique but failed to conduct appropriate clinical triage. The agenda was fundamentally misaligned with the patient's actual clinical needs and medical status.
