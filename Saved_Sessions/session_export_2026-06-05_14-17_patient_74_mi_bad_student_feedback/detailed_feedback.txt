
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and contains multiple instances of explicit judgment and disapproval. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels Linda's legitimate concerns as "excuses" (appearing 5+ times throughout the session), which directly communicates that her perspective is invalid and unworthy of respect. Phrases such as "you're just making excuses and avoiding responsibility," "stop overthinking this," and "you can't keep saying you're scared and not ready" are fundamentally judgmental and dismissive of her emotional experience.

The psychiatrist frames Linda's ambivalence—a normal and expected part of the contemplation stage—as a character flaw requiring correction rather than as understandable human struggle worthy of compassion. There is no affirmation of Linda's strengths (her self-referral, her acknowledgment of health risks, her willingness to engage in conversation, her repeated attempts to quit). Instead, the psychiatrist communicates that Linda is weak, irresponsible, and failing to "take this seriously"—language that undermines her dignity and worth as a person.

Critically, the psychiatrist never normalizes the difficulty of smoking cessation or acknowledges that ambivalence and fear are universal experiences in behavior change. There is no warmth, no genuine support, and no communication that Linda is valued as a person independent of her smoking status. The repeated pressure and confrontation create an adversarial dynamic rather than a supportive alliance.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental, disapproving stance that violates the foundational MI principle of unconditional positive regard. Linda is repeatedly labeled as making excuses and avoiding responsibility, with no affirmation of her worth or acknowledgment of the legitimate difficulty of change.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective from their internal frame of reference. This session contains virtually no reflective listening and demonstrates a striking failure to understand or validate Linda's experience.

When Linda expresses fear ("it scares me sometimes"), the psychiatrist does not reflect this fear or explore its meaning. When Linda describes the social and identity dimensions of smoking ("it's kind of how I connect with my friends," "part of who I am"), the psychiatrist dismisses these as irrelevant ("It's not about how long you've smoked or who you smoke with"). When Linda articulates her fear of repeated failure ("I'm worried that even if I start something like that now, I might just fail again, and then I'll feel even worse"), the psychiatrist does not acknowledge this legitimate concern but instead pressures her further.

The psychiatrist's responses are entirely factual and directive, focused on what Linda "should" do rather than understanding why she struggles. There are zero complex reflections that capture emotional nuance or deepen insight. The psychiatrist never reflects Linda's ambivalence (the simultaneous desire to quit and fear of failure), never explores her past failed attempts to understand what barriers exist, and never validates the emotional weight of her struggle.

Most tellingly, Linda explicitly states "talking about it like this, with someone who listens, it does help me start thinking that maybe I could try again"—a clear signal that she values being heard and understood. The psychiatrist completely ignores this feedback and continues with the same confrontational, non-empathic approach.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal empathic effort, with no reflective listening, no validation of Linda's emotional experience, and repeated dismissal of her legitimate concerns about fear and past failures. The session lacks any complex reflections that might deepen understanding.

### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

While the psychiatrist maintains a clear focus on smoking cessation (the target behavior), the approach to direction is fundamentally misaligned with MI principles. ## Recommendations

### 1. **Fundamental Shift from Confrontation to Collaboration**
- **Action:** Adopt a collaborative, partnership-based stance. Treat Linda as an equal expert on her own life, not as a non-compliant patient who needs correction.
- **Example:** Instead of "You need to stop making excuses and just quit," say: "I can see you're really wrestling with this. You want to quit for your health and to save money, and at the same time, you're worried about failing again. That's a real conflict. Help me understand what's making it hard for you right now."
- **Practice:** Before each session, remind yourself: "My job is not to convince Linda to quit. My job is to help her explore her own reasons for change and build her confidence. She is the expert on her own life."

---

### 2. **Develop Genuine Empathic Listening and Reflective Responses**
- **Action:** Use reflective listening to demonstrate understanding of Linda's perspective, especially her fears and ambivalence. Reflect both sides of her conflict.
- **Example:** When Linda says "I'm worried that even if I start something like that now, I might just fail again, and then I'll feel even worse," reflect: "You've tried before and it didn't work out, and you're worried that if you try again and fail, it will hurt even more. That makes sense—you're protecting yourself from disappointment."
- **Practice:** Record yourself and count the number of reflections vs. directives. Aim for at least 3 reflections for every 1 directive. Practice complex reflections that capture emotion and meaning, not just facts.

---

### 3. **Evoke Rather Than Tell: Ask Questions to Draw Out Motivation**
- **Action:** Replace directives with open-ended questions designed to help Linda explore her own reasons for change, her values, and her resources.
- **Example:** Instead of "You must cut down right away," ask: "You mentioned that smoking costs you a lot of money. Tell me more about how that affects your life. What could you do with that money if you weren't spending it on cigarettes?" Or: "You've tried to quit several times. What happened in those attempts? What did you learn about yourself?"
- **Practice:** Develop a list of evocative questions for smoking cessation (e.g., "What would be different in your life if you quit?", "What have you already tried that helped a little?", "What would give you confidence that you could do this?"). Practice asking these questions and then listening deeply to the answers.

---

### 4. **Respect Autonomy and Eliminate Pressure**
- **Action:** Stop demanding a specific commitment (quit date this week). Instead, explore Linda's readiness and collaborate on a timeline that feels achievable to her.
- **Example:** Instead of "Will you set a quit date for this week?", say: "It sounds like you're not quite ready to set a quit date right now, and that's okay. What would need to happen for you to feel ready? What would help you build confidence?"
- **Practice:** Notice when you're using language like "you must," "you have to," "you need to." Replace these with "What do you think?", "What feels right to you?", "What would help?"

---

### 5. **Recognize and Amplify Change Talk**
- **Action:** Listen carefully for Linda's own statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change. Reflect these back and ask follow-up questions to deepen them.
- **Example:** When Linda says "Talking about it like this, with someone who listens, it does help me start thinking that maybe I could try again," respond: "That's really important—talking and feeling heard helps you think about trying again. What is it about having support that makes a difference for you? What kind of support would be most helpful?"
- **Practice:** In each session, identify at least 3 instances of change talk and respond with a reflection and follow-up question designed to deepen it.

---

### 6. **Explore and Normalize Ambivalence Rather Than Attack It**
- **Action:** Recognize that ambivalence is normal and expected in the contemplation stage. Help Linda explore both sides of her conflict without judgment.
- **Example:** "Part of you really wants to quit—you're scared about your health, you want to save money, and you've tried many times. And another part of you is worried about failing again and is connected to smoking as part of your social life and routine. Both of those parts make sense. Let's talk about what would help the part that wants to quit get stronger."
- **Practice:** When you notice sustain talk, pause and ask yourself: "What is Linda protecting? What is she afraid of?" Then address the underlying fear with empathy rather than confrontation.

---

### 7. **Softly Handle Sustain Talk Through Double-Sided Reflections**
- **Action:** When Linda expresses reasons for not changing, acknowledge both her concern and her change talk. Don't attack the sustain talk; gently redirect.
- **Example:** When Linda says "It's part of my life," instead of dismissing this, reflect: "Smoking has been a big part of your life for a long time—it's connected to your friends, your breaks, your routine. And at the same time, you mentioned it scares you about your health and costs you money. So there's this tension between smoking being part of who you are and wanting to protect your health. Tell me more about that."
- **Practice:** Practice double-sided reflections that validate the sustain talk while gently highlighting the change talk.

---

### 8. **Build Self-Efficacy and Confidence**
- **Action:** Help Linda identify her strengths, past successes (even partial ones), and resources. Build confidence that she can change.
- **Example:** "You've tried to quit several times. That shows real commitment and courage. Even though you went back to smoking, you learned something each time. What did you learn? What would be different if you tried again with support?"
- **Practice:** In each session, explicitly affirm Linda's strengths and efforts. Ask about past successes and what helped, even if the quit didn't last.

---

### 9. **Establish a Collaborative Agenda and Invite Linda's Input**
- **Action:** At the beginning of the session, ask Linda what she wants to focus on and what would be most helpful. Let her shape the session direction.
- **Example:** "Linda, I'm glad you're here. What would be most helpful for you to talk about today? What's on your mind about smoking?"
- **Practice:** Begin each session with an open invitation for Linda to set the agenda. Take notes on what she identifies as important and make sure you address it.

---

### 10. **Develop Genuine Partnership Language and Stance**
- **Action:** Use inclusive language that conveys partnership: "Let's explore," "We can figure this out together," "What do you think?", "Help me understand."
- **Example:** Instead of "You must do X," say: "I'm wondering if we could explore together what might help you feel ready to set a quit date. What are your thoughts?"
- **Practice:** Record yourself and listen for the balance between "I" statements (expert positioning) and "we" statements (partnership). Aim for more "we" language.
