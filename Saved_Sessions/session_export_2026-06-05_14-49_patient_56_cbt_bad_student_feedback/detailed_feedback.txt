
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made an initial attempt to establish an agenda in the opening exchange, asking Yvonne "What do you think is the most important thing for us to focus on today?" This represents a collaborative opening that elicits the patient's priorities. Yvonne clearly identified her primary concerns: exhaustion, guilt about the pregnancy, and feeling inadequate as a mother, with the overarching goal of enjoying her children more. This was a reasonable start to agenda-setting.

However, the agenda was never operationalized into concrete, measurable session goals or a structured plan. After Yvonne articulated her concerns, the therapist abandoned systematic agenda management entirely. There was no explicit time allocation, no prioritization of which specific problem to address first, and no revisiting of the agenda throughout the session to maintain focus. The session drifted into general supportive conversation without a clear therapeutic strategy tied to the identified problems. Most critically, there was no agenda for *how* the session would address these issues—no mention of specific CBT interventions, behavioral experiments, thought records, or concrete action steps. The therapist never returned to the agenda or checked whether the session was addressing Yvonne's stated priorities.

The session lacked structure entirely in the latter half. After the initial agenda-setting question, the therapist pivoted to personal anecdotes, generic reassurance, and lifestyle suggestions (water intake, going outside, listening to music) without connecting these to the identified problems or explaining their therapeutic rationale. A proper agenda would have included: (1) identifying the core cognitions maintaining depression (e.g., "I'm a bad mother," "I don't deserve to be happy"), (2) assessing behavioral activation opportunities, (3) exploring the relationship between sleep deprivation and mood, and (4) developing a concrete plan for the week.

**Summary:**
The therapist initiated collaborative agenda-setting but failed to operationalize it into a structured session plan. The agenda was abandoned midway through, resulting in a session that lacked clear direction, measurable goals, or connection between identified problems and interventions.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made virtually no attempt to elicit feedback from Yvonne throughout the session. Feedback-seeking is a cornerstone of CBT—it ensures the patient understands the therapist's conceptualization, agrees with the treatment approach, and finds the session helpful. The CTRS standard for a score of 4 requires the therapist to "ask enough questions to be sure that the patient understood the therapist's line of reasoning throughout the session and to determine the patient's reactions."

In this session, the therapist never asked: "Does this make sense to you?" "How are you feeling about what we're discussing?" "Is this helpful?" "Do you understand why I'm suggesting this?" "What are your thoughts on this approach?" The therapist offered repeated reassurances ("You're doing great," "You've got this," "Keep your chin up") but never checked whether Yvonne found these statements helpful, believed them, or understood their relevance to her depression.

Most problematically, when Yvonne explicitly expressed doubt or resistance—such as "I don't think there are many good moments lately," "it just feels fake, like I'm lying to myself," and "stepping outside sounds nice in theory, but I'm not sure it would really help"—the therapist did not pause to explore her skepticism. These were critical moments to elicit feedback about whether the suggestions were realistic or aligned with her experience. Instead, the therapist continued offering generic advice without checking for understanding or buy-in.

At the end of the session, the therapist did not summarize key points, ask what Yvonne found most helpful, or solicit her feedback on the session's usefulness. The closing ("Remember, you're doing your best... Keep thinking positive... Take care, and I'm rooting for you!") was entirely one-directional, offering no opportunity for the patient to provide input.

**Summary:**
The therapist failed to elicit meaningful feedback at any point in the session. No checks for understanding, no exploration of patient skepticism, and no solicitation of the patient's reaction to suggestions or the session overall.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated minimal understanding of Yvonne's internal experience and the specific context of her depression. While the therapist made surface-level reflections ("Being tired all the time is the worst, right?" and "It sounds really tough"), these were generic statements that could apply to any fatigued person, not specific to Yvonne's postpartum depression with its unique constellation of factors.

Critically, the therapist missed or minimized several key aspects of Yvonne's presentation:

1. **The cognitive distortions driving her depression**: Yvonne repeatedly expressed core beliefs ("I'm not a good mom," "I'm not doing enough," "I don't deserve this," "I'm failing my kids") that are central to her depression. The therapist never explored these thoughts, challenged them, or helped Yvonne examine their validity. These overvalued ideas of punishment and guilt are documented in her psychiatric history and are prime targets for CBT intervention.

2. **The behavioral trap she's in**: Yvonne described a classic depression-maintaining cycle—fatigue leads to withdrawal and inactivity, which worsens mood and guilt, which further reduces motivation. The therapist never identified this pattern or discussed behavioral activation as a strategy.

3. **The specific stressors and their meaning**: Yvonne's guilt about not wanting the pregnancy, her husband's criticism, the baby's rejection of breastfeeding, and her isolation (no family nearby, husband working long hours) are not generic stressors—they are specific triggers for her depressive cognitions. The therapist never explored how these events connect to her current thoughts and feelings.

4. **Her explicit resistance to generic advice**: When Yvonne said "it just feels fake, like I'm lying to myself" about thinking positive, she was communicating that the therapist's suggestions felt inauthentic and disconnected from her reality. The therapist did not acknowledge this valid concern or adjust the approach.

5. **The severity of her symptoms**: Yvonne has 7 of 7 DSM-5 criteria for MDD (depressed mood, anhedonia, insomnia, psychomotor retardation, fatigue, guilt/worthlessness, concentration problems). She is describing suicidal ideation risk factors (hopelessness, feeling like a burden, questioning if she'll "ever feel like myself again"). The therapist's response—"everyone feels that way sometimes"—trivializes the severity of her condition.

The therapist's repeated use of personal anecdotes ("When I was swamped with stuff," "When I was going through a rough patch," "I once had a friend") shifted focus away from Yvonne's experience and onto the therapist's own narrative. This is a fundamental breach of empathic understanding—the session became about the therapist's coping strategies rather than Yvonne's specific depression.

**Summary:**
The therapist demonstrated surface-level empathy but failed to grasp the specific cognitive, behavioral, and contextual factors maintaining Yvonne's depression. Personal anecdotes replaced genuine understanding of her internal reality.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Learn and Apply Core CBT Techniques for Depression**
- **Action**: Develop competence in cognitive restructuring, behavioral activation, and thought records. These are the foundational techniques for treating depression and must be mastered.
- **Example**: In the next session with Yvonne, explicitly identify her core cognition: "I hear you saying that you're not a good mom and that you're not doing enough. - **Practice**: Complete a CBT training program focused on cognitive restructuring (e.g., "Cognitive Therapy for Depression" by Greenberger & Padesky). Practice writing thought records on your own depression or anxiety, then practice with role-play patients. Seek supervision from an experienced CBT therapist who can provide feedback on your technique.

### 2. **Develop a Coherent Conceptualization and Treatment Strategy**
- **Action**: Before each session, develop a clear understanding of how the patient's depression is maintained (cognitions, behaviors, environmental factors) and design interventions specifically targeting these maintaining factors. Write down your conceptualization and strategy.
- **Example**: For Yvonne, the conceptualization might be: "Yvonne's depression is maintained by: (1) core cognitions of guilt and self-blame related to not wanting the pregnancy, (2) the belief that the baby is rejecting her, (3) behavioral withdrawal and inactivity due to fatigue and guilt, (4) isolation and lack of support, and (5) sleep deprivation. Then design each session to address one or more of these elements.
- **Practice**: For each patient, write a one-page conceptualization and treatment plan before the session. Share this with your supervisor and get feedback. Over time, this will become more automatic.

### 3. **Eliminate Personal Anecdotes and Maintain Professional Boundaries**
- **Action**: Stop using personal anecdotes ("When I was swamped," "I once had a friend") in sessions. These shift focus from the patient to the therapist and violate professional boundaries. Instead, maintain focus on the patient's experience.
- **Example**: Instead of "When I was going through a rough patch, I just reminded myself to keep my head up," say: "Many people find it helpful to identify small wins, even if they seem tiny. I'm wondering if that might work for you. What do you think?"

If you find yourself starting to share a personal story, pause and redirect: "I'm noticing I'm thinking about my own experience. Let me refocus on you. What's your experience been?"

### 4. **Systematically Elicit and Respond to Feedback**
