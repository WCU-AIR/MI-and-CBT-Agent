# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates profound lack of unconditional positive regard. Uses judgmental language ("stop making excuses"), communicates disapproval of client's perspective, and pressures Eric despite clear refusals. Frames Eric's concerns as invalid rationalizations rather than legitimate experiences. Tone conveys impatience and frustration rather than genuine warmth. Repeatedly pressures commitments despite Eric's clear statements of unreadiness, violating fundamental MI principle of respecting client's autonomy and current stage of change.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal empathic understanding demonstrated. Fails to reflect Eric's perspective or validate his concerns about quitting difficulty. When Eric explains smoking helps him "wind down after a hard day," psychiatrist pivots immediately to prescriptive advice without exploring stress management. No complex reflections capture Eric's ambivalence or underlying motivations. Repeated restarting of conversation suggests clinician is not tracking Eric's experience or building on previous exchanges. ### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Maintains clear focus on smoking cessation as target behavior, but pursues it through external pressure and prescription rather than collaborative exploration. Direction is imposed rather than evoked. Repeatedly states directives forcefully ("You need to cut down immediately," "You must set a firm quit date now") without exploring Eric's own reasons for potentially changing. One brief evocative question asked ("What makes quitting difficult?") but not followed up with deeper exploration. No strategic adaptation when current approach fails to engage client. Repeated restarting suggests no coherent strategic direction.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration essentially absent. Psychiatrist consistently positions himself as expert with superior knowledge and Eric as someone needing correction and control. Hierarchical stance evident throughout: judges Eric's reasoning as invalid, determines what is serious, overrides Eric's stated preferences. When Eric explicitly states "it feels like you're pushing me," psychiatrist continues same directive approach rather than genuinely exploring feedback. Brief use of collaborative language ("work together on this") immediately contradicted by clinician-driven questions. Eric's input does not substantially influence session direction.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support fundamentally violated throughout session. Repeatedly pressures Eric into commitments using language of necessity ("need," "have to," "must"). Phrases like "You must set a firm quit date now" and "Don't wait around" communicate that Eric does not have genuine choice. When Eric explicitly states "I'm not ready to make that kind of commitment," psychiatrist does not respect boundary but continues pressure. Offers some choice-supportive language ("Would you be willing to try?") but immediately undermines by stating "Cutting down isn't enough—you have to quit completely." Never affirms Eric's right to make own decisions about health.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation almost entirely absent. Psychiatrist repeatedly tells Eric why he should quit rather than evoking Eric's internal motivations. Provides external reasons ("bad for lungs and heart," "serious health problems") rather than asking questions that help Eric articulate own reasons. One evocative question asked ("What makes quitting difficult?") but not followed with deeper exploration. Fails to explore Eric's values, goals, or reasons for potentially changing. Does not amplify change talk offered by Eric; instead undermines it ("Cutting down isn't enough" when Eric offers to reduce cigarettes). Intrinsic motivation never explored.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist assumes expert role and treats Eric as someone needing correction rather than as partner with valuable knowledge about own life. Language throughout reflects expert stance: judges reasoning as "excuses," determines what must happen, criticizes choices implicitly. True partnership would involve statements like "You know your life better than I do—what do you think would work?" Instead, psychiatrist tells Eric what should happen. Eric's input does not shape session. Repeated restarting demonstrates lack of genuine power-sharing and co-creation. No acknowledgment that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within client.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Cultivating change talk almost entirely absent. Psychiatrist provides external reasons for change rather than evoking and reinforcing Eric's own change talk. Eric offers tentative commitments ("maybe I could try smoking a couple less a day") but psychiatrist undermines rather than reinforces them ("Cutting down isn't enough"). When Eric mentions doctor visit about quitting, psychiatrist does not explore this potential change talk. No change talk related to desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment is cultivated. Minimal change talk appears in session itself—sign that psychiatrist's approach is not working. Psychiatrist fails to reinforce or amplify any change talk offered.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk addressed through direct confrontation rather than strategic MI techniques. Psychiatrist responds to Eric's sustain talk ("I feel fine," "smoking helps me relax," "I've tried cutting down before") with confrontational statements ("stop making excuses," "way too much," "no point in waiting"). This approach likely entrenches defensiveness rather than softens it. No double-sided reflections, reframing, or strategic amplification used. One moment of brief softening ("sounds like smoking is big part of how you manage stress") immediately followed by return to confrontation. Eric's responses become increasingly defensive, indicating confrontational approach is counterproductive.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.2/5**

**Strengths:**
- Maintains consistent focus on smoking cessation as treatment goal
- Asks one evocative question ("What makes quitting difficult?")
- Demonstrates occasional capacity for empathic listening through brief reflections
- Persists in engagement with topic throughout session

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
This session represents fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles. Psychiatrist demonstrates confrontational, expert-driven approach contraindicated for clients in pre-contemplation/early contemplation stages. Violates nearly every core MI principle: acceptance, empathy, collaboration, autonomy support, evocation, partnership, cultivation of change talk, and skillful handling of sustain talk.

**Impact on Client Engagement:**
Session is likely to **decrease** Eric's motivation for change and increase defensiveness. Confrontational approach violates fundamental MI principle that motivation resides within client and must be evoked rather than imposed. Evidence of negative impact: Eric becomes increasingly defensive, explicitly states he feels pushed, repeatedly reaffirms sustain talk. Psychiatrist's approach has moved Eric further from change, not closer.

**Current MI Competency Level:** Minimal MI competence demonstrated. While brief moments suggest awareness that exploration could be valuable, these are overwhelmed by confrontational, expert-driven behavior contradicting MI principles. Psychiatrist appears operating from traditional medical model (expert prescribes, patient complies) rather than MI model (collaborative exploration of ambivalence).

**Most Critical Recommendations:**
1. Shift from expert to collaborative stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
2. Develop genuine empathic reflections capturing ambivalence
3. Evoke and amplify Eric's own change talk rather than providing external reasons
4. Respect and affirm Eric's autonomy, even when disagreeing
5. Eliminate confrontational language ("need," "have to," "must," "should")
6. Use double-sided reflections to soften sustain talk
7. Build therapeutic alliance before pushing for change
8. Adapt approach when current strategy is not working
9. Understand stage of change and adjust accordingly
10. Recognize that at pre-contemplation stage, goal is exploration not prescription

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation for Change:** Very low. Current approach likely to entrench resistance and damage therapeutic alliance. Fundamental shift in approach required—from telling Eric why he should change to exploring with Eric his own thoughts, values, and readiness for change.
