# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Minimal unconditional positive regard demonstrated throughout most of the session. Psychiatrist uses directive language ("you really need to be strict," "you must quit") that conveys judgment rather than acceptance. Dismisses client's cancer fears with "stop worrying about cancer right now," invalidating legitimate emotional experiences. Only one brief moment of genuine acceptance appears ("That's completely understandable"), immediately followed by reversion to directive stance. Client's worth and emotional validity are not affirmed; fears are treated as obstacles rather than understandable human responses.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Minimal empathic reflection throughout session. One genuine complex reflection appears midway ("It sounds like you're feeling scared and uncertain...because you worry about potential harm or missing a serious diagnosis"). However, critical opportunities to explore client's internal experience are missed entirely. Psychiatrist fails to reflect or explore client's emerging insights about stress-symptom connections ("When my son got married, I was too busy to notice the pain"; "It gets worse when my husband is away"). These represent significant change talk that goes unexamined. Consistent dismissal of emotions and failure to understand client's frame of reference indicate surface-level empathy at best.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains consistent, unwavering focus on target behavior (medication adherence and lifestyle modification, specifically laxative use and dietary fiber). However, direction is maintained through directive prescription rather than collaborative exploration. Target behavior is imposed rather than co-constructed with client. Critically misses opportunity to redirect focus toward client's own emerging insights about stress-symptom fluctuation, which could naturally lead to discussion of stress management and mind-body connection. Strategic direction is undermined by failure to follow client's natural movement toward understanding.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Authoritarian rather than collaborative stance throughout most of session. Language patterns establish hierarchical relationship: "you really need," "you must," "you have to," "this is not optional." Questions are demands disguised as questions ("So, are you going to start the laxatives today, or not?"). Client's input does not substantially influence session direction; psychiatrist's predetermined solution (laxatives) is repeated regardless of client's expressed concerns. One moment of genuine collaboration appears ("Together, we can figure out a plan that feels manageable and safe for you"), immediately abandoned in final exchange. Client's repeated invitations to collaborate ("I need support," "Can we talk about how I can feel sure?") are largely ignored.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively undermines autonomy throughout session. Explicit statements remove perceived choice: "This is not optional if you want to feel better," "you have to start the laxatives now," "you must start something immediately." Fear-based pressure used to coerce compliance: "if you don't act now, your condition could get worse," "if you don't take this seriously, you risk complications." When client expresses hesitation ("I'm not yet ready"), psychiatrist intensifies pressure rather than respecting autonomy. No exploration of client's values, goals, or what would help her feel in control. Client is positioned as non-compliant patient requiring control, not as autonomous agent with right to make own decisions. Only one moment of autonomy support appears before being negated.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Minimal evocation; psychiatrist relies heavily on telling and prescribing rather than drawing out client's own motivations and resources. Some open questions asked early ("are you currently following any treatment"), but immediately followed by directive statements rather than genuine exploration. When client reveals important information (tried laxatives, felt worse; scared about cancer; doesn't understand why laxatives would help), psychiatrist dismisses rather than explores ("you probably just didn't try the right ones"). Most significantly, psychiatrist completely fails to evoke client's emerging change talk about stress-symptom connections—powerful statements like "When my son got married, I was too busy to notice the pain" and "It gets worse when my husband is away" are ignored entirely. One moment of genuine evocation appears ("Why don't we explore what worries you most about starting laxatives?") but is not sustained.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist positions himself as expert who knows what is best; client positioned as non-compliant patient requiring direction. Language patterns make this explicit: "I get that you're scared, but you have to stop letting that fear control you" (psychiatrist knows better than client what to do with emotions); "you probably just didn't try the right ones" (psychiatrist knows better why previous attempts failed); "you need to trust the process and commit" (psychiatrist's process, not client's). Client's knowledge about her own body, fears, and capacity for change is never acknowledged or valued. Client's repeated expressions of concerns and conditions for change do not substantially influence session direction. Psychiatrist's agenda takes precedence over client's input. One moment of genuine partnership language ("Together, we can figure out a plan") immediately contradicted in final exchange ("you have to start the laxatives now").

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no skill in cultivating change talk. Client spontaneously produces important change talk throughout session: "I want to feel better, believe me"; "I'm willing to think about increasing fiber slowly"; "Maybe if I had someone to explain...I'd feel more at ease"; "I want to get better." These statements are either ignored or immediately followed by pressure and directive advice that undermines them. When client says "I'm willing to think about increasing fiber slowly," psychiatrist responds with threat: "if you don't take this seriously, you risk complications." Most critically, psychiatrist completely misses client's most powerful change talk—emerging recognition of stress-symptom connections. Statements like "It does get worse when my husband is away" and "When my son got married, I was too busy to notice the pain" represent foundation for exploring stress management and mind-body connection, but are ignored entirely. No amplification of change talk occurs.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist uses confrontational rather than skillful approaches to sustain talk, which likely strengthens rather than softens client resistance. When client expresses fear about cancer, psychiatrist responds with direct confrontation: "Look, you need to stop worrying about cancer right now." Client's response is to defend fear more strongly, demonstrating that confrontation backfired. When client says "I don't think it's just in my head," psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflection or reframe; instead prescribes more directives. One attempted reframe ("If laxatives didn't work, you probably just didn't try the right ones") is actually blame-focused and counterproductive, communicating that client's previous failure was her fault. No skillful softening techniques employed. Sustain talk is met with pressure rather than understanding and gentle alternative perspectives.

### Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.6/5 – POOR MI PRACTICE**

#### **Strengths:**
- Maintains consistent focus on specific target behavior (medication adherence and lifestyle modification)
- Asks some open-ended questions early in session
- Demonstrates one genuine empathic reflection ("It sounds like you're feeling scared and uncertain...")
- Briefly acknowledges need to shift approach ("I realize I've been pushing you without fully understanding your concerns")
- Shows awareness that client has legitimate concerns worth exploring

#### **Critical Areas for Improvement:**

**1. Pervasive Directive Stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
Controlling language ("you must," "you have to," "this is not optional") undermines all MI principles throughout most of session. Psychiatrist positions self as expert who knows what is best rather than collaborative partner.

**2. Dismissal of Client Emotions**
Repeatedly tells client to "stop worrying," "stop overthinking," "stop letting fear control you" rather than accepting and exploring emotional experience. Communicates that client's fears are problematic obstacles rather than valid human responses.

**3. Failure to Evoke Change Talk**
Misses critical opportunities to amplify client's spontaneous insights about stress-symptom connections. Client's emerging recognition that symptoms improve during busy periods and worsen during stress is completely ignored—exactly the kind of change talk MI is designed to evoke and amplify.

**4. Minimal Empathic Reflection**
Only one genuine complex reflection appears; most responses are prescriptive rather than exploratory. Client's internal frame of reference is not genuinely understood or validated.

**5. Lack of Genuine Collaboration**
Client's repeated invitations to collaborate ("I need support," "Can we talk about how I can feel sure?") are largely ignored. Client's input does not substantially influence session direction.

**6. Undermining Autonomy**
Uses fear-based pressure and removes perceived choice rather than supporting client's right to make own decisions. Client is positioned as non-compliant patient requiring control.

**7. Confrontational Approach to Sustain Talk**
Directly challenges client's fears rather than using skillful techniques to soften resistance. This approach typically strengthens rather than weakens resistance.

**8. Inconsistency**
One moment of genuine MI practice (penultimate exchange) is immediately abandoned in final exchange, suggesting shift was tactical rather than genuine.

#### **Impact on Client Engagement:**
This session is likely to **decrease** rather than increase client's intrinsic motivation for change. Despite client's demonstrated willingness to consider change ("I'm willing to think about increasing fiber," "Maybe if I had someone to explain...I'd feel more at ease"), psychiatrist's controlling, dismissive approach is likely to entrench resistance. Client's repeated statements represent invitations to collaborate that are ignored, communicating that her concerns do not matter and autonomy is not respected. Research demonstrates this approach increases defensiveness and decreases intrinsic motivation.

#### **Key Recommendation:**
Psychiatrist must fundamentally shift from directive, expert-driven stance to evocative, collaborative one. This requires developing genuine curiosity about client's perspective, learning to evoke and amplify her own change talk (particularly emerging insights about stress-symptom connections), and respecting her autonomy. The penultimate exchange provides a model; psychiatrist must extend this approach throughout entire session and maintain it consistently, not abandon it when client does not immediately comply.
