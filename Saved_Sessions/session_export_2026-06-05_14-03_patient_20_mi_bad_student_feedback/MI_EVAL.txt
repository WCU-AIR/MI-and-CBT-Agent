## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.4/5 – Poor Motivational Interviewing Practice**

### Critical Findings

This session demonstrates **pervasive deficiencies across nearly all MI competencies**. The psychiatrist operates from a directive, expert-driven model fundamentally inconsistent with MI spirit and principles. The clinician tells the patient what she "must" do rather than evoking her own motivation for change.

### Key Concerns

- **Judgmental stance:** Disapproving language ("You must stop making excuses," "You can't keep justifying") communicates judgment rather than acceptance
- **Removal of autonomy:** Controlling language and dismissal of patient's preferences undermine self-determination
- **Failure to evoke:** Patient's powerful intrinsic motivations (travel, motherhood) are never referenced or amplified
- **Dismissal of change talk:** Patient's spontaneous change talk and self-generated strategies are not reinforced
- **Confrontational approach:** Sustain talk is met with pressure rather than skillful softening techniques

### Impact on Patient Engagement

This approach is likely to **reduce rather than enhance** intrinsic motivation for change. While patient begins with openness and articulates own goals, the psychiatrist's directive, judgmental approach triggers defensiveness and resistance. Patient may comply temporarily but sustained change is unlikely without intrinsic motivation.

### Primary Recommendation

**Fundamental shift required:** Psychiatrist must transition from directive, expert-driven approach to collaborative, evocative, client-centered approach. This requires adopting MI spirit (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation) and using MI-specific techniques (open-ended questions, affirmations, reflective listening, summarizing) to help patient discover and strengthen her own motivation for change.
