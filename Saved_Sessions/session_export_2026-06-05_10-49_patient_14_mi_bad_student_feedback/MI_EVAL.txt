# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Minimal acceptance demonstrated throughout majority of session. Psychiatrist uses prescriptive framing ("I'm going to give you some clear advice") and dismissive language ("you can't just keep using stress as an excuse") that communicate disapproval rather than unconditional positive regard. Patient's emotional experience is minimized and compared unfavorably to others. Only in final exchange does psychiatrist shift to acceptance ("It's completely understandable to feel that way"), but this single moment arrives too late to repair sustained judgmental stance and does not compensate for earlier lack of affirmation of patient's worth.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Empathy largely absent until final exchange. Early responses dismiss rather than explore patient's emotional content. When patient expresses fear ("I'm scared it'll make things worse"), psychiatrist responds with pressure rather than reflection. Statement "Everyone has stress, but not everyone smokes" demonstrates failure to understand patient's perspective and uses comparison to shame. Final reflection ("I hear that you're feeling stuck and overwhelmed") is basic and surface-level, capturing emotion but lacking depth. Does not explore interconnection between health anxiety, weight concerns, and smoking as coping mechanism. Empathic attunement arrives too late and remains shallow.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Direction is present but fundamentally anti-MI—imposed rather than collaborative. Psychiatrist unilaterally sets agenda and frames session as one-directional instruction from opening statement. Repeated attempts to force immediate commitment ("Are you ready to stop smoking today?") are ultimatums, not strategic explorations of readiness. No flexibility demonstrated; when patient expresses overwhelm and fear, clinician doubles down on pressure rather than adjusting course. Direction is entirely clinician-imposed and rigid. Only final exchange shows collaborative exploration of readiness, but this single moment cannot compensate for sustained coercive directiveness.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Collaboration severely deficient throughout majority of session. Psychiatrist operates from expert-authority stance ("I'm going to give you some clear advice") that immediately establishes hierarchy and negates partnership. Responses consistently shut down collaborative exploration; patient's concerns treated as obstacles rather than legitimate information. Closed questions designed to extract information for clinician's predetermined plan rather than invite patient's input into problem-solving. Patient explicitly signals desire for collaboration ("Maybe if I had more support..."), but psychiatrist dismisses until final exchange. Only final question ("What do you think could help you feel more supported?") engages collaboration, far too late to establish true partnership.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Autonomy fundamentally violated throughout session. Opening statement removes choice ("I'm going to give you some clear advice on what you need to do"). Repeated controlling language dominates: "you really should," "you need to," "you have to," "you can't," "you must." Pressure tactics include ultimatums ("will you commit to quitting today, or not?") designed to force compliance. Patient's hesitation and fear treated as defiance to overcome rather than legitimate autonomy to respect. Patient explicitly asserts autonomy ("I don't want to say yes just because you're asking"), which psychiatrist ignores until final exchange. No affirmation of client ownership occurs until end of session after sustained autonomy violation.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Evocation almost entirely absent. Psychiatrist provides reasons for change ("smoking causes serious health problems," "your health will get worse") without exploring patient's own motivations. Early patient change talk ("I know it's bad for me," "I want to quit") dismissed rather than amplified. No open-ended questions asked to elicit intrinsic motivation: "What do you think about your smoking?" "What concerns you most?" "What would be good about quitting?" "What do you value?" Patient's change talk suppressed by pressure and dismissal. Only final question ("What do you think could help you feel more supported?") demonstrates genuine evocation; patient responds with authentic reflection about support needs. Single evocative question cannot compensate for sustained absence of eliciting intrinsic motivation.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Partnership fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist explicitly assumes expert role ("I'm going to give you some clear advice") positioning clinician as sole source of knowledge and patient as passive recipient. Patient's perspective treated as irrelevant or incorrect; dismissal of fear and overwhelm ("You can't just keep using stress as an excuse") communicates patient's self-understanding is wrong. Patient demonstrates sophisticated self-awareness ("I'm scared it'll make things worse," "I need to feel like I can handle it"), but clinician never acknowledges patient knows their own barriers and readiness better than anyone. Only final exchange demonstrates shift toward partnership with collaborative language ("Let's explore") and genuine inquiry. Single moment does not establish true power-sharing after sustained expert-driven positioning throughout session.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Change talk cultivation nearly absent and actively suppressed. Patient offers early change talk ("I know it's bad for me," "I want to quit, but I'm just not sure I can") that represents opportunities to amplify intrinsic motivation. Instead, psychiatrist responds with pressure and external reasons for change, increasing defensiveness. When patient expresses ambivalence, psychiatrist responds with dismissal, pushing toward sustain talk. Patient's subsequent responses show diminished change talk and increased focus on barriers. No strategic questions asked to evoke change talk: "What would be different if you weren't smoking?" "What's your most important reason for quitting?" "How confident are you?" Only final question elicits genuine change talk about conditions for change ("checking in without judgment," "taking things slowly," "not being alone"). Single moment of cultivation cannot compensate for sustained suppression throughout session.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Sustain talk handled poorly through confrontation rather than skillful softening. Patient expresses legitimate concerns about costs of change (fear of failure, overwhelm, loss of coping mechanism) that should be softened through double-sided reflections and reframing. Instead, psychiatrist uses explicit confrontation: "You can't just keep using stress as an excuse. Everyone has stress, but not everyone smokes" and "There's no time to be scared or uncertain." These responses harden rather than soften resistance; patient becomes more entrenched, expressing increased focus on barriers and decreased change talk. Confrontation is explicitly contraindicated in MI. Only final exchange demonstrates appropriate approach with double-sided reflection ("It's completely understandable to feel that way") and gentle redirection toward exploring solutions. Single moment cannot repair damage of sustained confrontation; pattern has likely strengthened rather than softened patient's resistance.

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall Score: 1.4/5 – Poor Motivational Interviewing Practice**

### Critical Deficiencies
- **Expert-driven stance throughout majority of session** violates fundamental MI spirit of partnership and evocation
- **Sustained use of pressure and confrontation** (controlling language, ultimatums, dismissal) explicitly contraindicated in MI and actively suppresses intrinsic motivation
- **Suppression rather than amplification of change talk** when patient expresses recognition of problem and desire to quit
- **Confrontation of sustain talk** hardens resistance rather than softening it through skillful exploration
- **Minimal empathic understanding** until final exchange; early responses minimize and dismiss patient's emotional experience
- **Autonomy violation** through pressure for immediate commitment without respecting patient's stated need for time, support, and gradual change

### Single Strength
- **Late-session shift toward MI:** Final exchange demonstrates significant shift toward MI-consistent practice with acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation. Questions like "What do you think could help you feel more supported?" show psychiatrist is capable of MI-consistent behavior but has not yet integrated it as consistent clinical approach.

### Impact on Patient Engagement and Change
**Session demonstrates how non-MI practice actively undermines motivation for change.** Patient enters with normal ambivalence (contemplation stage) and leaves more entrenched in status quo with diminished change talk and increased defensiveness. Patient's statement "Maybe someday I'll be ready, but today? I just don't think I am" is direct result of confrontational approach. When psychiatrist shifts to genuine curiosity in final exchange, patient immediately becomes more reflective and expresses openness to exploring conditions for change, demonstrating resistance was evoked by clinician's approach, not inherent to patient.

**Likelihood of change:** Low. Patient unlikely to quit smoking as result of this session. Sustained pressure and dismissal have likely damaged therapeutic alliance and reinforced defensiveness.

**Prognosis for improvement:** Moderate to good. Psychiatrist demonstrates capacity for MI-consistent practice in final exchange and appears willing to shift approach. With focused training on eliminating confrontation, developing genuine curiosity, and learning to evoke rather than impose motivation, this clinician could develop solid MI competence.
