
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish Genuine Collaboration and Partnership (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Begin sessions by explicitly positioning the patient as the expert on his own life and explicitly inviting his input into the change process. Use language that conveys partnership and shared decision-making.

**Example:** Instead of *"I have some ideas that I think you really should try to improve things quickly. Let's get right to it—are you ready to hear what you need to do to feel better?"* try: *"I'd like to work together to explore your health and see what changes might feel important to you. You know your life and what's realistic for you better than anyone. What's on your mind about your health right now?"*

**Practice:** Before each session, write down three collaborative opening statements that invite the patient's perspective. Practice using these until they feel natural. Record yourself and listen for language that positions you as an expert versus language that positions the patient as the expert on his own life.

---

### 2. **Eliminate Controlling Language and Replace with Autonomy-Supportive Language**

**Action:** Systematically replace "you have to," "you need to," "you should," and "you must" with language that respects choice: "you might consider," "some people find it helpful to," "what do you think about," "would it be okay if we explored."

**Example:** Instead of *"You have to commit fully if you want to see any results,"* try: *"People often find that having a clear commitment helps. What do you think about that? Is that something that feels important to you?"*

**Practice:** Review recordings of your sessions and identify every instance of controlling language. For each one, write an autonomy-supportive alternative. Practice these alternatives until they become automatic. Ask a colleague to listen for controlling language and give you feedback.

---

### 3. **Develop Genuine Empathic Listening and Reflective Responses**

**Action:** When a patient shares barriers, concerns, or struggles, respond with reflective listening that demonstrates understanding before offering any suggestions. Use complex reflections that capture both content and emotion.

**Example:** When the patient says *"Between work stress and family stuff, it's tough to stay on track,"* instead of *"Why haven't you been able to stick to a routine?"* try: *"So you're juggling a lot—work stress, family responsibilities—and by the time you get to thinking about exercise and diet, you're already depleted. That makes sense. Tell me more about what a typical day looks like for you."*

**Practice:** In each session, aim for at least three complex reflections that capture both the patient's situation and emotion. Record yourself and listen for whether your reflections demonstrate genuine understanding or whether you're still focused on judgment or prescription. Practice reflective listening in role-plays with colleagues.

---

### 4. **Evoke Rather Than Impose Motivation for Change**

**Action:** Ask open-ended questions designed to draw out the patient's own reasons for change, values, and resources. Focus on what the patient wants, not what you think he should want.

**Example:** Instead of *"Not exercising regularly and still eating junk food will lead to serious health problems down the line,"* try: *"You mentioned quitting smoking, which is a big accomplishment. What made you decide to quit? What's important to you about your health? How do you see these changes fitting into what matters most to you?"*

**Practice:** Develop a set of evocative questions and practice using them:
- "What would be the benefits of making this change?"
- "How important is this to you on a scale of 1-10? What would move it up?"
- "What have you been able to accomplish in the past when you've set your mind to something?"
- "What would need to happen for you to feel confident about making a change?"
- "What do you think might work for you?"

Use these questions in every session and track how the patient responds.

---

### 5. **Amplify and Reinforce Change Talk**

**Action:** When the patient expresses desire, ability, reasons, or commitment to change, pause and explore this further. Ask follow-up questions that deepen and strengthen the change talk.

**Example:** When the patient says *"I did quit smoking not too long ago, which I'm pretty proud of,"* instead of *"Quitting smoking is great, but that's just the start,"* try: *"You're really proud of that—and rightfully so. That's a significant change. What helped you make that happen? What strengths did you draw on? How might those same strengths help you with other changes you want to make?"*

**Practice:** In each session, identify at least three instances of change talk from the patient. For each one, write down a follow-up question that would amplify or deepen it. Practice asking these questions naturally in sessions. Track how the patient's change talk evolves when you amplify it versus when you ignore it or contradict it.

---

### 6. **Affirm and Build on Patient Strengths and Past Successes**

**Action:** Explicitly recognize and affirm the patient's accomplishments, strengths, and realistic self-awareness. Use these as a foundation for exploring other changes.

**Example:** *"You quit smoking, which shows real commitment and ability to make changes that matter to you. You're also being realistic about what you can handle right now—you know that jumping into a strict routine might backfire. That's important self-knowledge. So let's build on what's worked for you. What do you think a realistic first step might be?"*

**Practice:** At the beginning of each session, identify at least one strength or accomplishment the patient has demonstrated. Explicitly affirm this. Ask the patient how he drew on this strength and how it might apply to current challenges.

---

### 7. **Replace Pressure and Demands with Collaborative Exploration**

**Action:** Stop asking yes/no questions that demand commitment. Instead, ask open-ended questions that invite exploration and collaborative problem-solving.

**Example:** Instead of *"Are you going to follow a strict schedule and plan your meals starting this week? Yes or no?"* try: *"What do you think might be a manageable first step for you this week? What would feel doable without being overwhelming?"*

**Practice:** Review your session recordings and identify every yes/no question you asked. For each one, write an open-ended alternative. Practice asking open-ended questions until they become your default. Ask a colleague to count how many yes/no questions you ask in a session and give you feedback.

---

### 8. **Develop Skill in Handling Ambivalence and Sustain Talk**

**Action:** When the patient expresses concern about feasibility or barriers, use double-sided reflections and reframing rather than pressure or blame. Acknowledge both sides of the ambivalence.

**Example:** When the patient says *"I'm worried I won't stick to it,"* instead of *"You need to commit fully,"* try: *"Part of you wants to make changes and knows it would be good for you. And another part is concerned about whether you can realistically stick with it given everything else going on. Both of those make sense. What do you think would help you feel more confident about being able to follow through?"*

**Practice:** In role-plays with colleagues, practice responding to sustain talk with double-sided reflections and reframing. Record yourself and listen for whether you're pressuring, blaming, or genuinely exploring the ambivalence. Practice until you can naturally acknowledge both sides of the patient's ambivalence.

---

### 9. **Establish Clear Behavioral Targets Collaboratively, Not Unilaterally**

**Action:** Work with the patient to identify specific, measurable goals, but do this collaboratively. Ask the patient what he thinks is realistic and important, rather than imposing targets.

**Example:** Instead of *"Are you exercising at least three times a week now? And are you cutting out fast food completely?"* try: *"What would feel like a meaningful change for you? How often do you think you could realistically exercise? What would be a realistic approach to eating better that fits your life?"*

**Practice:** In sessions, practice setting goals collaboratively. Ask the patient what he thinks is realistic. Negotiate targets together rather than imposing them. Track whether collaboratively-set goals are more likely to be achieved than psychiatrist-imposed goals.

---

### 10. **Receive Feedback and Engage in Deliberate Practice**

**Action:** Record your sessions (with patient consent) and review them with a supervisor or colleague trained in MI. Identify specific moments where you deviated from MI principles and practice alternative responses.

**Example:** Review this session and identify the moments where you used controlling language, imposed solutions, or failed to evoke change talk. For each moment, write an MI-consistent alternative and practice it until it feels natural.

**Practice:** Commit to recording and reviewing at least one session per week. Use the MI Fidelity Scale or similar tool to assess your adherence to MI principles. Set specific, measurable goals for improvement (e.g., "Reduce controlling language by 50% in the next month," "Increase evocative questions by 3 per session").
