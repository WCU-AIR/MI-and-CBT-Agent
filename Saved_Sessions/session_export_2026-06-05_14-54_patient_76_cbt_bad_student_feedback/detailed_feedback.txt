
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
### **Score: 3/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrates inconsistent interpersonal effectiveness. For example, when the patient says her mind "automatically jumps to the worst thoughts," the therapist doesn't ask what those thoughts are, when they occur, or how they affect her behavior—all essential for CBT.

The therapist maintains professional boundaries and is not inappropriate, but the interpersonal stance is more "cheerleader" than "collaborative scientist," which is misaligned with effective CBT.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish and Maintain a Structured Agenda**
- **Action**: At the beginning of each session, collaboratively establish 2-3 specific, measurable goals for that session. Write them down. Allocate time to each. Reference the agenda throughout the session ("We said we'd focus on X; let's make sure we cover that"). At the end, review whether you accomplished the agenda.
- **Example**: "Diane, I want to make sure we use our time well. I'm hearing that you feel stuck in low mood and that you're worried that trying new things will just prove you're not good enough. Those sound like two important things to work on. Which one would be most helpful to focus on today? And how much time do you think we need?"
- **Practice**: Record yourself in practice sessions and count how many times you reference the agenda. Aim for at least 3-4 references per session.

### 2. **Elicit and Respond to Feedback Throughout the Session**
- **Action**: After each major intervention or explanation, ask: "Does that make sense?" "How are you feeling about this?" "Do you agree with this approach?" Listen to the answer and adjust if needed. At the end of the session, ask: "What was most helpful today?" "Is there anything that didn't make sense?" "What will you take away from our session?"
- **Example**: After suggesting behavioral activation, say: "I'm suggesting we work on gradually increasing your activities because research shows that when depression keeps us inactive, it actually makes the depression worse. Does that make sense to you? And do you think that might be part of what's happening for you?"
- **Practice**: Set a goal to elicit feedback at least 5 times per session. Use a checklist to track this.

### 3. **Develop a Coherent Case Formulation and Treatment Strategy**
- **Action**: Before or early in treatment, explicitly develop a case formulation that connects the patient's presenting problems to maintaining factors. Share this with the patient. Use it to guide all interventions. For Diane, the formulation might be: "Your depression is maintained by a cycle: you have thoughts like 'I'm not good enough,' which leads you to avoid social situations and new activities, which then leads to isolation and inactivity, which makes your mood worse and confirms the thought 'I'm stuck.' We're going to work on breaking this cycle by: (1) identifying and testing these thoughts, (2) gradually increasing your activities, and (3) practicing social situations."
- **Example**: "Diane, based on what you've told me, here's how I understand your depression. You have thoughts that you're not good enough, so you avoid situations where you might be judged. But avoiding actually makes you feel more isolated and confirms the thought. Does that fit with your experience? If so, here's our plan: we're going to work on these thoughts and gradually get you back into activities and social situations."
- **Practice**: Write out a one-page case formulation for each patient. Review it at the beginning of each session. Use it to explain why you're recommending each intervention.

### 4. **Identify and Systematically Address Core Automatic Thoughts and Beliefs**
- **Action**: Use Socratic questioning to help the patient identify her automatic thoughts. Write them down. Then systematically examine them: What evidence supports this thought? What evidence contradicts it? Is there an alternative explanation? What would you tell a friend who had this thought? This is cognitive restructuring.
- **Example**: "You mentioned that during the performance review, you thought 'I'm not good enough.' Let's look at that thought. What evidence do you have that you're not good enough? [Listen.] Okay, and what evidence do you have that you ARE good enough? [Listen.] So it sounds like you're good enough at some things and not at others—like most people. Does that fit?"
- **Practice**: In each session, identify at least one automatic thought and work through it using Socratic questioning. Use a thought record template to structure this work.

### 5. **Implement Structured Behavioral Activation**
- **Action**: Don't just suggest "staying busy." Instead: (1) Assess the patient's current activity level and mood patterns. (2) Collaboratively identify activities that are meaningful and achievable. (3) Schedule specific activities for specific times. (4) Have the patient track mood before and after activities. (5) Review and adjust based on results.
- **Example**: "Let's look at your week. You mentioned jogging helps a bit. How often are you jogging now? [Listen.] Okay, and what other activities used to bring you pleasure or a sense of accomplishment? [Listen.] Let's pick one or two and schedule them for specific times this week. We'll track your mood before and after to see if there's a connection."
- **Practice**: Create an activity schedule with each patient. Have them track mood on a 0-10 scale before and after activities. Review the data at the next session to identify patterns.

### 6. **Stop Offering Repeated Ineffective Advice**
- **Action**: When a patient explicitly states that a strategy hasn't worked ("Even when I do all that, the sad or empty feeling doesn't really go away"), recognize this as important feedback. Don't repeat the same advice. Instead, say: "Okay, so positive thinking and staying busy haven't been enough. That tells me we need a different approach. Let's think about what might be getting in the way. What do you think?"
- **Example**: Instead of repeating "try to think positive," say: "I hear you that positive thinking hasn't been enough. That makes sense because your depression is chronic and deep. We need to work on this more systematically. Let's identify the specific thoughts that are keeping you stuck and really examine them together."
- **Practice**: After each session, review your notes. If you offered the same suggestion more than once, flag it. In supervision, discuss why you repeated it and what you could have done differently.

### 7. **Use Socratic Questioning Instead of Telling**
- **Action**: Replace statements like "You're stronger than you think" and "You've got this" with genuine questions that help the patient discover insights. Ask about evidence, alternatives, and implications.
- **Example**: Instead of "You're stronger than you think," ask: "You've managed to keep going to work and jogging even though you're feeling this low. What does that tell you about your strength?"
- **Practice**: Record yourself in practice sessions. Count how many times you make statements vs. ask questions. Aim for at least 70% questions. Review recordings with a supervisor.

### 8. **Position the Patient as an Active Collaborator**
- **Action**: Explicitly invite the patient's input on treatment decisions. Use language like "What do you think?", "Do you agree?", "What barriers do you anticipate?", "What have you already tried?" Position yourself as a consultant, not an expert telling the patient what to do.
- **Example**: "I'm thinking we should work on these thoughts and gradually increase your activities. But you know your situation best. What do you think? Does that sound like something you'd be willing to try? What concerns do you have?"
- **Practice**: In each session, make at least 3 explicit invitations for the patient to contribute to treatment planning.

### 9. **Assign Specific, Rationale-Based Homework**
- **Action**: At the end of each session, assign 1-2 specific between-session tasks. Explain the rationale. Ensure the patient understands and agrees. Plan to review at the next session.
- **Example**: "This week, I'd like you to keep a thought record. Here's how it works: [explain]. The reason is that we need to identify the specific thoughts that are keeping you stuck so we can work on them together. Can you commit to doing this 3-4 times this week? What day and time will you do it?"
- **Practice**: Assign homework in every session. Use a homework form. Review homework at the beginning of the next session.

### 10. **Reduce Personal Anecdotes; Increase Focus on the Patient**
- **Action**: Limit sharing personal experiences. When you're tempted to say "I felt that way too," instead ask: "Tell me more about what that's like for you." Keep the focus on the patient's experience, not yours.
- **Example**: Instead of "When I felt down, I reminded myself that tomorrow is a new day," say: "You mentioned that you've been feeling this way for years. What do you think has kept this going for so long?"
- **Practice**: In each session, limit personal anecdotes to no more than one brief example. Use a checklist to track this.

### 11. **Develop Competence in Core CBT Techniques**
- **Action**: Pursue training in specific CBT techniques: cognitive restructuring, behavioral activation, thought records, behavioral experiments, exposure, problem-solving. Practice these techniques in role-plays and with actual patients under supervision.
- **Example**: Take a workshop on cognitive restructuring. Practice the technique with a colleague. Record yourself using it with a patient. Review the recording with a supervisor.
- **Practice**: Identify one CBT technique per month to focus on. Practice it in every session. Get feedback from a supervisor.

### 12. **Seek Supervision and Feedback on CBT Competence**
- **Action**: Work with a CBT-trained supervisor who can review your sessions (ideally via recording), provide specific feedback on your use of CBT techniques, and help you develop competence. Use the Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS) as a framework for feedback.
- **Example**: Record this session and review it with a supervisor. Discuss: What CBT techniques were used? What was missing? What would you do differently? What specific skills do you need to develop?
- **Practice**: Arrange for weekly or bi-weekly supervision. Bring recordings of your sessions. Use the CTRS to structure feedback.
