# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no unconditional positive regard or acceptance throughout the session. Opening statement "I'm here to tell you exactly what you need to do" establishes judgmental, prescriptive stance. Language is consistently controlling ("You really need to," "No ifs, ands, or buts," "It's just not acceptable") conveying disapproval rather than respect. Client's strengths (clarity about values, commitment to family, willingness to reflect) are never affirmed. Client's stated preference against directive approach is ignored across multiple exchanges. No warmth, support, or affirmation of client as person of worth. Autonomy and stated values are dismissed entirely.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Complete absence of reflective listening and empathic understanding. Psychiatrist never reflects back client's clearly articulated core values regarding family happiness or her need to connect health changes to personal values. No reflective statements such as "It sounds like family happiness is central to your decisions" or "You're saying connecting changes to family well-being would help you feel motivated." Only closed, factual questions asked ("Are you smoking?"). Client's resistance is treated as obstacle rather than meaningful clinical information. No exploration of why family happiness is central, what it means, or how health connects to that value. Psychiatrist remains focused solely on extracting health behavior information and issuing commands.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains basic topical focus on health behaviors (smoking, drinking, exercise), preventing session drift. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively established. Same three questions repeated verbatim across multiple exchanges without adaptation based on client response—rigid rather than strategic. Client explicitly invites alternative direction ("Maybe if we explored how my health impacts my family..."), but psychiatrist ignores this opening and repeats directive approach. True strategic direction would involve: collaboratively identifying target behavior, exploring client's own reasons for change, connecting to client's values, and adjusting based on readiness. None of this occurs. Focus maintained but through authoritarian rather than collaborative means.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Completely authoritarian, expert-driven stance throughout. Opening statement positions psychiatrist as sole authority on what client should do with no invitation for client input or expertise. Language is commanding ("You really need to," "You have to," "No excuses," "It's just not acceptable") rather than collaborative. Client repeatedly attempts to establish partnership by offering perspective and values; all invitations are ignored. Client states: "Maybe we could explore together what quitting would mean for my family's happiness"—explicit invitation to collaborate that is dismissed. No shared decision-making, co-construction of solutions, or genuine teamwork. Psychiatrist makes all decisions; client expected to comply. Fundamentally inconsistent with MI principles of partnership.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively undermines autonomy throughout. Language explicitly minimizes choice: "You have to quit smoking right now. No ifs, ands, or buts," "It's just not acceptable if you want to get better," "It's the only way." Phrase "No ifs, ands, or buts" explicitly denies client any room for choice or negotiation. Threat language used ("if you don't stop now, you'll face serious consequences") creates coercion rather than autonomy support. Client explicitly states need for autonomy: "I don't usually respond well to being told exactly what to do" and "I'm not ready to make those promises right now." These statements are ignored and pressure is increased. True autonomy support would affirm client's right to choose, explore barriers, and emphasize client as expert on own life. None of this occurs.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist does virtually no evoking. Only closed, factual questions asked ("Are you smoking?" "Do you drink?" "Do you exercise?")—information-gathering, not evocative. No questions about client's own reasons for change, barriers, past successes, or intrinsic motivation. Client provides rich motivational material (family happiness as core value, reviews values weekly, sees this as lifelong commitment), but psychiatrist never evokes from this. Evocative questions would include: "What concerns you most about your smoking?" "How do you see your health affecting your ability to be present with your family?" "What have you tried before?" Instead, psychiatrist imposes motivation through directives. Complete failure to draw out client's own motivations, values, or resources.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist assumes expert role for entire interaction and explicitly denies client's expertise about own life. Opening statement positions psychiatrist as sole expert; client as passive recipient. Unilateral pronouncements made throughout: "You really need to quit smoking," "Smoking is destroying your health," "Drinking only makes things worse," "It's the only way to improve your health quickly." These position psychiatrist as authority on truth with no acknowledgment of client's knowledge about own life, values, and circumstances. Client repeatedly demonstrates self-knowledge ("I don't usually respond well to being told exactly what to do," "I need to understand how changes connect to what matters most to me"). This expertise is dismissed. No power-sharing, co-creation, or genuine equality. Psychiatrist fosters dependence on expert authority rather than supporting client's wisdom and agency.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist makes no effort to cultivate change talk. Client demonstrates openness to change: "I'm not against improving my health," "I am willing to think about how these changes could help me be a better partner and family member," "I want to make sure any health changes support that lifelong commitment." These soft change talk statements are ignored entirely. Psychiatrist never asks questions designed to evoke change talk: "What would be benefits of quitting?" "How important is your health to you?" "What would it mean for your family if you were healthier?" "What strengths could help you make changes?" Psychiatrist's directive, pressuring approach likely suppresses change talk by triggering defensiveness. Client becomes focused on explaining need for autonomy rather than articulating reasons to change. Directive approach amplifies resistance rather than motivation.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist does not actively reinforce sustain talk, preventing lower score. However, fails to skillfully soften it. Client's primary "sustain talk" is not about reasons to maintain unhealthy habits but about need for autonomy and connection to values: "I don't usually respond well to being told exactly what to do," "I'm not ready to make those promises right now," "I need to understand how changes connect to what matters most to me." These are resistance statements, not sustain talk about target behaviors. Skilled clinician would recognize this resistance reflects approach mismatch and would shift to more collaborative, evocative, values-connected method. Instead, psychiatrist repeats same directive approach, intensifying resistance. Psychiatrist does not explicitly reinforce sustain talk but also does not skillfully redirect. Repeated directive approach likely increases rather than decreases client resistance.

## Overall Session Assessment

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.2/5**

**Summary:** This session represents a **severe failure to implement motivational interviewing principles**. Psychiatrist demonstrates fundamentally authoritarian, expert-driven approach directly inconsistent with MI spirit and evidence-based behavior change practice. Across all nine MI competency domains, clinician scores 1/5 or 2/5, indicating minimal to absent MI skill.

**Current Competency Level:** Pre-MI. Clinician has not integrated MI principles and is using traditional directive, expert-authority approach that research shows is ineffective for evoking intrinsic motivation and often increases client resistance.

**Critical Findings:**
- Zero acceptance, empathy, collaboration, autonomy support, evocation, or partnership
- Repeated use of ineffective approach despite clear client feedback
- Systematic dismissal of client's core values and autonomy
- No reflective listening or genuine effort to understand client's perspective
- Commanding, pressuring language creates coercive rather than collaborative environment

**Impact on Client Engagement:** Highly likely to **decrease** client motivation for change. Client demonstrates clear values, willingness to reflect, and openness to exploring connections between health and family. Psychiatrist's controlling approach triggers defensiveness and entrench resistance. By session end, client has not committed to changes and repeatedly emphasizes need for autonomy and values connection.

**Likelihood of Evoking Change:** Very low. Current approach is inconsistent with evidence-based behavior change principles and likely to harm patient outcomes.

**Immediate Action Required:** Clinician should not continue this approach without significant training and skill development. Formal MI training and clinical supervision are essential.
