# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance or unconditional positive regard. From the opening statement ("I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do"), the clinician adopts a judgmental, directive stance that communicates disapproval of Camila's behavior. Repeated use of controlling language ("you really should," "you need to be serious," "you must take this seriously") conveys conditional regard based on compliance. Camila's perspective is dismissed rather than validated, and her strengths and openness are never affirmed. No genuine warmth or support is demonstrated.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Empathy is essentially absent. The psychiatrist makes no genuine effort to understand Camila's experience, perspective, or the emotional meaning of wine in her life. When Camila explains that wine helps her relax, the clinician pivots immediately to health risks rather than exploring this experience. The single reflective statement ("I understand you think your drinking isn't a big problem") is immediately contradicted by "but," negating the reflection. Critical opportunities to understand Camila's struggle with competing demands and difficulty of sudden change are missed entirely. No curiosity about her internal experience is demonstrated.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** While the psychiatrist maintains clear focus on reducing wine consumption and prevents aimless wandering, the manner of pursuing direction violates MI principles. The approach is prescriptive rather than exploratory, with unilateral declarations ("you really should cut back immediately," "you have to act now") rather than collaborative goal-setting. The clinician never clarifies Camila's actual goals or asks what she wants from the session. Direction is imposed through pressure and ultimatums rather than guided through strategic exploration. The focus remains consistent, but execution is fundamentally authoritarian.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration is entirely absent. The opening statement explicitly rejects partnership: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to get healthier." When Camila offers her perspective—that gradual reduction might be more manageable, that she wants to find balance—these contributions are dismissed rather than incorporated. Her suggestion to cut back gradually is met with "gradual cutting back isn't enough" rather than exploration. There is no joint problem-solving, no seeking of Camila's input on what might work, and no co-construction of solutions. Power dynamics are entirely one-directional.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is fundamentally violated throughout. Controlling language ("you really should," "you need to," "you must," "you have to") communicates control rather than choice. Questions like "Are you ready to commit to stopping right away?" are framed as yes/no ultimatums leaving no room for Camila's actual readiness or preferences. When Camila expresses that gradual reduction feels more manageable, the psychiatrist insists "there's no time for easing into it," directly contradicting her autonomy. The message communicated is that Camila's autonomy is conditional on compliance with the prescribed intervention. Her right to make her own choices about her health is not respected.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation is completely absent. The psychiatrist never asks Camila about her own concerns, values around health, or what might motivate her to change. Instead, reasons for change are imposed ("liver problems," "irreversible damage," "serious health risks"). When Camila offers change talk—"I've been reading that cutting back could help my health, so I'm open to talking about it" and "I do want to be healthier"—these golden opportunities to explore intrinsic motivation are ignored. No open-ended questions are used to elicit her perspective. The clinician tells rather than asks, prescribes rather than explores, and imposes rather than evokes.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership is completely absent. The psychiatrist explicitly assumes the expert role and communicates that the clinician, not Camila, possesses the knowledge and authority to determine what must happen. The opening statement establishes this clearly: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to get healthier." When Camila offers her own expertise ("I think I need a plan that feels manageable"), this is dismissed rather than honored. The language used throughout ("you must," "you need to," "you have to") reflects a hierarchical, expert-driven relationship rather than collaborative partnership between equals. Camila's self-knowledge about her own life and capacity for change is treated as irrelevant.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Change talk is neither cultivated nor reinforced; in fact, the psychiatrist's approach actively suppresses it. Camila provides multiple instances of change talk that go unrecognized and unamplified: "I've been reading that cutting back could help my health, so I'm open to talking about it," "I do want to be healthier and not have problems down the road," and "I want to work on this." Each reflects desire, ability, or commitment to change. Instead of asking follow-up questions to deepen these statements, the psychiatrist contradicts them ("gradual cutting back isn't enough") or ignores them entirely. The repeated pressure and demands for immediate action create conditions likely to evoke sustain talk—precisely the opposite of what should occur in MI.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** While the psychiatrist does not actively reinforce sustain talk, the confrontational approach predictably generates rather than softens it. When Camila expresses concerns about feasibility ("stopping right away feels overwhelming," "going too fast might be hard to maintain"), the psychiatrist responds with escalating pressure rather than strategic redirection. No double-sided reflections, reframing, or strategic questions are used to gently shift focus toward change while acknowledging her concerns. Instead, responses like "you need to be serious about this" and "there's no time for easing into it" directly contradict her expressed concerns and entrench resistance. The psychiatrist does not actively reinforce sustain talk, but fails to skillfully soften it through MI techniques.

## Overall Session Assessment

**Overall Session Score: 1.1/5**

### Summary of Strengths
- The psychiatrist maintains consistent focus on the target behavior (reducing wine consumption) throughout the session
- The clinician demonstrates clear concern for the client's health and safety
- The psychiatrist does not become hostile or aggressive despite the client's expressed resistance

### Summary of Critical Areas for Improvement
- **Complete absence of MI spirit:** The session violates virtually every core principle of Motivational Interviewing
- **No acceptance or unconditional positive regard:** Judgment and conditional regard based on compliance are communicated throughout
- **Minimal empathy:** No genuine effort to understand the client's experience, perspective, or the meaning of wine in her life
- **No collaboration or partnership:** Unilateral decisions and dismissal of the client's contributions characterize the interaction
- **Autonomy not supported:** Pressure and minimization of the client's right to choose alternative approaches
- **No evocation:** Reasons for change are imposed rather than drawn from the client's own motivations and values
- **Change talk not cultivated:** Existing change talk is ignored or contradicted; the approach generates resistance instead
- **Sustain talk not softened:** The confrontational approach entrenches rather than reduces client resistance

### Impact on Client Engagement and Likelihood of Behavior Change
This session is highly likely to **decrease** Camila's motivation for change and damage the therapeutic relationship. Camila enters with openness and reasonable concerns about feasibility. The psychiatrist's response—dismissing her concerns, pressuring compliance, and communicating that her perspective is invalid—triggers defensiveness and resistance. Research on MI consistently demonstrates that confrontational, expert-driven approaches **increase client resistance and decrease the likelihood of sustained behavior change**. By session end, Camila is likely to feel unheard, judged, and pressured rather than supported and understood. While she may comply superficially, sustained behavior change without intrinsic motivation is unlikely.

### Critical Recommendations
1. **Adopt a collaborative, partnership-based stance immediately** – This is foundational to MI and must change before continuing clinical work with this approach
2. **Develop genuine empathy through reflective listening** – Make reflections your primary tool; aim for a 2:1 ratio of reflections to questions
3. **Evoke rather than impose change talk** – Ask open-ended questions designed to draw out the client's own reasons for change
4. **Support autonomy and eliminate controlling language** – Replace "you must/need to/have to" with "you might consider" and "what feels doable to you"
5. **Seek formal MI training and supervision immediately** – This session demonstrates insufficient training in MI principles and evidence-based practice for behavior change

**CRITICAL NOTE:** This session represents a significant departure from evidence-based practice for behavior change. The psychiatrist should not continue using this approach without formal Motivational Interviewing training and clinical supervision. The current approach is likely to be ineffective and potentially harmful to therapeutic relationships and client outcomes.
