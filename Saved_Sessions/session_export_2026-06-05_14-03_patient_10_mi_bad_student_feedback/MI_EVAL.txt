### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Active undermining of autonomy through coercive language and removal of choice. Repeated use of mandatory language: "You have to," "You must," "You need to," "No excuses," "This is non-negotiable," "There's no excuse for not starting next week." Weaponizes client's values to eliminate autonomy: "You want to be there for your son, right? Coercive framing designed to override stated autonomy. No respect for client's boundaries or choice.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Complete absence of evocation. Never asks about client's own reasons for change, health values, goals, or ideas about what might work. Client offers genuine change talk multiple times ("I love my son dearly," "I want to do what's best for me and my son," "I want to be strong and healthy for my son," "I'm willing to try something small," "I'm open to trying to be more consistent"), yet clinician ignores this intrinsic motivation. Instead, clinician imposes external reasons: "You want to be strong and healthy for your son, right?" and "Exercise will actually help reduce your stress." Clinician-generated reasons replace client-generated motivation. Skilled MI clinician would explore: "Tell me more about what being there for him means to you" or "How do you see your health connecting to being the parent you want to be?" Instead, client's own motivations are dismissed.

Skilled MI clinician would recognize and amplify through reflections and follow-up questions. By repeatedly demanding same behavior client has rejected, psychiatrist reinforces sustain talk and creates resistance. ### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Complete failure to skillfully soften sustain talk; instead, clinician directly confronts and dismisses client concerns. Client's sustain talk includes: co-parenting conflict, time constraints, energy limitations, fear of burnout, need for realistic planning. Rather than skillfully reframing or using double-sided reflections, psychiatrist directly confronts: "No excuses," "You have to push through the anxiety and just do it," "You really have to prioritize this." This confrontational approach typically strengthens resistance, which is exactly what occurs—client becomes increasingly firm in refusal. Skilled approach would acknowledge concern, validate it, and gently redirect: "So you're worried about burnout—that makes sense. You want something sustainable. What would that look like?" Instead, clinician uses confrontation and dismissal, the least effective way to handle sustain talk. Minor positive: clinician does not actively reinforce sustain talk or agree change is impossible. However, confrontational approach is equally ineffective and likely increases defensiveness and resistance.

## Summary Assessment

### **Current MI Competency Level:** NOT COMPETENT

This session demonstrates a fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles and represents an authoritarian, coercive clinical approach that is contraindicated for behavior change work.

### **Key Findings:**

**Strengths:**
- Maintains consistent topical focus on exercise behavior
- Does not actively reinforce hopelessness or impossibility
- Demonstrates persistence in addressing health concern

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Establishes and maintains expert authority from opening statement
- Uses repeated demands, threats, and weaponization of client values
- Completely ignores client's genuine change talk and intrinsic motivation
- Dismisses legitimate barriers (co-parenting stress, time constraints, burnout risk) as excuses
- Fails to recognize or amplify client wisdom about her own life and needs
- Uses confrontational approach to sustain talk, increasing defensiveness
- Employs only closed yes/no questions, preventing full client expression
- Demonstrates no genuine collaborative problem-solving

### **Impact on Client Engagement:**

This session is likely to **decrease** Amina's motivation for change and damage the therapeutic relationship. Client enters with genuine motivation and willingness to explore change; by session end, she is increasingly defensive and firm in refusal to comply. Coercive, expert-driven approaches reduce intrinsic motivation and increase resistance—exactly what occurs here.

### **Recommendation:**

This clinician requires comprehensive Motivational Interviewing training and supervision before continuing behavior change counseling. Current approach risks harm through increased stress, decreased motivation, and damaged therapeutic alliance. Immediate remediation needed in: MI spirit and principles, collaborative inquiry, reflective listening, evoking client motivation, and autonomy support.
