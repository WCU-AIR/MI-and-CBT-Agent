# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal acceptance demonstrated. While opening is warm, clinician quickly shifts to directive problem-solving stance. Repeatedly imposes solutions without exploring client's perspective (e.g., "Maybe you should try setting your alarm across the room"). Fails to normalize Jamal's struggle or affirm his autonomy. Dismisses client concerns rather than validating them. Superficial encouragement ("You've got this!") undermines genuine acceptance. Missing affirmations of client's honesty and self-knowledge.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Superficial empathy with inconsistent application. Basic reflective listening present but lacks emotional depth and accuracy. Misses Jamal's actual point about motivation vs. sleep onset. Fails to capture ambivalence when client states "It's not like moving the alarm will change how I feel about waking up." Premature problem-solving interrupts emerging self-awareness. Critical missed opportunity when client reveals real motivation ("stop people nagging me")—clinician accepts without exploring deeper emotional conflict. Does not explore tension between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Adequate but inconsistent focus on target behavior (school punctuality). Clear opening identification of concern is appropriate. However, direction becomes muddled by premature narrowing to specific solutions rather than exploration of problem and motivation. Clinician directs toward solutions (alarm clocks, bedtime) rather than toward understanding and evoking change talk. Fails to maintain strategic focus on intrinsic motivation. Final summary accurately reflects discussion but represents missed opportunity to redirect toward deeper motivation before closing.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no genuine collaboration. Fundamentally expert-driven, hierarchical approach inconsistent with MI principles. Clinician consistently positions self as authority who knows what client should do. Premature advice-giving throughout ("Maybe you should try setting your alarm across the room"). Dismisses client input when Jamal expresses skepticism. Unilateral problem definition without exploring client's understanding. Prescriptive closing with multiple directives. Absent shared decision-making—no genuine invitations for client's input ("What do you think might help?" never asked). Power imbalance evident in language: "you should," "maybe you want," "it would probably help if."

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support almost entirely absent. Repeated directive advice without genuine choice presentation. Critical failure: when client explicitly states "It just feels like people always telling me what to do, you know?"—a direct autonomy threat—clinician does not acknowledge or validate. Instead, continues telling client what to do, directly amplifying autonomy concern. Closing statement ("Remember, it's your choice") contradicted by entire session of specific solution recommendations. No exploration of client preferences ("What feels manageable to you?" never asked). Subtle but clear pressure toward compliance with clinician recommendations. Ambivalence ("I'm not promising though") dismissed rather than explored.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation almost entirely absent. Clinician consistently tells rather than asks. Premature problem-solving replaces exploration of client's own understanding. Leading questions guide toward clinician's hypothesis rather than evoking client wisdom ("Is it because you have trouble waking up, or is it something else?"). Advice-giving substitutes for exploration ("Maybe you should try setting your alarm across the room"). Failure to explore intrinsic motivation when client reveals real reason ("Mostly, I just wanna stop people nagging me")—clinician accepts without deeper exploration. No exploration of values, resources, or past successes. Scaling questions asked without prior exploration of what would increase confidence. Session characterized by installing motivation through advice rather than evoking motivation through skillful questioning.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership virtually absent. Clinician actively assumes expert role throughout, positioning self as authority who knows what client needs. Expert positioning evident in language: "Maybe you should try," "it would probably help if," "you should probably try anyway." Clinician-driven agenda introduced without inviting client input. Client contributions dismissed—when Jamal offers insight ("It's not like moving the alarm will change how I feel about waking up"), clinician pivots to different suggestion rather than building on client wisdom. Unilateral summarization without collaborative co-construction. Power imbalance maintained throughout—clinician defines problems, proposes solutions, directs conversation; client remains reactive. Absence of genuine seeking of client expertise about own life. No moments where clinician affirms "You know yourself best" or invites collaborative problem-solving.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal effort to cultivate, evoke, or reinforce change talk. Initial change talk ("I have to start getting to school on time") not reinforced or deepened—clinician responds with closed question rather than exploration. External motivation accepted without evoking intrinsic reasons ("That's good you want to avoid the nagging" reinforces external rather than internal motivation). Missed opportunities throughout: no open questions evoking desire ("What would be good about getting to school on time?"), ability ("What strengths do you have?"), reasons ("What matters to you?"), need ("What happens if things stay the way they are?"), or commitment. When client states "I think I can do it," clinician doesn't deepen this confidence. Scaling questions asked without exploration of what would increase ratings. Session ends with compliance-based agreement rather than motivation-based commitment.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill in softening sustain talk. Clinician sometimes reinforces rather than redirects sustain talk. External motivation ("stop people nagging me") accepted and reinforced rather than explored for deeper intrinsic reasons. Critical failure: when client directly expresses autonomy concern ("It just feels like people always telling me what to do"), clinician continues directing, amplifying rather than softening this sustain talk. No double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence ("I'm not promising though" dismissed without exploration). Missed opportunity to reframe client's skepticism ("I could just turn it off and still go back to bed") as evidence of self-awareness. No exploration of function of sustain talk ("What concerns you about getting up earlier?" never asked). Low importance rating (7/10) not explored for what would increase it. Clinician's continued directiveness likely increases rather than decreases resistance.

### Summary of Key Findings

**Strengths:**
- Warm, welcoming opening tone
- Clear identification of target behavior
- Basic session structure with attempt at summarization
- Client remains engaged despite clinician approach

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Fundamental departure from MI principles and spirit
- Expert-driven, directive problem-solving model replaces collaborative, evocative approach
- Repeated imposition of solutions before establishing understanding
- Dismissal of client autonomy concerns; continued directiveness amplifies resistance
- Failure to explore intrinsic motivation; external motivation accepted without deeper exploration
- Nearly every client statement represents missed opportunity for deepening understanding or evoking motivation

**Impact on Client:**
This approach is likely to *decrease* rather than increase intrinsic motivation. Jamal's closing statement ("I'm not promising though") and agreement to "think about it" suggest compliance rather than genuine commitment. Compliance-based change is associated with poor long-term outcomes and increased relapse.

**Immediate Recommendation:**
Fundamental shift required from expert-driven to collaborative, evocative stance. Formal MI training and supervision strongly recommended. Immediate priority: commit to asking open-ended questions and resisting advice-giving for first 15-20 minutes of next session.
