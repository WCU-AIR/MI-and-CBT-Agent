
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Shift from Expert-Driven to Evocative Approach (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Fundamentally reframe your role from problem-solver to facilitator of self-discovery. Replace advice-giving with open-ended questions that invite the client to explore their own understanding, motivations, and solutions.

**Example:** 
- **Instead of:** "Maybe you should try setting your alarm across the room or something so you have to get up to turn it off."
- **Use:** "You mentioned that mornings are tough and that you don't always feel like getting up. What do you think is really going on there? What have you noticed about yourself in the mornings?"

**Practice:** 
- Before your next session, write out 5-10 open-ended questions you could ask to understand the client's perspective without proposing solutions. Practice asking these questions and resisting the urge to offer advice.
- Record yourself in role-play and count how many times you give advice vs. ask open-ended questions. Goal: 80% questions, 20% advice (and only advice when explicitly requested).
- Use the "OARS" framework (Open questions, Affirmations, Reflective listening, Summaries) for the first 15 minutes of your next session before moving to any problem-solving.

---

### 2. **Develop Genuine Empathy and Complex Reflections**

**Action:** Move beyond simple reflective listening to complex reflections that capture emotional nuance, ambivalence, and the client's underlying experience. When a client expresses a concern or insight, reflect it back with depth and curiosity rather than moving to problem-solving.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** "It sounds like sleep might be part of the problem."
- **Use:** "So you're saying the alarm goes off, but you don't feel like getting up. It's not that you can't wake up—it's that you don't want to. And that's a different problem than just needing a louder alarm. Tell me more about that."

**Practice:**
- After each client statement, pause and ask yourself: "What is the emotion or value underneath what they just said?" Reflect that back.
- Practice double-sided reflections: "On one hand, you want people to stop nagging you about being late, and on the other hand, you're not sure you really want to get up early for yourself. Both of those make sense."
- Listen for what's *not* being said. When Jamal says "I'm not promising though," this is ambivalence. Reflect it: "You're willing to try, but you're also not sure this will work or that you're ready to commit. What's that about?"

---

### 3. **Explore and Evoke Intrinsic Motivation Rather Than Accepting External Motivation**

**Action:** When a client expresses external motivation (avoiding nagging, pleasing others, avoiding consequences), acknowledge it but then explore deeper intrinsic reasons for change. Use questions that invite the client to discover their own reasons.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** "That's good you want to avoid the nagging."
- **Use:** "So avoiding nagging is one reason. I'm also curious—what would be good about getting to school on time for *you*? What would that make possible? What matters to you about school or your future?"

**Practice:**
- When a client mentions external motivation, use the "but" technique: "I hear that people nagging you is one reason, *but* I'm wondering what else might be important to you about this."
- Ask questions that explore desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment:
  - Desire: "What would be good about getting to school on time?"
  - Ability: "What strengths do you have that could help you with this?"
  - Reasons: "What are the reasons that matter to you?"
  - Need: "What happens if things stay the way they are?"
  - Commitment: "How ready are you to make this change?"

---

### 4. **Respect and Address Autonomy Concerns Directly**

**Action:** When a client expresses concern about being told what to do, take this seriously. Acknowledge the concern, validate it, and explicitly invite the client to be in control of the change process. Do not continue to direct after autonomy concerns are raised.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** Continuing to say "you should probably try anyway" after Jamal says "It just feels like people always telling me what to do."
- **Use:** "I hear you. It sounds like people telling you what to do actually makes it harder, not easier. So let's make sure this is something *you* want to do, not something I'm pushing you toward. What would feel right to you? What are you willing to try?"

**Practice:**
- When a client expresses autonomy concerns, pause and explicitly acknowledge: "That's important. You're right—this has to be your choice."
- Invite the client to lead: "What do you think might help?" "What have you already tried?" "What feels doable to you?"
- Use language that emphasizes choice: "You could try," "You might consider," "What do you think about..." rather than "You should," "You need to," "You have to."

---

### 5. **Build Genuine Partnership and Collaboration**

**Action:** Position the client as the expert on their own life. Seek their input, build on their ideas, and co-construct solutions rather than imposing them. Use language that emphasizes equality and shared problem-solving.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** "Maybe you could try putting your phone or alarm across the room and setting a couple of alarms. Also, going to bed a bit earlier might help."
- **Use:** "You know your mornings better than anyone. What do you think might help? What have you already tried? What ideas do you have? Let's figure this out together."

**Practice:**
- Before proposing any solution, ask: "What do you think might help?" and genuinely listen to the client's ideas.
- Build on client contributions: "That's a good idea. Tell me more about that." "How would that work for you?"
- Use collaborative language: "Let's think about this together," "What's your sense of...," "You know yourself best."
- Invite feedback on your ideas: "I have a thought—would that be helpful to explore?" rather than "You should try..."

---

### 6. **Strategically Cultivate and Reinforce Change Talk**

**Action:** Actively listen for change talk (statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment to change) and strategically amplify it through reflections, follow-up questions, and affirmations. When change talk emerges, deepen it rather than moving on.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** When Jamal says "I have to start getting to school on time," respond with a closed question about the cause.
- **Use:** "Tell me more about that. What makes you say you have to start getting to school on time? What's driving that for you?"

**Practice:**
- Listen for all forms of change talk: desire ("I want to..."), ability ("I can..."), reasons ("It would help because..."), need ("I have to..."), commitment ("I'm going to...").
- When you hear change talk, reflect it back and deepen it: "So you think you can do this. What makes you confident about that?"
- Reinforce change talk with affirmations: "That shows real insight," "I appreciate your honesty," "That's important."
- Count change talk statements in your sessions. Goal: increase from baseline.

---

### 7. **Skillfully Handle Sustain Talk and Ambivalence**

**Action:** When a client expresses reasons against changing or ambivalence, do not dismiss or argue against it. Instead, use double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to acknowledge both sides and gently shift focus toward change.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** Dismissing Jamal's concern ("I could just turn it off and still go back to bed") and proposing a different solution.
- **Use:** "You're right—just moving the alarm won't work if you're not ready to get up. That's actually important self-knowledge. So what *would* help? What would need to be different for you to want to get up?"

**Practice:**
- Use double-sided reflections: "On one hand, you want to avoid people nagging you, and on the other hand, you're not sure you really want to change. Both of those make sense."
- Reframe sustain talk as evidence of autonomy or self-knowledge: "You know yourself—you know that won't work for you. What would?"
- Ask about the function of sustain talk: "What concerns you about getting up earlier?" "What would be hard about that?"
- Use strategic silence: When a client expresses ambivalence, pause and let them sit with it. Often they will move toward change talk on their own.

---

### 8. **Avoid Premature Problem-Solving and Advice-Giving**

**Action:** Resist the urge to propose solutions before you have fully understood the client's perspective, explored their motivation, and built a collaborative relationship. Advice-giving should come *after* understanding and evocation, not before.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** Opening with "Is it because you have trouble waking up, or is it something else? Do you find that you get distracted in the morning?" (leading questions that guide toward the clinician's hypothesis)
- **Use:** "Tell me about what mornings are like for you. What's the struggle?" (open-ended question that invites the client's own understanding)

**Practice:**
- In your next session, commit to spending the first 20 minutes *only* asking open-ended questions and reflecting. No advice, no solutions, no problem-solving.
- When you feel the urge to give advice, pause and ask an open-ended question instead.
- Use the rule: "Understand before you advise." Only offer suggestions after you have demonstrated genuine understanding of the client's perspective.

---

### 9. **Use Scaling Questions Strategically, Not Prematurely**

**Action:** Scaling questions (confidence, importance, readiness) can be useful, but only after you have explored the client's perspective and evoked motivation. Use them to deepen understanding, not to move toward problem-solving.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** "On a scale from 1 to 10, how confident are you that you can get to school on time more often?" (asked without prior exploration)
- **Use:** After exploring motivation and building understanding: "On a scale from 1 to 10, how confident are you? And what would help you move from a 6 to a 7?"

**Practice:**
- Before using scaling questions, ask open-ended questions first: "What do you think would help?" "What are your concerns?" "What matters to you about this?"
- After scaling, always follow up: "What would help you move up one point?" "What's keeping you from being higher?" These questions evoke change talk.
- Use scaling to understand, not to direct. The goal is insight, not compliance.

---

### 10. **Develop Awareness of Your Own Expert Stance and Directive Tendencies**

**Action:** Recognize that your training as a psychiatrist has likely emphasized diagnosis, problem-solving, and directive intervention. MI requires a fundamentally different stance. Develop self-awareness about when you slip into expert mode and practice the alternative.

**Example:**
- Notice when you're thinking "I know what the problem is" or "I know what they should do." Pause and ask an open-ended question instead.
- Notice when you're proposing solutions. Pause and ask "What do you think might help?"

**Practice:**
- Record yourself in sessions and listen for directive language: "you should," "you need to," "you have to," "maybe you want to." Count these instances.
- After each session, reflect: "Did I spend more time telling or asking? Did I understand the client's perspective before proposing solutions? Did I evoke the client's own motivation?"
- Seek supervision or consultation with an MI trainer. This is a significant shift from traditional medical practice and benefits from expert feedback.
- Consider formal MI training (e.g., through the Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers). This session demonstrates that reading about MI is insufficient; experiential training is necessary.
