
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Feedback: Therapist Evaluation

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made no attempt to establish a collaborative agenda at any point during this session. A proper agenda-setting process would involve: (1) identifying the patient's primary concerns for the session, (2) prioritizing among multiple issues, (3) allocating time appropriately, and (4) establishing measurable session goals. None of these occurred.

Instead, the therapist opened with a generic social greeting ("How's your day been so far?") and immediately pivoted to offering personal anecdotes and generic advice when the patient disclosed severe suicidal ideation and depressive symptoms. The session drifted without structure, direction, or explicit goals. There was no discussion of what Olivia hoped to accomplish in the session, no prioritization of her suicidal thoughts versus other concerns, and no time management strategy.

For a patient presenting with active suicidal ideation, severe depression, and difficulty with basic self-care, agenda-setting is clinically essential—not optional. It establishes safety planning as a priority, clarifies what will be addressed, and demonstrates professional structure. The absence of agenda-setting in a high-risk case represents a fundamental failure in session structure.

**Summary:**

No agenda was established. The session lacked structure, direction, and explicit goals. This is a critical omission for a patient with active suicidal ideation.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist never elicited feedback from the patient about the session, her understanding of interventions, or her reactions to the therapist's suggestions. Evidence-based feedback elicitation includes: (1) asking if the patient understands the rationale for interventions, (2) checking whether suggestions feel relevant or helpful, (3) soliciting reactions to the session direction, and (4) adjusting approach based on patient input.

The therapist offered repeated suggestions ("try to think positive," "drink water," "get outside," "take deep breaths") without ever asking: "Does this make sense to you?" "Do you think this is something you could try?" "How are you feeling about what we've discussed?" or "Is there anything I've said that doesn't fit for you?"

Critically, when the patient explicitly stated that positive thinking felt "empty" and like "pretending or lying to myself," the therapist did not adjust course or explore why her suggestions weren't resonating. Instead, the therapist repeated the same advice. This represents a failure to use patient feedback to guide treatment.

For a patient with severe depression and suicidal ideation, feedback elicitation is essential to ensure interventions are: (1) understood, (2) perceived as credible, (3) aligned with the patient's experience, and (4) likely to be attempted. The complete absence of feedback-seeking represents poor clinical practice.

**Summary:**

No feedback was elicited at any point. The therapist did not check understanding, adjust based on patient responses, or solicit reactions to interventions. This is a serious clinical gap.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated minimal understanding of Olivia's internal experience and failed to grasp the severity and specificity of her presentation. 2. **Anhedonia and anergia misunderstanding:** When Olivia stated "everything feels so exhausting" and "I just forget to do basic things like eat or drink," the therapist responded with cheerful suggestions to "drink water" and "get outside." The therapist did not recognize that these are symptoms of severe depression, not motivational deficits that respond to willpower.

3. **Depersonalization and panic:** Olivia mentioned "depersonalization" and "panic waves that make it hard to breathe," but the therapist never explored these anxiety symptoms or their relationship to her mood disorder.

4. **Bipolar II context:** The therapist showed no awareness that Olivia has bipolar II disorder with a history of hypomanic episodes. This is critical because: (a) standard antidepressant monotherapy can trigger mood cycling, (b) behavioral activation must be carefully titrated to avoid hypomania, and (c) medication adherence is essential. 5. **Sleep disturbance pattern:** Olivia described hypersomnia alternating with racing thoughts and panic-induced insomnia—a pattern consistent with her bipolar presentation. The therapist treated this as a simple sleep hygiene issue.

The therapist's repeated personal anecdotes ("When I was feeling down," "When I had a bad breakup," "When I was learning to ride a bike") further demonstrated a failure to understand Olivia's experience as distinct from generic stress. Her presentation is not comparable to normal sadness or life stress—it is a severe psychiatric episode with suicidal risk.

**Summary:**

The therapist demonstrated surface-level empathy but fundamentally misunderstood Olivia's clinical presentation, severity, and psychiatric context. Critical symptoms were minimized or ignored.
