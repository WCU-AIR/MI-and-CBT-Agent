
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

2 = Barely Adequate

6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Conduct Comprehensive Psychiatric Assessment at Session Start**
- **Action**: Before beginning CBT work, systematically assess for current psychotic symptoms (auditory hallucinations, delusions, paranoia), depressive symptoms (suicidal ideation, hopelessness, guilt), and medication adherence. Use structured questions: "Have you been hearing voices or having thoughts that others don't share?" "Have you had any thoughts about harming yourself?" "How are you doing with your medications?"
- **Example**: "John, before we dive into today's work, I want to check in about your overall mental health. Have you noticed any changes in your mood, sleep, or energy? Any thoughts that feel intrusive or scary? Any voices or experiences that feel unusual? How's your medication routine going?"
- **Practice**: Develop a brief standardized assessment protocol (5-10 minutes) that you use at the start of every session with patients with psychotic disorders. This ensures safety and prevents missing relapse warning signs.

### 2. **Develop Comprehensive Case Conceptualization Addressing Full Diagnostic Picture**
- **Action**: Create a written case formulation that addresses all symptom domains: psychotic symptoms (auditory hallucinations, delusions), depressive symptoms (anhedonia, low energy, suicidal ideation), anxiety symptoms, and behavioral patterns (social withdrawal, avoidance). Explicitly discuss which symptoms are most central to current functioning and which should be prioritized in treatment.
- **Example**: "John, I want to make sure I understand the full picture. You're dealing with depression—the low energy and not wanting to do things—anxiety about social situations, and a history of psychotic episodes. For right now, which of these feels most important to address first? What would make the biggest difference in your life?"
- **Practice**: For every patient with schizoaffective disorder, write out a one-page case formulation that includes: (1) psychotic symptoms and relapse history, (2) depressive symptoms and suicide risk, (3) anxiety symptoms, (4) substance use history, (5) social/occupational functioning, (6) medication adherence, (7) treatment priorities. Share this with the patient and revise collaboratively.

### 3. **Integrate Behavioral Activation for Depression and Amotivation**
- **Action**: Recognize that the patient's low energy, anhedonia, and social withdrawal are core depressive symptoms that may be more impactful than anxiety. Assign behavioral activation homework (scheduling valued or pleasurable activities) in addition to anxiety-focused interventions. Use activity scheduling to increase social contact and engagement.
- **Example**: "John, I notice that low energy and not wanting to do things are really affecting you. One thing that helps with this is scheduling activities—even small ones—that matter to you or that you used to enjoy. What's one thing you could do this week, even for 15 minutes, that might feel good or meaningful?"
- **Practice**: For every patient with depression or anhedonia, assign at least one behavioral activation task per week. Use an activity schedule worksheet. Review completion and mood impact in the next session.

### 4. **Explicitly Explain the Cognitive Model and Rationale for Interventions**
- **Action**: Before applying CBT techniques, teach the patient how thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are connected. Explain why the behavioral experiment will help build conviction. Use diagrams or worksheets to make this concrete.
- **Example**: "The way anxiety works is that our thoughts trigger feelings, and then we avoid things to feel better temporarily. But avoidance actually keeps the anxiety going because we never get to find out that things aren't as scary as we think. When you send that message, you'll get real evidence about what actually happens, which will help you believe the alternative thought more."
- **Practice**: Develop a brief psychoeducational handout about the cognitive model (thoughts-feelings-behaviors) that you give to every patient. Spend 5-10 minutes explaining it and asking the patient to give examples from their own life.

### 5. **Provide Structured Thought Record Worksheet for Independent Practice**
- **Action**: Don't just elicit thoughts in session—give the patient a written thought record worksheet they can use between sessions to identify and challenge anxious thoughts independently. Make it simple and concrete with columns for: Situation, Automatic Thought, Evidence For, Evidence Against, Alternative Thought, Outcome.
- **Example**: Provide a one-page thought record template and say: "Here's a tool you can use when you notice anxious thoughts coming up. Write down what happened, what you thought, and then ask yourself: What's the evidence for this thought? What's against it? What's a more balanced way to think about it? This helps you practice what we did today."
- **Practice**: Create a library of simple, patient-friendly CBT worksheets (thought records, activity schedules, behavioral experiments, relapse prevention plans). Assign at least one worksheet per session and review completion in the next session.

### 6. **Explicitly Discuss the Role of Behavioral Experiments in Building Conviction**
- **Action**: When the patient expresses difficulty "really believing" the alternative thought, explicitly explain that behavioral experiments are designed to build conviction through experience, not just logic. Discuss how the brain learns through doing, not just thinking.
- **Example**: "You mentioned it's hard to really believe the alternative thought, and that makes sense. Our brains don't usually change beliefs just by thinking differently—we change them by having new experiences. That's why sending this message is so important. When you see that the person responds positively or neutrally, your brain will start to believe the alternative thought more because you'll have real evidence."
- **Practice**: In every session where you assign a behavioral experiment, explicitly discuss the mechanism: "This experiment will help you gather evidence that challenges your anxious thought. The more evidence you gather through experience, the more your brain will believe the alternative thought."

### 7. **Assess and Address Shame Related to Psychotic History**
- **Action**: Explore whether current shame about being "broken" or "exposing" himself connects to past psychotic delusions involving guilt and wrongdoing. Normalize shame as a common response to psychotic episodes and discuss strategies for managing it (self-compassion, psychoeducation about the disorder, gradual disclosure).
- **Example**: "I'm noticing you mentioned feeling like you're 'broken' and worried about exposing yourself. I'm wondering if that connects to some of the difficult experiences you've had in the past—the times when you were struggling with psychosis. Sometimes after those experiences, people feel a lot of shame. Is that something that's been on your mind?"
- **Practice**: For patients with psychotic disorders, routinely assess for shame and guilt related to psychotic episodes. Provide psychoeducation that psychotic symptoms are not a reflection of character or moral failing. Discuss gradual, selective disclosure to trusted others.

### 8. **Develop Relapse Prevention Plan**
- **Action**: Create a written relapse prevention plan that identifies: (1) early warning signs of psychotic relapse (e.g., sleep changes, paranoid thoughts, hearing voices), (2) early warning signs of depressive relapse (e.g., increased isolation, hopelessness, suicidal thoughts), (3) specific actions to take if warning signs appear (e.g., contact psychiatrist, increase medication adherence, reach out to support person), (4) medication adherence strategies, (5) stress management and coping skills.
- **Example**: "Let's create a plan for staying stable. What are the first signs you notice when you're starting to struggle? What should you do if you notice those signs? Who can you call? How will you remember to take your medications?"
- **Practice**: For every patient with schizoaffective disorder, develop a one-page relapse prevention plan by the third session. Review it regularly and update as needed.

### 9. **Assign Psychoeducational Materials About Schizoaffective Disorder**
- **Action**: Provide written materials or recommend resources about schizoaffective disorder, the role of medication, relapse warning signs, and coping strategies. Discuss these materials in session and answer questions.
- **Example**: "I'm going to give you some information about schizoaffective disorder and how CBT can help. I'd like you to read it before our next session and let me know if you have questions. Understanding your condition better will help us work together more effectively."
- **Practice**: Develop a patient library of evidence-based psychoeducational materials about schizoaffective disorder, medication, relapse prevention, and CBT. Assign relevant materials at each session.

### 10. **Establish Explicit Homework Review and Compliance Monitoring**
- **Action**: At the end of each session, explicitly discuss how homework will be reviewed in the next session and what to do if the patient doesn't complete it. Ask the patient to rate their confidence about completing homework and problem-solve barriers. At the start of the next session, spend 5-10 minutes reviewing homework completion, outcomes, and learning.
- **Example**: "Before we finish, let's make sure you're clear on the homework. You're going to send that message tomorrow evening. In our next session, I want to hear what happened—what did they say? How did you feel? What did you learn? If you don't get to it, that's okay—we'll figure out what got in the way and adjust the plan. How confident are you about doing this?"
- **Practice**: Develop a homework review protocol: (1) Ask about completion, (2) Explore barriers if not completed, (3) Review outcomes and learning if completed, (4) Adjust future homework based on what worked or didn't work.

### 11. **Use Measurement-Based Care to Track Symptom Change**
- **Action**: Use validated symptom rating scales (e.g., PHQ-9 for depression, GAD-7 for anxiety, PANSS for psychotic symptoms) at regular intervals (e.g., every 2-4 sessions) to objectively track symptom change and treatment response. Share results with the patient and adjust treatment if progress is insufficient.
- **Example**: "Let's use these rating scales to track how you're doing over time. This helps us see if the work we're doing is actually helping and if we need to adjust our approach."
- **Practice**: Select 2-3 brief validated scales appropriate for the patient's diagnosis. Administer at baseline and every 2-4 sessions. Graph results and discuss with the patient.
