# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal unconditional positive regard. While opening is warm, the stance quickly becomes directive and corrective rather than affirming client worth. No explicit validation of courage in acknowledging the problem, normalization of fear, or recognition of competing priorities as legitimate. Implicit message is judgmental: "your current approach isn't working; here's what you should do instead."

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflections remain at surface level, reflecting content but missing emotional nuance. When patient discloses exhaustion and fear of failure, psychiatrist does not explore deeper meaning or emotional core. Critical barrier of fear of failure is completely bypassed in favor of prescriptive solutions. No complex reflections capturing ambivalence, emotion, or meaning. Emotional understanding is superficial and inconsistent.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clear focus on weight loss and health behavior change maintained throughout session, demonstrating adequate directional consistency. However, direction is psychiatrist-imposed rather than collaboratively established. No exploration of whether weight loss is patient's actual priority. When patient rates importance 5/10 and confidence 3/10, critical moment to reassess direction is missed. Direction is maintained but not strategically adjusted based on client readiness or preference.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Profound absence of true collaboration. Psychiatrist adopts expert-driven, hierarchical stance throughout. Unilateral goal-setting without asking what patient wants to work on. Repeated use of "maybe you should" language (6+ instances) represents directive instruction, not collaboration. When patient expresses hesitation about walk commitment, psychiatrist does not explore or adjust plan collaboratively. Power imbalance maintained throughout; patient positioned as passive recipient of advice rather than equal partner in problem-solving.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy actively undermined through prescriptive language and maintained pressure toward predetermined goals. Despite patient's low importance (5/10) and confidence (3/10) ratings indicating low readiness, psychiatrist continues prescribing smaller steps rather than respecting patient's valid hesitation. No choice-framing language ("It's completely up to you," "You get to decide"). When patient expresses doubt, psychiatrist does not validate as legitimate concern patient has right to hold. Directive approach actively undermines client ownership of change process.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Nearly completely absent. Psychiatrist tells rather than asks, providing solutions without first exploring what patient thinks might work. No exploration of values, what matters to patient, or what she wants for herself. Past successes not explored despite opening question about previous attempts. Scaling questions used but not strategically followed up to evoke deeper exploration. No strategic questioning to elicit desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment. Minimal change talk elicitation; when change talk emerges, it is not reinforced or amplified.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Completely absent. Psychiatrist positions herself as authority on what patient should do through repeated "maybe you should" and "it would probably help if" language. Patient positioned as passive recipient needing to be told what to do, not as expert on own experience. Psychiatrist's ideas dominate entire session; patient's contributions (fear, exhaustion, doubt) acknowledged but not used to reshape direction. No co-construction of plan; psychiatrist proposes, patient complies or hesitates. Closing summary is psychiatrist's interpretation, not jointly negotiated agreement. No acknowledgment of patient's expertise.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal effort to evoke or cultivate change talk. While some change talk emerges ("I guess drinking more water sounds like something I could try"), this occurs despite psychiatrist's approach, not because of skillful cultivation. No strategic questioning to elicit desire, ability, reasons, or need. When patient expresses doubt ("Maybe it won't make a difference anyway"), psychiatrist does not skillfully redirect. When change talk does emerge, it is not reinforced or amplified. Minimal use of open-ended questions strategically designed to elicit change talk.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill in softening sustain talk. Critical sustain talk statements ("It feels like it's happening no matter what I do," "Maybe it won't make a difference anyway") revealing hopelessness and perceived inefficacy are not explored. Fear of failure ("If I start trying, I'll just fail, and that would make me feel worse") not addressed with double-sided reflection or reframing. Overwhelm repeatedly expressed but not reframed as manageable. Low confidence (3/10) not addressed through confidence-building questions. No strategic reframing of barriers as understandable or as opportunities for problem-solving. Prescribing solutions may inadvertently reinforce sustain talk.

---

### **Overall MI Competency Assessment**

**Current Session Score: 1.6/5**

**Summary:** This session demonstrates **fundamental misalignment with motivational interviewing principles and spirit**. While the psychiatrist is warm and maintains clear focus, the session is characterized by directive advice-giving, expert-driven decision-making, and minimal evocation or autonomy support. The psychiatrist tells the patient what to do rather than drawing out her own motivations, values, and ideas.

**Current MI Competency Level:** **Pre-MI/Non-MI.** The approach is more consistent with traditional medical advice-giving than with motivational interviewing. While some MI elements are present (warmth, some reflective listening, scaling questions), they are not integrated into a coherent MI approach.

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Directive advice-giving dominates throughout
- Collaboration and partnership completely absent
- Evocation minimal; telling replaces asking
- Sustain talk not skillfully addressed
- Autonomy undermined despite low readiness indicators
- Empathy superficial; emotional experience not explored

**Likelihood of Evoking Sustained Client Motivation:** **Low.** While patient may comply with water goal, underlying barriers (fear, overwhelm, doubt) remain unaddressed. Without genuine exploration of motivations and concerns, sustained behavior change is unlikely. Directive approach may trigger psychological reactance and decrease intrinsic motivation.

**Recommendation:** Formal MI training with skills practice, feedback, role-plays, and supervised clinical work essential. Foundational shift from expert-driven advice-giving to collaborative, evocative practice is critical for developing true MI competence.
