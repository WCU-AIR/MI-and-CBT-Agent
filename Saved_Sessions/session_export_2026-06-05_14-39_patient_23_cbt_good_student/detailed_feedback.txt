
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

0 = Poor
2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Implement Systematic Safety Assessment and Planning (URGENT - HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Establish a structured safety protocol as a standing component of every session. At the beginning of each session, systematically assess: (1) current suicidal ideation intensity (0-10 scale), (2) intent to act, (3) specific plan or method, (4) access to means, (5) protective factors, and (6) prior attempts/hospitalizations. Document all findings. Develop a written safety plan collaboratively with the patient that includes: warning signs, internal coping strategies, people to contact, professional resources, and crisis line numbers.

**Example:** 
"Before we dive into today's work, I want to check in about safety since you've mentioned chronic suicidal thoughts. On a scale of 0-10, where 0 is no thoughts of suicide and 10 is strong thoughts with a plan to act, where are you today? [Listen to response]. Have you had any thoughts about harming yourself this week? [Listen]. Do you have access to means—medications, weapons, anything you could use to hurt yourself? [Listen]. Let's develop a safety plan together so you know exactly what to do if suicidal thoughts intensify."

**Practice:** 
- Use a standardized safety assessment tool (e.g., Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale) to ensure consistency
- Practice the assessment with a supervisor or peer until it becomes automatic
- Develop a template safety plan that can be customized for each patient
- Review the safety plan at the beginning of each session and update as needed

### 2. **Reconceptualize Case as BPD Rather Than Depression-Only (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Conduct a comprehensive BPD-focused case formulation that explicitly addresses: (1) affective instability and emotion dysregulation, (2) identity disturbance and chronic emptiness, (3) impulsive behaviors (job quitting, spending), (4) relational patterns (family conflict, social isolation), (5) self-harm and suicidality, and (6) anger dysregulation. Recognize that standard CBT for depression is insufficient; integrate DBT elements (emotion regulation, distress tolerance, mindfulness, interpersonal effectiveness) into the treatment plan.

**Example:**
"Juanita's core difficulties are not just depressive cognitions but BPD-related emotion dysregulation and identity disturbance. Her 'I'm a failure' thought is not a discrete automatic thought but part of a broader identity disturbance where she rapidly shifts between seeing herself as worthless and seeing herself as having great potential. Her withdrawal is not just depressive avoidance but also a response to overwhelming emotions and fear of rejection. Her anger is not just a mood symptom but a dysregulated emotional response to perceived slights. Her impulsive spending and job quitting reflect poor distress tolerance and impulsivity. Treatment must address these core BPD features, not just depressive cognitions."

**Practice:**
- Review DBT treatment manual (Linehan, 1993) to understand BPD-specific interventions
- Attend training on DBT or comprehensive CBT for BPD
- Develop a BPD-informed case formulation for this patient that guides all interventions
- Discuss the formulation with the patient to ensure she understands the treatment rationale
