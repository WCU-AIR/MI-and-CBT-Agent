### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out client's own motivations and values—is minimal and poorly executed. Clinician relies on telling rather than asking. Multiple missed opportunities to elicit intrinsic motivation: when Jordan spontaneously expresses concern for grandparents ("maybe getting the shot could help with that"), response is brief validation followed by pivot to his concerns rather than deeper exploration. When Jordan states "maybe not getting the shot isn't as harmless as I thought," this significant change talk is acknowledged but not deepened. Clinician uses scaling questions and provides advice rather than asking open questions to explore emerging insights. Change talk emerges primarily through Jordan's own initiative, not through clinician evocation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership—understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside within client—is essentially absent. Clinician actively assumes expert role and maintains hierarchical power throughout. Diagnoses problem ("you're worried about side effects"), prescribes solution (talk to doctor, research side effects, plan vaccination), and uses authority language ("Maybe you should," "I'm sure you'll make the right choice"). Treats Jordan as someone who needs to be educated and redirected rather than as someone possessing valuable insight about his own situation. No genuine incorporation of Jordan's ideas into session direction. Session ends with clinician providing final guidance rather than positioning Jordan as expert in his own decision-making.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** While Jordan produces some change talk spontaneously (concern for grandparents, recognition that "maybe it's not as harmless as I thought," commitment to talk with doctor), clinician demonstrates minimal skill in evoking, amplifying, or reinforcing it. When Jordan expresses change talk, clinician often dilutes it through immediate redirection to advice-giving. First instance of change talk about grandparents receives brief validation then pivots to scaling questions rather than deepening exploration. Second instance of significant change talk is acknowledged but followed by directive advice rather than exploration of this emerging insight. Third instance of commitment language is met with more advice rather than reinforcement. Change talk treated as checkpoint to move past, not as material to cultivate and deepen.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Clinician demonstrates minimal skill in softening sustain talk (Jordan's arguments against vaccination). Uses direct counter-arguments ("Maybe you should consider the benefits") which can trigger defensive sustain talk rather than soften it. Minimizes concerns ("But sometimes knowing more can reduce that fear")—the word "but" directly contradicts rather than validates. Lacks strategic double-sided reflections acknowledging sustain talk while gently shifting focus. When Jordan mentions peer influence ("Most of them don't get the flu shot either"), clinician doesn't explore or gently reframe this social norm sustain talk. Direct confrontation and minimization may inadvertently strengthen Jordan's resistance rather than soften it. Validation of concerns should precede any redirection.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.6/5**

**MI Competency Level:** Below-beginner level. Clinician demonstrates conceptual understanding of MI (uses some reflections, acknowledges client values) but fundamentally misapplies principles in practice.

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Pervasive directive stance with prescriptive language ("Maybe you should" appears 8+ times)
- Minimal autonomy support despite stated commitment to client choice
- Expert-driven rather than collaborative approach
- Missed opportunities to evoke and deepen emerging change talk
- Superficial empathy focused on problem-solving rather than emotional validation
- Sustain talk addressed through direct counter-arguments rather than validation and strategic reframing

**Likelihood of Evoking Intrinsic Motivation:** Low. While Jordan leaves with agreement to "think about it more," this represents compliance rather than genuine motivation. Psychiatrist has positioned themselves as authority, which may trigger reactance. Jordan's most valuable change talk (concern for grandparents) was never fully explored or cultivated. Session unlikely to produce sustained behavior change.

**Priority Recommendations:**
1. Eliminate prescriptive language; replace with open curiosity
2. Deepen exploration of change talk when it emerges
3. Validate sustain talk before redirecting
4. Support autonomy explicitly and consistently
5. Shift from expert to collaborative partner
6. Develop skill in evoking rather than providing solutions

**Required Action:** Clinician requires intensive MI retraining with focus on spirit and principles, supervised practice with feedback on directive language, and recorded session review to identify and eliminate prescriptive patterns.
