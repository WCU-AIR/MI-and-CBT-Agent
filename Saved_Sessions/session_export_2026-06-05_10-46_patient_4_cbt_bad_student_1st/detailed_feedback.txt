
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Feedback: Therapist Evaluation

---

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made no attempt to establish a collaborative agenda at any point during this session. There was no discussion of session goals, prioritization of problems, time allocation, or what the patient hoped to accomplish during their time together. A proper CBT agenda-setting would have involved asking Gabriela what she most wanted to address today, identifying 2-3 specific target problems (e.g., "racing thoughts about test results," "difficulty sleeping," "reassurance-seeking behavior"), and establishing realistic objectives for the 45-50 minute session.

Instead, the therapist launched directly into generic reassurance and personal anecdotes without any structured framework. The session drifted aimlessly, with the therapist responding reactively to whatever the patient mentioned rather than collaboratively establishing priorities. For a patient with adjustment disorder presenting with anxiety, sleep disturbance, concentration problems, and repetitive reassurance-seeking, a clear agenda would have been essential to focus intervention on the most impactful targets.

The absence of agenda-setting is particularly problematic in a hospital setting where time is limited and the patient is acutely distressed. CBT requires explicit structure; without it, sessions become supportive conversations rather than targeted treatment.

**Summary:**
No agenda was established. The therapist did not collaborate with the patient to identify session goals, prioritize problems, or structure the session around specific, measurable objectives.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made virtually no attempt to elicit or respond to patient feedback throughout the session. There were no explicit check-ins for understanding (e.g., "Does that make sense?" "How are you feeling about what we've discussed?"). The therapist did not ask whether the suggestions were helpful, realistic, or aligned with the patient's values. There was no summary of key points at the end of the session, no verification that the patient understood the rationale for recommendations, and no solicitation of the patient's reactions to the session itself.

The only implicit feedback moment occurred when the patient said, "It's comforting to know that people care and want to help," but the therapist did not build on this or ask clarifying questions. A therapist scoring well on feedback would have asked: "What specifically felt most helpful today?" "Do you think the suggestions I mentioned are things you can actually do?" "Is there anything I said that didn't quite fit for you?" "What would be most useful to focus on next time?"

This represents a critical failure in collaborative empiricism—a cornerstone of CBT. Without feedback loops, the therapist cannot know whether interventions are landing, whether the patient feels heard, or whether the treatment plan is realistic.

**Summary:**
Minimal feedback elicitation. The therapist did not check for understanding, ask about helpfulness of suggestions, or solicit the patient's reactions to the session.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated surface-level empathy but failed to demonstrate genuine understanding of Gabriela's internal experience. The therapist accurately reflected some explicit content (e.g., acknowledging worry about test results, the importance of the wedding), but repeatedly missed the deeper emotional and cognitive patterns that should have been the focus of a CBT intervention.

Gabriela explicitly communicated several critical clinical features that the therapist failed to adequately understand:

1. **Catastrophic thinking and uncertainty intolerance**: "When the doctors talk about results or treatments, my mind just jumps to the worst possibilities." The therapist responded with generic reassurance ("things will get better") rather than exploring this cognitive pattern or validating the specific nature of her worry.

2. **Repetitive reassurance-seeking**: "I keep asking the nurses and doctors so many questions because I want to know, but I'm afraid of what they might say." The therapist did not acknowledge this pattern, explore its function, or discuss how reassurance-seeking paradoxically maintains anxiety.

The therapist suggested resuming puzzles as a "little break" without exploring whether this represents a symptom of depression that requires targeted behavioral activation, not just casual suggestion.

4. **Sleep disturbance with racing thoughts**: "Sometimes I just lay awake thinking about all the what-ifs." The therapist offered generic sleep hygiene tips (herbal tea, music) without exploring the cognitive content driving the insomnia or using evidence-based techniques like worry time or thought records.

The therapist's repeated use of personal anecdotes ("When I was stressed...") further demonstrates a failure to understand Gabriela's unique experience. Her situation—a 65-year-old widow facing potential disease progression, wanting to live for her son's wedding—is not equivalent to the therapist's exam stress or moving anxiety. **Summary:**

### 5. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration was essentially absent from this session. True collaboration in CBT involves treating the patient as an active partner in identifying problems, generating solutions, and testing hypotheses. Instead, this therapist adopted a directive, advice-giving stance throughout.

Evidence of failed collaboration:

1. **Unilateral advice-giving**: The therapist repeatedly offered suggestions without soliciting the patient's input: "Maybe you could try to find a little moment for those puzzles," "Maybe you could try that too?" (herbal tea), "You could try getting a good night's sleep," "Maybe you should try the deep breathing." These are presented as therapist recommendations, not collaboratively developed plans.

2. **No shared problem formulation**: The therapist never asked Gabriela to help identify what she thought was driving her anxiety, what maintaining factors she noticed, or what she thought might help. A collaborative approach would have involved: "What do you think is making the worry so hard to control right now?" "What have you noticed about when the worry is worse or better?" "What ideas do you have about what might help?"

3. **No exploration of patient preferences or barriers**: The therapist did not ask whether Gabriela had tried these suggestions before, whether they fit her lifestyle, or what obstacles might prevent her from implementing them. For example, suggesting puzzles without exploring her concentration difficulties or asking whether she's tried and failed.

4. **Patient as passive recipient**: Throughout the session, Gabriela is positioned as someone receiving wisdom from the therapist rather than as an expert on her own experience. She responds politely but does not actively co-create the treatment plan.

5. **No collaborative empiricism**: CBT's signature approach—testing beliefs and assumptions together through behavioral experiments—is completely absent. The therapist could have asked: "You're worried the test results will be bad. What evidence do you have for that? What evidence against it? What would help us figure out what's actually likely?" Instead, the therapist simply reassured.
