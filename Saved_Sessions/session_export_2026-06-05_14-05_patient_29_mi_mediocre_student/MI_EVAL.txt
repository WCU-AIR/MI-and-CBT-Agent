# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal unconditional positive regard. Opens warmly but quickly shifts to directive problem-solving. Uses leading questions ("Would you say it's mostly because you're busy, or maybe you don't feel motivated?") that impose the clinician's framework rather than inviting patient's perspective. Fails to affirm patient's past success, willingness to engage, or inherent capacity to solve problems. Implicit message: "You need to do what I'm telling you to do" rather than genuine acceptance of patient's autonomy and worth.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Empathy is largely absent despite occasional surface-level reflections. Fails to capture emotional nuance or explore patient's ambivalence (e.g., "I wonder if I'm just making excuses"). When patient expresses worry about sustaining change ("That part worries me"), psychiatrist responds with advice rather than deepening understanding. Does not reflect patient's hesitation, discouragement, or the gap between past success and current reality. Misses opportunity to explore the 6/10 importance rating as a window into ambivalence.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains strategic focus on exercise throughout the session—topic does not wander and clinician consistently returns to exercise-related planning. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. Uses leading questions and prescriptive statements to establish direction. A higher score would require helping patient discover his own direction through evocative questioning such as "What would need to happen for exercise to feel more doable?"

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration is fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist operates in expert-driven, hierarchical mode. Repeatedly uses "should" language ("Do you think maybe you should try to find some time," "Maybe you should try to set small goals"). Imposes categories and frameworks rather than inviting patient's own understanding. When patient expresses doubt, psychiatrist pushes forward with more advice rather than exploring collaboratively. Final plan is entirely clinician-generated; patient did not co-create it. True collaboration would involve genuine partnership in problem-solving.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support is essentially absent. Psychiatrist actively undermines patient's sense of choice through repeated "should" statements framing change as obligation rather than option. When patient expresses uncertainty ("I'm not sure if I'd keep it up"), psychiatrist responds with pressure to override concern rather than validating it. Never explicitly affirms patient's right to choose or explores what patient genuinely wants versus what he thinks he should do. Closing statements ("Keep it up!") are subtly pressuring rather than autonomy-supportive.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation is virtually absent—the heart of MI is missing. Psychiatrist tells rather than asks, prescribes rather than draws out. Multiple missed opportunities to amplify change talk: patient's past success ("I used to work out pretty good"), recognition of benefits ("I know I'd feel better"), explicit desire ("I guess I do want to get back to it"), and ability statements ("Ten minutes a day might be doable"). Never asks open-ended evocative questions like "What would it mean to you to get back to exercising?" or "What do you miss about your old routine?" Approach is fundamentally "tell and sell."

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership is absent. Psychiatrist positions himself as the problem-solver and authority rather than as an equal partner. Frames himself as expert with solutions: "Do you think maybe you should try to find some time...? Have you tried setting specific times...? It would probably help if you planned it out." Uses language positioning psychiatrist as monitor/evaluator ("We can check in next time and see how it's going"). True partnership would invite patient's expertise about his own life, schedule, and what might work for him.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk is minimally cultivated despite multiple opportunities. Patient offers change talk that psychiatrist fails to amplify: past success, desire to feel better, explicit desire to return to exercise, ability statements about 10-minute workouts, and concrete thinking about timing (morning or lunch break). Psychiatrist does not use strategic questioning to evoke more change talk, does not reflect and amplify when it appears, and does not reinforce patient's own language about change. Psychiatrist's prescriptive language dominates, drowning out opportunities to strengthen patient's intrinsic motivation.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist does not reinforce sustain talk, which is appropriate. However, response to sustain talk is largely prescriptive rather than skillfully reframed. When patient expresses concern ("I'm not sure if I'd keep it up. That part worries me"), psychiatrist offers mild reframe but remains directive. Does not use double-sided reflections to validate ambivalence or strategic questioning to help patient work through concerns. Approach is to override sustain talk with advice rather than to skillfully explore and reframe it. Avoids direct confrontation, which prevents escalation.

### **Overall Session Score: 1.7/5**

## Summary Assessment

**MI Competence Level:** Poor – Significant deficiencies in core MI principles

**Strengths:**
- Maintains focus on target behavior (exercise) throughout session
- Opening is warm and inviting
- Does not directly confront or argue with patient's sustain talk
- Acknowledges patient's concerns appropriately
- Does not dismiss patient's past success or current struggles

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Fundamental MI violation:** Expert-driven stance undermines all other principles
- **Absent evocation:** Tells rather than asks; prescribes rather than draws out
- **Absent collaboration:** Solutions installed rather than co-created
- **Undermined autonomy:** Repeated "should" language frames change as obligation
- **Superficial empathy:** Factual reflections miss emotional nuance and ambivalence
- **Minimal acceptance:** Lacks genuine affirmation and unconditional positive regard

**Impact on Client Motivation:** **Likely to reduce intrinsic motivation.** While patient enters with change talk, psychiatrist's directive approach may trigger psychological reactance, externalize locus of control, and leave ambivalence unaddressed. Patient may comply out of deference to authority but lacks genuine intrinsic motivation to sustain change when obstacles arise.

**Likelihood of Follow-Through:** Moderate to low. Patient may attempt prescribed plan initially but without developed intrinsic motivation, sustain talk will likely resurface.

**Critical Recommendation:** Psychiatrist must fundamentally shift from expert-driven, directive stance to evocative, collaborative, client-centered stance. Formal MI training and supervised practice are strongly recommended.
