
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

3. Would this lead to measurable symptom improvement?
4. Can the patient apply learnings independently?

2. What was missing (specific techniques that should have been used)

This document outlines a structured framework for assessing therapist competence in Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT). 
It is used for training, supervision, and evaluation. Each item is scored on a 0–6 scale:

0 = Poor
2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish Systematic Agenda-Setting and Session Structure**
- **Action:** Begin every session with explicit collaborative agenda-setting. Identify 2-3 specific problems to address, establish time allocation, and explain how today's work connects to broader treatment goals.
- **Example:** "We have 50 minutes today. I know sleep is your top priority. I'd like to spend about 20 minutes understanding your sleep pattern in detail, 20 minutes developing some specific strategies to try, and 10 minutes planning what you'll work on between sessions. Does that sound okay? Is there anything else you'd like to make sure we cover?"
- **Practice:** Role-play agenda-setting with supervisor or colleague. Practice with 3-5 patients, recording sessions and reviewing for explicit agenda statements, time allocation, and collaborative language.

### 2. **Develop Competence in Guided Discovery and Socratic Questioning**
- **Action:** Replace advice-giving with systematic Socratic questioning. When patient identifies a problem, ask questions to help him reach insights rather than telling him solutions.
- **Example:** Instead of "Try thinking positive," ask: "When you wake up at 2 a.m., what specific thoughts go through your mind? Let's write those down. Now, for each thought, what evidence do you have that it's true? What evidence contradicts it? What would be a more balanced way to think about this?"
- **Practice:** Study Socratic questioning techniques (Padesky's "Thought Records" and "Downward Arrow" techniques). Practice with supervisor using role-plays. Record sessions and count instances of questions vs. advice-giving. Goal: 80% questions, 20% directive statements.

### 3. **Conduct Systematic Assessment of Core Cognitions and Behaviors**
- **Action:** When patient identifies a problem area (insomnia, worry, withdrawal), conduct detailed functional analysis. Identify specific thoughts, behaviors, and maintaining factors.
- **Example:** For sleep: "When you wake at 2 a.m., what thoughts go through your mind? How long do you lie there? Do you get out of bed or stay in bed? Do you check the time? What do you do to try to fall back asleep? How often does this happen? What do you notice about the relationship between your worries and your ability to sleep?"
- **Practice:** Develop assessment templates for insomnia, worry, and behavioral withdrawal. Use these templates with every patient presenting with these problems. Practice with supervisor until you can conduct thorough 15-minute assessments.

### 4. **Develop Coherent, Evidence-Based Treatment Strategy for Alcohol-Induced Depressive Disorder**
- **Action:** Create a written case conceptualization for this patient that explains: (1) how alcohol use maintains depression and sleep disruption; (2) how depressive symptoms maintain alcohol use; (3) specific interventions to address each maintaining factor; (4) expected timeline for improvement.
- **Example:** "Matthew's moderate alcohol use disorder has led to alcohol-induced depressive disorder. His heavy drinking disrupts sleep architecture and depletes neurotransmitters, causing depression. Depression increases anxiety and worry, which disrupts sleep further. Poor sleep increases irritability and reduces coping capacity, increasing relapse risk. Treatment will focus on: (1) psychoeducation about alcohol-depression-sleep relationships; (2) cognitive work on worry patterns maintaining insomnia; (3) behavioral activation to counter withdrawal; (4) sleep-specific interventions; (5) relapse prevention. Depressive symptoms typically improve within 2-4 weeks of sustained abstinence."
- **Practice:** Develop written case conceptualizations for 5 patients with alcohol-induced depression. Review with supervisor. Ensure each conceptualization includes: diagnosis, maintaining factors, specific interventions, and expected timeline.

### 5. **Eliminate Excessive Therapist Self-Disclosure**
- **Action:** Recognize that sharing personal experiences shifts focus from patient and violates therapeutic boundaries. When tempted to share personal experience, instead ask patient-focused questions.
- **Example:** Instead of "When I was stressed, I found positive thinking helped," say: "Many people find it challenging to shift their thinking when they're stressed. What do you think might help you with the worries that keep you awake?"
- **Practice:** Review recordings of your sessions. Count instances of self-disclosure. For each instance, identify what patient-focused question could have been asked instead. Discuss with supervisor. Goal: eliminate unnecessary self-disclosure.

### 6. **Implement Specific CBT Techniques for Insomnia**
- **Action:** Learn and apply evidence-based sleep interventions: sleep hygiene, stimulus control, sleep restriction, cognitive techniques for pre-sleep rumination, and sleep diaries.
- **Example:** "Let's create a sleep diary where you track: what time you go to bed, what time you wake up, how many times you wake, how long you're awake, what you're thinking about when you wake, and your mood the next day. This will help us understand your sleep pattern and test whether certain strategies help."
- **Practice:** Study CBT for Insomnia (CBT-I) protocols. Complete training in sleep assessment and intervention. Practice with supervisor using role-plays. Implement with 3-5 patients with insomnia, recording sessions for review.

### 7. **Develop Competence in Cognitive Restructuring for Worry and Rumination**
- **Action:** Learn systematic cognitive restructuring: identify specific thoughts, examine evidence, develop balanced alternatives, and test new thoughts through behavioral experiments.
- **Example:** "You mentioned worrying that your business will fail. Let's examine that thought carefully. What evidence do you have that it will fail? What evidence contradicts that? What would be a more balanced way to think about your business? How could we test whether this new thought is more accurate?"
- **Practice:** Study cognitive restructuring techniques (thought records, evidence examination, alternative thought generation). Practice with supervisor. Implement with 5 patients, recording sessions for review.

### 8. **Implement Behavioral Activation for Depression**
- **Action:** Systematically assess behavioral withdrawal, identify valued activities, and schedule specific behavioral activation tasks with clear rationale and tracking.
- **Example:** "I notice you've been spending most evenings on the couch. Research shows that when we're depressed, we withdraw from activities, which actually makes depression worse. Let's identify activities you used to enjoy or that align with your values—like time with your family, outdoor activities, or work projects. We'll schedule specific times to do these activities and track your mood before and after to see if they help."
- **Practice:** Study behavioral activation protocols. Develop activity schedules with 5 patients. Track mood ratings before and after activities. Review with supervisor.

### 9. **Establish Systematic Homework Assignment and Follow-Up**
- **Action:** Assign specific, measurable, time-limited homework tasks with clear rationale. Verify understanding and commitment. Review homework at every session.
- **Example:** "Here's what I'd like you to do between now and our next session: (1) Keep a sleep diary every night—write down what time you go to bed, wake up, and what you're thinking about when you wake. (2) When you wake at 2 a.m., instead of lying in bed worrying, get up and write down your worries on paper. This helps get them out of your head. (3) Go for a 15-minute walk three times this week, preferably in the morning. Track your mood before and after. Does this seem doable? What might get in the way?"
- **Practice:** Develop homework assignment templates. Practice assigning homework with supervisor. Review homework at beginning of every session. Track homework completion rates. Goal: 80%+ completion.

### 10. **Develop Competence in Relapse Prevention for Alcohol Use Disorder**
- **Action:** Learn relapse prevention: identify high-risk situations, develop coping strategies, plan for lapses, and build self-efficacy for maintaining sobriety.
- **Example:** "Let's think about situations where you might be tempted to drink. What are your high-risk situations? For each one, let's develop a specific coping strategy. For example, if stress about work triggers urges to drink, we could practice problem-solving about work or use the cognitive techniques we've learned to manage worry."
- **Practice:** Study relapse prevention protocols for alcohol use disorder. Develop relapse prevention plans with 5 patients. Review with supervisor.

### 11. **Increase Feedback-Seeking Throughout Sessions**
- **Action:** Systematically elicit patient feedback about session content, interventions, and therapeutic relationship. Check understanding regularly. Summarize at end of session.
- **Example:** "Does this make sense? How are you feeling about what we've discussed? Is there anything that didn't feel helpful or that you'd like to revisit? What's one thing you're taking away from today's session?"
- **Practice:** Add feedback-seeking to every session. Use checklist: (1) Check understanding of interventions, (2) Elicit reactions to suggestions, (3) Ask about session helpfulness, (4) Summarize main points, (5) Verify homework understanding. Record sessions and review for feedback-seeking frequency.

### 12. **Receive Supervision and Training in Evidence-Based CBT**
- **Action:** Engage in regular clinical supervision with a CBT-trained supervisor. Complete formal CBT training (certification program or intensive workshop). Review recordings of your sessions with supervisor.
- **Example:** Enroll in a CBT certification program, attend monthly supervision, and have supervisor review recordings of your sessions using the Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale.
- **Practice:** Commit to 12 weeks of weekly supervision focused on CBT competence. Complete at least one formal CBT training course. Review 2-3 session recordings per month with supervisor.
