
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

## 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a near-complete absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly uses language that is judgmental, dismissive, and delegitimizing of Maria's concerns. In the opening statement, the clinician frames the session as "what you need to do to improve," immediately positioning the psychiatrist as the authority and Maria as deficient—a stance fundamentally opposed to MI acceptance.

Throughout the session, the psychiatrist uses explicitly judgmental language: "you really have to quit being so stubborn," "stop making excuses," "stop overthinking this," and "enough with the hesitation." These statements communicate disapproval of Maria's perspective and frame her legitimate concerns about medication safety as character flaws rather than valid worries deserving exploration. When Maria expresses fear about long-term side effects, the psychiatrist responds with "There's no room for fear or hesitation here"—directly invalidating her emotional experience and communicating that her feelings are unwelcome in this space.

The psychiatrist never affirms Maria's strengths (e.g., her proactive research, her engagement in the session despite time pressure, her desire to understand her treatment). Instead, the clinician repeatedly pressures Maria with ultimatums: "If not, you're just putting yourself at serious risk" and "you're just risking your health unnecessarily." These are fear-based threats, not expressions of genuine care for Maria's wellbeing. True acceptance would involve recognizing that Maria's caution reflects a desire to protect herself—a value worth affirming—even while exploring whether her current approach serves her asthma management.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance. Judgmental language, dismissal of legitimate concerns, absence of affirmation, and repeated use of pressure and threats create an environment where Maria feels disrespected and unheard rather than supported.

## 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening, genuine effort to understand the client's perspective, and capturing emotional nuance. This session contains almost no evidence of these MI core competencies.

The psychiatrist asks factual questions ("Do you smoke?") but never reflects back Maria's underlying concerns or emotional experience. When Maria states, "I'm worried about what all that medicine might do to me in the long run," the psychiatrist responds with "you really should be using those inhalers regularly"—a complete non-sequitur that ignores her stated worry. When Maria says, "being told to just comply without understanding or addressing my concerns isn't helping," the psychiatrist responds with "you need to stop overthinking this"—again, dismissing rather than understanding her perspective.

A single moment of minimal empathy occurs when the psychiatrist says, "I understand you have concerns," but this is immediately negated by "but honestly, you need to stop overthinking this." The "but" erases the acknowledgment and signals that the psychiatrist does not actually accept her concerns as valid.

Maria explicitly states multiple times: "I need more respect for where I'm coming from," "maybe you need to hear me first," and "I need to feel heard and respected." These are direct requests for empathic understanding. The psychiatrist never responds to these requests. Instead, the clinician repeats the same directive message ("you must use your inhalers regularly") without ever attempting to understand *why* Maria is resistant, what her fears are rooted in, or what values underlie her caution.

Complex reflections—which would demonstrate deep empathic understanding—are entirely absent. The psychiatrist never reflects statements like: "It sounds like you care deeply about your health and want to make informed decisions about what goes into your body, and right now you're caught between that desire and fear about the medication itself" or "You're feeling overwhelmed by competing demands on your time and worried about becoming dependent on medication—both real concerns."

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no genuine empathic understanding. Factual questioning replaces reflective listening, concerns are dismissed rather than explored, and Maria's explicit requests to be heard are ignored. This represents a fundamental failure of MI empathy.

## 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI means maintaining strategic focus on a target behavior while remaining client-centered. This psychiatrist does maintain focus on the target behavior (regular inhaler use), but does so in a way that is entirely clinician-driven and coercive rather than collaborative.

The psychiatrist consistently redirects toward the same goal: "you really have to be consistent," "you must use your inhalers regularly," "you have to take this seriously," "will you start using your inhalers as prescribed starting today?" This represents repetitive, directive focus without strategic variation or exploration of Maria's ambivalence.

However, the psychiatrist fails to maintain direction in a client-centered way. True direction in MI involves helping the client clarify their own goals and values, then strategically guiding toward change. Here, the psychiatrist imposes direction through authority and pressure. The clinician never asks exploratory questions like: "What would need to happen for you to feel safer about using the inhaler?" or "What matters most to you about your health?" or "Tell me more about your concerns—what specifically worries you?"

The psychiatrist also fails to explore the *reasons* Maria might want to manage her asthma better. Maria hints at underlying motivation ("I'm worried," "I don't feel like you're hearing me when I say I'm scared") but the psychiatrist never evokes or develops this. A more strategically directed approach would explore: "You mentioned you're scared about side effects. At the same time, you came to this appointment and you've researched inhalers—that tells me you care about your health. What would managing your asthma better mean for your life?"

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior but in a coercive, clinician-driven manner rather than through strategic, client-centered exploration. Direction is present but poorly executed and inconsistent with MI principles.

## 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have valuable knowledge. This session demonstrates an entirely hierarchical, expert-driven stance with no genuine collaboration.

From the opening statement—"I'm here to help you get on track with your health, so we're going to focus on what you need to do to improve"—the psychiatrist positions themselves as the authority who will tell Maria what she needs to do. This is not collaboration; it is prescription.

These statements leave no room for Maria's input, perspective, or co-construction of solutions. The psychiatrist never asks, "What do you think might work for you?" or "How could we work together on this?" or "What would make this feel more manageable for you?"

When Maria attempts to assert her perspective ("I'm not making excuses—I'm just trying to do what makes sense to me"), the psychiatrist responds with "you really need to quit being so stubborn"—directly dismissing her contribution rather than incorporating it into the conversation. When Maria says she needs to feel safe and respected, the psychiatrist responds with pressure and ultimatums rather than collaborative problem-solving.

The psychiatrist asks questions, but they are interrogative rather than collaborative: "Why haven't you been consistent from the start?" and "Are you going to commit to using your inhalers as prescribed starting today?" These are yes/no questions that demand compliance, not open questions that invite partnership.

A collaborative approach would sound like: "I hear that you're concerned about safety and that you're busy. Those are real challenges. What if we worked together to find a way to use your inhaler that feels safer to you and fits your schedule? What would that look like?"

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates zero collaboration. An expert-driven, hierarchical stance dominates throughout, with no genuine power-sharing, co-construction of solutions, or incorporation of Maria's perspective into the session direction.

## 5. Autonomy Support
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Autonomy support means respecting the client's right to choose and emphasizing their ownership of the change process. This session is characterized by pressure, coercion, and explicit denial of Maria's autonomy.

The psychiatrist repeatedly uses language that minimizes choice: "you really have to," "you must," "you can't just pick and choose," "the only way," and "you have to take responsibility." These statements communicate that Maria has no choice—that compliance is mandatory, not optional.

Most egregiously, the psychiatrist uses fear-based ultimatums to pressure compliance: "If not, you're just putting yourself at serious risk" (stated multiple times) and "you're just risking your health unnecessarily." These are coercive tactics designed to frighten Maria into compliance, not to support her autonomy.

When Maria explicitly states her choice ("No, I'm not ready to commit right now"), the psychiatrist responds with "you really need to stop overthinking this" and repeats the demand. This is a direct override of Maria's stated autonomy.

MI autonomy support would sound like: "This is ultimately your decision. I can share what the research shows about inhaler safety and effectiveness, but you get to decide what feels right for you. What would help you feel more confident about this choice?" This communicates respect for Maria's right to choose while still providing information.

Instead, the psychiatrist communicates: "You don't have a choice. You must do this or face serious consequences." This is the opposite of autonomy support and is likely to increase Maria's resistance (which is exactly what happens in the transcript—she becomes more entrenched in her position).

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no autonomy support. Repeated use of coercive language, fear-based ultimatums, and explicit pressure to comply directly violate Maria's autonomy and increase her defensiveness.

## 6. Evocation

# Recommendations

### 1. **CRITICAL: Shift from Directive to Evocative Stance**

**Action:** Fundamentally reframe your role from "expert who tells the client what to do" to "collaborative partner who helps the client discover their own reasons for change."

**Example:** Instead of "You really have to use your inhalers regularly," ask: "I'm curious—you mentioned you've researched inhalers and you came to this appointment. That tells me you care about your health. What would managing your asthma better mean for your life?"

**Practice:** 
- Before each session, remind yourself: "My job is to ask, not to tell. My job is to evoke, not to impose."
- Record yourself in practice sessions and count how many times you use "you have to/must/need to" vs. open-ended questions. Goal: 80% questions, 20% statements.
- Practice asking "Tell me more about..." instead of making statements.

---

### 2. **CRITICAL: Replace Confrontation with Reflection**

**Action:** When the client expresses sustain talk or resistance, reflect it back with empathy rather than confronting it directly.

**Example:** 
- **Instead of:** "You need to stop overthinking this. The facts are clear."
- **Use:** "It sounds like you've done a lot of research and you want to make an informed decision about what goes into your body. That makes sense. Tell me more about what specifically worries you about the inhalers."

**Practice:**
- When you feel the urge to say "but you're wrong" or "you need to stop," pause and ask yourself: "What is the client really saying? What value or concern is underneath this?"
- Practice double-sided reflections: "On one hand, you're concerned about side effects. On the other hand, you recognize your asthma needs to be managed. Both of those things are true."
- Record yourself and listen for confrontational language. Replace it with reflective language.

---

### 3. **CRITICAL: Develop Genuine Empathic Understanding**

**Action:** Make reflective listening your primary tool. Before giving any information or advice, demonstrate that you understand the client's perspective.

**Example:**
- **Listen to:** "I'm worried about what all that medicine might do to me in the long run."
- **Reflect:** "You care about your long-term health and you want to make sure any treatment you use is truly safe for you over time. That's important."
- **Then explore:** "What specifically worries you most? What have you read that concerns you?"

**Practice:**
- In every client interaction, aim for at least 3-5 reflections before making any statements about treatment.
- Use the formula: "It sounds like... [emotion] because [value/concern]."
- Ask yourself: "Do I genuinely understand why this client is resistant? Can I articulate their perspective in a way they would recognize as accurate?"

---

### 4. **CRITICAL: Explore and Validate Legitimate Concerns Rather Than Dismissing Them**

**Action:** Treat client concerns (medication safety, time constraints, etc.) as valid barriers to explore, not as excuses to dismiss.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** "You need to stop overthinking this."
- **Use:** "You're concerned about side effects and you're busy. Those are real challenges. Let's talk about them. What specifically worries you about the inhaler? And what would make managing it feel more doable with your schedule?"

**Practice:**
- When a client expresses a concern, ask: "Tell me more about that" rather than "That's not a real concern."
- Research the actual evidence about client concerns (e.g., inhaler side effects, dependency potential) so you can address them with accurate information.
- Normalize concerns: "Many people worry about medication side effects. That's a common concern."

---

### 5. **CRITICAL: Support Autonomy Rather Than Pressuring Compliance**

**Action:** Explicitly communicate that the client has the right to choose, while providing information and exploring their ambivalence.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** "You have to use your inhalers as prescribed or you're putting yourself at serious risk."
- **Use:** "This is ultimately your decision. I can share what the research shows about inhaler safety and how they work, but you get to decide what feels right for you. What would help you feel more confident about this choice?"

**Practice:**
- Replace "you have to/must/need to" with "you could consider," "one option is," "some people find it helpful to..."
- When the client refuses, respond with curiosity rather than pressure: "I hear you're not ready right now. What would need to happen for you to feel ready?"
- Affirm their autonomy: "I respect that this is your decision. I'm here to support you, whatever you decide."

---

### 6. **HIGH PRIORITY: Ask Open-Ended Questions to Evoke Change Talk**

**Action:** Develop a repertoire of questions designed to draw out the client's own reasons for change, confidence, and commitment.

**Example Questions:**
- "What concerns you most about your asthma if it's not managed?"
- "What would it mean for your life if you could breathe easier?"
- "What have you tried in the past that's worked for you?"
- "What would make you feel more confident about using the inhaler?"
- "If you did decide to use the inhaler regularly, what would be the first small step?"
- "What's most important to you about your health?"

**Practice:**
- Create a list of 10-15 evocative questions and practice using them in role-plays.
- In each session, aim to ask at least 5 open-ended questions before making any directive statements.
- When the client gives an answer, follow up with "Tell me more about that" rather than moving to the next question.

---

### 7. **HIGH PRIORITY: Recognize and Reinforce Change Talk**

**Action:** When the client expresses any language favoring change (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment), explicitly acknowledge and build on it.

**Example:**
- **Client says:** "I want to take care of myself."
- **Recognize it:** "That's important to you—taking care of your health. Tell me more about what that means to you."
- **Build on it:** "So managing your asthma is part of taking care of yourself. What would that look like?"

**Practice:**
- Listen for change talk: statements about desire ("I want to..."), ability ("I could..."), reasons ("It would help because..."), need ("I need to..."), and commitment ("I will...").
- When you hear it, pause and reflect it back: "So you're saying you want to feel better. That's important."
- Ask follow-up questions to deepen it: "What else? What would that mean for you?"

---

### 8. **HIGH PRIORITY: Develop Skill in Handling Sustain Talk Without Confrontation**

**Action:** When the client expresses reasons not to change, use strategic techniques (double-sided reflection, reframing, redirection) rather than direct confrontation.

**Example:**
- **Client says:** "I'm worried about getting hooked on inhalers."
- **Instead of confronting:** "That's not true. You won't get hooked."
- **Use double-sided reflection:** "You're concerned about becoming dependent on medication, and at the same time, you recognize your asthma needs to be managed. Both of those things make sense. Let me share what the research actually shows about inhaler dependency..."
- **Then redirect:** "What would help you feel more confident that the inhaler is safe?"

**Practice:**
- When you hear sustain talk, pause before responding. Ask yourself: "What is the underlying concern? How can I acknowledge it without confronting it?"
- Practice saying: "That's an important concern" before providing information.
- Use reframing: "Your caution shows you care about your health. The question is: how can we address your concerns so you feel safe?"

---

### 9. **MEDIUM PRIORITY: Provide Information in a Collaborative Way**

**Action:** When you do provide medical information, do so in a way that invites dialogue rather than imposing expertise.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** "The facts are clear: you must use your inhalers regularly or face serious health problems."
- **Use:** "I'd like to share what the research shows about how inhalers work and their safety profile. Then we can talk about what questions you have. Does that sound okay?"

**Practice:**
- Ask permission before providing information: "Would it be helpful if I shared some information about inhaler safety?"
- Provide information, then ask: "What questions do you have about that?"
- Invite dialogue: "How does that fit with what you've read or what you're concerned about?"

---

### 10. **MEDIUM PRIORITY: Develop Collaborative Problem-Solving Skills**

**Action:** When barriers emerge (time constraints, safety concerns), work with the client to problem-solve rather than dismissing the barriers.

**Example:**
- **Client says:** "I don't have time to manage this constantly."
- **Collaborate:** "I hear that time is tight for you. Let's think about this together. What if we found a way to make this simpler? What would work with your schedule? What if you used the inhaler at the same time you do something else—like with your morning coffee?"

**Practice:**
- When a barrier emerges, ask: "What would make this feel more doable for you?"
- Brainstorm together: "What are some ideas you have? What have you tried before that worked?"
- Emphasize collaboration: "Let's figure this out together."

---

### 11. **FOUNDATIONAL: Complete Formal MI Training**

**Action:** This session suggests significant gaps in MI knowledge and skill. Formal training is essential.

**Recommendation:**
- Enroll in a certified Motivational Interviewing training program (2-3 days minimum).
- Seek ongoing MI supervision with an MI-certified trainer.
- Read foundational MI texts: "Motivational Interviewing: Helping People Change" (Miller & Rollnick, 3rd edition).
- Practice MI in role-plays with feedback from a trained supervisor.

**Practice:**
- Commit to 6-12 months of deliberate practice with feedback.
- Record sessions and review them with a supervisor.
- Participate in MI consultation groups.

### 12. **FOUNDATIONAL: Examine Your Beliefs About Client Resistance**

**Action:** Reflect on your underlying beliefs about why clients don't follow medical advice. This shapes your approach.

**Reflection Questions:**
- Do you believe clients are "stubborn" or "in denial"? (This leads to confrontation.)
- Or do you believe clients have legitimate concerns and barriers that deserve exploration? (This leads to evocation.)
- Do you believe your job is to convince clients to change? (This leads to pressure.)
- Or do you believe your job is to help clients discover their own reasons for change? (This leads to collaboration.)

**Practice:**
- Before each session, remind yourself: "This client is not resistant because they're stubborn. They're resistant because they have concerns, barriers, or ambivalence that make sense from their perspective. My job is to understand and explore that, not to overcome it."
- When you feel frustrated with a client, pause and ask: "What is this client trying to tell me? What concern or value is underneath their resistance?"
