# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Minimal acceptance demonstrated throughout majority of session. Psychiatrist uses judgmental language ("no excuse," "you have to") and communicates conditional regard—patient only valued if compliant. Opening exchanges explicitly dismiss patient's lived experience. Only in fourth exchange does genuine unconditional positive regard emerge with apology: "I realize now that pushing you to make immediate changes without understanding your experience and feelings was not helpful." This represents approximately 15% of session; acceptance absent for 85% of interaction.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Empathy virtually absent. Psychiatrist fails to reflect or validate patient's emotional experience. When patient states "it feels overwhelming," psychiatrist responds with pseudo-empathy ("I understand, but...") followed by negation and pressure. No exploration of why overwhelm exists, what barriers patient faces, or what concerns reveal about values. No complex reflections capturing emotional nuance. Only in fourth exchange does genuine empathic understanding emerge: "Could you tell me more about what you feel ready to try right now?" This represents one exchange out of seven total exchanges.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Direction present but largely authoritarian and imposed rather than collaborative. Psychiatrist maintains rigid focus on specific behaviors (30 minutes daily exercise, vegetables at every meal, starting tomorrow) presented as non-negotiable: "This is not optional." Does not explore patient's goals, readiness, or barriers. When patient proposes smaller steps, psychiatrist explicitly rejects: "Small steps won't cut it if you want real change." Consistency of focus exists but lacks strategic collaboration. Final exchanges show improvement with collaborative goal-setting: "What feels more manageable to you?"

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Collaboration virtually absent for majority of session. Psychiatrist explicitly positions self as sole expert: "I want to make sure you follow my advice because that's how you'll get better." Uses unilateral directives ("you have to," "you must," "this is not optional") that exclude patient from decision-making. Patient's attempts to collaborate are rejected: when patient proposes smaller steps, psychiatrist responds with intensified pressure rather than exploration. Only in fourth exchange does genuine collaboration emerge with explicit affirmation: "Your perspective is really valuable in this process." Patient then contributes meaningfully to goal-setting.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Autonomy support almost entirely absent in first three exchanges. Psychiatrist repeatedly communicates patient has no choice: "This is not optional," "you have to," "you must." Uses coercive language and fear-based pressure: "If you don't, the anxiety will only get worse and you'll regret it later." Denies patient agency and frames compliance as mandatory. Only in fourth exchange does autonomy support emerge: "Remember, this is your journey, and we'll go at a pace that feels right for you." Patient's relief at this shift is palpable: "knowing I don't have to do it perfectly right away helps."

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1.5/5
**Comments:** Evocation largely absent. Psychiatrist imposes motivations and reasons for change without exploring what matters to patient. Does not ask: "What would feeling better look like to you?" "What matters most to you?" "What have you noticed about how exercise affects your mood?" Patient offers spontaneous change talk ("I want to feel better," "I want to improve") but psychiatrist does not amplify or reinforce through follow-up questions. Patient's past success (work presentation) never explored as resource. Only in fourth exchange does evocation begin through open questioning: "Could you tell me more about what you feel ready to try right now?" Patient then articulates self-generated goals.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Partnership virtually absent for majority of session. Psychiatrist positions self as sole expert and patient as passive recipient of directives. Language reinforces hierarchy: "you have to," "you must," "there's no excuse," "you need to." When patient asserts expertise about what feels manageable, psychiatrist dismisses: "Small steps won't cut it." Fails to acknowledge patient's expertise about own anxiety, barriers, and capacity. Only in fourth exchange does genuine partnership emerge with apology and explicit affirmation: "Your perspective is really valuable in this process." Psychiatrist then asks open questions and incorporates patient's ideas into plan.

When patient expresses ambivalence and concerns, psychiatrist responds with pressure and dismissal rather than exploring to strengthen change side. ### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Sustain talk handled poorly for majority of session. Patient expresses legitimate concerns ("I'm scared that if I can't keep these big changes, I'll just feel worse about myself") and psychiatrist intensifies pressure rather than softening through validation and exploration. Psychiatrist dismisses concerns as excuses: "you have to stop making excuses." Does not use double-sided reflections or explore what concerns reveal about patient's values. Psychiatrist actually reinforces patient's fear of failure rather than softening it. Only in fourth exchange does approach shift to less confrontational stance with acknowledgment: "I realize now that pushing you to make immediate changes without understanding your experience was not helpful." However, sophisticated MI techniques for softening sustain talk not demonstrated.

## Overall Session Assessment

**Overall Score: 1.8/5**

**Summary:**

This session represents a **severe departure from Motivational Interviewing principles** for approximately 85% of its duration, followed by a dramatic and positive shift in the final exchanges. The psychiatrist demonstrates **poor to absent competence** across nearly all MI domains in the opening three exchanges.

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Fundamentally violates MI spirit of partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation
- Explicitly communicates patient has no choice ("this is not optional," "you have to," "you must")
- Dismisses patient's legitimate concerns as "excuses"
- Uses coercive and fear-based pressure to enforce compliance
- Positions self as sole expert; patient as passive recipient of directives
- Fails to evoke or amplify patient's own motivations and resources

**Positive Development:**
- Demonstrates self-awareness and capacity to recognize ineffective approach
- Genuine apology in fourth exchange shows willingness to change course
- Shift to collaborative, autonomy-supportive stance results in immediate improvement in patient engagement
- Patient's relief and increased commitment demonstrate that resistance was to psychiatrist's approach, not to change itself

**Critical Finding:**
Patient's responses reveal that her resistance stems not from lack of motivation or unwillingness to change, but from the psychiatrist's non-MI-consistent approach. When approach shifts to MI principles, patient immediately becomes more engaged, hopeful, and committed: "That sounds a lot better, actually...I'm still nervous, but this feels like something I can try without getting overwhelmed right away."

**Recommendation:**
**This psychiatrist requires comprehensive MI training, ongoing clinical supervision, and systematic feedback to develop reliable MI competence.** While capacity for change is present, current level of MI skill is inadequate for independent practice. The damage done in opening exchanges is substantial; a truly skilled MI clinician would have avoided this harm entirely by using MI-consistent approaches from the beginning.
