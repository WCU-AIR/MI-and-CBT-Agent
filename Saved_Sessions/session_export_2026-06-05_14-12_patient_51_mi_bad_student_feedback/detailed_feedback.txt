
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

### 1. Acceptance

### 2. Empathy

### 3. Direction

True direction in MI would involve: (1) exploring what the client wants to change, (2) understanding her readiness and confidence, (3) collaboratively identifying realistic goals, and (4) strategically guiding toward those goals while maintaining client ownership.

### 4. Collaboration

# Recommendations

### 1. **Foundational MI Training and Competency Development**
- **Action:** Engage in comprehensive MI training that emphasizes the MI spirit (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation) as the foundation for all technique use. This is not a technical skill to be added to existing practice; it requires a fundamental shift in stance and worldview.
- **Example:** Before the session, the psychiatrist should have asked herself: "What does this patient want? What are her concerns? What does she already know about her own situation? How can I help her access her own motivation rather than imposing mine?" This reflective stance is the foundation of MI.
- **Practice:** Engage in role-play practice with feedback focused on MI spirit, not just technique. Record sessions and review them with an MI-trained supervisor. Attend MI workshops and practice groups. Read MI literature to deepen understanding of the theoretical foundation.

### 2. **Eliminate Coercive and Pressuring Language**
- **Action:** Systematically remove language that communicates that the patient has no choice or that compliance is mandatory. Replace "you have to," "you must," "this is not optional" with language that respects autonomy and invites collaboration.
- **Example:** Instead of *"You really must start exercising regularly. There's no excuse not to,"* say: *"I'm curious about exercise. What's your sense of how it might help with your anxiety? What would feel possible to you right now?"* Or: *"Some people find that movement helps with anxiety. Have you noticed that? What do you think?"*
- **Practice:** Before sessions, review your opening statements and identify any coercive language. Practice reframing these statements to be autonomy-supportive. Ask a colleague to listen for coercive language and give you feedback.

### 3. **Develop Genuine Empathic Listening and Complex Reflections**
- **Action:** Practice reflective listening that goes beyond surface-level acknowledgment to capture emotional nuance and meaning. Use complex reflections that deepen the patient's insight and demonstrate that you truly understand their experience.
- **Example:** When the patient says *"I'm scared that if I can't keep these big changes, I'll just feel worse about myself,"* respond with: *"So the fear isn't just about failing the goal itself—it's that not meeting it would reinforce the self-doubt you're already struggling with. It would feel like more evidence that you can't do this. That's a really important concern, and it makes sense that you'd want to protect yourself from that."* This reflection captures the emotional meaning and validates the concern.
- **Practice:** In sessions, pause after the patient speaks and ask yourself: "What is the emotion here? What does this reveal about what matters to them? What are they afraid of?" Then reflect back what you hear, not just the content but the feeling and meaning. Record sessions and review your reflections with a supervisor.

### 4. **Ask Open Questions to Evoke the Patient's Own Motivations and Resources**
- **Action:** Replace telling with asking. Instead of telling the patient why she should change or what she should do, ask questions that help her discover her own reasons for change and her own resources.
- **Example:** Instead of *"You'll feel so much better if you just stick to a healthy plan,"* ask: *"What would feeling better look like to you? How would your life be different? What would that make possible?"* Or: *"Tell me about a time when you've overcome anxiety or done something difficult despite being scared. What did you do? What did you learn about yourself?"*
- **Practice:** Before sessions, prepare open questions that invite the patient to explore their own motivations, values, and resources. During sessions, practice asking these questions and then listening deeply to the answers. Resist the urge to jump in with advice or direction. Let the patient's own wisdom emerge.

### 5. **Explore Ambivalence Rather Than Dismiss It**
- **Action:** When the patient expresses concerns, hesitation, or sustain talk, respond with curiosity and validation rather than pressure and dismissal. Use double-sided reflections that acknowledge both the desire for change and the concerns about change.
- **Example:** When the patient says *"it feels overwhelming,"* respond with: *"On one hand, you want to feel better and you're willing to try. On the other hand, you're worried that if you set a big goal and can't keep it, you'll feel worse about yourself. Both of those things make sense. Let's talk about what would feel more manageable and sustainable for you."* This validates both sides of the ambivalence and invites collaborative problem-solving.
- **Practice:** In sessions, when you hear sustain talk or ambivalence, pause and ask yourself: "What is this person afraid of? What does this concern reveal about what matters to them?" Then reflect back both sides of the ambivalence and invite exploration. Avoid the urge to argue for change or dismiss concerns.

### 6. **Establish True Collaboration and Partnership from the Beginning**
- **Action:** Explicitly position the patient as the expert on their own life and the collaborator in the change process. Use inclusive language ("we," "let's") and genuinely invite the patient's input into goal-setting and planning.
- **Example:** Opening statement: *"I'm glad you're here. I want to understand what brings you in and what you're hoping to work on. You know yourself best, and I'm here to support you in figuring out what might help. What's on your mind?"* Then listen and ask follow-up questions. When discussing goals, say: *"What do you think would be realistic and sustainable for you? What have you tried before? What worked? What didn't? Let's build on what you know about yourself."*
- **Practice:** In sessions, practice using collaborative language and genuinely incorporating the patient's ideas into the plan. Ask yourself: "Am I telling or asking? Am I deciding or collaborating? Is the patient's voice shaping this session?" Record sessions and review with a supervisor.

### 7. **Amplify and Reinforce Change Talk**
- **Action:** When the patient expresses desire, ability, reasons, or commitment to change, respond in ways that strengthen and deepen this change talk. Ask follow-up questions, affirm the statement, and help the patient elaborate on their own motivations.
- **Example:** When the patient says *"I want to feel better,"* respond with: *"Tell me more about that. What would feeling better look like? How would your life be different? What would that make possible for you?"* Then affirm: *"It sounds like feeling better and having that weight lifted off your shoulders is really important to you. That's a powerful motivation."*
- **Practice:** In sessions, listen for change talk and respond with genuine curiosity and affirmation. Ask follow-up questions that help the patient elaborate. Avoid moving on too quickly. Let the patient's own motivation build through exploration.

### 8. **Develop Skill in Softening Sustain Talk Without Confrontation**
- **Action:** When the patient expresses concerns or reasons against change, respond with validation and exploration rather than pressure or confrontation. Use techniques like double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic questioning to gently redirect toward change talk without dismissing the concern.
- **Example:** When the patient says *"I've been putting it off for years because of anxiety,"* respond with: *"So anxiety has been a real barrier for you. That makes sense. And at the same time, I'm hearing that you're here now and you're willing to try. What's different now? What's giving you hope that you might be able to move forward?"* This validates the barrier and redirects toward change talk.
- **Practice:** In sessions, practice responding to sustain talk with validation and curiosity rather than pressure. Use double-sided reflections. Ask strategic questions that redirect toward change talk. Record sessions and review with a supervisor.

### 9. **Conduct a Personal Reflection on Stance and Assumptions**
- **Action:** Reflect on your own assumptions about patients, change, and your role as a clinician. Do you believe patients are inherently motivated to change, or do you believe they need to be pushed? Do you see yourself as the expert who knows what's best, or as a collaborator who helps patients access their own wisdom? These fundamental beliefs shape your clinical approach.
- **Example:** Ask yourself: "Why did I respond to this patient's hesitation with pressure rather than curiosity? What was I afraid of? What assumptions was I making about her?" Honest self-reflection can reveal patterns that undermine MI-consistent practice.
- **Practice:** Engage in regular supervision or consultation focused on your own clinical stance. Discuss cases where you felt frustrated or where the patient seemed resistant. Explore your own beliefs about change and your role. Consider personal therapy to explore any patterns that might interfere with genuine collaboration and acceptance.

### 10. **Implement Systematic Feedback and Accountability**
- **Action:** Establish a system for receiving regular feedback on your MI practice. This could include recording sessions and reviewing them with an MI-trained supervisor, participating in MI consultation groups, or using validated MI assessment tools (e.g., MITI, MISC).
- **Example:** Record this session and review it with an MI-trained supervisor. Identify specific moments where you deviated from MI principles and discuss alternative responses. Practice these alternative responses in role-play.
- **Practice:** Commit to ongoing supervision or consultation. Set specific goals for MI skill development. Track your progress over time. Celebrate improvements and continue to identify areas for growth.
