
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **Develop a Coherent, Evidence-Based Treatment Strategy for Eating Disorders**
- **Action:** Learn and apply CBT-E (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Eating Disorders), the gold standard for bulimia nervosa. This includes: psychoeducation about the disorder, behavioral experiments, exposure and response prevention, cognitive restructuring of eating disorder beliefs, and relapse prevention.
- **Example:** In the next session, explain to the patient: "Bulimia is maintained by a cycle. You restrict food during the day (skipping breakfast and lunch), which creates intense hunger and urges to binge. When you binge, you feel guilty and afraid of weight gain, so you purge. This temporarily relieves anxiety but reinforces the cycle. We're going to break this cycle by: (1) establishing regular eating patterns, (2) resisting the urge to purge (which teaches your brain that you can tolerate the anxiety), and (3) changing the thoughts that keep you stuck."
- **Practice:** Complete CBT-E training (e.g., through the Center for Eating Disorders or formal supervision). Review case conceptualization for eating disorders. Practice articulating a clear treatment plan in supervision before each session.

### 2. **Integrate Treatment for Comorbid Depression**
- **Action:** Recognize that depression and eating disorder are interrelated. Use behavioral activation as a first-line intervention for depression, which will also reduce binge-purge episodes by increasing engagement in valued activities and improving mood.
- **Example:** "I notice you're feeling low energy and isolated, which makes it harder to resist urges. Let's work on increasing activities that give you a sense of accomplishment or pleasure. This might include: going for a walk, calling a friend, working on a hobby, or spending time in nature. As your mood improves, you'll likely find it easier to manage the eating urges."
- **Practice:** In supervision, develop integrated case conceptualizations that address both the eating disorder and depression. Practice identifying behavioral activation targets specific to each patient.

### 3. **Conduct Functional Analysis of Binge-Purge Episodes**
- **Action:** Before proposing interventions, systematically analyze a specific binge-purge episode to understand triggers, thoughts, feelings, and consequences. This informs tailored interventions.
- **Example:** "Let's look at last night's episode in detail. What time did it start? What were you doing before? What were you thinking and feeling? What triggered the urge? What did you eat? How did you feel afterward? What happened after you purged?" Map this on a functional analysis worksheet to identify the maintaining factors.
- **Practice:** In every session, conduct a detailed functional analysis of at least one binge-purge episode. Use this to guide intervention selection and help the patient understand their own patterns.

### 4. **Address Dietary Restriction Directly**
- **Action:** Recognize that restriction is a primary maintaining factor in bulimia. Work collaboratively with the patient to establish regular eating patterns (e.g., three meals + snacks) to reduce hunger and urges to binge.
- **Example:** "One reason you're having such strong urges at night is that you're skipping breakfast and lunch. This creates intense hunger. Let's work on eating breakfast and lunch regularly. I know this might feel scary because of concerns about weight, but research shows that regular eating actually reduces binge episodes. Would you be willing to try eating a small breakfast this week and see what happens?"
- **Practice:** Develop a structured eating plan collaboratively with the patient. Use behavioral experiments to test predictions (e.g., "If I eat breakfast, will I binge more?" Answer: typically no—regular eating reduces binges). Track eating patterns and binge-purge frequency to demonstrate the relationship.

### 5. **Use Guided Discovery Instead of Directive Advice**
- **Action:** Replace "You should..." and "You could..." with Socratic questions that help the patient generate insights and solutions.
- **Example:** Instead of "You should practice doing things that keep you engaged," ask: "You mentioned that urges are weaker when you're with friends or busy with work. What do you think is different about those situations? How could you create that sense of engagement or connection on nights when you're alone? What would that look like?"
- **Practice:** In supervision, record sessions and count the ratio of therapist questions to therapist statements. Aim for at least 60% questions. Practice Socratic questioning techniques (asking about evidence, alternatives, consequences, predictions).

### 6. **Implement Exposure and Response Prevention for Purging**
- **Action:** Introduce the concept that resisting purging after a binge is a critical intervention. This teaches the patient that anxiety decreases naturally over time and that purging is not necessary.
- **Example:** "One of the most important things we can do is help you resist the urge to purge after a binge. I know this sounds scary, but research shows that if you sit with the anxiety for 20-30 minutes without purging, the anxiety will naturally decrease. This teaches your brain that you can tolerate the discomfort. We'll start small and build up gradually."
- **Practice:** Introduce this concept gradually, with psychoeducation and collaborative planning. Start with a binge episode where the patient is willing to resist purging for a short period (e.g., 10 minutes). Debrief the experience and build from there.

### 7. **Establish Clear, Collaborative Agenda and Goals**
- **Action:** At the beginning of each session, collaboratively set an agenda with specific, prioritized topics and time allocation. Ensure the patient's priorities are included.
- **Example:** "Today we have about 50 minutes. I'd like to check in on how the eating plan went this week, and I also want to talk about the thoughts that come up when you feel the urge to binge. Is there anything else you'd like to cover? How should we prioritize these?"
- **Practice:** Use an agenda-setting worksheet at the start of each session. Practice asking the patient what they want to focus on and why. Ensure the agenda reflects both the patient's and therapist's priorities.

### 8. **Elicit and Respond to Feedback Throughout the Session**
- **Action:** Systematically check for understanding, solicit reactions to interventions, and assess session helpfulness. This ensures the patient feels heard and allows for real-time adjustment.
- **Example:** "Does that make sense?" "How does that land for you?" "What was most helpful today?" "What didn't make sense?" "What concerns do you have about trying this?"
- **Practice:** Identify 3-4 moments in each session to elicit feedback. Record sessions and review to ensure feedback is genuine (not leading) and that you respond to the patient's input.

### 9. **Design Specific, Rationale-Based Homework with Baseline Monitoring**
- **Action:** Assign homework that is specific, has clear rationale, includes baseline monitoring, and anticipates obstacles. Ensure the patient understands and commits.
- **Example:** "This week, I'd like you to keep a brief log of each time you have an urge to binge. - **Example:** "I want to make sure you're safe. Purging can cause serious medical problems, including heart problems and damage to your teeth and throat. ### 12. **Develop Competence in Eating Disorder-Specific Cognitive Restructuring**
- **Action:** Learn to identify and challenge eating disorder-specific beliefs (e.g., "I must be thin to be worthy," "One binge means I've failed," "I can't tolerate weight gain"). Use evidence-based cognitive restructuring, not just suggestion.
