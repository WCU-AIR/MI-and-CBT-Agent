
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda

**Explanation:**

No agenda was established at any point in this session. The therapist opened with a casual greeting ("Hey there! How's it going today?") but never collaboratively identified, prioritized, or structured specific treatment targets. For a patient with Borderline Personality Disorder presenting with chronic suicidal ideation, multiple psychiatric hospitalizations, social isolation, and treatment-resistant depression, the absence of a structured agenda is a critical clinical failure.

For a patient with BPD who has failed multiple prior treatments, establishing clear, achievable session goals and collaboratively prioritizing problems is essential for engagement and demonstrating that this treatment will be different. **Summary:**

The therapist completely failed to establish a session agenda, leaving the session without structure, direction, or explicit goals. This is a fundamental CBT failure that undermines all subsequent interventions.

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist displayed surface-level warmth and friendliness but demonstrated significant interpersonal problems that are potentially harmful with a BPD patient.

**Problematic elements:**

2. **Casual, insufficiently professional tone:** Phrases like "Hey there! How's it going today?" and "You've got this!" feel more like peer conversation than professional therapeutic engagement. 2. **Behavioral Activation:** Systematically scheduling activities to counteract depression and build positive reinforcement. For example:
   - Identifying activities the patient used to enjoy

3. **Thought Records:** Structured worksheets to help the patient identify situations, automatic thoughts, emotions, and alternative thoughts. For example:
   - Situation: "Thinking about calling a friend"
   - Automatic thought: "They'll judge me or get annoyed"

4. **Behavioral Experiments:** Testing beliefs through real-world experiments. For example:
   - Belief: "If I go outside, people will judge me"
   - Experiment: Go outside for 10 minutes and observe what actually happens
   - Review: What did you notice? Did people judge you? What does this tell you about your belief?

5. **Exposure:** Gradually facing feared situations to reduce anxiety. For example:
   - Fear: Social contact will lead to rejection

   - Warning signs that a crisis is developing

   - People and social settings that provide distraction

7. **Emotion Regulation Skills:** Teaching specific skills for managing intense emotions. For example:
   - Distress tolerance skills (TIPP technique, grounding)

8. **Schema Work:** Identifying and modifying core schemas. For example:

- Patient: [Develops alternative thought]

### 7. **Implement Thought Records for Identifying and Challenging Automatic Thoughts**

### 9. **Teach Emotion Regulation and Distress Tolerance Skills**

**Action:** Teach specific skills for managing intense emotions and tolerating distress without self-harm or suicidal behavior.

**Emotion regulation skills:**
- **Opposite action:** Do the opposite of what the emotion urges you to do (e.g., if sadness urges you to isolate, reach out to someone instead)

**Distress tolerance skills:**
- **TIPP technique:** Temperature (splash cold water on face), Intense exercise, Paced breathing, Paired muscle relaxation
- **Grounding:** 5 senses technique (name 5 things you see, 4 you hear, 3 you feel, 2 you smell, 1 you taste)
- **Distraction:** ACCEPTS (Activities, Contribute, Comparisons, Emotions, Pushing away, Thoughts, Sensations)

**Example:**
- Therapist: "When you're feeling really overwhelmed and want to disappear, what do you usually do?"
- Patient: "I just lie in bed and think about it more."
- Therapist: "That makes sense. When we're in emotional pain, we often do things that make it worse. I want to teach you some skills that can help you get through those moments without making things worse. One skill is called 'opposite action.' The idea is that emotions urge us to do certain things, but sometimes doing the opposite can help. For example, when sadness urges you to isolate, reaching out to someone can actually help. When anxiety urges you to avoid, facing the situation can help. Does that make sense?"
- Patient: "Yeah, I think so."
- Therapist: "Let's practice. When you're feeling really overwhelmed and want to disappear, what's one thing you could do that's the opposite of isolating? Something that would get you moving or engaged?"
- Patient: "I could go for a walk, I guess."
- Therapist: "That's a great opposite action. Or you could call someone, or go to a coffee shop, or do something you enjoy. The key is doing something that engages you with the world instead of pulling away. Let's also teach you a skill for when emotions are really intense and you need quick relief. It's called the TIPP technique..."

**Practice:** Learn emotion regulation and distress tolerance skills from DBT resources. Practice teaching these skills to patients. Create skill cards or worksheets that patients can use between sessions.
