### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no genuine collaboration demonstrated. Psychiatrist consistently assumes expert role and positions client as problem to be fixed rather than partner in problem-solving. Evidence includes: unilateral plan creation ("I'm going to give you a plan"), dismissal of client input ("you really shouldn't use smoking as a crutch"), rapid-fire questions without exploration, and rhetorical questions implying judgment ("Why haven't you quit yet?"). Power imbalance language ("You have to," "You must," "You need to") establishes hierarchical rather than partnership dynamic. Client's ideas, concerns, and preferences do not substantially influence session direction.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Severe deficiency in softening sustain talk. Psychiatrist actively reinforces and intensifies client resistance through confrontation and pressure rather than skillfully softening it. When client expresses sustain talk ("It's part of my routine," "It calms me down," "It helps me get through the day"), psychiatrist responds with direct confrontation ("you really shouldn't use smoking as a crutch," "Why haven't you tried those yet?"). This approach escalates resistance rather than softening it. Client's explicit feedback that pressure causes defensiveness ("that just makes me want to hold on tighter to my smokes") is not addressed through strategic MI responses. ### Overall Clinical Summary

**Session Score: 1.4/5**

**Critical Findings:**

This session demonstrates severe deficiency in Motivational Interviewing practice. The psychiatrist relies almost exclusively on directive persuasion, confrontation, and pressure—approaches fundamentally inconsistent with MI principles and research-demonstrated to be ineffective and counterproductive.

**Key Deficiencies:**
- Pervasive expert-driven, directive approach violating MI collaboration principle
- Confrontational handling of resistance that escalates rather than softens client defensiveness
- Minimal autonomy support with repeated pressure and demands
- Failure to evoke client's own motivations and reasons for change
- Dismissal of client perspective and explicit feedback about what is unhelpful
- Lack of genuine partnership; client input does not substantially influence session

**Impact on Client:**

The psychiatrist's approach directly undermines Lucía's motivation for change. Despite initial ambivalence (normal in contemplation stage), the confrontational approach escalates resistance. By session end, client explicitly states: "the pressure to 'commit now' just makes me less willing" and "that just makes me want to hold on tighter to my smokes." This demonstrates the approach is counterproductive and moving client away from change, not toward it.

**Positive Elements:**

- Clear focus on target behavior maintained throughout
- Eventual recognition that directive approach is not working
- Brief moments of collaborative exploration near session end show capacity for different approach
- Some basic reflective listening attempts present

**Recommendations:**

Immediate action required. Psychiatrist needs:
1. Formal MI training (MINT-certified or equivalent)
2. Regular clinical supervision with MI expert
3. Fundamental shift from directive persuasion to collaborative exploration
4. Practice cultivating and amplifying client change talk
5. Development of genuine autonomy support and partnership skills
6. Recorded session review with feedback on confrontational language patterns

**Without intervention, this psychiatrist will continue to harm client motivation for change through confrontational, directive methods that increase resistance and decrease intrinsic motivation.**
