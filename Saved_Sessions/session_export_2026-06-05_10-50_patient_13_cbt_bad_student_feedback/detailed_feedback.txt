
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

This document outlines a structured framework for assessing therapist competence in Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT). 
It is used for training, supervision, and evaluation. Each item is scored on a 0–6 scale:

0 = Poor
2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

### 3. Understanding

## Recommendations

### 1. Establish a Collaborative Agenda and Case Conceptualization (HIGHEST PRIORITY)

**Action:** At the beginning of the next session, explicitly establish a collaborative agenda and develop a shared case conceptualization.

**Example:**
"Mathilda, I want to make sure we're working on what's most important to you. From what you've told me, I understand that you experience anxiety in social situations—especially in class—which leads you to avoid speaking up. You also feel lonely since moving and would like to have more social connections. Is that right? [Wait for confirmation.] Here's what I think is happening: When you're in a social situation, you have thoughts like 'I'll say something stupid and people will judge me.' Avoiding feels safe in the moment, but it actually strengthens the anxiety over time and prevents you from learning that your fears don't come true. Does that make sense? [Wait for confirmation.] Our goal is to break this cycle. **Practice:** Write out your case conceptualization for this patient before each session. Include: (1) her presenting problems, (2) how her thoughts, feelings, and behaviors interact to maintain the problems, (3) what maintains the cycle, (4) what needs to change. Use this to guide your interventions.

### 2. Reduce Reassurance and Increase Guided Discovery (SECOND PRIORITY)

**Action:** Stop offering reassurance as your primary intervention. Instead, use Socratic questioning to help the patient reach her own conclusions.

**Example - What NOT to do:**
Patient: "If I mess up once, everyone will think I'm useless or weird forever."
Therapist: "That's not true. Everyone makes mistakes. People won't think you're useless."

**Example - What TO do:**
Patient: "If I mess up once, everyone will think I'm useless or weird forever."
Therapist: "That's an important thought. Let's examine it together. Have you seen that happen with other people? If a friend made a mistake in class, would you think they were useless or weird forever?"
Patient: "No, I guess not. I'd probably forget about it pretty quickly."
Therapist: "So based on your own experience, is it true that one mistake means someone is useless forever?"
Patient: "No, I guess not."
Therapist: "What do you think might actually happen if you made a mistake in class?"

**Practice:** For every reassuring statement you're tempted to make, ask yourself: "What question could I ask instead that would help her reach this conclusion herself?" Record your sessions and count how many times you offer reassurance vs. ask questions. Goal: Shift the ratio so that 70% of your responses are questions, not statements.

### 3. Systematically Apply Cognitive Restructuring (THIRD PRIORITY)

**Action:** Teach the patient to identify, examine, and challenge her catastrophic thoughts using a structured thought record.

**Example:**
"I want to teach you a technique called a thought record. It helps us identify the specific thoughts that trigger your anxiety, so we can examine them together. Here's how it works: [Show the form.] When you feel anxious, you write down: (1) What situation triggered the anxiety? (2) What thoughts went through your mind? (3) How anxious did you feel (0-100)? (4) What evidence supports this thought? (5) What evidence goes against it? (6) What's a more balanced way to think about it? Let me show you an example. Let's say you're in class and the teacher asks a question. You think, 'If I raise my hand and answer wrong, everyone will think I'm stupid.' Let's examine this thought. What evidence supports it? [Patient responds.] What evidence goes against it? [Patient responds.] What's a more balanced thought? [Patient develops alternative.] This week, I'd like you to fill out one thought record every time you feel anxious. Bring them to our next session and we'll review them together."

**Practice:** Learn the cognitive model thoroughly. Practice identifying automatic thoughts, examining evidence, and developing alternatives. Practice this with your own anxious thoughts first, so you can model the process authentically.

---

### 4. Plan and Implement Behavioral Experiments (FOURTH PRIORITY)

**Action:** Design specific behavioral experiments to test the patient's predictions about social situations.

**Example:**
"You mentioned that you're afraid to speak up in class because you think people will judge you. Let's test this belief. This week, I'd like you to raise your hand once in class and answer a question. Before you do it, write down: (1) What do you predict will happen? (2) How anxious do you expect to feel? After you do it, write down: (1) What actually happened? (2) How anxious did you actually feel? (3) Did people judge you the way you predicted? We'll compare your prediction to reality. This will help us see whether your fears are actually coming true. Does that sound okay?"

**Practice:** Develop a hierarchy of behavioral experiments, starting with low-anxiety situations and gradually increasing difficulty. Plan these collaboratively with the patient.

### 5. Eliminate Excessive Self-Disclosure (FIFTH PRIORITY)

**Action:** Significantly reduce personal stories about your own anxiety. Instead, focus entirely on the patient's experience.

**What NOT to do:**
"I totally get that! School can be super tough sometimes. You know, I remember when I was in school, I used to mess up all the time in front of people, and it felt awful."

**What TO do:**
"I hear you. School anxiety is really challenging. Tell me more about what happens when you're in class and feeling anxious. What goes through your mind?"

**Why this matters:** For a patient with Avoidant Personality Disorder who struggles with feelings of inadequacy and social comparison, excessive therapist self-disclosure can reinforce her belief that others manage anxiety better than she does. It also shifts focus from her experience to yours, reducing the therapeutic focus.

**Practice:** Review your recordings. Count instances of self-disclosure. Goal: Eliminate personal stories about your own anxiety. If you feel the urge to share a personal experience, ask yourself: "How does this serve the patient's treatment? Or am I meeting my own need to be relatable?" If it's the latter, don't do it.

### 6. Implement Systematic Behavioral Activation (SIXTH PRIORITY)

**Action:** Create a structured behavioral activation plan with specific activities, times, and monitoring.

**Example:**
"You mentioned feeling lonely since moving and that you used to enjoy camping and hiking with your family. Let's use that. This week, I'd like you to: (1) Go for one outdoor activity (walk, hike, or sit in a park) for at least 30 minutes. (2) Before you go, rate your mood 0-10. (3) During the activity, notice what you observe—trees, sounds, other people, your thoughts. (4) After the activity, rate your mood again. (5) Write down what you noticed. We'll review this next session. The goal isn't to feel happy immediately—it's to gradually re-engage with activities that are important to you. Does this make sense?"

**Practice:** Learn the behavioral activation model. Understand that the goal is not immediate mood improvement but gradual re-engagement with valued activities. Help the patient identify activities that are both achievable and meaningful.

### 9. Address Anxiety Sensitivity Directly (NINTH PRIORITY)

**Practice:** Learn about anxiety sensitivity. Understand that for some patients, the fear of anxiety itself is a major maintaining factor. Address this directly rather than ignoring it.
