
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and contains multiple instances of explicit judgment, disapproval, and labeling. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses Irina's legitimate constraints (full-time work, night school, single parenting of a toddler) as "excuses," directly contradicting MI's foundational principle of acceptance. Specific examples include: *"You need to stop making excuses right now,"* *"Why didn't you make time for exercise before? You really should quit smoking immediately,"* and most damaging, *"If you keep saying you're scared or overwhelmed, you're just avoiding responsibility."* This last statement pathologizes normal human emotion and explicitly rejects Irina's internal experience.

The psychiatrist's repeated use of "no, no, no" creates an adversarial, dismissive tone that communicates contempt for the client's perspective. There is zero affirmation of Irina's strengths—her commitment to her son, her willingness to engage in the session, her honesty about her struggles, her openness to change. Instead, the psychiatrist frames her as lazy ("only those with real willpower make their health a priority"), irresponsible, and dishonest. The statement *"Everybody has stress, but only those who take control of their health avoid the worst outcomes"* is a thinly veiled judgment that Irina lacks control and will face poor health outcomes due to personal failure.

There is no warmth, no genuine support, and no communication that Irina has inherent worth independent of her compliance with the psychiatrist's demands. The session violates the MI spirit entirely on this dimension.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates active rejection of acceptance principles, using judgment, dismissal, and pathologizing language throughout. This creates a fundamentally unsafe therapeutic environment that undermines any possibility of genuine engagement.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate understanding of the client's perspective and emotional experience. This psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no effort to understand Irina's world. When Irina states she is "barely have time to breathe," the psychiatrist responds with *"Don't tell me you're too busy; everyone can find time if they really want to."* This is a direct rejection of her lived experience, not an attempt to understand it.

When Irina expresses fear about quitting cold turkey (*"I'm scared that if I try to quit everything at once, I might end up worse off"*), the psychiatrist responds: *"If you keep saying you're scared or overwhelmed, you're just avoiding responsibility."* This is not empathic reflection—it is pathologizing her fear as avoidance. A truly empathic response would explore what underlies that fear, validate the legitimacy of gradual change, and understand her concern about relapse.

There are zero reflective listening statements in this entire session. The psychiatrist never once mirrors back what Irina has said to demonstrate understanding. Instead, every response is prescriptive, dismissive, or argumentative. The psychiatrist never asks clarifying questions about Irina's stress, her coping mechanisms, her values regarding her son, or her previous attempts at change. There is no curiosity about her internal world.

The psychiatrist also fails to recognize or reflect the change talk Irina does offer: *"I want to be healthier,"* *"I'm willing to try something,"* *"I want to change."* These statements are ignored entirely, replaced with demands for more dramatic change.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no empathic stance, no reflective listening, and active rejection of the client's emotional experience. This represents a fundamental failure of MI's core relational skill.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

While the psychiatrist does maintain a clear focus (smoking cessation and exercise), the *manner* in which direction is maintained is entirely inconsistent with MI principles. Direction in MI means strategically guiding the conversation toward change goals while remaining collaborative and client-centered. This psychiatrist uses authoritarian prescription instead.

The psychiatrist does maintain focus on two target behaviors: smoking and exercise. However, the direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. Irina attempts to redirect toward a more realistic, step-by-step approach multiple times (*"Maybe starting with quitting smoking and exercising 30 minutes a day tomorrow is too much for me. Couldn't I try cutting back on smoking first?"*), and the psychiatrist refuses to negotiate or explore this alternative direction. Instead, the psychiatrist repeats the same demands verbatim: *"No, no, no. You're just not seeing the seriousness here. Cutting back isn't enough—you have to quit completely."*

This rigidity is problematic because it ignores evidence-based harm reduction principles and fails to meet clients where they are. The psychiatrist's insistence on "all or nothing" may actually reduce Irina's likelihood of any change, as she explicitly states: *"if I try too much too fast, I might just give up altogether."* A more strategically directed MI approach would explore whether gradual reduction might be a viable stepping stone to abstinence, or whether it represents meaningful progress toward health.

The psychiatrist does not clarify what the actual change goal is—is it abstinence, or is it harm reduction? Is it 30 minutes daily exercise, or is it increased movement? These conversations never happen.

**Summary:**

While direction is maintained, it is imposed authoritatively rather than collaboratively developed. The psychiatrist refuses to explore alternative pathways or negotiate realistic goals, which undermines strategic direction in MI.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have valuable knowledge. This session demonstrates zero collaboration. The psychiatrist explicitly positions themselves as the sole expert and decision-maker from the opening statement: *"Today, I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to get healthier."* This is not a collaborative stance; it is unilateral prescription.

Throughout the session, the psychiatrist refuses to incorporate Irina's input into the session structure or goals. When Irina suggests alternatives (*"Maybe I could try cutting back on smoking first or maybe adding a little movement here and there instead of this all-or-nothing approach?"*), the psychiatrist responds: *"No, no, no. ## Recommendations

### 1. **Fundamental Reorientation to MI Spirit (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Recognize that this session violates the core spirit of MI. The psychiatrist must fundamentally shift from an expert-prescriptive stance to a collaborative-evocative stance.
- **Example:** Instead of opening with *"Today, I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do,"* begin with: *"I'd like to understand what brings you in today and what you're thinking about your health. What's on your mind?"*
- **Practice:** Complete formal MI training (minimum 2-day workshop) followed by supervised practice with feedback. This is not optional—the current approach is contraindicated for behavior change.

### 2. **Develop Reflective Listening Skills**
- **Action:** Learn and practice reflective listening as the primary response mode. For every client statement, offer a reflection before asking a follow-up question.
- **Example:** When Irina says *"I smoke about five cigarettes a day and I barely have time to exercise,"* respond with: *"So you're managing five cigarettes daily, and finding time for exercise feels really difficult with everything on your plate right now."* Then ask: *"Tell me more about what a typical day looks like for you."*
- **Practice:** Record sessions and count reflections vs. questions vs. directives. Goal: 60% reflections, 30% questions, 10% directives (or less). Use MI fidelity scales to self-assess.

### 3. **Replace Demands with Open-Ended Questions**
- **Action:** Eliminate commanding language (*"You must,"* *"You have to,"* *"This is not optional"*) and replace with genuine curiosity about the client's perspective.
- **Example:** Instead of *"You must exercise 30 minutes daily,"* ask: *"What would feel realistic for you in terms of adding movement to your day? What might be a first step?"*
- **Practice:** Before each session, write 5-10 open-ended questions about the client's values, concerns, previous successes, and ideas. Use these to guide the session rather than predetermined prescriptions.

### 4. **Validate Client Constraints and Explore Realistic Goals**
- **Action:** Acknowledge that Irina's constraints (full-time work, night school, single parenting) are real, not excuses. Collaborate on goals that fit her actual life.
- **Example:** *"You're working full-time, going to school at night, and raising a two-year-old. That's a lot. I hear you that adding a 30-minute daily exercise routine feels impossible right now. What would feel doable? Even five minutes of movement a few times a week would be progress."*
- **Practice:** In each session, explicitly validate the client's real-world constraints before discussing change. Ask: *"What would need to be true for this to work for you?"*

### 5. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk**
- **Action:** Actively listen for and reflect back client statements supporting change. Ask questions designed to strengthen motivation.
- **Example:** When Irina says *"I want to be healthier for my son,"* respond: *"Your son's wellbeing is really important to you. Tell me more about what being healthier would mean for him and for you."* Then ask: *"What's one small thing you could try this week that might move you in that direction?"*
- **Practice:** After each session, identify all instances of change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment). Did you reflect and amplify each one? If not, what could you have said?

### 6. **Explore Ambivalence Rather Than Confronting It**
- **Action:** When the client expresses both reasons for and against change, use double-sided reflections to acknowledge both sides rather than arguing against the "against" side.
- **Example:** Instead of *"No, no, no. Cutting back isn't enough,"* say: *"On one hand, you're worried that quitting cold turkey might make you relapse, and on the other hand, you recognize that your health matters to you and to your son. Both of those things are true. Let's explore what might work."*
- **Practice:** When you feel the urge to argue against a client's concern, pause and offer a double-sided reflection instead. This softens resistance and maintains partnership.

### 7. **Support Autonomy Explicitly**
- **Action:** Communicate respect for the client's right to choose, even if you disagree with their choice. Emphasize their ownership of the change process.
- **Example:** *"This is your decision to make. I can share what the research shows about health risks, and I can support you in whatever direction you choose. What feels right to you?"*
- **Practice:** In each session, use language that emphasizes choice: *"What do you think?"* *"What feels doable to you?"* *"You're the expert on your own life."* Avoid: *"You must,"* *"You have to,"* *"This is not optional."*

### 8. **Assess Readiness and Meet Clients Where They Are**
- **Action:** Recognize that Irina is in contemplation/preparation stage, not action stage. Goals should reflect her current readiness, not where you wish she were.
- **Example:** Instead of demanding immediate abstinence and 30 minutes daily exercise, explore: *"You mentioned you might be able to cut back to one drink a night. What would it take to try that? And for exercise, what's one small thing you could add this week?"*
- **Practice:** Use the Stages of Change model to assess readiness. Tailor your approach accordingly. For contemplation, focus on exploring ambivalence. For preparation, focus on building confidence and concrete planning.

### 9. **Eliminate Fear-Based Pressure**
- **Action:** Stop using catastrophic health outcomes (*"heart disease, lung cancer"*) as motivational tools. This increases anxiety without increasing intrinsic motivation and often backfires.
- **Example:** Instead of *"If you don't quit smoking now, you're going to face serious health problems,"* say: *"I care about your health, and I know you do too. What would help you feel more confident about making changes?"*
- **Practice:** Notice when you're using fear-based language. Replace it with collaborative exploration of values and goals.

### 10. **Seek Supervision and Feedback**
- **Action:** This session represents a significant departure from evidence-based MI practice. Seek regular supervision from an MI-trained clinician who can provide feedback on recorded sessions.
- **Example:** Record sessions and review them with a supervisor using MI fidelity scales (MITI or CAMI). Identify specific moments where you could have used better MI technique.
- **Practice:** Commit to ongoing MI training, supervision, and self-reflection. This is not a one-time fix but an ongoing professional development commitment.
