### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Session characterized by systematic undermining of autonomy and explicit denial of choice. Psychiatrist frames prescribed behavior as non-negotiable and mandatory: "Two hours without your phone before bed is non-negotiable," "You have to commit," "You must cut down your screen time right now." When client expresses hesitation, psychiatrist responds with explicit denials of autonomy: "No, you don't get to decide how this goes," "This isn't about your feelings or preferences." ### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Session demonstrates virtually no evocation. Psychiatrist tells client what to do rather than asking what client wants to do. Tells why change matters rather than asking client to articulate own reasons. Tells what solution is rather than asking client to generate solutions. When client offers change talk ("my grades did slip last semester, and feeling tired all the time sucks"), psychiatrist does not amplify or build on this. Immediately pivots to demanding compliance with specific plan. Asks only closed, yes/no questions that shut down exploration rather than open it. No open questions like "What concerns you most about your phone use?" or "What would be different if you made a change?" Approach is entirely extrinsic (imposing external reasons for change) rather than intrinsic (evoking client's own reasons). Opposite of evocation.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Session demonstrates minimal cultivation of change talk. Client offers spontaneous change talk: "my grades did slip last semester, and feeling tired all the time sucks," "I do want to feel better and get my act together with school," "I want to do better in school and feel better too," "I do want to work on this." These reveal intrinsic motivation for change. However, psychiatrist does not amplify, explore, or build on any of these statements. When client expresses motivations, psychiatrist immediately pivots to demanding compliance with specific plan, shutting down further exploration. Never asks questions designed to evoke change talk: "What would be different if you made a change?", "What's important to you about your health?", "How would better sleep help you?" Psychiatrist uses extrinsic motivation (fear, consequences, authority) to try to force behavior change. This approach backfires by increasing resistance and decreasing intrinsic motivation—exactly what occurs in client's responses: "being told I *have* to do this just makes me feel more trapped and less motivated to even try."

#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Client offers sustain talk throughout: "it's how I stay in touch with my friends," "it helps me relax when I'm stressed," "I feel like the only way to relax," "When I'm stressed, scrolling helps me relax and feel less alone," "I worry I'll just get anxious or bored," "I worry I'll end up feeling more anxious or disconnected." This is confrontational and invalidating. Does not explore function of phone use, does not acknowledge legitimate concerns, does not collaboratively problem-solve around barriers. Psychiatrist does prevent session from becoming entirely focused on sustain talk (by repeatedly redirecting to prescribed plan), which prevents score of 1/5. However, method used—authoritarian dismissal and confrontation—is not skillful MI technique and actually increases resistance. Client's responses show increasing defensiveness and entrenchment: "I'm not saying no to trying to change, but..." Skillful approach would use double-sided reflections acknowledging both sustain talk and change talk without reinforcing resistance.

### Summary of Findings

#### **Does This Session Embody the MI Spirit?**
**No.** This session fundamentally violates MI spirit. MI is built on partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation. This session demonstrates none of these. Instead, it exemplifies hierarchy, judgment, control, and imposition. The psychiatrist operates from authoritarian expert model fundamentally incompatible with MI.

#### **Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation for Change**
**Very Low.** Session is likely to be counterproductive. Client enters with emerging ambivalence and some recognition that change might be needed. By session end, client has become increasingly defensive and explicitly states: "being told I *have* to do this just makes me feel more trapped and less motivated to even try." This is opposite of goal. Extrinsic pressure (fear, consequences, authority) typically backfires by increasing resistance and decreasing intrinsic motivation—exactly what occurs here.

#### **Impact on Therapeutic Relationship**
**Damaging.** Psychiatrist has explicitly invalidated client's perspective, denied her autonomy, and communicated that her input is irrelevant. Client is unlikely to return for follow-up or trust this provider's guidance. Therapeutic relationship is foundation for any effective intervention; this session has eroded that foundation.

---

### Critical Recommendations for Improvement

**1. Fundamental Shift Required:** Psychiatrist must shift from expert-driven to genuinely collaborative stance. This requires formal MI training and ongoing supervision with MI expert.

**2. Replace Coercive Language:** Eliminate "you must/have to/need to" language. Replace with collaborative framing: "I wonder," "What do you think," "Let's explore together."

**3. Develop Reflective Listening:** Practice complex reflections capturing emotional nuance and client's internal frame of reference. Aim for 3:1 ratio of reflections to statements.

**4. Evoke Rather Than Impose:** Ask open questions about client's own reasons for change. When client offers change talk, explore and amplify rather than immediately pivoting to prescribed plan.

**5. Respect Autonomy:** Explicitly communicate that client has right to choose and that change is their decision. Replace ultimatums with collaborative goal-setting.

**6. Explore Ambivalence:** Acknowledge both sides of client's ambivalence as legitimate and understandable. Use double-sided reflections to help client move toward change talk.

**7. Formal MI Training:** Enroll in MINT-certified MI training program and engage in ongoing supervision with validated MI coding systems (MITI, MISC).

---

### Conclusion

This session requires **fundamental retraining in MI principles and practice** before clinician should continue providing MI-based interventions. Current approach violates core MI principles and is likely to harm therapeutic relationship and decrease client motivation for change. Immediate action needed to address authoritarian stance and develop genuine collaborative, client-centered practice.
