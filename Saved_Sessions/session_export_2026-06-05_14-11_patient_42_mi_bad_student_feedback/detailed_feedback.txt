
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Foundational Shift: Adopt MI Spirit and Stance**
- **Action:** Fundamentally reframe your role from "expert who tells patients what to do" to "collaborative partner who helps patients explore their own motivations and resources for change."
- **Example:** Instead of "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do," begin with: "I'd like to understand what brought you in today and what you're hoping to work on. What's most important to you right now about your health?"
- **Practice:** Before your next patient session, spend 5 minutes reflecting on: "What does this patient know about their own barriers, readiness, and capacity that I don't? How can I learn from them rather than tell them what to do?" Read Miller & Rollnick's *Motivational Interviewing: Helping People Change* (3rd ed.), focusing on the spirit of MI (partnership, compassion, evocation, autonomy support).

---

### 2. **Eliminate Blame, Shame, and Judgment Language**
- **Action:** Remove all language that judges, blames, or shames the patient. Replace it with acceptance and normalization of struggle.
- **Examples of language to eliminate:**
  - "You're just not trying hard enough" → "It sounds like you're struggling with this, and that's understandable."
  - "There's no excuse" → "I hear that this feels really hard right now."
  - "It's your fault if your health gets worse" → "I'm concerned about your health, and I want to support you in making changes that feel right for you."
  - "Why haven't you just done it?" → "What's gotten in the way of making this change?"
- **Practice:** Record yourself in a session (with patient consent) and listen for judgment language. Count instances. Set a goal of zero instances in your next session.

---

### 3. **Develop Reflective Listening and Empathy**
- **Action:** Practice accurate reflective listening that demonstrates genuine understanding of the patient's perspective, especially their barriers and emotions.
- **Examples of reflective responses:**
  - Patient: "I have a lot of anxiety, and I just avoid it and put it off."
  - **Poor response:** "You need to stop avoiding and just do it."
  - **MI-consistent response:** "So anxiety is really getting in the way, and when it shows up, you find yourself putting things off. That's a real struggle for you."
  
  - Patient: "I'm scared of failing if I try to quit when I'm not ready."
  - **Poor response:** "People with willpower don't let fear stop them."
  - **MI-consistent response:** "You're worried that if you try before you're ready, you might fail, and that failure would be really hard. So you're being thoughtful about when to make this move."

- **Practice:** In your next 3 sessions, aim for at least 5 reflective statements per session. Use the formula: "So [emotion/experience], and [meaning/consequence]." Record and review.

---

### 4. **Ask Open-Ended Questions to Evoke Change Talk**
- **Action:** Replace closed, accusatory questions with open-ended questions designed to draw out the patient's own reasons, values, and resources for change.
- **Examples:**
  - Instead of: "Why haven't you quit smoking yet?" (accusatory, invites defensiveness)
  - Ask: "What would be important to you about quitting smoking?" (evokes reasons for change)
  
  - Instead of: "Are you just not serious about getting better?" (judgmental)
  - Ask: "Tell me about what getting healthier would mean for you." (evokes values and motivation)
  
  - Instead of: "Will you commit to quitting today or not?" (binary pressure)
  - Ask: "What do you see as the first step you might take?" (evokes self-efficacy and autonomy)
  
  - Instead of: "Why haven't you followed the plan?" (blame)
  - Ask: "What got in the way of following the plan we discussed?" (explores barriers collaboratively)

- **Practice:** Prepare 5-7 open-ended questions before your next patient session. Use them to explore the patient's perspective before offering any advice or direction.

---

### 5. **Support Autonomy and Collaborative Goal-Setting**
- **Action:** Explicitly communicate that the patient has choice and control over their change process. Collaborate on goals rather than imposing them.
- **Examples:**
  - Instead of: "You have to quit smoking now. It's not optional."
  - Say: "Quitting smoking would have a big impact on your health. I'm wondering what you think about that as a goal. What feels right to you?"
  
  - Instead of: "You need to do all of these things immediately."
  - Say: "There are several things we could work on—smoking, diet, exercise. What feels most important or most doable for you right now? Let's start there."
  
  - Instead of: "There's no easier way, and you don't get to wait."
  - Say: "I hear that trying to change everything at once feels overwhelming. What would feel manageable as a first step?"

- **Practice:** In your next session, explicitly ask the patient: "What would feel like a realistic goal for you?" and "What pace of change feels right to you?" Listen to their answer and build from there.

---

### 6. **Amplify and Reinforce Change Talk**
- **Action:** When the patient expresses desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment to change, reflect it back, explore it further, and reinforce it.
- **Examples:**
  - Patient: "I do want to get better."
  - **Poor response:** "You're just not trying hard enough."
  - **MI-consistent response:** "You do want to get better. Tell me more about that. What would getting better look like for you?"
  
  - Patient: "Maybe I could try cutting back on junk food a little."
  - **Poor response:** "But you really should quit smoking first."
  - **MI-consistent response:** "So cutting back on junk food feels like something you could do. What appeals to you about starting there?"
  
  - Patient: "I'm willing to try working on other things first."
  - **Poor response:** "There's no time for that."
  - **MI-consistent response:** "You're willing to work on this. That's important. What do you think might help you stay with it?"

- **Practice:** In your next session, identify every instance of change talk. Write it down. Reflect it back and explore it further. Count how many times you amplify vs. dismiss change talk.

---

### 7. **Skillfully Handle Sustain Talk Without Confrontation**
- **Action:** When the patient expresses barriers or reasons for not changing, use double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic redirection rather than confrontation.
- **Examples:**
  - Patient: "The anxiety just takes over, and I can't do it."
  - **Poor response:** "You need to snap out of that anxiety and just do it."
  - **MI-consistent response (double-sided reflection):** "So on one hand, you want to make this change, and on the other hand, the anxiety feels really overwhelming right now. That's a real tension you're in."
  
  - Patient: "I'm scared of failing if I try to quit when I'm not ready."
  - **Poor response:** "People who are serious just quit."
  - **MI-consistent response (reframing):** "It sounds like you're being thoughtful about this—you want to set yourself up for success rather than just jumping in and failing. That's actually a strength. What would help you feel more ready?"
  
  - Patient: "I just avoid it and put it off."
  - **Poor response:** "Stop making excuses."
  - **MI-consistent response (strategic redirection):** "Avoidance has been your pattern. I'm curious—have there been times when you've pushed through anxiety or avoidance successfully? What was different then?"

- **Practice:** In your next session, when the patient expresses a barrier or reason for not changing, pause and ask yourself: "How can I acknowledge this as real without arguing with it?" Use a double-sided reflection or reframe.

---

### 8. **Assess Readiness and Pace Collaboratively**
- **Action:** Rather than imposing a timeline, assess the patient's readiness and pace, and build from there. Use scaling questions to understand their current state.
- **Examples:**
  - "On a scale of 0-10, where 0 is not ready at all and 10 is completely ready, where are you with quitting smoking right now?"
  - "What would it take to move from a [current number] to a [higher number]?"
  - "What feels like a realistic first step given where you're at right now?"
  - "What would help you feel more confident about making this change?"

- **Practice:** Use scaling questions in your next 3 sessions. Listen to the patient's answer and build your approach around their stated readiness, not your predetermined timeline.

---

### 9. **Affirm Strengths and Build Self-Efficacy**
- **Action:** Identify and affirm the patient's strengths, willingness, and capacity. Build confidence through recognition of past successes and current efforts.
- **Examples:**
  - "You're here, you're willing to talk about this, and you're being honest about your struggles. That takes courage."
  - "You've already identified that anxiety is a barrier. That self-awareness is important."
  - "You're thinking carefully about what you can handle. That's actually a strength."
  - "Tell me about a time you successfully made a change. What helped you then?"

- **Practice:** In your next session, identify at least 3 genuine affirmations of the patient's strengths, willingness, or self-awareness. Deliver them authentically.

---

### 10. **Receive Training in Motivational Interviewing**
- **Action:** Pursue formal MI training through accredited programs (e.g., MINT-certified trainers, university-based MI courses).
- **Resources:**
  - Miller & Rollnick's *Motivational Interviewing: Helping People Change* (3rd ed., 2013)
  - MINT (Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers): www.motivationalinterviewing.org
  - Online MI training courses and workshops
  - Peer consultation groups focused on MI skill development
- **Practice:** Commit to at least 16 hours of formal MI training in the next 6 months. Practice with feedback from a supervisor or peer.
