### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Significantly undermined throughout session. While using inclusive language ("we could talk"), actual interaction pattern is clinician-driven and directive. Repeated "should" and "would probably help if" statements position psychiatrist as expert prescribing solutions rather than as partner exploring options. Fails to invite client's input into problem-solving; client follows clinician's lead rather than co-constructing plan.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Severely compromised by pervasive prescriptive language. Repeated directives about information-seeking methods, medication timing, organizational strategies, and symptom tracking communicate that clinician knows the right path rather than trusting client's capacity for informed decisions. Critical missed opportunity when identifying citalopram-phenelzine interaction—prescribes action rather than supporting client's autonomy to decide how to proceed.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Largely absent. Clinician provides information and prescriptions rather than drawing out client's motivations, values, and resources. When client expresses nervousness about starting new medication, clinician validates but doesn't explore deeper ("What specifically makes you nervous?" or "What would help you feel ready?"). Misses opportunity to explore client's demonstrated safety-consciousness as internal resource.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal true partnership demonstrated. Repeated prescriptive framing positions clinician as expert and client as recipient of guidance. When client raises citalopram-phenelzine concern, clinician responds with directive rather than "What do you think we should do?" Multiple action items presented as clinician recommendations rather than co-constructed plans. Power dynamics consistently favor clinician direction over shared decision-making.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Largely absent despite client producing organic change talk ("I'm willing to do that if it means I'm more informed," "I'll make sure to contact my doctor"). Clinician rarely evokes change talk proactively or reinforces it strategically. Missed opportunities to deepen commitment through questions like "What will you say to your doctor?" or "What's your instinct telling you?" Session relies on clinician-directed information rather than amplifying client's own motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Reasonably handled without confrontation. Clinician validates nervousness, provides information to address knowledge gaps, and takes safety concerns seriously (appropriately identifies citalopram-phenelzine interaction). However, approach relies on information-provision and prescriptive guidance rather than MI-specific techniques like double-sided reflection or strategic reframing. Doesn't explore meaning behind sustain talk to help client move beyond barriers independently.

## Summary Assessment

### Strengths
- Maintains respectful, nonjudgmental demeanor throughout
- Provides accurate, accessible information when requested
- Demonstrates clinical competence in identifying serious medication interaction
- Validates client concerns without dismissal or minimization
- Avoids confrontational language or pressure tactics

### Critical Areas for Improvement
- **Excessive prescriptive language** (most pervasive deficiency): Repeated "should" and "would probably help if" statements undermine autonomy support and collaboration
- **Minimal evocation**: Provides solutions rather than drawing out client's own motivations and problem-solving capacity
- **Weak partnership**: Assumes expert role rather than positioning client as expert on own experience
- **Missed opportunities for deeper exploration**: Rarely explores concerns beyond surface level to deepen understanding
- **Limited strategic change talk cultivation**: While client produces change talk, clinician doesn't proactively evoke or reinforce it
- **Scattered action items**: Multiple suggestions offered without clear prioritization or connection to core goals

### Clinical Impact
While client appears compliant and cooperative, session achieves "compliance without commitment"—a weaker predictor of sustained behavior change. Client follows clinician's recommendations but demonstrates limited evidence of internalized rationale or intrinsic commitment to plan. This approach risks undermining medication adherence and client's sense of agency in own care.

### Key Recommendations
1. Eliminate prescriptive language; replace with evocative questions
2. Develop true collaborative problem-solving by inviting client input
3. Deepen exploration through complex reflections capturing underlying meaning
4. Strategically evoke and reinforce client's change talk
5. Support autonomy by emphasizing client choice and agency
6. Establish clear, collaborative goals at session outset
7. Prioritize and organize action items collaboratively
8. Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence
9. Close sessions by reflecting client commitment and inviting their reflection
