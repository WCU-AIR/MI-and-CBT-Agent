### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance, normalizing Marcus's overwhelm ("That's completely understandable") and affirming his capability with medication management. Affirmations of effort and resilience are present throughout. However, opportunities were missed to explicitly normalize the emotional burden of chronic illness adjustment and to deeply validate the legitimacy of his ambivalence about effort versus benefit. The clinician could have more thoroughly explored the emotional weight of transitioning to a new diagnosis.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates solid reflective listening with accurate capture of Marcus's core ambivalence between avoiding hassle and worrying about missing important health information. Reflections are emotionally attuned early in the session ("You're feeling caught between..."). However, empathy becomes somewhat more surface-level in later exchanges. The clinician does not explore deeper emotional dimensions such as fear of failure, shame about past inconsistency, or the sense of pressure embedded in Marcus's statement "I'm supposed to figure this out somehow." Reflections in the latter half, while accurate, are somewhat generic and miss unique emotional nuances.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains clear, strategic focus on blood sugar monitoring as the target behavior throughout the session, appropriately narrowing from general overwhelm to specific concern. The clinician skillfully guides Marcus from vague distress toward specificity without allowing tangential discussions. However, there is a subtle but notable shift in the latter portion from exploration ("What do you think would be a good first step?") toward more directive suggestion-giving ("Many people find that setting specific times..."). While the psychiatrist does ask Marcus's opinion afterward, this represents a movement away from pure evocation toward a more expert-driven stance. Asking "What ideas do you have about how to make monitoring feel more manageable?" before offering strategies would have maintained stronger client-centered direction.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates partial collaboration, particularly in early and middle portions through agenda-setting ("What would you like to focus on?") and perspective-seeking questions. However, collaboration weakens noticeably in the latter half as the clinician shifts toward providing expert-driven solutions. The framing "Would it be okay if I shared some practical strategies..." carries an implicit assumption that the psychiatrist will be the primary source of solutions. The clinician does not fully explore what Marcus has already tried or what ideas he might generate independently. Additionally, when Marcus identifies his wife's support as helpful, the psychiatrist acknowledges this but does not deeply collaborate on leveraging this existing resource. True partnership would involve asking how to work together with the wife as part of the plan.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates inconsistent autonomy support. Early autonomy support is present through agenda-setting and perspective-seeking questions that affirm Marcus's right to choose and direct the process. However, autonomy support weakens significantly in the latter half when strategies are provided without explicitly affirming Marcus's freedom to choose, modify, or reject them. When Marcus mentions needing accountability, the clinician does not explore whether this is genuine preference or compliance. A more autonomy-supportive response would clarify: "Is that something you'd genuinely find helpful, or does it feel like something you think you should do?" The psychiatrist does not explicitly address Marcus's ambivalence about effort or affirm his right to decide whether the effort is worth it for him.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates some evocation but inconsistently and with notable gaps. Early evocative questions are present ("What part feels most important?" and "What do you wish could be different?"). The clinician also effectively evokes past successes ("What has helped you successfully build or stick to new routines?"), drawing out Marcus's own resources. However, evocation becomes significantly weaker in the latter half as the clinician shifts toward providing information and strategies rather than asking "What would clear instructions look like to you?" or "What ideas do you have about how to make monitoring feel manageable?" Most critically, the psychiatrist does not adequately evoke Marcus's own change talk and motivation. When Marcus expresses change talk ("I want to get a handle on it," "I'm willing to try"), the clinician affirms but does not strategically amplify or deepen it. The psychiatrist also does not explore Marcus's values, reasons for change, or what good health means to him—significant gaps in MI practice.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates early partnership through agenda-setting and perspective-seeking but increasingly assumes an expert role as the session progresses. Early partnership is evident in inviting Marcus to set the agenda and repeatedly seeking his perspective. However, partnership weakens significantly in the latter half as the clinician provides expert-driven strategies before fully exploring Marcus's own ideas and resources. The power dynamic shifts—the clinician presents solutions and Marcus evaluates them rather than co-creating them. The psychiatrist does not actively foster power-sharing by positioning Marcus as the expert on his own life and circumstances. When Marcus's wife's support is identified, the clinician does not genuinely collaborate on leveraging this resource. The psychiatrist does not explicitly acknowledge that Marcus has wisdom about what will work for him or position him as the expert on his own change process.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** This represents a significant weakness in the session. The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal strategic cultivation of Marcus's change talk. While Marcus does express some change talk organically ("I want to get a handle on it," "I'm willing to try," "I could probably handle checking once a day"), the clinician does not strategically evoke, amplify, or reinforce this language. When Marcus states "I want to get a handle on it," the psychiatrist does not ask follow-up questions to deepen motivation. When Marcus says "I'm willing to try," the clinician affirms but does not strengthen it by exploring what makes him willing or what's important about managing his blood sugar. The psychiatrist does not systematically evoke different dimensions of change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment). Most critically, when Marcus expresses sustain talk ("Sometimes I wonder if it's going to be more hassle than it's worth"), the clinician does not use this as an opportunity to evoke competing change talk. The psychiatrist's approach is more focused on problem-solving and confidence-building than on systematically evoking and strengthening Marcus's own language about change—a fundamental gap in MI technique.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates moderate skill in handling Marcus's sustain talk and ambivalence, though there are notable gaps. When Marcus expresses doubt about consistency ("I'm still kind of worried about forgetting or messing it up"), the clinician does not reinforce this concern but instead reframes it positively ("It's really positive that you're willing to start despite those worries"). This is a soft pivot away from sustain talk toward change talk—a reasonable MI technique. Similarly, when Marcus states "Sometimes I wonder if it's going to be more hassle than it's worth," the psychiatrist moves toward confidence-building rather than exploring or amplifying the concern. However, the clinician does not employ more sophisticated MI techniques for softening sustain talk, such as double-sided reflections that acknowledge both sides of ambivalence while gently emphasizing the change side. When Marcus expresses his confidence level as "around a 5," the psychiatrist does not explore what would move him higher—a missed opportunity to evoke change talk and address sustain talk simultaneously. The psychiatrist also does not strategically reframe obligation language ("I'm supposed to learn...") as agency and choice.

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 3.4/5 (Adequate)**

### Key Strengths
- Strong opening and agenda-setting that establishes collaborative foundation
- Accurate reflective listening, particularly in capturing core ambivalence
- Consistent affirmation and validation of Marcus's efforts and capabilities
- Strategic focus on blood sugar monitoring as the target behavior
- Effective exploration of past successes to evoke client resources
- Practical problem-solving with concrete, manageable first steps
- Appropriate use of confidence scaling to assess readiness

### Critical Areas for Improvement
- **Insufficient systematic evocation of change talk** across all dimensions (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment)
- **Shift toward expert-driven problem-solving** in latter half, reducing genuine collaboration
- **Limited exploration of values and deeper intrinsic motivation** for change
- **Inadequate handling of ambivalence** without sophisticated MI techniques
- **Weak autonomy support** with insufficient explicit affirmation of client choice
- **Diminished partnership** as session progresses, positioning client as passive recipient
- **Minimal strategic amplification** of Marcus's own change language

### Clinical Impact
Marcus leaves the session with modest confidence increase (5 to 6) and concrete first steps (phone alarm, recording numbers, asking doctor about target ranges). However, the session's effectiveness in evoking sustained intrinsic motivation is limited. Marcus may follow through due to compliance with clinician suggestions rather than genuine strengthening of his own motivation. When obstacles arise or external structure fades, consistency may suffer. The psychiatrist has not fully connected blood sugar monitoring to Marcus's deeper values and hopes, which is crucial for sustaining motivation over time.

### Recommendations for Development
1. Systematically evoke and amplify change talk across all dimensions using OARS framework
2. Explore client values, hopes, and intrinsic motivation before problem-solving
3. Delay expert-driven solutions; evoke client ideas first
4. Use double-sided reflections to address ambivalence without reinforcing sustain talk
5. Explicitly affirm client autonomy and freedom to choose or modify recommendations
6. Explore confidence scaling more deeply to evoke change talk and identify barriers
7. Leverage existing support systems collaboratively, potentially involving support persons
8. Develop genuine curiosity about client expertise regarding their own life and circumstances
