
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **URGENT: Implement Comprehensive Suicide Risk Assessment and Safety Planning**
- **Action:** In the next session (or immediately via phone if there is any indication of imminent risk), conduct a detailed suicide risk assessment including: frequency and intensity of ideation, specificity of plan, access to means, timeline, previous attempts, protective factors, and reasons for living.
- **Example:** "I want to understand what's been happening with your thoughts about ending your life. Can you tell me more about the specific thoughts you've been having? When do they happen most? How often? Have you thought about when or how you might do this? Do you have access to a gun or other means? What's kept you from acting on these thoughts so far?"
- **Practice:** Review the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) or similar structured assessment tool. Practice asking direct, non-judgmental questions about suicidal ideation. Role-play with a supervisor.

### 2. **Develop and Document a Collaborative Safety Plan**
- **Action:** Work with the patient to create a written safety plan that includes: warning signs of crisis, internal coping strategies, people and social settings that provide distraction, people to ask for help, professional contacts (therapist, psychiatrist, crisis line), and means restriction strategies.
- **Example:** "Let's create a plan together for what you can do if you start having strong thoughts about ending your life. What are the warning signs that you're getting into a dangerous place? What have you done in the past that helped? Who could you call? What could you do to make it harder to act on these thoughts?"
- **Practice:** Use a standardized safety planning template (e.g., Stanley & Brown, 2012). Practice collaboratively developing plans with role-play patients.

### 3. **Establish Clear Crisis Resources and Follow-Up Protocol**
- **Action:** Provide the patient with written crisis resources: National Suicide Prevention Lifeline (988), Crisis Text Line, local emergency services, hospital emergency department. Establish a clear follow-up plan with specific date, time, and contact method. Discuss what circumstances warrant emergency care.
- **Example:** "Here are the resources you can use if you're in crisis. You can call 988 anytime, day or night. We'll meet again on [specific date] at [specific time]. If you feel unsafe before then, please go to the emergency department or call 911. Can you tell me back what you'll do if you start having strong urges to hurt yourself?"
- **Practice:** Develop a standard crisis resource handout for all high-risk patients. Practice explaining when to use each resource.

### 4. **Conduct Bipolar-Specific Assessment and Treatment Planning**
- **Action:** Assess medication adherence, current mood state, and bipolar-specific symptoms. Coordinate with the patient's psychiatrist regarding mood stabilization. Implement mood monitoring (e.g., daily mood tracking on 0-10 scale). Educate the patient about bipolar II disorder, including early warning signs of mood episodes.
- **Example:** "I want to make sure your medications are working well for you. Are you taking your lithium and sertraline as prescribed? How's your sleep been? Have you noticed any changes in your energy or thoughts? Let's set up a simple mood chart so we can track how you're feeling each day."
- **Practice:** Review bipolar II disorder treatment guidelines. Learn to differentiate depressive rumination from hypomanic racing thoughts. Coordinate care with psychiatry.

### 5. **Reframe Cognitive Work: Use Behavioral Activation First, Cognitive Work Second**
- **Action:** Recognize that direct cognitive challenge is ineffective in acute depression. Prioritize behavioral activation and behavioral experiments before cognitive restructuring. Once mood improves slightly, introduce systematic cognitive work.
- **Example:** "I notice that when I suggest thinking about things differently, it feels fake to you right now. That makes sense—when we're in deep depression, positive thinking can feel impossible. Let's focus first on getting you moving and reconnected with people. As you do that, your thoughts may naturally shift. Then we can work on the thoughts more directly."
- **Practice:** Study the behavioral model of depression. Practice sequencing interventions appropriately for depression severity.

### 6. **Use Socratic Questioning Instead of Directive Suggestions**
- **Action:** Replace statements like "Maybe it would help to think about..." with genuine questions that facilitate discovery: "What do you think might help?" "What have you tried before?" "What would you tell a friend in this situation?" "What evidence do you have for that thought?"
- **Example:** Instead of "Maybe it would help to think about a time when someone showed they care about you," ask: "Have there been times when you felt less alone or cared for? What was that like? What made a difference?"
- **Practice:** Record yourself in role-play sessions and count directive statements vs. Socratic questions. Aim for 70%+ Socratic questions. Practice with a supervisor.

### 7. **Establish Explicit Agenda and Prioritization**
- **Action:** At the beginning of each session, collaboratively set an agenda that prioritizes problems by severity and impact. Allocate time accordingly. For high-risk patients, safety should always be the first agenda item.
- **Example:** "I want to make sure we use our time well. Based on what you've told me, I'm most concerned about your safety and the thoughts you're having about ending your life. I also want to understand what's been happening with your mood and energy. And we can talk about ways to feel less stuck. Does that sound right? What's most important to you to focus on today?"
- **Practice:** Develop a standard agenda-setting template. Practice with role-play patients.

### 8. **Implement Structured Behavioral Activation with Specificity and Monitoring**
- **Action:** Replace vague suggestions ("do small activities") with structured behavioral activation: identify valued activities, schedule specific activities with time/place, monitor mood before and after, gradually increase activity level.
- **Example:** "Let's identify some activities that matter to you or that you used to enjoy. [Patient identifies activities.] Now let's pick one or two to schedule this week. When could you do them? For how long? Let's write it down. And let's track your mood before and after so we can see if it helps."
- **Practice:** Learn the behavioral activation protocol. Practice scheduling activities with specificity. Use a mood monitoring form.

### 9. **Develop Systematic Thought Records for Cognitive Work**
- **Action:** When introducing cognitive work, use structured thought records that guide the patient through: situation, automatic thought, emotion, evidence for/against, alternative thought, outcome. Do not suggest alternatives; guide the patient to discover them.
- **Example:** "Let's use this form to understand your thoughts better. When you woke up this morning and thought 'I'm a burden,' what was happening? What made you think that? What evidence do you have for that thought? What evidence might go against it? What else could you think instead?"
- **Practice:** Use the thought record template consistently. Practice guiding patients through the process without suggesting answers.

### 10. **Elicit and Respond to Feedback Regularly**
- **Action:** Build feedback loops into every session. Ask: "Does this make sense?" "How are you feeling about this approach?" "Is this helpful?" "What's your honest reaction?" "Are we working on what matters most to you?" Adjust based on patient input.
- **Example:** "I want to check in with you. How are you feeling about the things we've talked about today? Is this approach helpful? Is there anything you'd like to do differently?"
- **Practice:** Set a goal to ask for feedback at least 3 times per session. Practice adjusting your approach based on patient feedback.

### 11. **Provide Clear, Specific, Rationale-Based Homework**
- **Action:** Assign homework that is specific, has clear rationale, is feasible, and is collaboratively developed. Always review prior homework. For high-risk patients, include safety-related homework (e.g., safety plan review, crisis resource familiarization).
- **Example:** "This week, I'd like you to keep a simple mood chart—just rate your mood 0-10 each morning and evening. This will help us see patterns. I'd also like you to schedule one activity you used to enjoy, even if it's just 15 minutes. And let's review your safety plan together—can you keep it somewhere you can find it easily? We'll talk about how it went when we meet next week."
- **Practice:** Develop a homework template that includes: task, rationale, specificity, feasibility check, and prior homework review.

### 12. **Consult with Psychiatry and Establish Coordinated Care**
- **Action:** Ensure the patient is under active psychiatric care with regular medication monitoring. Communicate with the psychiatrist about the patient's suicidal ideation, current mood state, and treatment plan. Coordinate on medication adjustments, hospitalization decisions, and crisis protocols.
- **Example:** "I want to make sure we're coordinating your care. I'm going to reach out to your psychiatrist to discuss what we've talked about today and make sure your medications are optimized. If you're having thoughts about ending your life, we may need to consider more intensive treatment, like hospitalization, to keep you safe."
- **Practice:** Develop a standard protocol for communicating with psychiatry. Learn when to recommend hospitalization.
