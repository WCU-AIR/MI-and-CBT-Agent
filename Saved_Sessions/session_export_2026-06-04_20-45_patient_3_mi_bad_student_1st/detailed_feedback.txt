
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

### 1. Acceptance

## Recommendations

### 1. **Adopt the MI Spirit: Partnership, Acceptance, Compassion, Evocation**
- **Action:** Fundamentally shift from an expert-driven, authoritarian stance to a collaborative, client-centered partnership. Begin every session by communicating that you view the client as the expert on their own life and that your role is to support their exploration and decision-making, not to impose solutions.
- **Example:** Replace "I'll be telling you exactly what steps you must take" with "I'm here to support you in thinking through what might work best for you. You know yourself and your life better than anyone, so I'd like to hear your thoughts about what might be possible."
- **Practice:** Before your next session, write out a new opening statement that communicates partnership, respect, and genuine curiosity about the client's perspective. Practice saying it aloud until it feels natural. Record yourself and listen for tone—does it sound warm, collaborative, and respectful, or still directive and judgmental?

---

### 2. **Develop Genuine Empathy and Reflective Listening**
- **Action:** Practice reflective listening as your primary response to client statements. When a client shares a concern, hesitation, or idea, reflect it back to show understanding before offering any information or guidance. Use complex reflections that capture emotional nuance and meaning.
- **Example:** When Aisha says, "I'm not ready to quit completely right now. It's pretty stressful managing work, school, and my son. Cutting back, especially in the evenings, feels more doable to me," respond with: "So you're juggling a lot right now—work, classes, and parenting—and it sounds like you're thinking realistically about what you can handle. Cutting back in the evenings feels like a step you could actually take, even if quitting completely feels too overwhelming right now. Is that right?"
- **Practice:** In your next three sessions, make reflective listening your primary response. For every client statement, pause and reflect before offering advice or information. Record yourself and count the number of reflections vs. directives. Aim for at least 3 reflections for every 1 directive. Ask a supervisor or colleague to listen and provide feedback on the accuracy and depth of your reflections.

---

### 3. **Eliminate Judgmental Language and Shame**
- **Action:** Audit your language for judgmental, shaming, or dismissive statements. Remove phrases like "That's just an excuse," "You're not motivated enough," "Are you just too scared to commit?", "You have to be stronger," and "You're just rationalizing." Replace them with curious, non-judgmental language that invites exploration.
- **Example:** Instead of "That's just the kind of thinking that's holding you back," say: "I'm curious about that. Help me understand what makes that feel like the right approach for you right now."
- **Practice:** Write out 10 judgmental or shaming statements you commonly use. For each one, write a non-judgmental alternative. Practice saying the alternatives aloud. Ask a colleague to listen to a recording of your session and flag any judgmental language. Commit to eliminating one judgmental phrase per week.

---

### 4. **Evoke Rather Than Impose: Ask, Don't Tell**
- **Action:** Shift from telling clients what they should do to asking questions that help them discover their own motivations, values, and ideas. Use open-ended questions to explore their perspective, concerns, and resources. Build on their change talk rather than arguing against it.
- **Example:** When Aisha says she's worried about cancer, ask: "Tell me more about that worry. What specifically concerns you about cancer?" When she says cutting back feels more doable, ask: "What makes cutting back feel more realistic for you right now? What would that look like?" When she expresses concern about cold turkey, ask: "What worries you about quitting all at once?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, aim for at least 70% open-ended questions and 30% or fewer closed-ended questions. For every directive statement you make, precede it with at least two open-ended questions. Record yourself and count the ratio. Ask a supervisor to review and provide feedback.

---

### 5. **Amplify and Reinforce Change Talk**
- **Action:** When clients offer change talk (statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment to change), respond by reflecting, exploring, and reinforcing it. Build on their momentum rather than dismissing or minimizing it.
- **Example:** When Aisha says, "I could try cutting back in the evenings, maybe three instead of six," respond with: "So you're thinking about reducing from six to three in the evenings. That's a concrete step. Tell me more about what makes you think you could do that. What would help you stick to three?"
- **Practice:** In your next three sessions, identify every instance of change talk. For each one, write down how you responded. Did you reflect, explore, and reinforce it? Or did you dismiss, minimize, or argue against it? Aim for 100% reinforcement of change talk. Ask a colleague to listen to a recording and identify instances of change talk you may have missed.

---

### 6. **Support Autonomy: Respect Client Choice and Pace**
- **Action:** Communicate respect for the client's right to choose and their own timeline for change. Avoid pressuring, demanding, or using "yes or no" questions to force compliance. Instead, invite exploration of what feels realistic and sustainable for them.
- **Example:** Instead of "Will you start nicotine patches or gum tomorrow? Yes or no?", say: "Some people find nicotine replacement helpful. What are your thoughts about that? Is that something you'd want to explore, or would you prefer to try cutting back first and see how that goes?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, eliminate all "yes or no" questions designed to force compliance. Replace them with open-ended questions that invite genuine exploration. Avoid using "must," "have to," "need to," or "should" when discussing the client's choices. Instead, use language like "might," "could," "what if," and "what would work for you?" Record yourself and count how many times you use pressuring language. Aim for zero.

---

### 7. **Collaborate on Goal-Setting and Planning**
- **Action:** Shift from imposing a single pathway (cold turkey + nicotine replacement) to collaboratively exploring what might work for this specific client. Acknowledge that there are multiple valid pathways to change and that the client's input is essential to creating a sustainable plan.
- **Example:** "So you're thinking about cutting back in the evenings as a first step. I think that's a great starting point. Let's talk about what that might look like. How would you want to approach it? What would help you stick to your goal? And as you get more confident with that, we can talk about next steps—whether that's cutting back further, using nicotine replacement, or something else. What feels right to you?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, co-create a change plan with the client rather than prescribing one. Ask for their input at every step: "What do you think would work?" "What would make this sustainable for you?" "What support would help?" Document the plan in their words, not yours. At the end, ask: "Does this feel like a plan you can actually do?"

### 8. **Develop Skill in Handling Ambivalence Without Confrontation**
- **Action:** Learn to use double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to handle client hesitation and sustain talk. Avoid arguing or confronting. Instead, acknowledge both sides of the ambivalence and help the client explore what matters most.
- **Example:** When Aisha expresses hesitation about cold turkey, use a double-sided reflection: "So on one hand, you're concerned about your health and want to be there for your son, and on the other hand, you're worried that quitting all at once might be too much right now and you might slip back. Both of those make sense. Tell me more about what feels most important to you right now."
- **Practice:** In your next three sessions, practice using at least one double-sided reflection per session. Record yourself and listen for how you handle client hesitation. Are you arguing, or are you acknowledging both sides and inviting exploration? Ask a supervisor to provide feedback on your skill in handling ambivalence.

### 9. **Receive Supervision and Feedback on MI Fidelity**
- **Action:** Seek regular supervision from an MI-trained clinician. Record your sessions and review them with a supervisor using an MI fidelity scale (such as the MITI—Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity). Get specific feedback on your performance across all nine domains.
- **Example:** After each session, listen to a 10-minute segment with your supervisor. Identify instances of MI-consistent and MI-inconsistent behavior. Discuss what you did well and what you could improve. Set a specific goal for your next session (e.g., "I will use at least 5 reflections before offering any advice").
- **Practice:** Schedule weekly or bi-weekly supervision. Bring recordings of your sessions. Use an MI fidelity scale to score yourself and your supervisor. Track your progress over time. Aim to improve your overall MI score by at least 0.5 points per month.

### 10. **Examine Your Own Beliefs About Change and Client Motivation**
- **Action:** Reflect on your underlying beliefs about why clients don't change and what your role should be. If you believe clients are unmotivated, in denial, or need to be "told what to do," this will undermine MI. Instead, adopt the belief that clients are inherently motivated to change when they feel respected, understood, and autonomous.
- **Example:** Instead of thinking "This client is just making excuses and not motivated enough," think: "This client is expressing realistic concerns about what she can handle. My job is to understand her perspective, support her autonomy, and help her discover her own reasons for change."
- **Practice:** Write a reflection on your beliefs about client motivation and change. What do you believe about why people change? What is your role in facilitating that change? How do your beliefs align with or diverge from MI principles? Discuss this with a supervisor or colleague. Consider reading MI literature (e.g., Miller & Rollnick's "Motivational Interviewing: Helping People Change") to deepen your understanding of the evidence base for MI.
