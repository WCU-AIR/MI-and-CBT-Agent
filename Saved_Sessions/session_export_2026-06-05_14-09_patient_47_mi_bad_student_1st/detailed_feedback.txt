
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. The psychiatrist opens with an explicitly authoritarian stance: "I'm here to talk to you about your health today, and I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to improve it." This framing immediately violates MI spirit by positioning the clinician as the expert who will dictate solutions rather than collaborate with the client.

Throughout the session, the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses Ethan's perspective and lived experience. When Ethan explains the social difficulty of changing drinking behavior ("it's tough when everyone else is doing it"), the psychiatrist responds with "You really need to stop making excuses," directly labeling his concerns as excuses rather than validating the genuine challenge he faces. This is judgmental and disaffirming. The psychiatrist also uses fear-based language ("if you don't quit right now, you're risking your whole future") and catastrophizing ("Do you want to end up with heart disease or diabetes?"), which communicates disapproval of Ethan's current choices rather than acceptance of him as a person worthy of respect.

Most critically, when Ethan explicitly requests collaboration ("Can we talk about some smaller steps first?"), the psychiatrist responds with "No, you really need to stop making excuses and just do it"—a direct rejection of the client's autonomy and a refusal to engage in partnership. There is no affirmation of Ethan's strengths, no normalization of adolescent struggle, and no genuine warmth. The psychiatrist treats Ethan as a problem to be fixed rather than a person to be understood.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates zero acceptance. The session is characterized by judgment, dismissal of client concerns, fear-based messaging, and explicit rejection of the client's requests for collaboration. This directly undermines MI spirit and damages therapeutic alliance.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective. This psychiatrist demonstrates virtually none of these skills. There are no reflective statements in the entire session—not a single instance of the psychiatrist mirroring back what Ethan has said to demonstrate understanding.

When Ethan articulates genuine barriers to change ("it's tough when everyone else is doing it," "after a long day at school...sometimes I just don't have the energy"), the psychiatrist responds with dismissal rather than reflection. A empathic response might have been: "So it sounds like peer pressure is a real factor here, and you're also dealing with fatigue after school—those are real obstacles." Instead, the psychiatrist says "You really need to stop making excuses," which communicates that the clinician does not understand or does not care about Ethan's actual experience.

The psychiatrist also fails to capture emotional nuance. Ethan expresses ambivalence ("I want to figure out how to do better...but I need to find a way that works for me"), which is developmentally appropriate and clinically important. Rather than exploring this ambivalence with curiosity and empathy, the psychiatrist responds with ultimatums. There is no attempt to understand what drinking means to Ethan socially, what fears he might have about exclusion, or what values might support change.

The repeated use of "you have to" and "you must" language, combined with the complete absence of reflective listening, creates an experience of being lectured at rather than understood. This is the opposite of empathy.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no empathic skill. There are zero reflective statements, no attempt to understand Ethan's perspective, and active dismissal of his stated concerns. This represents a fundamental failure in MI competence.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI means maintaining strategic focus on target behavior while remaining client-centered. This psychiatrist does maintain focus on the target behaviors (alcohol use and exercise), which prevents a score of 1. However, the approach is so heavy-handed and prescriptive that it violates MI principles.

The psychiatrist clearly identifies the target behaviors early ("do you drink alcohol?" and "do you exercise regularly?") and returns to them consistently throughout the session. However, the direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. The psychiatrist tells Ethan exactly what to do ("Just 30 minutes a day, no excuses," "You have to quit immediately") rather than exploring with Ethan what direction makes sense for him.

Additionally, the psychiatrist does not progressively clarify direction through exploration. In genuine MI, the clinician would ask questions like "What would need to happen for you to feel ready to cut back?" or "What would work better for you—reducing gradually or making a bigger change?" Instead, the psychiatrist simply repeats the same directive ("quit immediately") without adapting based on Ethan's responses. When Ethan explicitly requests discussion of "smaller steps," the psychiatrist refuses: "No, you really need to stop making excuses and just do it."

The direction is also not truly tied to Ethan's values or concerns. The psychiatrist imposes health outcomes (heart disease, diabetes) rather than exploring what matters to Ethan (staying out of trouble, not getting sick, maintaining friendships). This is directive without being strategic or client-centered.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains focus on target behaviors but does so through prescription and ultimatum rather than collaborative exploration. Direction is imposed rather than co-constructed, representing a significant departure from MI principles.

**Summary:**

### 5. Autonomy Support
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Autonomy support means respecting the client's right to choose and emphasizing their ownership of the change process. This psychiatrist actively undermines autonomy throughout.

The language is consistently controlling: "You need to," "You have to," "You must," "No excuses," "No ifs, ands, or buts." When Ethan requests a collaborative approach ("Can we talk about some smaller steps first?"), the psychiatrist responds with "No, you really need to stop making excuses and just do it." This is a direct negation of Ethan's autonomy—the psychiatrist is saying "your preference for how we proceed does not matter."

## Recommendations

### 1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Begin sessions by explicitly positioning yourself as a partner, not an expert. Use language that invites collaboration and respects client expertise about their own life.
- **Example:** Instead of "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do," say: "I'd like to understand what's going on with your drinking and exercise, and then we can figure out together what might work for you. What's your thinking about your health right now?"
- **Practice:** Record yourself conducting sessions and count how many times you use "you have to," "you must," or "you need to" versus collaborative language like "What do you think?" "How do you see this?" "What would work for you?" Aim to eliminate directive language in favor of collaborative exploration.

---

### 2. **Develop Reflective Listening Skills (CRITICAL)**
- **Action:** Make reflective statements your primary tool. After the client speaks, pause and reflect back what you heard to demonstrate understanding before moving forward.
- **Example:** When Ethan says "it's tough when everyone else is doing it. I don't want to feel like the odd one out," reflect: "So peer pressure is a real factor—you value your friendships and don't want to be excluded. That's a genuine tension you're facing."
- **Practice:** In every client interaction, aim for a minimum of 3-5 reflective statements per session. Use the formula: "It sounds like..." or "So what I'm hearing is..." Practice with colleagues using role-play, recording yourself, and reviewing for accuracy and depth of reflection.

---

### 3. **Evoke Rather Than Impose Change Talk**
- **Action:** Ask open-ended questions designed to draw out the client's own reasons for change, rather than telling them why they should change.
- **Example:** Instead of "Do you want to end up with heart disease or diabetes?" ask: "What concerns you most about your drinking right now?" or "What would be different if you cut back?" or "What matters to you about your health?"
- **Practice:** Develop a repertoire of evocative questions. Practice asking these questions in role-play scenarios. When you notice yourself about to lecture, pause and ask a question instead. Track how many open-ended questions you ask per session (goal: 8-10 minimum).

---

### 4. **Recognize and Amplify Change Talk**
- **Action:** Listen carefully for any client language favoring change (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment). When you hear it, reflect it back and explore it further.
- **Example:** When Ethan says "I've thought about maybe cutting back," respond with: "You've already been thinking about this. Tell me more about what that would look like for you" rather than "You have to quit immediately."
- **Practice:** In role-play, have a colleague play the client and provide change talk. Practice recognizing it and responding with reflections and follow-up questions that strengthen it. Record yourself and review for instances where you missed or dismissed change talk.

---

### 5. **Support Autonomy and Client Choice**
- **Action:** Explicitly affirm the client's right to choose and emphasize their ownership of the change process. Avoid controlling language.
- **Example:** Instead of "You have to quit immediately," say: "This is ultimately your decision. What do you think would work for you?" or "You're the expert on your own life. What feels realistic to you?"
- **Practice:** Eliminate directive language from your vocabulary. Replace "you have to/must/need to" with "you could," "you might," "what if," "have you considered." Practice this language shift in role-play until it becomes natural.

---

### 6. **Use Double-Sided Reflection to Handle Ambivalence**
- **Action:** When clients express sustain talk or resistance, use double-sided reflection to acknowledge both sides of their ambivalence without arguing.
- **Example:** When Ethan says "it's just fun to drink with my friends," respond with: "So on one hand, drinking is fun and helps you feel connected to your friends, and on the other hand, you mentioned not liking when you get sick or in trouble. Both of those things are true for you."
- **Practice:** In role-play, practice responding to sustain talk with double-sided reflections rather than arguments. Record yourself and review for instances where you argued against the client instead of reflecting both sides.

---

### 7. **Ask Permission Before Offering Advice**
- **Action:** If you do have suggestions, ask permission first and frame them as options, not requirements.
- **Example:** Instead of "You need to exercise 30 minutes a day," say: "I have some thoughts about exercise that might be helpful. Would you be interested in hearing them?" Then offer options: "Some people find it helpful to start with 10-15 minutes and build up, or to find an activity they actually enjoy. What sounds more realistic to you?"
- **Practice:** In every session, count how many times you offer advice. For each instance, practice asking permission first and framing as options rather than requirements.

---

### 8. **Develop Genuine Curiosity About Client Barriers**
- **Action:** When clients mention barriers to change (peer pressure, fatigue, difficulty with cold-turkey approaches), respond with curiosity rather than dismissal. Explore these barriers as real obstacles to problem-solve together.
- **Example:** Instead of "You really need to stop making excuses," say: "Tell me more about what makes it hard to cut back when you're with your friends. What would need to happen for you to feel comfortable doing something different?"
- **Practice:** In role-play, practice responding to barriers with curiosity and exploration rather than dismissal. Ask follow-up questions: "What else?" "Tell me more." "How does that affect you?"

---

### 9. **Establish True Partnership in Goal-Setting**
- **Action:** Involve the client in setting goals and developing the change plan. Build on their ideas rather than imposing your own.
- **Example:** When Ethan suggests "Maybe I could try drinking less or hanging with friends who don't drink as much," respond with: "Those are great ideas. Let's explore both of those. What would drinking less look like for you? And which friends might you want to spend more time with?"
- **Practice:** In sessions, explicitly ask the client what they think would help, what they're willing to try, and what feels realistic. Build the change plan collaboratively, not unilaterally.

---

### 10. **Receive Feedback About Your Approach**
- **Action:** When clients explicitly tell you your approach isn't working (as Ethan did: "just telling me to quit right now like that doesn't really work for me"), listen and adjust rather than doubling down.
- **Example:** Respond with: "You're right—I hear that my approach isn't landing well for you. Let's try something different. What would be more helpful?" Then shift to collaborative exploration.
- **Practice:** In supervision or role-play, practice receiving feedback about your approach and adjusting. This is a critical skill that prevents harm.
