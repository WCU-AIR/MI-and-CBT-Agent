### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal true collaboration, adopting expert-driven, directive stance throughout. Clinician frames predetermined agenda without seeking client input. When client offers boundary ("not really looking to talk about anything major"), clinician redirects rather than honoring it as collaborative starting point. Makes unilateral suggestions without seeking input: "Maybe using nicotine gum," "It would probably help if you set some goals," "You might want to reconsider nicotine replacement." When client offers own idea (cutting to three cigarettes), clinician affirms but immediately adds prescriptive suggestions. Power is not shared; clinician's agenda drives session.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist frequently undermines client autonomy through subtle and overt pressure, directive language, and failure to respect stated boundaries. Most egregiously, when client states "I'm not really here to talk about my drinking," clinician responds with "But just to check..." directly contradicting stated preference. Uses prescriptive language minimizing choice: "should," "would probably help," "maybe you should focus." When client expresses reluctance about nicotine replacement, clinician does not respect preference but suggests reconsidering. Does not emphasize client control of change process. Autonomy support is minimal; clinician's agenda takes precedence over client choice and ownership.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist relies on advice-giving and problem-identification rather than evoking client's own motivations, values, and ideas. When client spontaneously offers change talk ("I could try to smoke less in the evenings..."), clinician affirms but does not deepen or explore. Fails to evoke further exploration of deepest motivation ("Being a mother, I don't want something to happen to me"). Does not ask strategic open questions designed to elicit change talk about desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment. Misses opportunities to evoke client's own resources and strengths (already not smoking around son, considering change, has supportive sister). Client's own wisdom and resources are not drawn out.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role; power is not genuinely shared. Clinician positions self as authority on what client needs through repeated statements: "It would probably help if we understood," "It would probably help if you set some goals," "Try to notice what makes you want to smoke." Language patterns reveal power imbalance: "you should," "you might want to," "it would probably help if you." Does not seek client input on change plan. Fails to acknowledge client's expertise about her own life (schedule, stress management, preferences, relationships). One moment of partnership occurs when client suggests telling sister, but this is single instance in otherwise directive session. Clinician's knowledge and recommendations drive session.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal skill in actively encouraging client's own language favoring change. When client spontaneously offers change talk ("I could try to smoke less..."), clinician responds with simple reflection affirming idea but does not evoke further change talk through follow-up questions. When client expresses deepest motivation ("Being a mother, I don't want something to happen to me"), clinician responds with scaling question that reduces emotional power rather than exploring meaning. Does not ask strategic open questions designed to evoke change talk about desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment. Fails to deepen or reinforce change talk when it emerges. Does not ask: "What makes you confident?" "What would help you succeed?" "Tell me more about that?" Change talk is acknowledged but not amplified.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates inconsistent skill in handling client arguments for status quo. Skillfully accepts client's preference for smaller change (cutting down vs. quitting completely) rather than confronting sustain talk—this is good reframing. However, misses opportunities to address minimization ("I wouldn't say it's a huge problem," "I don't smoke much compared to people at work"). Does not use double-sided reflections to acknowledge client's perspective while gently redirecting to values. Directly confronts boundary statement ("I'm not here to talk about my drinking") with "But just to check..." rather than softening it. Some responses are skillful (accepting smaller changes), but approach is inconsistent and misses strategic opportunities to address sustain talk.

## Summary Assessment

**Strengths:**
- Warm, respectful opening sets positive tone
- Basic reflective listening demonstrates awareness of MI skill
- Accepts client's preference for smaller change (cutting down vs. quitting)
- Provides concrete, measurable goal at session end
- Affirms client's willingness to attempt change

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- **Expert-driven stance:** Presents predetermined solutions rather than collaboratively exploring client's ideas
- **Failure to evoke change talk:** Misses critical opportunity when client spontaneously links behavior to core value (motherhood, health concerns)
- **Overrides autonomy:** Subtly pressures discussion of topics client explicitly declined; suggests solutions client resisted
- **Prescriptive language:** Frequent use of "should," "would probably help," conveys clinician's agenda
- **Shallow empathy:** Lacks emotional depth; fails to capture meaning and nuance of client statements
- **Missed partnership:** Does not seek client input on change plan; fails to acknowledge client expertise about own life
- **Inadequate sustain talk management:** Misses opportunities to address minimization; directly confronts boundaries

---

## Clinical Impact

**Likely Outcome:** Short-term compliance (client will probably attempt cutting to three cigarettes) but **weak intrinsic motivation**. Without deeper exploration of core values and genuine partnership, commitment is fragile. If client encounters difficulty, she may not persist. Clinician's expert-driven approach may activate psychological reactance, particularly given client's stated preference for autonomy ("I need to handle this myself").

**Recommendation:** Shift from expert-driven, prescriptive stance to genuine MI partnership that:
1. Evokes and amplifies client's own motivations (especially statements linking behavior to values)
2. Respects autonomy boundaries completely
3. Co-constructs change plan with client input
4. Uses collaborative language emphasizing client choice and expertise
5. Deepens spontaneous change talk through strategic follow-up questions
