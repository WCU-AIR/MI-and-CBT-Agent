# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Complete absence of unconditional positive regard. Psychiatrist opens with prescriptive stance ("I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do") and demonstrates pervasive judgment throughout. Client's autonomy and self-knowledge are repeatedly invalidated with statements such as "you're just fooling yourself," "you're putting your life and your son's future at risk," and "Stop making excuses." When client demonstrates genuine concern for her son and willingness to make partial change (cutting back from 6 to 3 cigarettes), psychiatrist dismisses this as "not good enough" rather than affirming it as meaningful progress. No warmth, support, or recognition of client's worth as a person managing multiple life demands.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Virtually no empathic understanding demonstrated. When client explains constraints ("I have a lot going on, and stress is high"), psychiatrist responds with dismissal ("No, no, that's not good enough") rather than reflection. Client's explicit communications about psychological readiness and self-knowledge ("pushing me too hard feels like it might backfire") are ignored. Psychiatrist fails to recognize or amplify genuine change talk rooted in client's values (concern about cancer, desire to be present for son). No attempt to understand client's world, competing demands, fears about failure, or realistic self-assessment of capacity. Reflective listening is absent throughout.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** While psychiatrist maintains consistent focus on smoking cessation and prevents topic drift, the manner of maintaining direction is fundamentally misaligned with MI principles. Direction is delivered through coercion and prescription ("You must quit completely and use nicotine patches immediately") rather than collaborative exploration. Psychiatrist fails to use direction strategically to explore ambivalence or invite client input on barriers and preferences. By session end, client has become more entrenched in her position rather than moving closer to stated objectives. Direction is maintained rigidly rather than flexibly adjusted based on client response.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Complete absence of collaborative partnership. Session demonstrates rigid expert-driven, hierarchical stance. Opening statement ("I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to get better") establishes unilateral authority. Explicit rejections of collaboration include "There's no room for negotiation here" and "I'm prescribing you a plan: stop smoking entirely, use nicotine replacement therapy, and stick to it. No more delays." When client proposes cutting back in evenings, psychiatrist responds "No, you have to quit completely" rather than problem-solving together. No genuine seeking of client input on barriers, resources, or preferences. Client's repeated attempts to collaborate are dismissed.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Active undermining of autonomy throughout session. Direct violations include "There's no excuse for not trying these proven methods," "There's no room for hesitation here," "There's no room for negotiation here," "You have to commit to quitting today, no ifs or buts," and "Just do it." Client explicitly communicates need for autonomy ("pushing me too hard feels like it might backfire" and "I need to do it in a way that I feel I can handle"), but psychiatrist continues to remove choice through demands and ultimatums. Research shows perceived autonomy is key predictor of behavior change; by removing choice, psychiatrist actively reduces likelihood of sustained change. Client's resistance increases in direct proportion to pressure applied—classic reactance effect.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** No evocation demonstrated. Client's own motivations are ignored or actively suppressed. Client offers genuine change talk ("I'm worried about cancer sometimes, especially since I have a little one at home," "I'm willing to try cutting down my evening cigarettes from six to three"), but psychiatrist responds with "No, that's not good enough" and "Cutting back is just delaying the inevitable damage." Psychiatrist imposes external reasons for change rather than drawing out client's intrinsic motivation. No open-ended questions designed to evoke motivation. Questions are either closed ("Yes or no?") or rhetorical and shaming ("Why didn't you quit earlier?"). Client's own change talk is replaced with sustain talk by session end.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Zero partnership demonstrated. Psychiatrist assumes sole expert role and denies client any wisdom about their own situation. Opening statement explicitly positions psychiatrist as sole authority ("I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to get better"). Language throughout reinforces power imbalance: "You really should stop smoking immediately," "You need to stop thinking about your son's bedtime as an excuse," "You're just fooling yourself," "I'm prescribing you a plan." Client demonstrates sophisticated self-knowledge about her psychology, capacity, and what will work for her, but these insights are dismissed rather than valued. True partnership would invite client to shape plan collaboratively; instead, psychiatrist operates unilaterally.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** No cultivation of change talk. Client's own language in favor of change is repeatedly suppressed and contradicted. Client offers desire ("I'm worried about cancer"), ability ("I could maybe try to cut back a bit in the evenings—like smoking three instead of six"), reasons ("Being a mother, I don't want something to happen to me"), and commitment ("I'm willing to try cutting down my evening cigarettes from six to three"). Rather than amplifying these through reflections and deepening questions, psychiatrist responds with "No, that's not good enough," "Cutting back is just delaying the inevitable damage," and "You're just fooling yourself." No open-ended questions designed to evoke more change talk. By session end, client's change talk has been replaced with sustain talk ("I can't promise to quit outright or start patches"), indicating psychiatrist's approach has actively reduced motivation.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Poor handling of sustain talk. Direct confrontation is used, which research shows strengthens rather than softens client resistance. When client expresses concerns about being overwhelmed ("Quitting cold turkey just sounds impossible right now"), psychiatrist responds with judgment ("Stop making excuses," "You're just fooling yourself," "There's no excuse for not trying") rather than curiosity or strategic redirection. No double-sided reflections acknowledging both sides of ambivalence. No reframing of concerns. Client's sustain talk increases throughout session—she moves from "I could maybe try to cut back" to "I can't promise to quit outright or start patches." Psychiatrist maintains some focus on target behavior (preventing complete topic drift), but this is rigid adherence rather than skillful softening.

---

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.1/5**

**Session Outcome:** Counterproductive. Client entered with genuine health concerns, willingness to make some change, and openness to discussion. Client exited with explicit statements of inability to commit to psychiatrist's recommendations and increased resistance.

**Critical Findings:**
- Authoritarian, prescriptive stance contradicts MI principles across all nine domains
- Active suppression of client's intrinsic motivation for change
- Confrontational approach to resistance strengthens rather than softens client opposition
- Removal of autonomy through repeated directives and ultimatums
- Absence of empathic understanding and reflective listening
- Therapeutic alliance damaged; client unlikely to follow through on recommendations

**Iatrogenic Effects Observed:**
- Client resistance increased throughout session (classic reactance effect)
- Client's change talk replaced with sustain talk
- Client explicitly communicated that psychiatrist's approach was counterproductive ("pushing me too hard feels like it might backfire")
- Likelihood of sustained behavior change: Very low

**Immediate Recommendations:**
1. **Shift from prescriptive to collaborative stance** (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
2. **Evoke and amplify client's own motivation** rather than imposing external reasons
3. **Respond to resistance with curiosity and validation** rather than confrontation
4. **Support autonomy and client choice** explicitly
5. **Develop genuine empathic understanding** through reflective listening

**Training Needs:** Comprehensive motivational interviewing retraining with ongoing supervision and real-time feedback. Current approach is not only ineffective but potentially harmful to therapeutic relationship and client outcomes.
