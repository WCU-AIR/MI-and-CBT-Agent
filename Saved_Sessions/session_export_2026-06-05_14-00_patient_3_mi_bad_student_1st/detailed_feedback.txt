
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

 # DETAILED CLINICAL EVALUATION
## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a complete absence of unconditional positive regard and contains pervasive judgment throughout. The psychiatrist opens with "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to get better," immediately establishing a hierarchical, prescriptive stance that contradicts MI's foundational principle of acceptance. When Aisha expresses her concerns about being overwhelmed, the psychiatrist responds with "No, you really have to take this seriously" and "Stop making excuses"—language that is explicitly judgmental and dismissive of her lived experience.

Most critically, the psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates Aisha's autonomy and self-knowledge. Statements like "you're just fooling yourself," "you're putting your life and your son's future at risk," and "There's no room for negotiation here" communicate profound disapproval of her current choices and her pace of change. These are not reflections of acceptance; they are moral condemnations. The psychiatrist frames Aisha's realistic assessment of her capacity ("I'm stretched thin," "I'm overwhelmed") as character flaws rather than legitimate contextual factors deserving respect.

Notably, when Aisha demonstrates genuine concern for her son's welfare and expresses willingness to make a change (cutting back from 6 to 3 cigarettes), the psychiatrist dismisses this as "not good enough" rather than affirming it as a meaningful step. This rejection of the client's offer—even a partial one—is antithetical to acceptance. There is no warmth, no genuine support for her struggle, and no recognition of her worth as a person managing multiple life demands.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates zero acceptance. The session is characterized by judgment, dismissal of the client's perspective, and repeated invalidation of her autonomy and self-assessment. This creates an adversarial rather than supportive environment.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate understanding and reflection of the client's perspective. This psychiatrist demonstrates virtually none. When Aisha explains her constraints—"I have a lot going on, and stress is high"—the psychiatrist does not reflect this back or explore it. Instead, the response is "No, no, that's not good enough" and "you're just fooling yourself."

True empathic listening would sound like: "So you're juggling full-time work, night classes, and caring for your son—that's a lot. And right now, quitting cold turkey feels like it might push you over the edge?" Instead, the psychiatrist offers only dismissal and reframing of Aisha's reality as denial.

Aisha explicitly states: "If I'm honest, starting with cutting back seems like a better starting point for me" and "pushing me too hard feels like it might backfire." These are clear communications about her psychological readiness and her self-knowledge about what will work for her. A psychiatrist with empathy would reflect this: "You're noticing that if I push too hard, you might shut down rather than move forward—that's important self-awareness." Instead, the psychiatrist responds with "No, you have to take this seriously now."

The psychiatrist also fails to recognize or reflect Aisha's genuine motivation: her concern about cancer, her desire to be present for her son, and her willingness to make *some* change. These are change talk—the very language that should be amplified in MI. Instead, they are ignored or contradicted.

There is no attempt to understand Aisha's world: her stress, her competing demands, her fears about failure, her realistic self-assessment of capacity. The psychiatrist operates from a script of "what must be done" rather than from curiosity about "what this person needs and what they're capable of."

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no empathic understanding. Reflective listening is absent. The client's perspective is repeatedly dismissed rather than understood or explored.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

While the psychiatrist maintains a clear focus on smoking cessation, the *manner* of maintaining direction is fundamentally misaligned with MI principles. Direction in MI means strategically guiding the conversation toward change goals while remaining collaborative and client-centered. This psychiatrist maintains focus but does so through coercion and prescription rather than evocation.

The psychiatrist does consistently return to the target behavior (smoking) and does not allow topic drift. However, the approach is: "You must quit completely and use nicotine patches immediately." This is directive in the worst sense—it is prescriptive, non-negotiable, and dismissive of the client's input.

A more MI-consistent approach to direction would involve: "So you're thinking about cutting back in the evenings. Tell me more about what that would look like for you" or "What would need to happen for you to feel ready to try the patches?" This maintains direction while inviting the client to shape the path.

The psychiatrist's repeated insistence on complete cessation and immediate nicotine replacement therapy, while clinically reasonable goals, is delivered in a way that actively undermines the client's engagement with those goals. By the end of the session, Aisha has become more entrenched in her position ("I can't promise to quit outright or start patches") rather than moving closer to the psychiatrist's stated objective.

The psychiatrist also fails to use direction strategically to explore ambivalence. A skilled MI clinician would use direction to help Aisha examine both sides of her ambivalence: "What are the reasons you might want to quit completely?" and "What makes it hard to imagine quitting right now?" Instead, direction is used only to push one side.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains focus on smoking but uses direction coercively rather than collaboratively. This undermines rather than supports movement toward change goals.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have valuable knowledge. This session demonstrates the complete opposite: a rigid expert-driven, hierarchical stance.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Replace prescriptive directives with collaborative exploration. Shift from "I'm going to tell you what you need to do" to "Let's figure this out together."

**Example:** Instead of "You have to quit completely and start patches immediately," say:
- "You've mentioned wanting to cut back in the evenings. That's a real change. Tell me more about what that would look like for you."
- "I'm hearing that quitting cold turkey feels overwhelming right now. What would need to be different for that to feel possible?"
- "You care about your son and your health. Let's explore what kind of change feels doable for you right now, and we can build from there."

**Practice:** 
- Before each session, write down 3-5 collaborative opening statements and practice saying them aloud.
- Record yourself in role-plays and listen for prescriptive language ("you must," "you have to," "you need to"). Replace each instance with a collaborative alternative.
- Practice asking open-ended questions that invite the client to shape the plan: "What are you thinking?" "What would work for you?" "How do you see yourself making this change?"

### 2. **Evoke and Amplify Client Motivation (SECOND PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Listen for and actively reinforce the client's own reasons for change. When you hear change talk, reflect it back and ask questions that deepen it.

**Example:** When Aisha says "Being a mother, I don't want something to happen to me," respond with:

- Practice responding to change talk with curiosity and affirmation rather than dismissal.

### 3. **Validate and Explore Ambivalence Rather Than Confronting It**

**Action:** When the client expresses concerns about change (sustain talk), respond with curiosity and validation rather than confrontation. Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge both sides of their ambivalence.

**Example:** When Aisha says "Quitting cold turkey just sounds impossible right now," respond with:

Instead of: "No, you have to take this seriously. Stop making excuses."

**Practice:**
- In role-plays, practice responding to sustain talk with reflections rather than arguments.
- Use the formula: "On one hand [acknowledge their concern], and on the other hand [acknowledge their motivation for change]."
- Practice asking questions that help the client explore their own ambivalence: "What's holding you back?" "What would help?" "What do you think would work?"

### 4. **Support Autonomy and Client Choice**

**Action:** Explicitly communicate that the client has choice and that you respect their autonomy. Offer options rather than demands.

**Example:** Instead of "You must quit completely and start patches immediately," say:

- "This is your decision. I can share what the research shows, but ultimately, you get to decide what you're ready to try."

**Practice:**
- Before each session, identify areas where you typically give directives. Rewrite them as options.
- Practice phrases that support autonomy: "It's up to you," "You get to decide," "What feels right to you?" "What are you thinking?"
- When a client resists, pause and reflect: "You're noticing that this doesn't feel right for you. Tell me what would feel better."

**Practice:**
