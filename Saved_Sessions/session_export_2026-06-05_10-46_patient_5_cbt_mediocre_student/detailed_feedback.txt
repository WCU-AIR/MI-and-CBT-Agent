
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrates surface-level understanding of Nicholas's explicit statements but misses critical clinical nuances and the patient's underlying experience. When Nicholas describes waking up shaky and sweaty with racing thoughts about failure, the therapist accurately reflects the content: "it sounds like when you woke up shaky and sweaty, you had some pretty intense thoughts about failing and losing your wife." This is basic empathic reflection.

However, the therapist fundamentally misses the distinction between withdrawal symptoms and anxiety-driven cognitions. Nicholas's "shakiness" and "sweat" are physiological withdrawal symptoms—autonomic hyperactivity requiring medical assessment and potentially pharmacological management (benzodiazepines, thiamine, magnesium). The therapist treats these as anxiety symptoms amenable to cognitive restructuring and distraction, which is clinically inappropriate. A therapist with adequate understanding would recognize: "Your hands shaking and sweating are physical withdrawal symptoms. We need to make sure you're medically safe and may need medication to help your body stabilize. Let's also work on the thoughts, but the physical symptoms need medical attention."

Additionally, Nicholas explicitly states, "Trying to think, 'I'm doing my best,' sounds nice, but honestly, it feels kind of empty when I'm in the thick of it." This is a critical communication: the patient is telling the therapist that cognitive restructuring alone feels hollow and ineffective during acute distress. A therapist with strong understanding would recognize this as resistance rooted in the inadequacy of the intervention for acute withdrawal and would adjust: "I hear you—when your body is in withdrawal, a thought alone won't fix the physical symptoms. Let's talk about what medical support might help, and then we can work on thoughts too."

Instead, the therapist responds by reframing the thought as "kinder" and suggesting the patient "practice" it—doubling down on an intervention the patient has already indicated is insufficient.

In Session 2, when Nicholas reports that "even though I'm trying to tell myself those kinder thoughts, sometimes it just feels like the cravings and worries take over," the therapist does not recognize this as a sign that cognitive interventions alone are failing. The therapist does not explore: Are you having withdrawal symptoms? Are you drinking to relieve physical discomfort? Do you need medical support? Instead, the therapist continues with cognitive work: "Maybe it helps to remind yourself that these thoughts are just your mind's way of warning you."

The therapist also misses the severity and chronicity of Nicholas's substance use disorder. **Summary:**

The therapist demonstrates basic empathic listening but misses critical clinical distinctions: withdrawal symptoms vs. anxiety, inadequacy of cognitive interventions for acute withdrawal, and the severity of AUD requiring multimodal treatment. Understanding is superficial and clinically unsafe.
