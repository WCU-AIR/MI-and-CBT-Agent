### Direction

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1

Evidence includes: unilateral problem definition ("we need to talk about your smoking"), rhetorical questions designed to pressure ("Do you realize how important it is...?"), directives without consultation ("Just start with that tomorrow"), dismissal of client input ("I understand you're trying to express your feelings, but..."), and no solicitation of client ideas or preferences. Client explicitly requests collaboration ("Maybe we could explore what that might look like together?"); psychiatrist does not respond to this invitation. ### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Completely absent; psychiatrist actively undermines autonomy throughout. Prescriptive language dominates: "you really should quit now," "you must take this seriously," "you need to set a quit date now," "just start tomorrow," "there's no room for hesitation," "if you don't, your health will keep getting worse." Client explicitly expresses autonomy needs ("I want to do it on my terms," "I want to make sure I'm ready," "I want my quit plan to be something I own"); psychiatrist treats these as obstacles rather than strengths. Directives, demands, and threats create pressure that triggers reactance and undermines client's sense of ownership over change.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Entirely absent. Psychiatrist employs "telling" approach, repeatedly imposing reasons for change without exploring client's intrinsic motivations. Client demonstrates substantial intrinsic motivation ("I want to take care of myself," "I want to respect myself," "I want changes that feel right and stick," "I've been thinking about what really matters to me"). These powerful change talk statements go unrecognized and unreinforced. Psychiatrist ignores client's intrinsic motivation and substitutes external reasons ("you really should quit," "your health will keep getting worse," "there's no time to waste"). No open-ended questions designed to evoke client's wisdom, values, or resources.

### Softening Sustain Talk

Her only "sustain talk" is repeated expression of wanting to approach change thoughtfully ("I want to do it on my terms," "I'm not ready to start tomorrow," "I want to feel confident")—which are actually expressions of readiness concerns and autonomy needs, not true sustain talk. Psychiatrist does not reinforce resistance, which is appropriate. However, this is not skillful softening of sustain talk; it is simply absence of sustain talk to begin with. Psychiatrist misinterprets Elena's readiness concerns as resistance rather than wisdom, but does not actively reinforce them.

### Key Findings

**Strengths:**
- Maintains clear topical focus on smoking cessation
- Does not actively reinforce or amplify client resistance
- Demonstrates genuine concern for client's health (though misapplied)

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Complete absence of acceptance, empathy, and collaboration—the foundational pillars of MI
- Authoritarian, expert-driven stance that suppresses rather than evokes client motivation
- Active undermining of client autonomy through directives, demands, and threats
- Fundamental misinterpretation of client's thoughtfulness as resistance rather than wisdom
- Failure to recognize and amplify substantial intrinsic motivation and change talk
- Dismissal of client strengths (values clarification, self-awareness, desire for sustainable change)

---

### Impact on Client Engagement and Likelihood of Sustained Change

**This session significantly reduces the likelihood of sustained behavior change.** While Elena enters with intrinsic motivation and readiness to explore change, the psychiatrist's controlling, pressuring approach will likely trigger reactance, resentment, and disengagement. Research on MI consistently demonstrates that autonomy-supportive approaches increase adherence and sustained behavior change, while controlling approaches decrease both.

**Likelihood of evoking client motivation: Very Low.** Client may comply superficially (set a quit date to end session) but will not develop sustained intrinsic motivation. This approach may damage the therapeutic relationship and reduce client's willingness to engage in future mental health treatment.

---

### Recommendation

**This psychiatrist requires formal Motivational Interviewing training and ongoing clinical supervision.** Without significant change in approach, this clinician will continue to undermine client motivation and reduce treatment effectiveness. A fundamental mindset shift from "expert-authority" to "collaborative partner" is essential.
