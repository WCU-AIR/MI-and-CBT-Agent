### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal unconditional positive regard. While opening is warm, clinician quickly shifts to problem-identification and solution-imposition. Critical missed affirmation: when patient states "22 days clean," clinician bypasses opportunity to celebrate this milestone meaningfully. Prescriptive stance undermines acceptance throughout. Patient's ambivalence is treated as deficit rather than normal change process. No explicit affirmation of patient's worth or autonomy.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Reflective listening is superficial and often inaccurate, missing emotional nuance. When patient expresses hopelessness ("why bother?"), clinician responds with closed questions seeking information rather than understanding. Fails to capture emotional core of patient's fear about losing friends. Misses deeper experience of stigma and rejection regarding employment barriers. Surface-level reflections lack complexity and fail to deepen patient's self-understanding. Generic reflections dominate; emotional attunement is absent.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3/5
**Comments:** General focus on substance abstinence and pro-social transition is appropriate but muddled. Direction lacks strategic coherence—multiple targets introduced without exploring patient's own goals and readiness first. Solutions presented prematurely before adequate exploration of ambivalence. Session fails to strategically address patient's most salient intrinsic motivators (relationship with Fiona, small joys, identity). Clinician focuses on external barriers (job training, support programs) without connecting to deeper values. Sustain talk accepted without exploration or reframing.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** **Critical deficiency.** Psychiatrist adopts expert-driven, directive stance throughout. Consistently tells patient what to do rather than exploring options together. Prescriptive language dominates: "Maybe you should think about trying some programs," "You might want to write down what you want to say," "Maybe setting a specific time to make the call could keep you on track." Disguised collaboration—clinician predetermines plan and seeks rubber-stamp approval. Never asks patient: "What do you think would help?" or "What feels right to you?" True partnership and shared decision-making entirely absent.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** **Fundamental violation of MI principles.** Psychiatrist consistently undermines patient's autonomy and sense of choice. Prescriptive language frames suggestions as clinician's recommendations, not patient's choices. No explicit affirmation that "this is your decision to make." When patient expresses low confidence (4/10), clinician responds with encouragement and concrete steps rather than exploring what would genuinely support autonomy. Assumes readiness for action without exploring whether patient believes this is right time/approach. Ambivalence treated as something to overcome, not honored as legitimate. Patient positioned as passive recipient of expert advice, not active agent of change.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Minimal effort to evoke patient's own motivations, values, and resources. Critical missed opportunities: (1) Relationship with Fiona mentioned but never deeply explored—powerful intrinsic motivator left undeveloped; (2) Small joys ("cup of tea on porch") revealed but never revisited to explore meaning; (3) Identity strength ("I stick to decisions") never amplified; (4) Barriers to change talk accepted without exploration—"I'm lazy" and "Maybe I can't" never reframed; (5) No exploration of past successes or resources; (6) Clinician tells patient what should motivate him rather than asking what actually motivates him. Only mild evocation occurs with scaling question, but used to assess readiness for predetermined action, not to evoke deeper motivation.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** **Virtually non-existent.** Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role; wisdom and expertise positioned with clinician, not patient. Clinician controls agenda without exploring what patient thinks is important. Solutions provided rather than co-created. Problem framed by clinician without exploring how patient understands his situation. Next steps determined by clinician rather than collaboratively identified. Unequal power dynamic maintained throughout via soft directives ("You might want to..."). Patient's input not genuinely sought or valued—when offered, briefly acknowledged but not incorporated into session direction. True partnership would involve asking patient to lead problem-solving and genuinely considering his ideas as equally valid.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Minimal effort to evoke, amplify, or reinforce change talk. When change talk emerges, often unrecognized or not strategically developed. Critical missed moments: (1) "22 days clean" (commitment/ability)—clinician pivots to sustain talk instead of amplifying; (2) "Maybe I should give it a shot" (desire/ability)—acknowledged but not deepened; (3) "Maybe there's a version of me worth sticking around for" (identity/motivation)—generic response, not explored; (4) No strategic questioning designed to elicit desire, ability, reasons, or commitment; (5) Change talk not reinforced with follow-up questions; (6) Clinician tells patient why he should change rather than asking him to articulate own reasons. Only mild evocation via scaling question, but used to assess readiness for predetermined action rather than to evoke deeper motivation.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Minimal skill in addressing sustain talk. When patient expresses doubt, self-criticism, or barriers, often accepted without strategic response. Critical missed opportunities: (1) "I'm lazy"—clinician accepts and reinforces rather than gently challenging; (2) "If I get a job I hate, I won't stick with it"—reframed as barrier rather than explored for meaning; (3) "Maybe I can't"—not addressed directly; (4) Stigma/rejection concerns—met with practical advice rather than addressing underlying sustain talk. No double-sided reflections acknowledging both sides of ambivalence. No reframing that opens possibility. No exploration of sustain talk to understand it more deeply. Sustain talk sometimes inadvertently reinforced through clinician's acceptance of self-criticism.

## **Summary Assessment**

**MI Competency Level:** Pre-MI to Early-MI (Foundational skills present but MI-specific competencies underdeveloped)

**Key Strengths:**
- Basic warmth and courtesy maintained throughout
- Some reflective listening attempted
- General focus on substance abstinence and pro-social transition appropriate
- Concrete action planning provided (specific first step identified)
- Mild acknowledgment of patient progress

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- **Expert-driven stance dominates** — Prescriptive language and predetermined solutions violate core MI principle of partnership
- **Evocation minimal** — Patient's intrinsic motivations (Fiona, identity, values, small joys) not explored or amplified
- **Change talk not strategically cultivated** — Significant change talk moments missed or not deepened
- **Sustain talk accepted passively** — Self-criticism and doubts not gently challenged or reframed
- **Autonomy undermined** — Prescriptive language and predetermined planning reduce patient's sense of choice and ownership
- **Empathy superficial** — Reflections miss emotional depth, shame, fear, and identity struggle
- **Affirmations absent** — 22-day achievement not meaningfully celebrated; strengths not identified
- **Ambivalence not explored** — Treated as problem to overcome rather than normal part of contemplation

---

## **Clinical Impact**

**Likelihood of Evoking Sustained Intrinsic Motivation:** LOW TO MODERATE

This session is **unlikely to significantly increase patient's intrinsic motivation** for sustained pro-social change. While patient may comply with action plan (calling support service) due to external pressure or desire to please clinician, without genuine exploration of intrinsic motivations, resolution of ambivalence, and autonomy support, relapse risk remains high. Session reflects **transactional approach** (clinician directs) rather than **transformational approach** (clinician evokes patient's own motivation).

---

## **Priority Recommendations**

1. **Shift from expert to collaborative stance** — Eliminate prescriptive language; replace with open questions and collaborative exploration
2. **Evoke and amplify intrinsic motivation** — Strategically explore patient's relationship with Fiona, identity, values, and small joys
3. **Strategically cultivate change talk** — Use DARN-CAT framework; amplify change talk through follow-up questions
4. **Meaningfully affirm progress and strengths** — Celebrate 22-day achievement; identify specific strengths
5. **Explore and honor ambivalence** — Use double-sided reflections; explore both sides with genuine curiosity
6. **Soften sustain talk** — Gently challenge self-criticism; reframe barriers; explore underlying concerns
7. **Deepen empathy** — Move to complex reflections capturing emotional nuance and meaning
8. **Support autonomy throughout** — Communicate that patient's choices matter; emphasize his ownership of change
9. **Collaboratively plan action steps** — Co-create steps reflecting patient's ideas and readiness, not clinician's agenda
10. **Engage in formal MI training** — Pursue MI certification and supervision to develop competencies

---

**Recommendation:** Psychiatrist should engage in structured MI training and clinical supervision. With focused practice and feedback, significant improvement in MI competency is achievable.
