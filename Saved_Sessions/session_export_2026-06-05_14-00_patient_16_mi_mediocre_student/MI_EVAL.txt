# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal acceptance demonstrated. While opening establishes warm tone, psychiatrist quickly undermines unconditional positive regard through subtle judgment ("It sounds like you might be at risk"), pressure tactics ("It's important to act before things get worse"), and failure to affirm patient strengths or autonomy. Psychiatrist positions himself as arbiter of what patient "should" do rather than genuinely accepting patient's current perspective. No affirmation of existing health behaviors or normalization of ambivalence.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Empathy largely absent. Reflective listening is minimal and superficial—only factual summaries provided rather than emotionally attuned reflections. Psychiatrist never explores patient's internal experience, values, or worldview. Multiple missed opportunities for complex reflections (e.g., when patient states "Everything seems fine," no exploration of what "fine" means or why urgency feels absent). No genuine curiosity about patient's emotional state, fears, hopes, or resistance. Reflections fail to capture emotional nuance or deepen understanding of patient's perspective.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clear focus on hypertension management and lifestyle change maintained throughout session. However, direction becomes problematic when it shifts from collaborative guidance to prescriptive control. Psychiatrist directs patient toward predetermined outcomes (higher importance/confidence ratings, more aggressive lifestyle changes) rather than collaboratively exploring patient's own desired direction. Psychiatrist's goals are never clarified with patient. Direction is entirely provider-driven without exploration of patient's goals or meaningful change from patient's perspective.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Collaboration virtually absent. Psychiatrist consistently positions himself as expert with correct answers, telling patient what he "should" do ("You should probably think about making some changes soon") rather than asking what patient thinks would help. When patient expresses perspective ("I'm already doing what I'm comfortable with," "I don't see a need to push too hard"), psychiatrist counters rather than engages it as valid input. No evidence of seeking patient input on treatment plan, exploring anticipated barriers, or co-constructing solutions. Power dynamic entirely hierarchical—expert and passive recipient, not partners.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy fundamentally undermined throughout session. Psychiatrist pressures and prescribes rather than fostering perception of choice: "It's important to act before things get worse," "you might want to push that number higher," "You should probably think about making some changes soon." Never affirms patient's right to choose or emphasizes patient ownership. When patient expresses comfort level ("I'm already doing what I'm comfortable with"), psychiatrist does not respect boundary but pushes for more. Session ends with compliance language ("Sure, I can try") rather than ownership language—predictable outcome of autonomy violation.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation almost entirely absent. Psychiatrist relies on telling rather than asking. Provides own reasons for change ("If you don't, the risk of heart problems can increase") without exploring patient's reasons. Questions are closed and information-gathering rather than evocative ("Have you been taking medication?" "Do you have family history?"). Leading questions presuppose barriers exist ("Do you find it hard to exercise?"). Never asks about patient's own reasons for change, values, strengths, or ideas. No exploration of what matters most to patient or what would make change feel important. Imposes external motivation (fear, clinical urgency) rather than evoking intrinsic motivation.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership fundamentally violated. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role and maintains this stance consistently, positioning himself as authority on what patient needs. Never acknowledges patient's expertise about own life, body, readiness for change, or barriers. When patient states "I'm already doing what I'm comfortable with," psychiatrist does not honor this as valid self-knowledge but suggests patient should do more. No statements affirming patient's self-knowledge or inviting patient's perspective as expert contribution. Power dynamic entirely hierarchical with psychiatrist speaking with authority and patient responding with brief compliance. Patient's contributions do not substantially influence session direction.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk minimally evoked and not strategically reinforced. Patient offers some change talk ("I can try watching salt better," "I'll give it a shot," "I do some walking sometimes"), but psychiatrist does not amplify or strengthen this language. When patient expresses willingness to cut salt, psychiatrist pivots away and introduces new demands. Psychiatrist never asks evocative questions designed to elicit desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment. Weak change talk that emerges is compliance-based ("Sure," "I'll give it a shot") rather than intrinsically motivated. Psychiatrist fails to recognize low importance/confidence ratings (4 and 5) as sustain talk indicators requiring evocative response, instead pressuring patient to increase ratings.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Sustain talk handled poorly throughout session. Patient's resistance ("Nothing really feels wrong," "Everything seems fine," "I'm already doing what I'm comfortable with," "I don't see a need to push too hard") is met with direct contradiction and pressure rather than skillful softening. Psychiatrist argues against sustain talk ("you might want to push that number higher," "it's really better to start early"), which typically strengthens resistance. No empathic reflection, reframing, or strategic redirection employed. Psychiatrist never uses double-sided reflection or explores ambivalence gently. Confrontational approach likely strengthens patient's resistance rather than softening it. Patient's responses become more entrenched and noncommittal ("I'll think about it").

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.4/5**

### Strengths
- Clear opening rapport with warm, welcoming tone
- Consistent focus on target behavior (hypertension management) throughout session
- Organized closing with summary of agreed-upon steps
- Maintains respectful, professional demeanor

### Critical Deficiencies
This session demonstrates **fundamental misalignment with Motivational Interviewing principles** across nearly all domains. The core problem is operation from an **expert-driven, directive model** rather than an **evocative, collaborative model**:

1. **Absence of genuine empathy**: Never explores patient's worldview, values, or concerns; reflections are factual summaries only
2. **Pressure instead of evocation**: Counters ambivalence with clinical reasoning rather than evoking intrinsic motivation through curiosity
3. **Autonomy violation**: Repeatedly prescribes what patient "should" do, generating compliance not commitment
4. **No partnership**: Positions self as expert with correct answers; never acknowledges patient's expertise about own life
5. **Minimal change talk evocation**: Provides own reasons for change; weak change talk that emerges is compliance-based
6. **Confrontation of sustain talk**: Argues against resistance, likely strengthening it rather than softening it

### Impact on Client Engagement
Session is **unlikely to generate sustained behavior change**. Patient's agreement is compliance-based and noncommittal ("Sure," "I'll try," "I'll think about it"). No genuine internal motivation articulated. Research demonstrates intrinsic motivation predicts sustained change; external pressure predicts relapse. Session may increase psychological reactance and damage therapeutic alliance by communicating lack of understanding, disrespect for autonomy, and pressure to comply with clinician's agenda rather than patient's own values.

### Recommendation
Psychiatrist requires comprehensive Motivational Interviewing training focused on: shifting from expert-driven to evocative stance, developing genuine empathic reflections, respecting autonomy, understanding that client's own motivation—not clinician pressure—predicts sustained change, and embodying MI spirit of partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation.
