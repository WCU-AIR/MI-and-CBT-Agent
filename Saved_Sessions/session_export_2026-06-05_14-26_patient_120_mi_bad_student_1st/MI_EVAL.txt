# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance. From the opening statement ("I'll be telling you exactly what steps to take"), an authoritarian framework is established. The clinician repeatedly invalidates Selene's experience, dismissing concerns as "excuses" ("Time is just an excuse," "No, no, you're wrong to think that way"). There is zero affirmation of Selene's strengths—her non-smoking status, genuine health concerns, and willingness to consider change are never acknowledged. The message communicated throughout is one of rejection and shame rather than support. By the final exchange, Selene's capitulation appears coerced rather than genuinely motivated.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The session contains zero reflective listening and demonstrates active resistance to understanding Selene's perspective. When she explains time constraints, the response is dismissal ("Time is just an excuse"). When she expresses fear and overwhelm, the psychiatrist explicitly rejects empathic engagement ("This isn't about your feelings or time"). There are no complex reflections that deepen insight or validate her ambivalence as normal. The repeated use of confrontational language ("No," "you're wrong," "you're not getting it") communicates: *I don't care to understand your perspective; I only care that you comply.* Selene's emotional state shifts from cooperative to increasingly defensive and resigned—a sign that the approach is counterproductive and unrecognized by the clinician.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains clear, consistent direction on two specific behaviors (complete alcohol cessation and three weekly exercise sessions). However, this direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. The clinician never explores why these targets matter to Selene, never asks about her priorities, and never invites her input into goal-setting. Direction is delivered as non-negotiable mandate ("You have to," "No negotiations, no excuses"). Critically, the psychiatrist fails to use direction strategically to evoke client motivation. When Selene offers compromise ("small walks a week," "cutting down"), these are dismissed rather than explored. This represents rigid direction without flexibility—ignoring evidence that incremental change often precedes sustained behavior modification.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** This session demonstrates the complete absence of collaboration—it is entirely unilateral and expert-driven. From the opening ("I'll be telling you exactly what steps to take"), a hierarchical, non-collaborative framework is established. The psychiatrist never asks Selene's input on goals, never invites problem-solving, and repeatedly rejects her suggestions without exploration ("No, cutting down isn't enough," "No, small steps won't cut it"). When Selene offers collaborative language ("Can we maybe start with small steps?" "Can't we find a way to work with that?"), every invitation to partnership is refused. The power dynamic is entirely one-directional: psychiatrist as expert issuing commands; Selene as passive recipient expected to comply. This is the antithesis of collaboration.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** This session is characterized by systematic undermining of autonomy and explicit denial of client choice. The psychiatrist repeatedly communicates that Selene has no choice: "You have to," "You must," "There's no room for hesitation," "This isn't optional," "No negotiations, no excuses." When Selene expresses her own preferences and limitations, the response is pressure and coercion, not autonomy support. The psychiatrist fails to affirm Selene's self-efficacy or capacity for self-direction. There is no language like "It's your choice" or "You know yourself best." Instead, the message is: *You cannot be trusted to make good decisions; I will decide for you.* Research demonstrates that pressure and control reduce intrinsic motivation and increase reactance. Selene's final capitulation appears to be compliance under duress rather than genuine commitment.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** This session demonstrates the complete absence of evocation—it is entirely prescriptive and directive. The psychiatrist never asks open-ended questions designed to elicit Selene's own reasons for change, values, or resources. Instead, only closed, leading questions designed to secure compliance are used ("Are you willing to commit?" "Are you going to do it or not?"). Critically, Selene offers genuine change talk throughout ("I want to stay healthy," "I want to avoid health problems," "I'm willing to think about that," "I'm open to trying") that the psychiatrist completely fails to evoke or amplify. A skilled MI clinician would recognize these as precious opportunities to deepen motivation. Instead, they are ignored entirely. This represents a fundamental failure to evoke—the core of MI.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** This session demonstrates the complete absence of partnership—it is entirely expert-driven and hierarchical. The psychiatrist explicitly assumes the sole expert role and denies client expertise: "I'll be telling you exactly what steps to take," "You're wrong to think that way," "You're not getting it," "This isn't about what you feel or want; it's about what you have to do." The language throughout is unilateral and controlling ("You have to," "You must," "No exceptions," "No negotiations"). There is no inclusive language like "we," "let's," or "together." When Selene attempts to establish partnership ("Can we work on a plan that's firm but also realistic for me?"), the psychiatrist explicitly rejects these invitations. Partnership is not a technique; it is the foundational stance that makes all other MI skills possible. Without it, MI becomes coercive counseling—which is what this session demonstrates.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Selene offers multiple instances of genuine change talk that should be amplified: desire ("I want to stay healthy," "I want to do better"), reasons ("I don't want my health to get worse," "I don't want to regret it"), and commitment ("I'm willing to think about that," "I'm open to trying"). A skilled MI clinician would recognize these as critical moments to deepen motivation through reflective listening and exploration. Instead, the psychiatrist completely ignores Selene's change talk and continues with prescriptive demands. When she says "I'm willing to think about that," the response is "No, that's not good enough." When she says "I'm open to trying," the response is "No, small steps won't cut it." This represents not merely a failure to cultivate change talk but active suppression of it. The repeated "No" responses communicate: *Your motivation is insufficient; your willingness is not enough.* This is the opposite of cultivation—it is extinguishment.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal skill in softening sustain talk. When Selene offers reasons against change ("It's mostly a time thing," "It's just a little wine," "Jumping straight to 'quit everything' feels overwhelming"), the response is confrontation rather than skillful reflection: "Time is just an excuse," "That little bit of wine? It's still damaging," "No, cutting down isn't enough." Research demonstrates that confronting sustain talk directly typically strengthens it through psychological reactance. By dismissing her real-world constraints as "excuses," the psychiatrist communicates that her concerns are invalid, likely increasing defensiveness and commitment to sustain talk. The psychiatrist does attempt to address sustain talk through fear appeals ("your health will get worse," "you'll regret it"), but these are used without the collaborative foundation needed for effectiveness and likely backfire when combined with pressure and control.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.1/5**

**Assessment Level:** Pre-MI (Traditional Directive Counseling)

This session represents a **severe and comprehensive failure of Motivational Interviewing practice**. The psychiatrist demonstrates deficiency across all nine MI competency domains, with scores of 1/5 in seven of nine areas. The session is characterized by authoritarianism, coercion, invalidation, and systematic suppression of client motivation.

**Critical Findings:**

- **No genuine partnership or collaboration** established at any point
- **Systematic invalidation** of client's lived experience and emotional reality
- **Active suppression** of multiple instances of genuine change talk
- **Confrontational handling** of sustain talk, likely strengthening resistance
- **Coercive pressure tactics** including ultimatums and false binaries
- **Complete absence** of reflective listening and empathic engagement

**Impact on Client Engagement:**

Selene begins with genuine ambivalence and some change talk ("I want to stay healthy," "I'm willing to think about that"). By the end, her language has shifted to compliance under duress ("I'll try to quit alcohol starting today"). This apparent agreement masks a critical problem: **Selene has not been motivated; she has been pressured.** Research predicts poor adherence and likely relapse once external pressure diminishes. Moreover, this negative experience may reduce her willingness to engage in future health interventions.

**Recommendation:**

The psychiatrist should not continue seeing clients for motivational interviewing until completing formal MI training and demonstrating competency through supervised practice. Continuing to practice in this manner risks harm to clients through reduced motivation, increased reactance, and damaged therapeutic relationships.
