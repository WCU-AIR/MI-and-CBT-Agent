
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance—the foundational pillars of MI acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates Selene's experience, dismisses her concerns as "excuses," and communicates explicit disapproval of her perspective throughout the interaction. 

From the opening statement ("I'll be telling you exactly what steps to take"), the clinician establishes an authoritarian, judgmental framework. When Selene explains her genuine time constraints and ambivalence, the psychiatrist responds with "No, no, you're wrong to think that way" and "Time is just an excuse"—directly contradicting her lived experience and communicating that her reality is invalid. This pattern intensifies across nine exchanges, with statements like "No, you're not getting it," "This isn't about what feels comfortable for you," and most damaging: "This isn't about your feelings or want; it's about what you have to do." These responses communicate fundamental rejection of Selene as a person worthy of respect.

Critically, there is zero affirmation of Selene's strengths. She explicitly states "I don't smoke" (a health asset), expresses genuine concern about health complications, and demonstrates willingness to consider change ("I want to stay healthy," "I'm willing to think about that"). The psychiatrist never acknowledges, normalizes, or affirm any of these positive elements. Instead, the clinician focuses exclusively on what Selene is doing "wrong," creating a climate of shame rather than support. By the final exchange, Selene's capitulation ("I'll try to quit alcohol starting today") appears coerced rather than genuinely motivated—a compliance born from pressure, not acceptance.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance. The session is characterized by judgment, invalidation, and rejection of the client's autonomy and experience. This directly violates MI spirit and creates a therapeutic climate antithetical to change motivation.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains clear but inflexible direction imposed without collaboration. Direction is used coercively rather than strategically to evoke motivation. This approach violates MI principles and likely reduces rather than enhances client commitment to change.

# Recommendations

### 1. **Fundamental Shift from Expert-Driven to Collaborative Partnership**
- **Action:** Establish true partnership from the opening. Use inclusive language ("we," "let's," "together") and explicitly invite client input into goal-setting and problem-solving.
- **Example:** Instead of "I'll be telling you exactly what steps to take," say: "I'm here to support you in figuring out what changes might work for you. What brought you in today, and what are you hoping we can explore together?"
- **Practice:** Before each session, write down three ways you will invite client input and three times you will use "we" language. Record sessions and count instances of collaborative vs. directive language. Aim for 70%+ collaborative language.

---

### 2. **Develop Genuine Empathic Listening and Reflective Responses**
- **Action:** Practice reflective listening as your primary response to client statements. Reflect back what you hear—both content and emotion—before offering any guidance.
- **Example:** When Selene says "Between work and family, it's hard to fit in," respond with: "So you're juggling a lot right now—work, family responsibilities—and finding time for exercise feels nearly impossible with everything on your plate. That's a real constraint you're facing." Then explore: "Tell me more about what a realistic schedule might look like for you."
- **Practice:** In your next 5 sessions, make reflective listening your first response to every client statement. Count the number of reflections you make. Aim for at least 3-5 reflections per client statement before offering any advice or direction.

---

### 3. **Learn to Evoke Rather Than Prescribe**
- **Action:** Replace directive statements with open-ended questions designed to draw out client's own motivations, values, and ideas. Ask rather than tell.
- **Example:** Instead of "You have to quit alcohol completely," ask: "What concerns you most about your drinking? What would be different if you cut back? What would staying healthy mean to you?" Listen for change talk and amplify it.
- **Practice:** Develop a repertoire of evocative questions:
  - "What worries you about your current health habits?"
  - "What would be the benefits of making a change?"
  - "What have you tried before that's worked?"
  - "What would need to happen for you to feel ready?"
  - "How confident are you that you could make this change?"
  - Record sessions and count: How many open-ended evocative questions did you ask? How many closed/directive statements did you make? Aim for 80%+ evocative questions.

---

### 4. **Recognize and Amplify Change Talk**
- **Action:** Train yourself to identify change talk (statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment to change). When you hear it, reflect it back and explore it further.
- **Example:** When Selene says "I want to stay healthy," respond with: "Staying healthy is really important to you. Tell me more about what that means and why it matters." Then ask: "What would be one small step you could take this week toward that goal?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, write down every instance of change talk you hear. After the session, review: Did you amplify it? Did you explore it? Did you reinforce it? Create a goal to amplify at least 80% of change talk you identify.

---

### 5. **Soften Sustain Talk Through Reflection and Reframing, Not Confrontation**
- **Action:** When you hear sustain talk (reasons against change), respond with double-sided reflections and strategic reframing rather than confrontation.
- **Example:** When Selene says "It's just a little wine, and I don't think it's causing any harm," respond with: "So on one hand, you enjoy having a glass of wine to unwind—that's important to you—and on the other hand, you're concerned about your health. Both of those things matter. I'm curious: what would help you feel like you could take care of both—your need to relax and your health?"
- **Practice:** Develop responses to common sustain talk:
  - Sustain talk: "I don't have time"
  - MI response: "Time is tight. What if we looked at small ways to move more that fit into what you're already doing?"
  - Sustain talk: "I feel fine most of the time"
  - MI response: "You feel okay now, and you also care about preventing problems down the road. Both make sense. What would help you balance those?"

---

### 6. **Support Autonomy and Client Choice**
- **Action:** Explicitly affirm client's right to choose. Use language that emphasizes client ownership and self-efficacy. Avoid ultimatums and false binaries.
- **Example:** Instead of "You have to quit alcohol completely or your health will suffer," say: "It's completely your choice what you do. You know yourself best. What do you think might work for you? What feels doable?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, count how many times you use controlling language ("have to," "must," "need to," "should") vs. autonomy-supportive language ("could," "might," "what do you think," "your choice"). Aim for 80%+ autonomy-supportive language.

---

### 7. **Develop Skill in Handling Ambivalence**
- **Action:** Recognize that ambivalence is normal and healthy. Rather than trying to eliminate it through pressure, help clients explore both sides and move toward change at their own pace.
- **Example:** When Selene expresses ambivalence ("I want to stay healthy, but I also need to be realistic"), respond with: "You're holding two important things—wanting to be healthy and being realistic about what you can manage. That makes sense. Let's explore both. What would realistic change look like for you?"
- **Practice:** Study the MI concept of "rolling with resistance." When you encounter client ambivalence or resistance, pause and ask yourself: "How can I explore this with curiosity rather than confrontation? What is the client trying to tell me?"

---

### 8. **Establish Collaborative Goal-Setting**
- **Action:** Invite client input into goals. Explore what matters to them, what they're ready for, and what feels doable. Co-construct goals rather than imposing them.
- **Example:** Instead of "You need to exercise three times a week and quit alcohol completely," say: "Let's think about what changes might make sense for you. What feels most important to address first? What would be a realistic first step? What support would help you succeed?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, use this structure:
  1. Ask: "What would you like to work on?"
  2. Listen and reflect
  3. Ask: "What feels like a realistic first step?"
  4. Listen and reflect
  5. Ask: "What would help you succeed?"
  6. Co-construct a plan based on client input

---

### 9. **Eliminate Confrontational Language and Pressure Tactics**
- **Action:** Remove from your vocabulary: "No," "You're wrong," "You're not getting it," "You have to," "No exceptions," "This isn't optional." Replace with collaborative, curious language.
- **Example:** Instead of "No, cutting down isn't enough," say: "I hear you're thinking about cutting down. Tell me more about what that would look like. What do you think would be a good target for you?"
- **Practice:** Record your next 3 sessions. Listen for confrontational language. Count instances. Create a goal to eliminate confrontational language entirely and replace with collaborative language.

### 10. **Pursue Formal Motivational Interviewing Training**
- **Action:** Enroll in a comprehensive MI training program (2-3 day workshop minimum). Practice with feedback from trained MI supervisors. This session demonstrates that self-directed learning is insufficient.
- **Example:** Organizations like the Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers (MINT) offer certified trainings. Seek out a trainer in your area.
- **Practice:** After training, engage in ongoing supervision with an MI-trained clinician. Record sessions and review them with feedback. Aim for at least monthly supervision for 6-12 months.
