
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Fundamental Shift from Expert-Driven to Collaborative Stance** (HIGHEST PRIORITY)

**Action:** Abandon the opening statement "I'll be telling you exactly what you need to do" and replace it with a collaborative invitation. Establish partnership from the first moment.

**Example:** 
- Instead of: "I'll be telling you exactly what you need to do to improve."
- Use: "I'd like to understand what's going on with you and what you think might be helpful. What brings you in today?"

Or after Lukas discloses drinking:
- Instead of: "You need to cut down immediately. Don't make excuses, it's just that simple."
- Use: "I hear you've been experimenting with alcohol at parties. I'm curious—what's been your experience with that? What have you noticed?"

**Practice:** Before every client session, write down your opening statement and ask: "Does this invite collaboration or impose direction? Would the client feel heard or judged?" Rewrite until it reflects genuine curiosity and partnership.

---

### 2. **Implement Reflective Listening as the Primary Response** (HIGHEST PRIORITY)

**Action:** For every client statement, pause and reflect back what you heard before offering any advice, judgment, or direction. Use both simple and complex reflections to demonstrate understanding.

**Example:**
- Client: "I've been noticing I've been feeling a bit off the next day sometimes."
- Instead of: "That's exactly why you need to stop drinking so much."
- Use: "So you're noticing some physical effects the next day. Tell me more about what that's like for you. How does that affect you?"

- Client: "I'm just trying to fit in a bit with my friends."
- Instead of: "Find new friends who don't drink."
- Use: "Fitting in with your friends is really important to you. I'm hearing that it's hard to say no when they invite you. What's that like?"

**Practice:** Record yourself in sessions (with permission) and count reflections vs. directives. Aim for a 3:1 ratio of reflections to directives. Use reflections to deepen understanding, not to set up for advice.

---

### 3. **Eliminate Judgmental Language and Shame-Based Messaging** (HIGHEST PRIORITY)

**Action:** Remove all pejorative language ("excuses," "weak," "rationalizing") and replace with neutral, curious language. Never attack the client's character.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "You're just making excuses. You have to stop being so weak."
- Use: "I hear you're thinking about this carefully. Help me understand what feels hard about quitting completely right now."

- Instead of: "That's not acceptable."
- Use: "I hear you're not ready for that right now. What would need to happen for you to feel ready?"

**Practice:** Before responding to client resistance, pause and ask yourself: "Am I about to shame or judge? If yes, reframe to curiosity." Use phrases like "Help me understand," "Tell me more," "I'm curious about," "That makes sense because..."

---

### 4. **Evoke Rather Than Impose: Ask Open Questions to Draw Out Client Motivation** (CRITICAL)

**Action:** Replace closed yes/no questions and directives with open-ended questions designed to elicit the client's own reasons for change, values, and ideas.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "Are you drinking more than three drinks a week? Yes or no?"
- Use: "Help me understand your drinking pattern. When do you drink? What's that like for you?"

- Instead of: "You need to cut down immediately."
- Use: "You mentioned feeling off the next day. What concerns you most about that? How does that fit with your goals as an athlete and student?"

- Instead of: "Why didn't you stop sooner if you felt bad?"
- Use: "You care about your performance in school and sports. How do you see drinking fitting with those goals? What would it look like to handle this in a way that honors what matters to you?"

**Practice:** Write out 5-10 open-ended questions specific to this client's situation before each session. Practice asking them aloud. Notice how they invite exploration rather than defensiveness.

---

### 5. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk: Recognize and Strengthen Client's Own Motivation** (CRITICAL)

**Action:** When the client expresses change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment for change), pause and amplify it through reflection and elaboration questions.

**Example:**
- Client: "I've been noticing I've been feeling a bit off the next day sometimes."
- Response: "You're noticing effects. That's important self-awareness. What does 'feeling off' mean for you? How does that affect your training or schoolwork?"

- Client: "I do care about my future—being an honors student and athlete matters to me."
- Response: "Those things are really important to you. Tell me more about what being an athlete means to you. How do you see drinking fitting with that?"

- Client: "Maybe I could think about cutting back more seriously."
- Response: "Cutting back—I'm glad you're thinking about that. What would cutting back look like for you? What would help you do that?"

**Practice:** In each session, identify at least 3 instances of change talk and write down how you amplified it. If you missed opportunities, note them and plan how you would respond differently next time.

---

### 6. **Validate Ambivalence Rather Than Dismiss It: Use Double-Sided Reflections** (CRITICAL)

**Action:** When the client expresses ambivalence (conflicting feelings about change), validate both sides and explore them collaboratively rather than dismissing one side as "excuses."

**Example:**
- Client: "I'm kind of stuck between wanting to do well and wanting to fit in. It's confusing."
- Instead of: "No, you're not stuck. You just need to stop making excuses."
- Use: "So on one hand, you want to do well in school and sports—that matters to you. And on the other hand, fitting in with your friends is also really important. Both of those are real. Tell me more about how you're thinking about balancing those."

- Client: "I don't want to lose my friends either."
- Use: "Losing your friends would be hard. That makes sense. I'm wondering—do you think your real friends would want you to hurt yourself? How do you think they'd respond if you talked to them about this?"

**Practice:** When you hear ambivalence, pause and reflect both sides before moving forward. Use language like "On one hand... and on the other hand..." This validates the client's experience and opens space for exploration rather than forcing a false choice.

---

### 7. **Support Autonomy: Emphasize Client Choice and Self-Efficacy** (CRITICAL)

**Action:** Replace controlling language ("you have to," "you must," "you need to") with autonomy-supportive language that emphasizes the client's right to choose and capacity to decide.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "You have to quit drinking completely starting today, yes or no?"
- Use: "This is your decision to make. What feels realistic and important to you right now? What would help you feel more confident about making a change?"

- Instead of: "That's not acceptable."
- Use: "I hear you're not ready for complete abstinence right now. You know yourself best. What do you think might work for you?"

- Instead of: "You have to be strong and say no."
- Use: "Saying no to your friends is hard. What do you think would help you feel more confident doing that? What's important to you that would make it worth it?"

**Practice:** Before each session, identify moments where you might use controlling language. Rewrite them using autonomy-supportive language. Notice how this invites collaboration rather than resistance.

### 8. **Explore Sustain Talk With Empathy Rather Than Confrontation: Reframe and Problem-Solve** (IMPORTANT)

**Action:** When the client expresses reasons for not changing (sustain talk), respond with empathy and exploration rather than confrontation. Help the client problem-solve around the underlying concerns.

**Example:**
- Client: "I'm just trying to fit in a bit with my friends."
- Instead of: "Find new friends who don't drink."
- Use: "Fitting in with your friends is really important. I get that. I'm wondering—are there ways you could stay connected with your friends while also taking care of yourself? What do you think?"

- Client: "I don't want to lose that either" (referring to social connection).
- Use: "That makes sense. Losing your friends would be hard. Let's think about this together. What do you think your real friends would want for you? How do you think they'd respond if you talked to them about wanting to cut back?"

**Practice:** When you hear sustain talk, resist the urge to argue or confront. Instead, ask: "What's the underlying concern here? How can I help the client problem-solve around this concern while respecting their autonomy?"

---

### 9. **Develop Genuine Curiosity About the Client's World: Slow Down and Listen** (IMPORTANT)

**Action:** Approach each client interaction with genuine curiosity about their experience, their world, their values, and their perspective. Slow down. Listen more than you speak.

**Example:**
- When Lukas says "It was a unique experience, as you can imagine," pause and ask: "Tell me about that experience. What was it like for you? What did you notice?"
- When Lukas mentions peer pressure, ask: "Help me understand your friend group. What's important about those friendships? What would happen if you said no?"
- When Lukas expresses ambivalence, ask: "What's the hardest part about this for you right now?"

**Practice:** In each session, aim to spend 70% listening and 30% speaking. Ask open questions and then *wait* for the client to respond fully. Resist the urge to fill silence or jump to advice. Notice what you learn when you truly listen.
