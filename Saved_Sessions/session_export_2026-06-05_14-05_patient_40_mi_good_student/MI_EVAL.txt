# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance throughout the session. Client values are affirmed ("staying healthy is deeply connected to your love and commitment to your wife") and strengths are recognized. However, deeper normalization of fear as a protective response to chronic illness and more explicit emotional warmth beyond professional validation would strengthen acceptance. The clinician respects the client's experience but could deepen genuine emotional resonance.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Solid reflective listening with several complex, emotionally attuned reflections that capture both content and emotional nuance ("That tension between wanting to move forward and the reality of physical discomfort seems like a heavy weight to carry"). The psychiatrist accurately identifies core values and fears. However, empathy could deepen through more exploration of emotional terrain beneath surface statements—for example, exploring what the confidence rating of "3" feels like emotionally, or the significance of the client's memory of his wife's past support.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist maintains clear, strategic focus on increasing physical activity within medical limitations. The session progresses logically from exploration to values clarification to concrete action planning without drift. However, the connection between identified values and the specific action plan could be made more explicit. Additionally, while the referral included both physical activity and medical adherence, the session focuses primarily on activity, leaving the medical adherence component incompletely addressed.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong collaborative stance with genuine power-sharing throughout. The opening question ("What's on your mind that you'd like to focus on?") establishes partnership, and the psychiatrist frequently seeks client input and perspective. Action planning is co-constructed with questions like "What do you think might be a first small step?" However, collaboration could strengthen by inviting more client-generated language rather than offering clinician-generated reflections (e.g., "It's like you want to be part of the dance of life"), and by explicitly inviting client feedback on the session itself.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Exemplary autonomy support demonstrated consistently throughout the session. The psychiatrist emphasizes client choice from the opening ("I want to make sure this time is useful for you") and uses language that positions the client as the agent of change ("What do you think might be a first small step?"). Critically, the clinician explicitly affirms client freedom: "And remember, it's completely your choice how and when to proceed." No pressure, directives, or "shoulds" are imposed. This is a clear strength of the session.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Adequate but inconsistent evocation of client's own motivations, values, and resources. Strong moments include asking about what matters most regarding activity and drawing out past resilience through the memory of his wife's support. However, significant missed opportunities exist: when the client expresses ambivalence or uncertainty, the psychiatrist tends to move relatively quickly to offering suggestions rather than asking exploratory questions. For example, when the client says "I'm not sure" about what small step to take, deeper evocation ("What do you think might be possible?") would have generated more client-generated insight. The action plan emerges somewhat from clinician suggestion rather than full client brainstorming.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong partnership demonstrated through genuine power-sharing and collaborative stance. The psychiatrist positions the client as the expert on his own experience and uses inclusive language. However, partnership could strengthen by reducing clinician-generated insights (e.g., the "dance of life" metaphor, while poetic, is clinician-generated rather than co-discovered) and by explicitly inviting client feedback on what was helpful in the session and what should be addressed next time.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Adequate but inconsistent cultivation of change talk. The psychiatrist acknowledges instances of change talk ("I want to stay healthy for my wife," "I think I could try that") but does not strategically amplify or explore them through follow-up questions. Critical missed opportunity: when the client states "I'd say maybe a 3" on confidence, the psychiatrist reflects this but doesn't evoke what fuels that number or what would strengthen it. More strategic questions ("What's keeping it from being a 2? What gives you that spark of hope?") would deepen and amplify change talk. The psychiatrist could also use more open-ended questions to elicit change talk (e.g., "What would be different if you were able to spend time in the garden regularly?").

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Adequate but inconsistent softening of sustain talk. The psychiatrist uses some effective double-sided reflections that acknowledge both desire for change and barriers without reinforcing barriers as insurmountable. However, significant sustain talk ("What if it makes things worse?", "my body feels so weak," "I don't know if it'll make a big difference") is acknowledged but not strategically addressed. More explicit softening would involve: (1) validating the fear as legitimate, (2) normalizing it as protective wisdom, and (3) gently shifting focus toward capability. For example, when the client asks "What if it makes things worse?", a response acknowledging the fear while highlighting past resilience would strengthen softening.

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall Score: 3.9/5**

### Key Strengths:
- ✓ Exemplary autonomy support with consistent affirmation of client choice
- ✓ Strong collaborative stance with genuine power-sharing
- ✓ Good reflective listening with emotionally attuned reflections
- ✓ Clear directional focus with logical session progression
- ✓ Solid acceptance with unconditional positive regard
- ✓ Effective use of client resources and past resilience
- ✓ Concrete, doable action planning that respects client limits

### Areas for Development:
- ⚠ Underutilized evocation—missed opportunities to draw out client's own ideas about solutions and barriers
- ⚠ Inconsistent change talk cultivation—change talk acknowledged but not strategically amplified
- ⚠ Incomplete sustain talk softening—fears and barriers acknowledged but not explicitly reframed
- ⚠ Limited emotional depth—validation is professional but somewhat distant
- ⚠ Incomplete referral focus—medical adherence component not addressed
- ⚠ Clinician-generated insights—some reflections emerge from clinician wisdom rather than co-discovery
- ⚠ Confidence scale underutilized as evocation tool—number reflected but not deeply explored

### Clinical Impact:
This session demonstrates adequate MI practice that will likely maintain client engagement and generate movement toward change. The client's confidence increased from 3 to 5, and a concrete action plan with identified support was established. However, the session's impact on sustained intrinsic motivation is limited by underutilized evocation and inconsistent change talk cultivation. The client leaves with a psychiatrist-supported plan rather than deeply activated personal motivation. To move from "good" to "excellent" MI practice, the psychiatrist should focus on asking more evocative questions before offering suggestions, strategically amplifying change talk, and explicitly softening sustain talk through validation and reframing.
