# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal acceptance and unconditional positive regard demonstrated. Psychiatrist quickly shifts to directive, problem-solving mode. Repeated use of prescriptive language ("you might want," "you should," "it would probably help if") positions clinician as expert rather than accepting client as expert on own life. Lacks explicit affirmation of client's strengths, values, and self-awareness. Misses opportunities to celebrate commitment to son or recognize insight about emotional reactivity patterns. Does not normalize co-parenting struggle or validate difficulty of situation.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Superficial and inconsistently demonstrated empathy. Basic reflective listening present in isolated moments but frequently followed by questions or advice that shift focus away from emotional experience. Misses emotional depth when client expresses frustration ("going in circles"), hopelessness about change, and relational pain (father "shutting down"). When client states "feels like this big mountain to climb," clinician responds with confidence scaling rather than acknowledging emotional weight. Fails to pick up on relational disconnection and its toll. Lacks depth in understanding client's internal experience and underlying pain.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Clear focus on target behavior maintained—improving co-parenting communication and pursuing counseling. Logical progression from problem identification to motivation exploration to action planning prevents session drift. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. Psychiatrist predetermines that counseling and clear rules are solutions, then systematically guides client toward accepting this direction. Lacks strategic depth regarding MI principles. Direction is tactical (how to have conversation) rather than strategic (exploring what client truly wants, what barriers are real vs. perceived, what resources exist). Misses opportunity to maintain focus on client's deeper values and motivations.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Profound absence of genuine collaboration throughout session. Psychiatrist consistently positions self as expert with solutions; client positioned as recipient of advice. Expert-driven problem-solving evident: asks diagnostic questions then immediately provides solutions without exploring client's own thinking. Unilateral decision-making: assumes counseling is answer without exploring alternatives or what client thinks would help. Provides scripted opening line without first exploring what would resonate with client or what language feels authentic. Treats client's concerns as obstacles to overcome rather than valuable information. No shared decision-making present. Session structured as psychiatrist telling client what to do, with client agreeing or expressing mild doubt addressed with more advice.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Almost entirely absent. Psychiatrist repeatedly uses prescriptive language throughout: "should try," "might want," "would probably help," "it's important to." Examples: "Maybe you should try to have a more structured conversation," "You might want to bring up counseling," "It would probably help if you also set small goals." Language is softer than direct commands but communicates clinician knows what client should do. When client expresses uncertainty about counseling, clinician does not explore as legitimate concern but assumes counseling is right choice. Confidence scales used as pressure to assess readiness to comply rather than genuinely explore preferences. No exploration of alternatives or invitation to generate own options. Detailed directives (write it down, practice in mirror, have backup plan) implicitly communicate client cannot figure this out alone, undermining sense of agency and self-efficacy.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Almost entirely absent. Psychiatrist consistently tells rather than asks, imposes rather than elicits. Amina spontaneously generates powerful change talk early ("I love my son dearly," "I just want a calm, safe place for him to grow up") but psychiatrist does not strategically evoke, amplify, or deepen it. When client expresses love for son, clinician responds with diagnostic questions rather than exploring what "best" looks like or what environment client envisions. Never asks about core values regarding parenting or family. Fails to explore client's own resources and competence. When client expresses worry about father's reaction, clinician provides tactical advice rather than evoking client's own thinking about barrier. Tells client what to say rather than asking what would resonate. Does not explore ambivalence as valuable information. Misses numerous opportunities to draw out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Almost entirely absent. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role throughout with implicit message that clinician has knowledge client needs. From opening question through closing, clinician positions self as one with answers. Asks diagnostic questions, identifies problem, proposes solutions, provides detailed instructions. Hierarchical language reinforces power differential: "It would probably help if," "Maybe you should," "It's important to," "I'm sure you'll make progress." No co-construction of change process. When client expresses doubt, clinician does not explore as potentially valuable insight but moves to confidence scaling and reassurance. Never acknowledges client's expertise regarding own life or relationship. Unilateral goal-setting: decides counseling and clear rules are goals without exploring whether these are client's goals or if other ideas exist. No genuine sense that clinician and client are equals working together or that client's wisdom is valued.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Amina spontaneously generates significant change talk (desire, reasons, commitment) without prompting, but psychiatrist does minimal work to actively evoke, amplify, or reinforce it. Missed opportunities: when client says "I love my son dearly," clinician shifts to diagnostic categorization rather than exploring motivation. Minimal evocation of change talk—few questions designed to draw out client's own reasons for change. No amplification of insights (e.g., "starting small won't feel overwhelming"). Reinforcement occurs primarily through affirming agreement with clinician's suggestions rather than genuine reflection of client's own motivations. Brief acknowledgments ("That's good to hear") rather than deep reinforcement. No questions designed to evoke commitment language. Sustain talk (doubts, worries) not skillfully addressed; clinician provides reassurance and advice, potentially reinforcing doubt rather than exploring it.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill in addressing sustain talk. While clinician does not reinforce it, responses are often inadequate and may inadvertently strengthen doubt. Multiple forms of sustain talk present: "going in circles," "don't want to push too hard," "worry about his reaction," "feels like mountain to climb," "shuts down," "worry if this will really change things." Inadequate responses: when client expresses worry about reaction and feeling overwhelmed, clinician responds with tactical advice and confidence scaling rather than exploring emotional weight. Provides reassurance that dismisses doubt rather than exploring it. No double-sided reflections acknowledging both motivation and concerns. No reframing of barriers as potentially valuable caution or wisdom. When client expresses worry about pushing too hard, clinician provides tactical solution rather than reframing as wisdom about relationship. Sustain talk about effort and imbalance ("doing most of the trying") not addressed. Responses may inadvertently strengthen doubts by suggesting they are obstacles to overcome rather than valid concerns to understand.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.6/5**

**Session Embodies MI Spirit:** No. While psychiatrist is warm and organized, fundamental stance is expert-driven rather than collaborative, prescriptive rather than evocative, and focused on compliance rather than intrinsic motivation.

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation:** Moderate to Low. While client appears to comply with suggestions and agrees to try recommended approach, this compliance does not necessarily reflect genuine intrinsic motivation. Client's initial change talk (love for son, desire for calm environment) is powerful and intrinsic, but psychiatrist's expert-driven approach may inadvertently undermine this by positioning clinician as source of solutions rather than supporting client in discovering own path.

**Key Strengths:**
- Clear focus on target behavior prevents session drift
- Organized action planning provides structure and clarity
- Warm opening tone conveys basic friendliness
- Brief acknowledgment of client's stated motivation (7-8/10)
- Consistent approach throughout

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Fundamental MI spirit violations across nearly all domains
- Expert-driven rather than client-centered approach
- Prescriptive language throughout undermines autonomy support
- Minimal evocation of client's own motivations and resources
- Inadequate response to sustain talk through exploration and reframing
- Absence of genuine collaboration and power-sharing
- Missed opportunities for deeper empathic connection
- Compliance-focused rather than intrinsic motivation-focused

**Likelihood of Sustained Change:** Moderate to Low. While client may attempt recommended steps, without deepened intrinsic motivation and genuine ownership of change process, client is at risk of abandoning approach if initial attempts don't succeed or obstacles encountered. Psychiatrist provided plan but did not support client in strengthening own commitment to why change matters.

**Primary Recommendation:** Engage in focused MI training emphasizing fundamental shift from expert-driven to evocative practice. Develop skill in asking rather than telling, evoking rather than imposing, and positioning client as expert on own life. Video review of sessions with feedback on core competencies would be particularly valuable.
