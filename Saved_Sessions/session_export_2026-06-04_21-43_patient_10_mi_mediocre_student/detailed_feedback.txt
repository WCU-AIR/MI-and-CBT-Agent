
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal acceptance and unconditional positive regard throughout this session. ## Recommendations

### 1. **Shift from Expert-Driven to Evocative Stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Fundamentally reframe your role from the expert with solutions to a facilitator who helps clients discover their own wisdom and resources. Replace prescriptive language ("you should," "you might want") with genuine questions that evoke the client's own thinking.

**Example:** 
- **Instead of:** "Maybe you should try to have a more structured conversation or even get some outside help?"
- **Use:** "You've been thinking about this situation for a while. What do you think might help? What have you already tried that's worked, even a little bit?"

- **Instead of:** "You might want to start by saying something simple like, 'I care about us working together for our son.'"
- **Use:** "What do you think would help him understand that you're not trying to fight but trying to work together? What language feels authentic to you?"

**Practice:** In your next MI session, count how many times you use prescriptive language ("should," "might want," "would probably help"). Challenge yourself to replace each instance with a genuine question that invites the client to think. Record yourself and listen for the shift in tone and stance.

---

### 2. **Develop Deeper Empathic Reflections That Capture Emotional Nuance**

**Action:** When clients express emotion or struggle, pause and reflect back not just the content but the feeling and meaning. Use complex reflections that deepen understanding rather than moving quickly to problem-solving.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** "It sounds like you're concerned about the inconsistency in parenting styles and how that might be affecting your son's behavior."
- **Use:** "It sounds like you've been trying really hard to work this out with him, but it feels like you're not being heard. And that's frustrating because you care so much about getting it right for your son. That must feel really lonely sometimes."

- **Instead of:** "That's good to hear that you're pretty motivated."
- **Use:** "Your love for your son really comes through. You want to create a safe, calm environment for him, and you're willing to do the hard work of improving things with his father even though it's scary. That takes real courage."

**Practice:** In your next session, identify moments when the client expresses emotion or struggle. Before moving to problem-solving, pause and offer a complex reflection that captures both the content and the emotional meaning. Notice how the client responds—do they feel more understood? Do they open up more?

---

### 3. **Actively Evoke and Amplify Change Talk Rather Than Providing Solutions**

**Action:** When clients spontaneously express motivation, desire, ability, reasons, or commitment to change, respond by exploring and deepening that statement rather than moving to advice-giving. Use follow-up questions like: "Tell me more about that," "What makes that important to you?" "How would that help?" "What else?"

**Example:**
- **Client says:** "I love my son dearly, and I really want the best for him."
- **Instead of:** "I see, so it's about your son and some differences between you and his father. Is it mostly about his health, behavior, or something else?"
- **Use:** "Tell me more about what 'the best' looks like to you. What kind of environment do you envision for him? What do you want him to learn from how you handle challenges?"

- **Client says:** "I think if we start small, it won't feel so overwhelming."
- **Instead of:** Moving to the next piece of advice
- **Use:** "That's a really important insight. You're recognizing that taking it step by step makes this feel more manageable. What would a small first step look like to you?"

**Practice:** In your next session, identify every instance of change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment). For each one, respond with a follow-up question that deepens the client's own articulation rather than moving to advice. Track how many times you do this successfully.

---

### 4. **Skillfully Address Sustain Talk Through Exploration and Reframing Rather Than Reassurance**

**Action:** When clients express doubts, barriers, or reasons not to change, resist the urge to provide reassurance or tactical solutions. Instead, explore the concern with genuine curiosity, validate it as potentially wise, and help the client see it from a different perspective.

**Example:**
- **Client says:** "I still worry about how he'll react. It's hard when you want so much good stuff, but getting there feels like this big mountain to climb."
- **Instead of:** "Maybe you should try to bring it up when things are calm. How confident do you feel?"
- **Use:** "It sounds like you're carrying a lot of worry about how this will go. That makes sense—you care so much about getting it right. Tell me more about what you're worried might happen. What's the worst-case scenario you're imagining? And what's the best-case scenario?"

- **Client says:** "I'm not sure how he'd feel about counseling. Sometimes he shuts down when I bring up stuff like this."
- **Instead of:** "Do you think it would probably help if you and his dad tried counseling together?"
- **Use:** "It sounds like you've learned that he tends to shut down when certain topics come up. That's actually important information about your relationship. What do you think is behind that? What would need to be different for him to stay open?"

**Practice:** In your next session, when you hear sustain talk, pause before responding. Ask yourself: "What is the client worried about? What is the wisdom in this concern? How can I explore it rather than dismiss it?" Practice using double-sided reflections: "On one hand, you really want to improve things for your son. On the other hand, you're worried that your son's father might shut down. Both of those things are true for you right now."

### 5. **Establish True Collaboration by Exploring the Client's Own Ideas and Resources**

### 6. **Support Autonomy by Emphasizing Client Choice and Ownership**

**Action:** Replace prescriptive language with language that emphasizes the client's freedom to choose and their ownership of the change process. ### 7. **Develop Skill in Evoking Values and Vision**

**Action:** Ask questions that help clients articulate their core values, their vision for the future, and what matters most to them. This deepens intrinsic motivation and provides a foundation for sustained change.

### 8. **Use Confidence and Importance Scales Strategically, Not as Pressure**

**Action:** When using scales (confidence, importance, readiness), use them as a tool for exploration rather than as a way to assess whether the client is ready to comply with your plan. Follow up with genuine curiosity about what the number means to the client.

**Example:**
- **Instead of:** "On a scale from 1 to 10, how confident do you feel about making that happen?"

- **Instead of:** "How important is it for you to improve this situation right now?"
- **Use:** "On a scale from 1 to 10, how important is it to you to improve this situation? What makes it a [number] and not a [lower number]? What would need to happen for it to be even more important?"

**Practice:** In your next session, when you use a scale, follow up with genuine curiosity about what the number means. Ask: "What would it take to move from a [current number] to a [higher number]?" This shifts the scale from a compliance-checking tool to an exploration tool.

### 9. **Strengthen Your Reflective Listening Skills, Particularly Complex Reflections**

**Action:** Practice offering reflections that go beyond paraphrasing content to capturing emotional meaning, underlying values, and deeper significance. Use reflections that deepen insight rather than just acknowledging what was said.

**Example:**
- **Simple reflection (paraphrasing):** "So you're worried about how he'll react."

**Practice:** In your next session, aim for at least 50% complex reflections. Record yourself and listen for reflections that capture emotional nuance, underlying values, and deeper meaning. Ask yourself: "Does this reflection deepen the client's insight, or does it just acknowledge what they said?"

### 10. **Develop Awareness of Your Own Expert Stance and Practice Shifting It**

**Action:** Develop metacognitive awareness of when you are slipping into an expert stance. Notice the urge to provide solutions and practice pausing instead. Ask yourself: "What is the client's own wisdom here? How can I help them discover it rather than tell them?"

**Example:** When you feel the urge to say, "You should try..." pause and ask yourself: "What would happen if I asked them what they think instead? What would they discover about themselves?"

**Practice:** After each session, reflect on moments when you shifted into expert mode. What triggered it? What was the client saying or doing? What could you have done differently? Over time, you'll develop greater awareness and flexibility in your stance.
