### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates exemplary acceptance throughout the session. From the opening, unconditional positive regard and genuine warmth are established. When the patient expresses resistance to quitting ("I don't really wanna quit. I mean, I like smoking"), the clinician does not judge or minimize. Instead, the response validates smoking's meaningful role in the patient's life: "Smoking isn't just about the nicotine—it's tied to your moments of relaxation, focus, and social connection, which makes it deeply meaningful and hard to let go of." Affirmations are specific and genuine, tied to observable patient behavior rather than generic praise. The psychiatrist never conveys judgment about continued smoking or resistance to complete cessation. The stance remains consistently warm, supportive, and respectful of the patient's inherent worth and capacity for change.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Evocation—drawing out the patient's own motivations, values, and resources—is masterfully demonstrated. Rather than telling the patient why he should cut back, the psychiatrist asks: "What are some reasons you might want to cut back on smoking?" The patient then generates his own reasons: feeling better physically, easing his partner's worries, and saving money. These are his motivations, not clinician-imposed. The psychiatrist amplifies this change talk: "It sounds like feeling better physically, easing your partner's worries, and saving some money are meaningful reasons for you to think about cutting back." When exploring past change attempts, the psychiatrist asks: "What has helped you in the past when you've tried to make changes?" This evokes the patient's own resources rather than offering generic advice. The psychiatrist asks the patient to identify his own first step: "What do you think would be a first small step you might feel comfortable trying?" The patient generates the specific strategy (waiting longer before the first cigarette, using patches during work). Throughout, the psychiatrist uses "ask, don't tell" consistently.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates strong skill in handling sustain talk (arguments for maintaining the status quo) without confrontation. When the patient expresses initial resistance ("I don't really wanna quit. I mean, I like smoking"), the psychiatrist uses a sophisticated double-sided reflection that validates the sustain talk, reframes it positively, and pivots toward change talk: "Smoking isn't just about the nicotine—it's tied to your moments of relaxation, focus, and social connection, which makes it deeply meaningful and hard to let go of. At the same time, you're curious about finding a way to reduce the impact on your health without losing those important parts." The current response normalizes the struggle but could pivot more strategically toward change talk about growth and resilience.

## **Overall Session Score: 4.8/5**

---

### **Summary of Strengths**

- **Masterful empathy and acceptance:** The psychiatrist consistently demonstrates unconditional positive regard, normalizes ambivalence, and uses complex reflections that capture emotional nuance and functional meaning. The patient never feels judged for resistance to quitting.

- **Exemplary evocation:** Rather than telling the patient why he should change, the psychiatrist asks strategic questions that draw out his own motivations, resources, and solutions. The patient generates his own reasons for change and his own plan.

- **True partnership and collaboration:** The psychiatrist explicitly seeks permission before offering suggestions, frames ideas as options to explore, and consistently returns power to the patient. The patient's contributions substantially shape the session's direction and content.

- **Consistent autonomy support:** Throughout the session, the psychiatrist emphasizes the patient's right to choose, respects his decision to pursue reduction rather than abstinence, and frames the change plan as his to adjust as needed.

- **Strategic cultivation of change talk:** The psychiatrist skillfully evokes and amplifies change talk across multiple dimensions (desire, reasons, ability, commitment, confidence). Change talk is reinforced through affirmations, complex reflections, and strategic questioning.

- **Sophisticated sustain talk management:** When the patient expresses resistance or doubt, the psychiatrist validates these concerns, reframes them positively, and pivots toward change talk without confrontation or persuasion.

---

### **Recommendations for Future Practice**

1. **Strengthen evocation before offering psychoeducation:** Ask the patient to first identify what he thinks might help before offering a menu of strategies. This deepens evocation and ensures his ideas are heard first.

2. **Reframe past failures as learning experiences:** When patients mention past unsuccessful attempts to change, explicitly reframe these as learning opportunities and evoke what they learned that might help them now.

3. **Deepen exploration of confidence ratings:** After confidence scaling questions, ask follow-up questions about what would increase or decrease confidence. This deepens exploration and helps patients identify their own resources and barriers.

4. **Explore functional role of target behavior more deeply:** Ask "What does smoking do for you?" "What would happen without it?" "What are you managing through smoking?" This deepens understanding and often reveals additional motivations for change.

5. **Strengthen commitment language:** After patients identify concrete steps, ask them to articulate their own commitment in their own words: "What are you committing to?" "Why does this matter to you?"

6. **Explore anticipated barriers and problem-solve collaboratively:** After patients identify concrete steps, ask about anticipated barriers: "What might make this hard?" "What could get in the way?" Then collaboratively problem-solve.
