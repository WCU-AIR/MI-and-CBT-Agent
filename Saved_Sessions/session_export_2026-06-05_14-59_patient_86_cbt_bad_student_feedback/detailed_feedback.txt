
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda

## Recommendations

### 1. **URGENT: Recognize This Is Not a CBT Case—Refer for Psychiatric Evaluation**
- **Action:** Immediately refer the patient to psychiatry for medication evaluation and assessment of hospitalization needs. This is not a case for psychotherapy alone; it requires psychiatric stabilization first.

### 2. **Conduct Proper Safety Assessment**
- **Action:** In any psychiatric encounter, assess for suicidality, homicidality, and command hallucinations using structured questions.
- **Example:** "Have you had any thoughts about harming yourself or others? Are you hearing voices that tell you to do things?"
- **Practice:** Learn and practice the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) or similar structured assessment tool. Make safety assessment automatic in every psychiatric encounter.

### 3. **Eliminate Inappropriate Self-Disclosure**
- **Action:** Stop sharing personal anecdotes in psychiatric encounters, especially with acutely ill patients. Self-disclosure normalizes symptoms and shifts focus from the patient's crisis.
- **Example:** Instead of "I once felt really out of control too," say "That sounds really distressing. Let me help you get the right support."
- **Practice:** In supervision, review recordings and identify every instance of self-disclosure. Practice redirecting to the patient's experience without personal narrative.

### 4. **Stop Offering False Reassurance**
- **Action:** Replace false reassurance ("It'll get better, I promise") with honest, realistic statements based on clinical assessment.
- **Example:** "I can't promise how you'll feel, but I know that with the right treatment—which may include medication—many people with these symptoms do improve. Let's get you connected with a psychiatrist to explore options."
- **Practice:** Distinguish between supportive statements ("I'm here to help") and false reassurance (promising outcomes without basis). Practice offering hope grounded in evidence-based treatment.

### 5. **Learn to Recognize and Assess Delusions**
- **Action:** Develop skill in identifying delusions and assessing their content, conviction, and impact without confrontation or debate.
- **Example:** "You mentioned feeling like the Joker. Tell me more about that. When did that start? How often do you feel that way?"
- **Practice:** Study the phenomenology of delusions in schizophrenia. Practice non-confrontational exploration of delusional content. Learn that reality-testing delusions requires specialized training and should not be attempted by untrained therapists.

### 6. **Learn to Recognize Disorganized Behavior and Negative Symptoms**
- **Action:** Develop ability to distinguish between normal coping and signs of acute psychosis (disorganized behavior, negative symptoms).
- **Example:** Recognize that standing on a roof conducting music is not a "coping mechanism" but a sign of disorganized behavior requiring psychiatric evaluation.
- **Practice:** Study the DSM-5 criteria for schizophrenia. Review case examples. Learn to recognize negative symptoms (social withdrawal, anhedonia, avolition) as psychiatric symptoms, not character flaws.

### 7. **Establish Structured Agenda and Treatment Plan**
- **Action:** In every session, establish a clear agenda with prioritized goals, time allocation, and explicit treatment plan.
- **Example:** "Today I want to: (1) understand what you're experiencing, (2) assess your safety, (3) connect you with psychiatric care. We have 45 minutes. Does that work for you?"
- **Practice:** Use a written agenda template. Practice prioritizing clinical concerns. Learn to distinguish between urgent (safety, medication) and non-urgent (lifestyle suggestions) issues.

- **Action:** Stop offering generic suggestions (hydration, fresh air, positive thinking) as primary interventions for psychotic symptoms. These are not evidence-based for schizophrenia.
- **Example:** Instead of "Have you tried getting fresh air?" say "I'm concerned about your safety given what you've shared. Let's talk about getting you psychiatric evaluation."
