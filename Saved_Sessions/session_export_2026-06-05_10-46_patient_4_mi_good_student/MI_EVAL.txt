### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates strong collaborative stance by inviting Aisha to set the agenda ("What would you most like to focus on?") and explicitly seeking permission before offering ideas ("If you're open to it, I can share some ideas—would you like to hear a few?"). The clinician invites Aisha's input on feasibility ("How do these ideas fit with what you were thinking?") and affirms her contributions. Phrasing such as "One approach that often helps is..." positions the clinician as expert. A more collaborative approach would frame ideas as emerging from dialogue: "Based on what worked before, what do you think might help now?"

#### **Score (1–5):** 5

From the opening question onward, the clinician communicates that Aisha's preferences and decisions drive the session. ### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates solid evocation of Aisha's own motivations, values, and resources through strategic questioning. Rather than telling Aisha why exercise is important, the clinician asks: "What are some reasons or hopes that make you want to push through these challenges?" Aisha articulates powerful change talk: "I'm just sick of feeling the way I do," "I don't want to look back in a year and still be stuck." The psychiatrist evokes past resources by asking about the 3-month walking routine and then invites Aisha to apply her own wisdom: "What do you think might help you tap into that same strength now?" However, when Aisha expresses 6/10 confidence with lingering doubts, the psychiatrist could have evoked more: "What would need to happen for that confidence to move from a 6 to a 7 or 8?" or "What do you already know about yourself that tells you you *can* do this?" Additionally, opportunities exist to more deeply evoke core values through questions like "What does feeling comfortable in your own skin really mean to you?"

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates strong partnership by consistently positioning Aisha as the expert on her own experience and needs. The clinician avoids the expert role by asking rather than telling and affirms that Aisha is generating solutions: "You've clearly thought through how to make this feel less overwhelming and more enjoyable." Inclusive language such as "Let's think about" and invitations for Aisha's input on feasibility and support needs reinforce partnership. When Aisha generates her own accountability strategy (texting a friend), the psychiatrist affirms this as her contribution. However, when offering the three specific strategies, the psychiatrist presents these as clinician-generated ideas rather than emerging from dialogue. Phrasing such as "One approach that often helps is..." and "Another tip is..." positions the clinician as expert offering guidance. A more partnership-oriented approach would frame ideas as co-constructed: "Based on what you've told me about what worked before, I'm wondering what other elements might help. What are your thoughts?"

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates solid cultivation of change talk through strategic questioning and amplification of Aisha's own motivation statements. When asked "What are some reasons or hopes that make you want to push through?" Aisha responds with powerful change talk. The clinician then amplifies this by reflecting back with emphasis: "You're carrying a strong desire to shift from feeling stuck and uncomfortable to a place where you feel confident, energized, and more at home in your body." When Aisha articulates her plan, the psychiatrist affirms and amplifies: "That sounds like a fantastic first step... It's a thoughtful, achievable plan that honors your pace and preferences." However, when Aisha expresses doubt ("I worry I won't be able to keep up"), the psychiatrist could have evoked ability talk: "What tells you that you *could* keep up, even with that worry?" When Aisha reports 6/10 confidence, the psychiatrist could have asked "What would help move it to a 7?" to evoke more confidence talk. Additionally, opportunities exist to evoke need talk through questions like "What would happen if you don't make this change?"

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates exemplary skill in softening and redirecting sustain talk throughout the session. When Aisha presents barriers ("finding the time and motivation has been tough," "I worry I won't be able to keep up," "fear or embarrassment that holds me back"), the clinician does not focus on or reinforce these obstacles. Instead, the psychiatrist acknowledges them while immediately pivoting toward change talk and capability: "It sounds like there's a real tension between wanting to start moving again and the practical challenges... and yet you still hold onto this hope of feeling better in your body and reclaiming confidence." The use of "and yet" is particularly skillful—it acknowledges difficulty while redirecting focus to her strength. When Aisha reports 6/10 confidence with doubts about "setting herself up for failure," the psychiatrist reframes: "A 6 shows you have a meaningful sense of hope and motivation, even while some doubts are still present." The clinician then pivots to evoking past success rather than dwelling on current doubts. Throughout the session, sustain talk is never amplified or explored in depth, which is appropriate given Aisha's high motivation.

- ⚠ Strengthen complex reflections by capturing emotional nuance and underlying themes

This session demonstrates **solid, competent motivational interviewing practice** that is likely to support Aisha's initial change efforts. However, insufficient exploration of psychological barriers (shame, fear of judgment) means Aisha may encounter these obstacles during implementation without having fully processed them in session. The psychiatrist demonstrates foundational MI competence but would benefit from developing greater depth in emotional attunement and strategic evocation of confidence statements to move from "good" to "excellent" MI practice.
