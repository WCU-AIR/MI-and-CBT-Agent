--- DISORDER ---
Psychotic Disorder / Neurocognitive Disorder

--- CASE STUDY ---

Dorothy Franklin was a 54-year-old married former waitress brought to the psychiatric emergency room by her husband for escalating delusions and visual hallucinations. She had been episodically agitated for about 10 years, increasingly suspicious for about 6 months, and experiencing daily hallucinations for several weeks.

She described her experience as "my nightmare," stating: "I see a judge standing in front of me. Plain as day. He is a good judge, but he threatens to kill me, and I can't stop him. I can't believe no one else can see him. Can you help?"

Her husband reported she had been "completely fine" until her early 40s, when she became frequently agitated, paranoid, worried, and occasionally aggressive. Over the years, she had been diagnosed with late-onset schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, depression, paranoid personality, and anxiety. She had been prescribed numerous psychiatric medications, including antidepressants, antipsychotics, antianxiety, and mood stabilizers.

She had smoked half a pack of cigarettes daily for many years, rarely drank alcohol, and never used recreational drugs. She had worked as a waitress for 20 years but quit 10 years earlier due to irritability, misremembered orders, and dropped trays. She had one arrest for aggression in a shopping mall and subsequently reduced her stress level.

She had two adult children in their late 20s. Her sister had depression and irritability. Her mother had severe dementia and passed away at age 70; her maternal grandfather completed suicide at age 62.

On mental status examination, Ms. Franklin was frightened but cooperative, sitting next to her husband and holding his hand. She had moderate psychomotor slowing, involuntary “dancelike” movements of her trunk and upper extremities, intermittent but intense eye contact, blunted affect, slowed soft speech, and minimal spontaneity. Thought processes were linear but slowed. She experienced active visual hallucinations and paranoid delusions. She denied suicidal or homicidal ideation. Cognitive examination showed alertness, orientation, good attention, but impaired short- and long-term memory, with moderate deficits on MMSE and clock drawing tests.

Extensive labs were normal. MRI ruled out mass lesions and demyelinating processes. EEG showed no seizure activity. Genetic testing confirmed Huntington's disease (44 CAG repeats).

Discussion: Ms. Franklin’s late-onset psychosis, visual hallucinations, cognitive deficits, and motor symptoms suggested Huntington's disease. She was admitted with a diagnosis of unspecified schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorder, later confirmed to have mild neurocognitive disorder due to Huntington's disease and psychotic disorder due to Huntington's disease. She and her family were referred to a specialized outpatient Huntington’s disease clinic for ongoing management.
