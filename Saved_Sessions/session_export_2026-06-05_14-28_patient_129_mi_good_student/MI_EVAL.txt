# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance throughout the session. Early validation of Tyler's ambivalence ("That sense of wanting to avoid future problems while still having a good time seems really important to you") and normalization of hesitation about change ("that fear of stepping outside the usual social pattern is completely understandable and valid") are evident. However, acceptance could be deepened through more explicit affirmation of Tyler's inherent worth independent of behavior change. Limited affirmation of his willingness to engage in difficult conversation and demonstrated self-reflection. More unconditional affirmation of Tyler as a person of worth would strengthen this foundation.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Solid reflective listening and perspective-taking demonstrated throughout. Complex reflections accurately capture Tyler's emotional experience and internal conflict, such as the tension between wanting relaxed hangouts and fearing social judgment. Accurate identification of core ambivalence shows genuine understanding. However, empathy could deepen through more exploration of emotional nuance behind uncertainty—what specifically feels scary about stepping out? Limited exploration of emotions related to the original incident with campus security and police, including potential shame or fear of consequences. Remains somewhat surface-level regarding emotional weight of potential outcomes.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintains appropriate strategic focus on alcohol use and its relationship to social life, friendships, and academic goals. Progressive and logical session structure: exploring ambivalence → identifying social role of drinking → discovering alternatives → addressing barriers → identifying resources → developing concrete first step → establishing accountability. However, the session does not explicitly clarify Tyler's ultimate change goal. Does he want abstinence, reduction to specific quantities, or context-specific changes? This ambiguity could lead to misalignment. Additionally, the original conduct violation and its consequences are minimally explored, missing opportunity to strengthen discrepancy and motivation.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong collaborative stance demonstrated throughout. Opening question positions Tyler as expert on his own experience. Frequent solicitation of Tyler's input ("What do you think would be different...?" "What do you think might make it easier...?") and explicit language reinforcing shared decision-making ("you're steering this journey"). However, subtle moments where clinician's suggestions shape direction without fully checking in first. When proposing follow-up check-in, this is presented as suggestion rather than collaboratively developed. Could enhance collaboration by asking "How would you like to follow up on this?" to more fully co-construct accountability structure.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistently affirms Tyler's right to choose and emphasizes his ownership of change process. Key autonomy-supporting statements include "it's totally your choice how and when to move forward" and "you're steering this journey." Avoids prescriptive advice-giving; instead uses evocative questions inviting Tyler to generate own solutions. However, autonomy support could be strengthened by explicitly acknowledging that Tyler could choose not to change his drinking behavior—that non-change is genuinely optional. More explicit validation that ambivalence is legitimate and acceptable would further reinforce clinician's non-investment in particular outcome.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates some evocative skill but falls short of consistently drawing out Tyler's own motivations, values, and resources. Some early evocative moments present (asking what he'd like to focus on, evoking past success with study group). However, session characterized by more reflection and validation than true evocation. When Tyler mentions education is important, clinician does not deeply explore this value. Does not systematically evoke change talk across four domains (desire, ability, reasons, need). Minimal exploration of Tyler's ability to change, reasons beyond avoiding trouble, or sense of need. Most significantly, does not ask about values, what matters most, or what kind of person he wants to be—questions that would evoke intrinsic motivation. Remains somewhat surface-level in accessing deeper motivations.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong partnership demonstrated by consistently positioning Tyler as expert on his own experience. Inclusive language ("we can see where things go from there") and explicit statement "you're steering this journey" communicate genuine power-sharing. Avoids expert role by asking questions rather than providing advice. However, partnership could be strengthened by inviting Tyler to identify his own strengths rather than clinician pointing them out ("It's clear you have a knack..."). True partnership would involve asking Tyler to discover and name strengths himself. Additionally, clinician does not invite Tyler to co-construct understanding of his drinking problem or what it means to him, representing missed opportunity for collaborative meaning-making.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates inconsistent cultivation of change talk. Some change talk evoked and reinforced ("I don't want to keep getting into trouble," "less stress and maybe more focus on school," "those times without drinking have been pretty chill"), and clinician does reflect and affirm some of this. However, approach is not systematic or strategic. Does not systematically ask questions designed to evoke specific types of change talk: desire ("What would be best about making this change?"), ability ("What have you successfully changed before?"), reasons ("Why would this matter?"), or need ("How important is it to address this now?"). Confidence scaling at end (5, then 6-7) provides some change talk but comes late and is not deeply explored or reinforced. Session relies more on reflection than active amplification of client motivation.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates adequate but inconsistent softening of sustain talk. Tyler expresses normalizing statements ("drinking feels like the normal way to chill," "that's just how we all socialize," "everyone expects drinking to be part of the night"). Clinician's primary response is reflection and validation rather than strategic redirection. Does not gently challenge assumptions or explore alternatives. Some softening occurs through strategic questioning about barriers (asking what would help him feel comfortable suggesting alternatives) and highlighting past success with study group. However, does not employ more direct MI techniques such as double-sided reflection, reframing, or amplified reflection. Approach is somewhat passive rather than actively redirecting client language toward change.

---

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Session Strengths:**
- Strong collaborative stance with explicit autonomy-supporting language throughout
- Solid reflective listening accurately capturing core ambivalence
- Effective use of past successes to build confidence and demonstrate capacity for change
- Appropriate strategic focus on central tension between drinking as social norm and desire to avoid consequences
- Consistent unconditional positive regard with minimal judgment
- Concrete, manageable action planning emerging collaboratively
- Good pacing allowing Tyler to process without feeling rushed

**Areas for Improvement:**
- Insufficient systematic evocation of change talk across all domains
- Limited exploration of core values and intrinsic motivation beyond avoiding trouble
- Inadequate softening of sustain talk when Tyler expresses normalizing statements
- Minimal exploration of consequences and discrepancy to strengthen motivation
- Unclear change goal definition (abstinence vs. reduction vs. context-specific changes)
- Limited explicit affirmation of inherent worth independent of behavior change
- Clinician-identified strengths rather than inviting Tyler to discover his own

**Overall MI Competency Assessment: 3.7/5 (Adequate to Good)**

The psychiatrist demonstrates solid foundational MI practice with particular strength in collaboration, acceptance, and empathy. However, the session lacks the strategic depth and systematic approach to evoking intrinsic motivation that characterizes excellent MI practice. The clinician is an effective listener and validator but not a strategic amplifier of client motivation for change.
