
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

This document outlines a structured framework for assessing therapist competence in Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT). 
It is used for training, supervision, and evaluation. Each item is scored on a 0–6 scale:

0 = Poor
2 = Barely Adequate
4 = Good
6 = Excellent

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

No agenda was established at any point during this session. The therapist opened with a generic greeting ("Hey there! How's it going today?") and immediately invited the patient to discuss whatever was on her mind without any collaborative goal-setting, prioritization, or time structure. For a patient with severe intellectual disability (IQ 31), autism spectrum disorder, and significant communication deficits, an explicit, concrete agenda is not merely helpful—it is clinically essential. The absence of agenda-setting represents a fundamental failure to adapt CBT to this patient's cognitive and communication needs. For patients with IDD, explicit structure is not optional—it is a core therapeutic requirement.

**Summary:**
No agenda was established. The session lacked structure, prioritization, or collaborative goal-setting. This represents a critical failure to adapt to the patient's severe cognitive and communication limitations.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made minimal, inadequate attempts to elicit feedback from the patient. Across the entire session, there were no explicit questions asking whether the patient understood the therapist's explanations, whether the suggestions were helpful, whether the patient agreed with the direction of the session, or what the patient's takeaways were. The only quasi-feedback moment occurred when the therapist said, "Thanks for sharing that," which is acknowledgment but not genuine feedback elicitation. True feedback would involve asking the patient to evaluate the session's usefulness, checking comprehension of complex concepts (e.g., "Can you tell me back what I just said about positive thinking?"), or asking whether the patient felt heard and understood.

This is especially critical for Ashley, who has significant language and comprehension deficits (Verbal IQ: 23). Without explicit feedback checks, the therapist has no way to know whether Ashley understood the suggestions, whether they were appropriate for her cognitive level, or whether she felt the session was helpful. The therapist's repeated personal anecdotes ("I remember when I felt that way...") were never evaluated for relevance or utility to the patient. A therapist working with a patient of Ashley's cognitive capacity must systematically verify understanding and satisfaction.

**Summary:**
Minimal feedback elicitation. The therapist did not check understanding, verify comprehension, or solicit the patient's evaluation of session helpfulness. This represents inadequate adaptation to the patient's severe communication and cognitive limitations.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist demonstrated surface-level understanding of explicit statements but repeatedly missed critical nuances of Ashley's experience and failed to grasp the functional significance of her symptoms. For example:

**What the therapist understood:**
- Ashley experiences anxiety with schedule changes and increased demands
- She engages in self-injury (biting wrist) and aggression (pinching, hair pulling)
- She finds comfort in license plates, stories, and her mother's presence
- She struggles with sleep and social interaction

**What the therapist missed or minimized:**

1. **The functional role of self-injury and stereotyped behaviors:** Ashley explicitly stated that biting her wrist and rocking "help me feel like I'm not going to break" and provide safety. The therapist never explored this—that these behaviors serve a critical regulatory function for someone with severe anxiety and limited coping skills. Instead, the therapist normalized them ("Everyone goes through tough times") without understanding their necessity in Ashley's neurobiological profile.

2. **The severity of her communication deficits:** Ashley said, "Talking is hard because I don't always find the right words" and "It's easier to look at my drawings or think about the numbers on the plates." The therapist responded by suggesting cognitive strategies ("tell yourself, 'I'm okay'") that require abstract language processing—precisely what Ashley cannot do reliably. The therapist did not recognize that Ashley's preference for visual/concrete thinking (license plates, drawings) reflects her cognitive strengths and limitations, not a choice.

3. **Existential anxiety about abandonment and loss:** Ashley expressed fear that "the story will end or the birds will fly away" and that she gets "scared it won't come back." This reflects profound anxiety about loss and discontinuity—a core feature of her presentation. The therapist responded with platitudes ("It's okay to feel scared sometimes") rather than exploring this or developing concrete strategies to address it.

4. **The distinction between her intellectual disability and her anxiety:** The therapist conflated general stress management with Ashley's specific needs. Ashley's anxiety is not merely situational stress; it is tied to her inability to predict, understand, or control her environment—a direct consequence of her severe cognitive limitations. The therapist's suggestions (positive self-talk, deep breathing, thinking happy thoughts) are cognitively demanding interventions inappropriate for someone with a Verbal IQ of 23.

5. **Her dependence on external regulation:** Ashley repeatedly emphasized that her mother's presence, physical comfort (hand-holding, hugs), and concrete sensory input (cold water, music, stories) are what actually help. The therapist acknowledged these but then continued to suggest internal cognitive strategies, missing that Ashley's regulatory capacity is fundamentally dependent on external support.

The therapist's repeated personal anecdotes ("When I was in school..." The therapist grasped explicit content but missed critical functional understanding of Ashley's symptoms, regulatory needs, cognitive limitations, and the distinction between her anxiety and her intellectual disability. Empathy was present but lacked clinical depth.
