
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

No agenda was established at any point during either session. The therapist did not collaboratively identify, prioritize, or structure session goals with the patient. ## Recommendations

### 1. **Immediately Establish a CBT-Based Case Conceptualization for OCPD**
- **Action:** Before the next session, review the DSM-5 criteria for OCPD and the cognitive-behavioral model of personality disorders. Develop a written case formulation that identifies: (a) core beliefs (e.g., "Things must be perfect or they'll fall apart"; "I must control everything to prevent disaster"), (b) behavioral patterns (compulsive organizing, avoidance of delegation, sleep deprivation), (c) emotional consequences (anxiety, irritability, guilt), and (d) relationship impact (controlling Peter, creating conflict).
- **Example:** "Ogden's perfectionism and need for control stem from catastrophic beliefs about loss and disorder. His compulsive organizing temporarily reduces anxiety but prevents him from learning that imperfection is tolerable. This maintains the disorder and damages his relationship with Peter."
- **Practice:** Write a one-page case formulation and review it before each session. Use it to guide intervention selection.

### 2. **Establish Explicit Collaborative Agenda at the Start of Every Session**
- **Action:** Begin Session 3 with: "I'd like to make sure we use our time well today. What feels most important to focus on? What would be most helpful?" Collaboratively identify 2-3 specific goals for the session. Write them down and refer back to them.
- **Example:** "So today we're going to: (1) understand what happens when you try to relax, (2) explore what you're afraid will happen if Peter helps pack, and (3) plan a small experiment to test that fear. Does that sound good?"
- **Practice:** Use this agenda-setting approach in every session. Track whether you covered the agreed-upon topics.

### 3. **Replace Generic Advice with Socratic Questioning**
- **Action:** When the patient describes perfectionism or control-seeking, use guided discovery instead of advice. Ask questions that help the patient reach insights independently.
- **Example:** Instead of "Try to think positive," ask: "When you imagine not organizing tonight, what specifically do you worry will happen?" Then: "Has that actually happened before?" Then: "What evidence do you have that it won't happen?" Then: "What would it mean if one thing did get lost?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, count how many times you give advice vs. ask questions. Aim for at least 70% questions. Record yourself and review.

### 4. **Implement Cognitive Restructuring for Catastrophic Thoughts**
- **Action:** Identify the patient's core catastrophic thoughts ("If I don't control it, things will be lost/ruined"). Use a thought record to examine evidence for and against these beliefs. Help the patient develop more balanced thoughts.
- **Example:** 
  - Thought: "If I don't organize everything, something important will get lost"
  - Evidence for: "I worry about this"
  - Evidence against: "Peter has organized his own apartment for years without losing important things. I've delegated tasks at work and nothing was lost."
  - Balanced thought: "Some things might be organized differently, but important items are unlikely to be lost. Peter is capable."
- **Practice:** Assign a thought record as homework. Review it in the next session.

### 5. **Design and Conduct Behavioral Experiments to Test Catastrophic Predictions**
- **Action:** Collaboratively design experiments that test the patient's fears in a controlled way. Start small and build.
- **Example:** 
  - Experiment 1: "Let Peter pack one box without your oversight. Observe what actually happens. Rate your anxiety before (predicted) and after (actual)."
  - Experiment 2: "Leave one area of the apartment unpacked/disorganized for 24 hours. Rate your anxiety. Did the predicted catastrophe occur?"
  - Experiment 3: "Spend one evening with Peter without organizing. Track your mood and relationship quality."
- **Practice:** Assign one behavioral experiment as homework. Review results in detail in the next session. Use the results to challenge catastrophic beliefs.

### 6. **Implement Exposure and Response Prevention for Compulsive Organizing**
- **Action:** Gradually reduce compulsive organizing and increase tolerance for imperfection. Use a hierarchy of situations ranked by difficulty.
- **Example:**
  - Level 1: Leave one drawer slightly disorganized for 1 hour
  - Level 2: Leave one room unpacked for 4 hours
  - Level 3: Let Peter organize one area his way for one day
  - Level 4: Spend an evening with Peter without checking/organizing anything
- **Practice:** Assign one exposure task per week. Have the patient rate anxiety before, during, and after. Review in session.

### 7. **Address Perfectionism and Guilt Directly**
- **Action:** Explore the patient's belief that relaxation or imperfection equals failure or irresponsibility. Challenge the equation of productivity with worth.
- **Example:** "You said you feel guilty when you're not doing something productive. What would it mean about you if you took an evening off?" Listen for beliefs like "I'd be lazy," "I'd be irresponsible," "Things would fall apart." Challenge these with evidence.
- **Practice:** Use Socratic questioning to help the patient examine whether his worth truly depends on productivity, or whether he can be valuable while relaxing.

### 8. **Eliminate Personal Anecdotes and Maintain Professional Boundaries**
