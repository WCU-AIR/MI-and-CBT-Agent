### Acceptance

**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal unconditional positive regard. While opening establishes basic warmth, clinician quickly becomes prescriptive and directive. Repeated use of "should" language and pressure ("try to push yourself," "make some changes sooner rather than later") conveys judgment rather than acceptance of client's ambivalence. Fails to normalize client's struggle with stress, fatigue, and single parenting. Lacks genuine affirmation of client's strengths, awareness, or willingness to engage. Superficial praise ("It's good you're thinking about this") rather than authentic validation of worth or effort.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal reflective listening and failure to explore emotional depth. Two brief reflections present ("That sounds tough," "I understand that fear is a big barrier") but superficial and not followed by deeper exploration. Uses "but" to negate reflections, contradicting empathic stance. Critically misses emotional core of resistance: fear. Client explicitly states fear multiple times, yet clinician treats it as problem to solve with practical solutions rather than emotion to understand. Fails to pick up on client's implicit wisdom when identifying own solutions. Does not reflect back client's fear with genuine curiosity or depth.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Almost no genuine collaboration. Clinician adopts expert-driven, authoritarian stance throughout. Prescriptive language dominates: "I think you should," "you might want to," "you should try," "you should just make the appointment." Client input not genuinely sought or incorporated. When client identifies own solution ("Maybe if someone could come with me"), psychiatrist doesn't acknowledge as client's wisdom but prescribes same solution as if clinician's idea. Power held entirely by clinician. Client positioned as passive recipient of advice rather than active partner. No co-construction of solutions. Clinician decides what discussed, what changes needed, what timeline appropriate.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Actively undermines autonomy throughout session. Prescriptive language communicates pressure and external control: "you should try to push yourself," "make some changes sooner rather than later," "you should just make the appointment," "you should try to bring a friend." Never explicitly affirms client's right to choose. No language like "This is your decision" or "You get to decide." When client expresses ambivalence ("I still don't know if I'll actually go"), clinician doesn't respect as legitimate expression of autonomy but reframes as if commitment already made. Subtle but powerful negation of client self-determination. Creates pressure around timing and action rather than supporting choice.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Fundamental failure of MI core principle. Clinician consistently tells client what to do rather than asking to explore own reasons for change. Leading questions suggest answers rather than evoking thinking: "Do you think it would help if you tried to eat healthier?" Doesn't explore stress eating further to understand client's own insights. Uses scaling question but not evocatively—doesn't ask "What would help you move from 6 to 7?" or "What's already working?" Instead prescribes: "I think you should try to push yourself." When client identifies own solution, clinician doesn't evoke further exploration. Never asks about client's values, what health means to her, or her own reasons for change. Consistently imposes clinician's agenda and solutions rather than drawing out client's own motivations and resources.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** No genuine partnership. Clinician positions self as expert with superior knowledge. Phrases like "I think you should," "It would probably help if," "Maybe you should" consistently position psychiatrist as authority. Client's contributions not valued or incorporated. When client identifies barrier ("feels like too much to think about"), clinician doesn't explore but contradicts with "but maybe you should try." When client identifies solution, clinician doesn't acknowledge wisdom but prescribes it. Power held entirely by clinician. Clinician dismisses client concerns: "It probably won't be as bad as you think" minimizes experience and suggests clinician knows better. No co-construction of solutions. True partnership would position client as expert on own life and change process.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Misses multiple opportunities to evoke and reinforce client's change talk. Client offers recognizable change talk: "I know there's something in there," "Maybe if someone could come with me...then maybe I'd think about it," "I think I could try that," "Maybe I can try to take one small step." However, clinician fails to strategically amplify through open-ended questions, reflections, and reinforcement. When client expresses awareness of problem, clinician doesn't explore or reinforce. When client identifies own solution, clinician doesn't reinforce problem-solving ability. When client offers tentative commitment, clinician doesn't reinforce or explore further. Never asks questions designed to evoke change talk: "What would be good about making changes? What could you do? What's your idea?" Generic praise at end ("It's good you're thinking about this") rather than specific amplification of client's change talk.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Responds to sustain talk with confrontation and pressure rather than empathic exploration and strategic softening. Client offers significant sustain talk: overwhelm, fear of failure, fear of doctor, uncertainty about commitment. Clinician's response problematic: uses direct confrontation ("but maybe you should try to start small anyway"), dismisses concerns ("It probably won't be as bad as you think"), and argues against client's stated concerns. Does not employ skillful techniques: no double-sided reflections, no exploration of sustain talk, no reframing, no empathic validation without reinforcement. Approach likely strengthens sustain talk rather than softening it. Missing opportunities to explore fear, which often allows client to process and move forward. Pressure-based responses typically reinforce resistance.

## Overall Assessment

**Overall Session Score: 1.6/5**

**MI Competency Level:** Below-beginner level

**Summary:** This session demonstrates fundamental misalignment with Motivational Interviewing principles. While psychiatrist maintains basic respect and warmth, session is characterized by prescriptive advice-giving, minimal evocation, failed collaboration, and undermined autonomy. Clinician adopts expert-driven stance positioning client as passive recipient of guidance rather than active partner in change process. Appears to believe MI involves being supportive while giving advice—a fundamental misunderstanding. True MI requires evoking intrinsic motivation through partnership, empathy, and respect for autonomy, none consistently demonstrated here.

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Pervasive prescriptive language and pressure undermining autonomy and collaboration
- Failure to evoke client's own motivations, ideas, and resources
- Minimal empathic exploration of emotional experience, particularly fear
- Expert-driven stance maintaining control over session content and direction
- Confrontation with sustain talk rather than skillful softening
- Missed opportunities to amplify client's change talk and problem-solving

**Most Critical Improvements Needed:**
1. Shift from prescriptive to evocative stance—replace "you should" with genuine open-ended questions
2. Develop genuine collaboration—ask before telling; invite client to generate ideas
3. Explore rather than dismiss emotions—respond to fear with curiosity and validation
4. Recognize and reinforce client problem-solving—reflect back as client's own wisdom
5. Seek formal MI training—intuitive supportive counseling insufficient for MI competency

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation:** Low. While client may comply superficially with recommendations, unlikely to develop genuine, sustained motivation for health behavior change. Core barriers (fear, overwhelm) unaddressed. Autonomy undermined. Client's problem-solving not valued. Pressure-based approach may strengthen resistance. For genuine motivation development, clinician must shift to evocative, collaborative, autonomy-supportive approach.
