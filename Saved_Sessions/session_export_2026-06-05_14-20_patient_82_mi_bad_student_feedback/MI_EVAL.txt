### **SUMMARY OF CLINICAL EVALUATION**

#### **Strengths:**
- **Late-session shift:** Final two exchanges demonstrate significant movement toward MI-consistent practice, including reflective listening, validation of client's values, and open-ended questions about his perspective and readiness for change.
- **Eventual recognition of complexity:** Psychiatrist eventually recognizes that "your community and sense of belonging are really important to you," demonstrating some capacity for perspective-taking.
- **Openness to exploration:** Final questions ("What do you think might help you feel more supported?" and "What are your biggest hopes or concerns?") invite genuine collaboration and evocation.

#### **Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- **Pervasive expert-driven stance:** Psychiatrist maintains authoritarian, directive approach for majority of session, repeatedly telling client what he "should," "needs," and "has to" do rather than exploring his perspective collaboratively.
- **Confrontational responses to ambivalence:** Clinician argues against client's concerns, labels them as excuses, and uses pressure tactics that explicitly shut down client ("it just makes me shut down"). This violates MI spirit and is counterproductive.
- **Suppression of change talk:** When client offers change talk (concern about harm, desire for control, willingness to consider change), psychiatrist fails to amplify it and instead responds with pressure and dismissal.
- **Lack of genuine acceptance:** Psychiatrist demonstrates judgment and disapproval throughout most of session, using language like "You can't just brush it off like it's no big deal" and "You can't keep making excuses."
- **Failure to evoke intrinsic motivation:** Psychiatrist imposes reasons for change rather than drawing out client's own motivations, values, and concerns. This external pressure is likely to increase resistance rather than facilitate change.
- **Rigid, non-collaborative goal-setting:** Psychiatrist imposes single goal (cold turkey cessation) without exploring client's readiness, concerns, or alternative pathways. This violates autonomy support and collaboration.
- **Inconsistent approach:** Dramatic shift from confrontational to collaborative in final exchanges suggests psychiatrist may be following script or responding to perceived failure rather than embodying MI spirit consistently throughout.

#### **Impact on Client Engagement and Change:**
This session is likely to **decrease** client's intrinsic motivation for change and **damage** the therapeutic alliance. Psychiatrist's controlling, dismissive approach triggers defensive responding and explicit shutdown. Client's change talk is suppressed rather than amplified, and core concerns about identity and community are dismissed as excuses rather than validated as legitimate. Rigid, imposed goal of cold turkey cessation is misaligned with client's stage of change (contemplation) and stated readiness, making it unlikely he will commit to this goal or engage in further treatment. Late shift toward MI-consistent practice may partially repair alliance, but comes after substantial damage and is insufficient to overcome earlier rupture. Client is likely to leave session feeling unheard, judged, and pressured rather than supported and understood, significantly reducing likelihood of return for further treatment or engagement in meaningful change work.

### **PRIORITY RECOMMENDATIONS**

1. **Establish genuine partnership from outset** – Replace directive language with collaborative framing that positions client as expert on his own life.

2. **Validate and explore ambivalence rather than confront it** – Use double-sided reflections that acknowledge both appeal of status quo and concerns about change.

3. **Evoke rather than impose reasons for change** – Ask open-ended questions designed to draw out client's own motivations, values, and concerns.

4. **Amplify and develop change talk** – When clients offer change talk, respond with reflections that deepen and strengthen this language.

5. **Support autonomy and client choice** – Consistently affirm client's right to choose and ownership of change process; avoid threats or pressure tactics.

6. **Explore meaning and function of behavior** – Before attempting to change behavior, deeply understand what it means to client and what function it serves in their life.

7. **Avoid confrontation and argument** – Recognize that direct argument tends to increase defensive responding; use strategic reflection and validation instead.

8. **Maintain consistency throughout session** – Embody MI spirit consistently from beginning to end, not just at end.

9. **Develop skill in handling contemplation-stage clients** – Adjust approach based on client's stage of change; for contemplation stage, focus on exploring ambivalence and evoking motivation rather than pushing for commitment.

10. **Seek formal MI training and supervision** – This session demonstrates significant gaps in MI competence; pursue formal training, certification, or consultation with MI expert.
