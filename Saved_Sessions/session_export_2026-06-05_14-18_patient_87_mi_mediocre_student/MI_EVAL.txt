# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist establishes basic warmth in opening but quickly abandons acceptance by delivering unsolicited advice despite patient's explicit request against lectures. Minimal affirmation of patient's autonomy, 40-year survival, or willingness to listen. Conditional regard communicated through repeated "you should" language (8+ instances). No validation of patient's right to choose or ambivalence about change.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Surface-level reflective listening present but fails to capture emotional nuance or underlying perspective. Misses critical emotional content including hopelessness ("I can't see myself changing now"), autonomy concerns ("I don't like lectures"), and fatigue. Clinician hears words but not the person. Fails to reflect ambivalence or explore why patient feels tired and unmotivated. No clarifying questions to deepen understanding of internal experience.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains adequate but inconsistent focus on smoking cessation as target behavior. Session does not wander aimlessly; clinician consistently returns to smoking, cutting back, triggers, and coping strategies. However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively established. Clinician decides smoking is "the main thing" without genuinely exploring patient's priorities. Direction becomes increasingly prescriptive rather than exploratory by mid-session.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Severe failure in collaboration. Psychiatrist adopts expert-driven, hierarchical stance throughout, treating patient as passive recipient of advice rather than equal partner. Unilateral problem-solving with 8+ instances of "you should" or "you might want to." Dismisses patient's stated preferences ("not much of a gym guy") by reframing as problem to solve. No genuine exploration of patient's ideas. Clinician controls agenda, pace, solutions, and final summary without inviting patient input.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support nearly absent despite closing statement that "it's up to you." Repeated directive language throughout undermines this performative autonomy statement. Psychiatrist explicitly ignores patient's stated preference against lectures by delivering exactly that. Prescriptive framing ("it would probably help if") presents clinician's suggestions as objectively correct with no room for patient choice. When patient rates confidence at 4/10, clinician proceeds with plan rather than exploring what would increase readiness. Conditional support implied.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Evocation almost entirely absent. Psychiatrist operates in "telling" mode rather than "asking" mode. Patient generates zero ideas; clinician proposes all solutions (cutting down gradually, setting small goals, identifying triggers, exercising, listening to music, tracking progress). No exploration of patient's values, resources, past attempts, or wisdom about change. Closed or leading questions dominate. No exploration of patient's confidence level or what would strengthen motivation. Patient's ambivalence never explored.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist positions himself as authority on smoking cessation; patient positioned as novice requiring education. Clinician generates entire plan; patient's role is to evaluate psychiatrist's ideas. Final summary delivered without patient input ("So to sum up, you've been smoking for a long time..."). No acknowledgment of patient's expertise about himself. Clinician controls pace and direction unilaterally. Implicit hierarchy established through prescriptive language. No power-sharing or mutual decision-making.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** While psychiatrist does not actively suppress change talk, clinician fails to skillfully evoke, amplify, or reinforce it. Patient's change talk is minimal and weak ("could go for that," "maybe," "might be more doable"). When patient expresses change talk, clinician does not follow up with amplifying questions. No reinforcement of patient's insights. When patient rates confidence at 4/10, clinician does not explore what gives that confidence or what would strengthen it. Session ends without eliciting commitment language or exploring patient's willingness to try specific steps.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist does not actively reinforce sustain talk but fails to skillfully soften or redirect it. Significant sustain talk remains largely unaddressed: "I can't see myself changing now," "I've tried before, didn't work," "I don't like lectures," "I'm not much of a gym guy," "I'm about as motivated as I am tired." Sustain talk not directly explored or reframed. No double-sided reflections to help patient explore ambivalence. Fatigue and underlying barriers bypassed rather than addressed. Sustain talk not normalized or validated. Psychiatrist's pressure toward change may inadvertently strengthen resistance through psychological reactance.

---

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.6/5 – Poor MI Practice**

### Key Strengths
- Basic warmth and respect in opening
- Acknowledgment of patient's willingness to listen
- Consistency of focus on smoking cessation
- Non-hostile tone maintained throughout

### Critical Deficiencies
1. **Directive advice-giving dominates** – Fundamentally inconsistent with MI spirit
2. **Expert-driven stance** – Violates core principle that expertise about change resides within client
3. **Ignores explicit autonomy concerns** – Patient states "I don't like lectures"; clinician delivers exactly that
4. **Minimal evocation** – Clinician tells rather than asks; patient generates zero ideas
5. **Weak empathy** – Surface-level reflections; no exploration of emotional experience or ambivalence
6. **Sustain talk bypassed** – Significant resistance not skillfully addressed
7. **Change talk not amplified** – Faint change talk not strategically strengthened
8. **Collaboration absent** – Unilateral decision-making throughout
9. **Autonomy violated** – Prescriptive language and conditional support

### Impact on Client Engagement
**This session is likely to undermine rather than enhance motivation for change.** Patient's explicit request against lectures was violated, triggering potential psychological reactance. Directive stance contradicts MI principle that intrinsic motivation is more powerful than external pressure. Patient's faint change talk was not evoked or amplified. Commitment is weak and tentative (confidence 4/10). Without genuine engagement and intrinsic motivation, patient is unlikely to follow through on suggestions and will likely return to smoking as after previous quit attempts.

### Recommendation
Psychiatrist must fundamentally shift from **telling to asking**, from **expert to partner**, and from **imposing solutions to evoking patient's own wisdom about change**. Develop genuine curiosity about patient's perspective, honor autonomy, and help patient discover intrinsic reasons for change rather than delivering reasons from outside. This session does not embody MI spirit and requires significant practice and reflection to improve.
