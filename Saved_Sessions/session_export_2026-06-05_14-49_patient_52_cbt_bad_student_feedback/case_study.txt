--- CASE STUDY ---

Isaac Ulysse, a 29-year-old surgery resident at a large urban hospital, was referred to his employee assistance program for evaluation due to burnout. Supervisors noted increasing distractibility, sadness, sloppiness, and tardiness. Dr. Ulysse reported chronic exhaustion, poor sleep, concentration difficulties, and feelings of worthlessness over the past 12–18 months. He had resisted evaluation until threatened with work suspension.

He described a high-achieving childhood as the only Black child in a predominantly white school, with parents frequently working and a grandmother serving as primary caregiver. His grandmother died of COVID-19 18 months prior, after which he experienced intense grief, recurrent nightmares, guilt over not convincing her to get vaccinated, and preoccupation with her death. He also reported social withdrawal, cynicism about work, anger toward colleagues, and feelings of numbness. He denied suicidal intent but experienced recurrent daydreams about dying to reunite with his grandmother.

Dr. Ulysse initially requested a burnout diagnosis, perceiving MDD or grief-related labels as stigmatizing. His symptoms met criteria for ICD-11 burnout but also fulfilled DSM-5-TR criteria for major depressive disorder (persistent low mood, insomnia, fatigue, guilt, impaired concentration, recurrent thoughts of death) and for prolonged grief disorder (identity disruption, disbelief about the death, intense emotional pain, social and occupational impairment, loneliness, sense of life as meaningless). His grief-related symptoms were maladaptive relative to cultural norms, causing significant functional impairment.

Discussion: Dr. Ulysse’s depression and prolonged grief disorder are exacerbated by stigma around mental illness and isolation. He is at potential risk for substance misuse to self-medicate insomnia or fatigue, though none was reported. Therapeutic engagement and accurate diagnosis are critical to treatment planning and safe return to work.
