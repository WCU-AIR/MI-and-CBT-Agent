# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance with strong validation of emotional experience. Affirms client self-awareness multiple times ("That shows a lot of insight and readiness"). However, could deepen acceptance by more fully acknowledging legitimate fears about relapse and affirming client's inherent worth independent of appearance-focused goals. Moves quickly past hesitation rather than sitting with it fully.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong reflective listening with accurate perspective-taking and emotional attunement. Mountain metaphor demonstrates sophisticated understanding of client's abstract feelings. Captures distinction between external change and internal emotional transformation. Accurately reflects ambivalence regarding confidence level. Could be strengthened through more complex reflections exploring deeper emotional meaning (e.g., shame beneath "feeling gross") and emotional resonance of past successes rather than remaining at surface level.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Maintains clear, appropriate focus on exercise initiation and body image goals with logical session progression: exploring meaning → identifying barriers → exploring past successes → collaborative planning → identifying support → establishing next steps. However, misses opportunity to explore family responsibilities as potential barrier when client mentions "family stuff going on." Does not reassess confidence rating after plan development to reinforce progress toward change goal.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Early collaboration is genuine with open invitations ("what would you most like to focus on"). Explicitly seeks permission before offering advice. However, collaboration becomes more unidirectional during planning phase. Psychiatrist presents specific recommendations (10-minute walks, three times weekly) as relatively fixed ideas rather than co-constructing options. Limited exploration of alternatives or back-and-forth problem-solving. Does not collaboratively problem-solve implementation of client's identified support needs.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Generally supports client autonomy through choice-affirming questions and permission-seeking before advice-giving. Frames suggestions as "one approach that many find helpful" rather than prescriptive. However, could be strengthened by explicitly acknowledging client's freedom to modify or reject suggestions ("You can take what feels useful and leave the rest"). Does not position client as primary problem-solver before offering suggestions. Could more explicitly emphasize client's freedom and pace.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates some evocation with early questions drawing out client's own motivations and ideas. Excellent past-success exploration skillfully evokes client's resources and knowledge of what works. However, evocation weakens significantly during planning phase—shifts from evoking ideas to presenting psychiatrist's suggestions. Does not ask about client's own ideas for getting started or preferred activities. Does not deeply explore underlying emotional reasons for change beyond initial statements. Misses opportunity to evoke client's own ideas about support implementation.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Early partnership is genuine through collaborative language and curiosity ("I'd love to hear from you"). However, partnership erodes during planning phase as psychiatrist transitions to expert role ("One approach that many find helpful is..."). Does not explicitly acknowledge client as expert on what will work for her body, schedule, and life. When client identifies support needs, psychiatrist affirms but does not position client as expert on implementation. Shifts from co-explorer to consultant stance.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Demonstrates inconsistent cultivation of change talk. Early evocation elicits strong change talk ("I really want to," "I'm sick of feeling this way") which psychiatrist acknowledges but does not strategically amplify. Excellent past-success exploration evokes capability change talk. However, significant missed opportunities: does not redirect ambivalence toward change talk when client expresses worry about relapse; does not ask commitment questions after plan development ("How are you feeling about this plan? What makes you think you can do this?"). Fails to cultivate self-efficacy and commitment change talk at pivotal moment.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Demonstrates solid skill in managing minimal sustain talk. Effectively uses double-sided reflection when client expresses worry about falling back into habits, validating concern while emphasizing confidence. Uses past-success exploration to implicitly counter sustain talk by evoking evidence of capability. Could employ more sophisticated techniques such as strategic amplification of concerns or exploring capability despite barriers. Does not explore family responsibilities barrier or reframe it as manageable given past successes.

---

### **Overall Session Summary**

**Strengths:**
- Strong early engagement with welcoming, curious stance
- Accurate, emotionally attuned reflective listening
- Excellent past-success exploration evoking client resources
- Permission-seeking before advice-giving respects autonomy
- Consistent validation and affirmation of client insight
- Clear, logical session structure with coherent progression
- Effective sustain-talk management with validation rather than confrontation

**Areas for Improvement:**
- Shift from evocation to suggestion during planning phase reduces collaborative problem-solving
- Limited co-construction of solutions; alternatives not explored
- Missed opportunities to deepen change talk and cultivate commitment
- Insufficient exploration of real-world barriers (family responsibilities)
- Limited explicit autonomy support language positioning client as primary problem-solver
- Confidence rating not reassessed after planning
- Shallow exploration of deeper emotional motivations
- Limited partnership language explicitly acknowledging client expertise

**Overall MI Competence Score: 3.6/5 – Adequate with Notable Room for Improvement**

This session demonstrates adequate MI competence with strong rapport and early collaborative exploration. However, MI fidelity weakens during planning phase when psychiatrist shifts from evoking to installing motivation. While client leaves with concrete plan and maintained engagement, deep intrinsic motivation and self-efficacy cultivation are limited. Client has agreed to plan but hasn't fully articulated own commitment or explored confidence in ability to succeed. Represents solid supportive counseling with MI elements, but falls short of exemplary MI practice. Most critical improvements needed: maintain evocation throughout planning, strategically cultivate commitment change talk, co-construct solutions, and strengthen partnership language positioning client as expert on her own life.
