
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. The psychiatrist opens with a confrontational, authoritarian stance: *"Let's cut to the chase"* and immediately frames the interaction as prescriptive rather than collaborative. Most critically, the psychiatrist repeatedly uses language that communicates judgment and disapproval of Marco's choices and character.

The psychiatrist explicitly questions Marco's seriousness and integrity: *"Are you just not taking this seriously?"* and *"You need to be honest with me,"* implying Marco is being dishonest or evasive. When Marco expresses his autonomy and resistance, the psychiatrist responds with dismissal: *"Stop making excuses"* (repeated across multiple exchanges). This language pathologizes Marco's legitimate ambivalence and frames his perspective as a character flaw rather than understandable human resistance.

Most damaging is the psychiatrist's failure to normalize Marco's struggle or affirm his worth despite his smoking. There is no acknowledgment of Marco's courage in attending the appointment, his honesty about his history, or his stated openness to listening. Instead, the psychiatrist communicates: *"You must understand... you need to... you have to"*—language that strips away Marco's agency and communicates conditional regard contingent on compliance. The psychiatrist never affirms Marco as a person of value independent of his smoking behavior.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental, conditional stance that communicates disapproval of Marco's choices and implicitly his character. Acceptance is entirely absent; the session is built on confrontation and prescription rather than unconditional positive regard.

---

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective from their internal frame of reference. This psychiatrist demonstrates minimal empathetic engagement and no complex reflections that capture Marco's emotional experience.

The psychiatrist makes no reflective statements that demonstrate understanding of Marco's perspective. When Marco says *"I can't see myself changing now"* and *"I don't like lectures,"* the psychiatrist does not reflect back understanding of these concerns. Instead, the psychiatrist immediately counters with facts and directives: *"That's exactly why you have to quit immediately."* This is advice-giving, not empathy.

When Marco shares his history of failed quit attempts, the psychiatrist responds with judgment (*"Stop making excuses"*) rather than curiosity or understanding. A empathetic response might be: *"You've tried multiple times and it hasn't stuck—that must be frustrating"* or *"Tell me more about what happened when you tried before."* Instead, the psychiatrist dismisses the experience as insufficient willpower.

Most tellingly, the psychiatrist never acknowledges Marco's stated openness (*"I'm always open to listen"*) as a potential entry point for collaboration. This change talk is completely ignored. The psychiatrist also fails to recognize or reflect Marco's ambivalence—the tension between his recognition of health risks and his difficulty changing. A truly empathetic response would explore this internal conflict with curiosity rather than pressure.

The psychiatrist's repeated use of *"I understand"* followed immediately by contradiction (*"I understand quitting is tough, but you really need to stop making excuses"*) is pseudo-empathy—it mimics empathetic language while actively dismissing the client's experience.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no genuine empathetic engagement. There are no reflective statements, no exploration of Marco's internal experience, and no validation of his perspective. Pseudo-empathetic language is used but immediately contradicted by directive pressure.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI involves maintaining strategic focus on a target behavior while remaining client-centered and collaborative. This psychiatrist does maintain a clear focus on smoking cessation—the target behavior is never ambiguous. However, the execution is entirely inconsistent with MI principles.

The psychiatrist does keep returning to smoking as the topic, which demonstrates some directional consistency. However, the direction is imposed rather than collaboratively established. The psychiatrist never explores with Marco what *he* sees as the priority or what *he* might want to work toward. Instead, the psychiatrist unilaterally declares smoking cessation as non-negotiable: *"It's not optional."*

Additionally, the psychiatrist's direction lacks strategic depth. Rather than progressively clarifying Marco's own reasons for change or exploring his values, the psychiatrist simply repeats the same directive: *"You have to quit smoking right now."* This is repeated across multiple exchanges with no variation in approach, no exploration of barriers, and no attempt to understand what might actually move Marco toward change.

The psychiatrist also fails to maintain focus on the collaborative exploration of Marco's ambivalence. When Marco expresses openness to listening, the psychiatrist does not use this as an opportunity to explore his motivations—instead, the psychiatrist continues with the same confrontational approach. A strategically directed MI session would use Marco's stated openness to deepen exploration of his values, concerns, and potential change pathways.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains clear focus on smoking cessation but does so through imposition rather than collaboration. Direction lacks strategic depth and fails to progressively clarify Marco's own motivations or explore his ambivalence.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, with both parties' knowledge and perspectives valued. This session demonstrates an entirely hierarchical, expert-driven stance with no genuine collaboration.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Fundamentally Shift from Expert to Collaborative Stance**
- **Action**: Begin sessions with genuine curiosity about the client's perspective rather than prescription. Use open-ended questions to understand the client's experience, values, and concerns before offering any direction.
- **Example**: Instead of *"You have to quit smoking right now—no excuses,"* try: *"I'm curious about your experience with smoking. You mentioned you've tried quitting before—tell me about that. What happened?"*
- **Practice**: Record yourself conducting sessions and count how many times you use directive language (*"You need to... You must... You should..."*) versus collaborative language (*"I'm wondering... Tell me more... What do you think?"*). Aim for a 1:3 ratio of directive to collaborative language.

---

### 2. **Recognize and Develop Change Talk**
- **Action**: Train yourself to identify change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment to change) and respond by asking open-ended questions that deepen and develop it. When a client says something positive about change, explore it rather than ignore it.
- **Example**: When Marco says *"I'm always open to listen,"* respond with: *"That's good to hear. What are you open to hearing about? What would be helpful for you to explore?"* rather than continuing with pressure.
- **Practice**: In your next 5 sessions, identify every instance of change talk and write down what you said in response. Did you develop it, ignore it, or undermine it? Commit to developing at least one instance per session.

---

### 3. **Replace Confrontation with Validation and Curiosity**
- **Action**: When clients express sustain talk or resistance, respond with validation and curiosity rather than confrontation. Acknowledge their concerns as understandable and explore them with genuine interest.
- **Example**: Instead of *"Stop making excuses. You just need more willpower,"* try: *"You've tried quitting multiple times and it hasn't stuck. That must be frustrating. Help me understand what happened when you tried before. What got in the way?"*
- **Practice**: In your next session, when a client expresses resistance, pause and ask yourself: *"What is this person's concern underneath this resistance?"* Then ask a curious question rather than a confrontational one.

---

### 4. **Develop Reflective Listening Skills**
- **Action**: Use reflective statements to demonstrate understanding of the client's perspective and emotional experience. Reflections should capture both content and emotion, and should be offered with genuine curiosity rather than as a prelude to advice.
- **Example**: *"You've been smoking for 40 years—it's deeply woven into your identity and your daily life. And you've had some serious health scares that made you think about it, but they didn't quite push you to change. That's a complicated place to be."*
- **Practice**: In your next session, aim for a 2:1 ratio of reflective statements to questions. Record yourself and count. Reflections should outnumber questions.

---

### 5. **Actively Support Client Autonomy**
- **Action**: Explicitly affirm the client's right to choose, even if you disagree with their choice. Use language that emphasizes their agency and self-determination. Avoid language that removes choice or implies coercion.
- **Example**: Instead of *"It's not optional. You have to quit right now,"* try: *"This is ultimately your decision. You know yourself best. What feels right to you? What would need to be different for you to consider making a change?"*
- **Practice**: Eliminate the following phrases from your vocabulary: "You need to," "You must," "You have to," "You should." Replace them with: "You might consider," "Some people find it helpful to," "What do you think about," "How do you see this?"

---

### 6. **Explore and Evoke Client Values and Motivations**
- **Action**: Ask open-ended questions designed to help clients articulate their own reasons for change, their values, and what matters most to them. Trust that the client's own motivations exist and draw them out through skillful questioning.
- **Example**: *"You mentioned your health scares—your open-heart surgery and prostate cancer. What do those experiences mean to you? What do they tell you about what matters in your life? How does smoking fit into that picture?"*
- **Practice**: Develop a set of evocative questions specific to smoking cessation and practice asking them: "What would be better about your life if you quit?" "What concerns you most about your smoking?" "What have you learned from your past attempts?" "What would your partner say about this?" "What do you value most in your life?"

---

### 7. **Use Double-Sided Reflections to Address Ambivalence**
- **Action**: When clients express ambivalence (both reasons for and against change), reflect both sides to demonstrate understanding and help the client explore the tension. This softens resistance without confrontation.
- **Example**: *"On one hand, you've had serious health problems that make you think about quitting. On the other hand, you've been smoking for 40 years and it's a big part of your life, and you're not sure you can change. Both of those things are true for you right now."*
- **Practice**: In your next session with an ambivalent client, offer at least two double-sided reflections. Notice how the client responds—they typically become less defensive and more exploratory.

---

### 8. **Eliminate Pseudo-Empathy and Genuine Empathetic Engagement**
- **Action**: Do not use empathetic language (*"I understand"*) as a prelude to contradiction or pressure. Genuine empathy means truly understanding the client's perspective and allowing that understanding to shape your response.
- **Example**: Instead of *"I understand quitting is tough, but you really need to stop making excuses,"* try: *"I understand quitting is tough. You've tried multiple times and it hasn't worked. That's genuinely difficult. What do you think would need to be different this time?"*
- **Practice**: After each empathetic statement, pause and ask yourself: *"Am I about to contradict or pressure the client?"* If yes, revise your response to remain genuinely empathetic.

### 9. **Reframe Client Resistance as Information, Not Defiance**
- **Action**: Recognize that client resistance and sustain talk are not character flaws or defiance—they are valuable information about the client's concerns, values, and barriers. Use this information to deepen understanding and adjust your approach.
- **Example**: When Marco says *"I don't like being told what to do,"* this is not defiance—it is valuable information that he values autonomy and that pressure will backfire. Adjust your approach: *"I hear you. This is your life and your choice. What would feel right to you?"*
- **Practice**: In your next session, when a client resists, write down what their resistance tells you about their values, concerns, or barriers. Use this information to adjust your approach rather than increase pressure.
