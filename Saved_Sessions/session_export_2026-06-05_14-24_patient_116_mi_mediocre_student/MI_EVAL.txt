### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal collaboration; session reflects expert-driven, hierarchical dynamic. Prescriptive language throughout: "It would probably help if you tried relaxation techniques," "Maybe setting a limit on cigarettes could work," "It would probably help if you keep track." These frame clinician as expert dispensing solutions, not collaborative problem-solving. True collaboration would invite Samir's expertise: "What do you think might help?" "How would you want to track progress?" When Samir expresses hesitation, clinician investigates barriers rather than exploring ambivalence collaboratively. Plan presented as clinician's creation that Samir accepts, not co-created partnership. Power held by clinician, not shared.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal autonomy support; repeated "It would probably help if..." subtly pressures toward clinician's recommendations without explicitly affirming choice. Leading questions presuppose desired answers rather than genuinely inviting autonomous choice. Fails to explicitly affirm Samir's right to choose own pace. When Samir chooses cutting back over quitting, clinician responds investigatively ("Do you think there's anything stopping you from quitting entirely?") rather than supporting his choice. No explicit statement that Samir can choose not to change or proceed at own pace. Session structure implies change is expected outcome; clinician's role is to guide toward it, not support genuine autonomy.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal evocation; clinician relies on information-gathering and advice-giving rather than drawing out client's own motivations and resources. Questions are investigative, not evocative: "Do you think those are the main things?" "Have you noticed anything else?" "Do you think there's anything stopping you?" Evocative questions would explore: "What worries you most about asthma worsening?" "What would better control mean for your life?" When Samir provides change talk ("I don't want to end up in serious trouble"), clinician briefly acknowledges rather than deepens: "That's good to hear" instead of "Tell me more about what serious trouble looks like." Misses opportunities to evoke resources and past successes. Solutions prescribed rather than drawn from Samir's own motivation.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1

Consistently prescriptive language positions psychiatrist as knowing what would help, Samir as needing to be told. Fails to acknowledge Samir's existing expertise and engagement: already identified triggers, cut back on behaviors, taking medication as prescribed, had allergy testing. ## Overall Summary

### **Overall Session Score: 1.6/5**

**Key Strengths:**
- Warm, respectful opening tone establishes initial rapport
- Maintains general focus on target behavior (asthma management and lifestyle triggers)
- Provides some affirmation of effort ("That's good to hear," "That's a good start")
- Uses scaling questions (1-10 confidence/importance ratings) appropriately
- Ends with supportive message acknowledging that change takes time

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
- **Expert-driven stance dominates** — Prescriptive language ("It would probably help if...") frames clinician as expert dispensing solutions rather than collaborative partner
- **Minimal evocation of intrinsic motivation** — Questions are investigative; change talk acknowledged but not amplified; client's resources and past successes not explored
- **Sustain talk validated but not redirected** — When Samir expresses doubt or difficulty, clinician accepts without exploring ambivalence or gently challenging pessimism
- **Missed opportunities to deepen empathy** — Reflections are surface-level and factual; emotional weight of Samir's experience not explored
- **Autonomy not explicitly supported** — No clear message that Samir has right to choose own pace or that decision is entirely his
- **Too many action items introduced too quickly** — Smoking, drinking, stress management, relaxation, tracking, family support, breathing exercises all introduced without depth or prioritization
- **Existing efforts not acknowledged** — Samir's progress (cutting back, medication adherence, allergy testing) framed as insufficient rather than foundation to build upon

**Clinical Impact:**

This session demonstrates significant deficiencies in core MI competencies. While psychiatrist shows basic warmth and maintains focus on target behavior, the session is fundamentally expert-driven and prescriptive rather than collaborative and evocative. Samir is positioned as passive recipient of clinical guidance rather than active expert in his own change process.

While Samir expresses willingness to try proposed steps, this appears to be compliance with clinician's agenda rather than genuine commitment to own goals. The psychiatrist's failure to amplify change talk, explore ambivalence, or acknowledge existing efforts misses crucial opportunities to strengthen intrinsic motivation.

**This approach is unlikely to sustain long-term behavior change, particularly for smoking—a behavior deeply entrenched in Samir's stress management system.** The psychiatrist must fundamentally shift from expert-driven to collaborative stance, prioritize evocation of intrinsic motivation over prescription of solutions, and develop genuine partnership with Samir as expert in his own change process.

---

### **Immediate Priorities for Improvement:**

1. **Eliminate prescriptive language** — Replace "It would probably help if..." with collaborative exploration: "What do you think would help?" "What's your instinct?" "How would you want to approach this?"

2. **Amplify change talk** — When Samir expresses motivation, pause and explore deeply: "Tell me more about that. What else concerns you about your asthma?"

3. **Explore ambivalence strategically** — Use double-sided reflections when Samir expresses sustain talk: "Part of you recognizes the need to quit, and another part feels it's too big a leap right now. Tell me more about that tension."

4. **Explicitly support autonomy** — Clearly communicate: "This is entirely up to you. You get to decide what feels right for your life."

5. **Deepen empathic reflections** — Move beyond facts to emotional meaning: "Smoking feels like your lifeline when you're stuck in traffic dealing with difficult passengers—your way to reclaim control and calm in a stressful situation."

6. **Acknowledge existing efforts** — Begin by recognizing what Samir is already doing: "You've already identified your triggers, you're taking your medication, you've started cutting back. That shows real commitment. What's working for you so far?"

7. **Prioritize and slow down** — Collaboratively focus on one behavior at a time rather than introducing multiple action items simultaneously.

8. **Develop genuine partnership language** — Invite Samir's expertise throughout: "You know your life better than I do. What do you think would actually work?"

---

**Recommendation:** Engage in focused deliberate practice on core MI competencies with supervisor feedback and recorded session review. Priority focus: shifting from expert-driven to collaborative stance and developing skill in evoking and amplifying intrinsic motivation for change.
