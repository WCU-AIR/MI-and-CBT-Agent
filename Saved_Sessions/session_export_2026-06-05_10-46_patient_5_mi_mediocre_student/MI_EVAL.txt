### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Collaboration is essentially absent. Psychiatrist adopts expert-driven, directive stance fundamentally inconsistent with MI principles. Repeatedly tells Aizada what to do rather than exploring her ideas, preferences, and wisdom. Examples: recommends specialist referral, suggests symptom tracking, advises doctor-switching, instructs relaxation techniques—all without asking what Aizada thinks might help or what barriers exist. **Comments:** Minimal autonomy support. Directive language constrains Aizada's sense of choice and agency. Repeated use of softened directives ("probably," "might want to," "should consider") still conveys prescription. Examples: "It would probably help if you keep track of symptoms"; "You might want to push a bit more with Dr. Brown." No exploration of Aizada's own ideas or genuine respect for her right to choose. ### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Evocation is essentially absent—a critical MI deficiency. Psychiatrist tells rather than asks, imposes solutions rather than evoking them. Does not draw out: Aizada's own theories about symptoms, her ideas about what might help, her values and priorities, her resources and strengths, her own motivation for change. Missed opportunities: When Aizada mentions wedding stress-symptom connection, psychiatrist does not ask "Tell me more about that"; when expressing exhaustion, psychiatrist does not ask "What would help you feel less overwhelmed?" Fundamental approach is prescriptive rather than exploratory. No questions like "What do you think?" "What have you tried that helped?" "What matters most to you?"

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Partnership is fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role and maintains unilateral power throughout. Positions self as authority on what Aizada should do, offering prescriptive advice on referrals, tracking, relaxation, and doctor-switching. Language reflects expert authority: "You might want to..." "It would probably help if..." "You should consider..." No genuine invitation for Aizada's expertise about her own experience. No co-construction of solutions; instead, solutions presented by psychiatrist with expectation of compliance. No shared decision-making or power-sharing. Aizada's final statement—"I feel like no one really understands how hard this has been"—directly reflects absence of partnership and leaves her feeling unheard and disempowered.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Minimal skill in evoking, recognizing, and reinforcing Aizada's own language favoring change. Some change talk emerges naturally but is not strategically amplified. Example: When Aizada recognizes stress-symptom connection ("It wasn't so bad during my son's wedding"), psychiatrist does not explore or reinforce—moves instead to asking about Dr. Brown. Missed opportunities to deepen insight through open questions: "Tell me more about that. What was different?" Change talk that emerges is largely extrinsic (complying with suggestions) rather than intrinsic (Aizada's own reasons). No strategic amplification techniques: open questions to evoke more, reflections that strengthen, summaries that emphasize change talk.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Minimal skill in addressing sustain talk (reasons against change, arguments for status quo). Allows sustain talk to persist or inadvertently reinforces it. Examples of unaddressed sustain talk: "It's not in my head"; "Stress doesn't cause stabbing pain"; "I don't trust that explanation"; "It feels like a waste of time." When Aizada expresses cancer fear, psychiatrist responds with practical advice rather than exploring or softening fear. When expressing skepticism about relaxation, psychiatrist does not genuinely accept skepticism but encourages compliance. Missed double-sided reflections: "You're clear this is real pain—and you're also noticing stress makes it worse. Both are true." Recommending specialist referral may inadvertently reinforce health anxiety by validating need for more testing.

## **OVERALL ASSESSMENT**

### **Global MI Competency Score: 1.6/5**

**Summary:** Session demonstrates **poor MI practice** with significant deficiencies in core competencies. While psychiatrist maintains basic courtesy and engagement, session is fundamentally **expert-driven and directive** rather than collaborative and evocative. Psychiatrist tells Aizada what to do rather than exploring her own ideas, imposes solutions rather than drawing out motivations, and fails to skillfully address health anxiety or sustain talk.

**Current MI Competency Level:** **Minimal MI competence.** Basic supportive counseling present, but true MI—characterized by partnership, evocation, autonomy support, and skillful handling of ambivalence—is largely absent. Session reflects traditional medical model (expert diagnosing and prescribing) rather than MI model (collaborative exploration of motivation and change).

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- ☐ Directive, expert-driven approach contradicts MI principles
- ☐ Minimal evocation; solutions imposed rather than drawn out
- ☐ Failure to address health anxiety without reinforcing it
- ☐ Missed opportunities to cultivate spontaneous change talk
- ☐ Inadequate handling of sustain talk; no double-sided reflections
- ☐ Shallow empathy; factual summaries rather than emotional attunement
- ☐ Lack of genuine collaboration and partnership
- ☐ Autonomy undermined through subtle pressure and directive language

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation for Change:** **Low.** Session unlikely to increase intrinsic motivation. May reinforce focus on biomedical solutions, intensify health anxiety, and leave Aizada feeling unheard. Final statement—"I feel like no one really understands how hard this has been"—indicates therapeutic relationship not established as foundation for change.

**Recommendation:** **Significant retraining in MI principles and techniques required.** Consider formal MI training/certification, supervised practice with feedback, audio/video review of sessions, study of MI literature (particularly health anxiety and sustain talk), and role-play practice with focus on shifting from directive to collaborative stance.

**This session embodies traditional medical expert model rather than MI spirit of partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation. Fundamental reorientation toward MI principles is essential.**
