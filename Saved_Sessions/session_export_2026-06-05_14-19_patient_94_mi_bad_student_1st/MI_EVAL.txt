### Collaboration

Opening statement establishes expert-driven, non-collaborative frame: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to quit." ### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no evocation of client's own motivations, values, ideas, or resources. Provides reasons for change (liver disease, brain damage) without exploring patient's intrinsic motivation. Never asks questions designed to evoke motivation: "What concerns you most?" "What would be different if you reduced drinking?" "What strengths do you have?" Tells patient what should concern her rather than exploring what patient values. Patient offers multiple instances of change talk ("I'm willing to work on drinking less," "I want to be healthy," "I'm open to that") but psychiatrist dismisses each instance. Fails to amplify, reinforce, or build upon emerging change talk. Critical failure in evocation technique.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1

Opening statement explicitly positions psychiatrist as sole expert: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to quit." ### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Actively discourages and dismisses change talk rather than cultivating it. Patient provides multiple instances of change talk: "I'm willing to cut back," "I want to be healthy," "I'm open to that," "I'm willing to work on drinking less." Psychiatrist's response to each instance is rejection or dismissal: "No, no, cutting back isn't enough," "No, you can't just say 'maybe' or 'try'—that's not good enough." Message conveyed: "Your willingness to change is not acceptable unless it matches my predetermined outcome." Predictably leads to client withdrawing change talk and increasing resistance. Actively works against client's emerging motivation rather than amplifying it. Critical failure in MI technique.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Approach to sustain talk is confrontational and amplifying rather than softening. Patient offers sustain talk: "Seven drinks a week doesn't seem like that much," "I just don't think I'm ready to quit completely." Psychiatrist responds with direct confrontation and labeling: "No, you're wrong to think that way" and "That's just procrastination." Violates MI principle of softening sustain talk through gentle redirection or reframing. Likely to strengthen sustain talk rather than soften it. Maintains some focus on change goal (abstinence) rather than getting pulled into extended discussion, which prevents score of 1/5. However, method used—direct confrontation and labeling—is ineffective and counterproductive in MI framework.

## Overall Session Assessment

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.2/5**

**Summary:** This session represents a fundamental failure to implement Motivational Interviewing principles. The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance, empathy, collaboration, autonomy support, or evocation. The clinician employs a confrontational, expert-driven, coercive approach that directly contradicts evidence-based practice for brief interventions in substance use. The session is likely to be counterproductive, increasing client defensiveness and resistance rather than evoking motivation for change.

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Absence of acceptance and empathy with explicit judgment of patient as "wrong" and "irresponsible"
- Elimination of autonomy and collaboration through explicit denial of patient's right to participate in decisions
- Suppression of change talk through dismissal of patient's emerging motivation
- Confrontational approach to resistance that predictably strengthens rather than softens it
- Failure to evoke patient's intrinsic motivation through questions and exploration
- Repeated ineffective strategy despite escalating client defensiveness and explicit feedback that approach is not working

**Impact on Client Engagement:** Patient's responses show escalating defensiveness throughout session. Early openness ("I think I can cut back") deteriorates to explicit resistance ("feeling forced like this just makes me want to dig in my heels"). Patient is likely to leave session feeling judged, unheard, and less motivated to engage with treatment. Session exemplifies psychological reactance—threat to autonomy increases commitment to opposite position.

**Recommendation:** Clinician requires comprehensive retraining in MI principles, including foundational shift from expert-driven to client-centered approach, implementation of reflective listening as primary intervention, and explicit support for client autonomy throughout sessions.
