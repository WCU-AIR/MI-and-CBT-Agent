
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Foundational Shift: Adopt the MI Spirit**
- **Action:** Recognize that the psychiatrist's role is to evoke the patient's own motivation, not to impose it. The patient already wants to be healthier; the psychiatrist's job is to support that intrinsic motivation, not to override it with directives.
- **Example:** Instead of "You have to quit fast food completely starting today," say: "You mentioned wanting to be healthier for your kids. That's important to you. What do you think would be a realistic first step toward that goal?"
- **Practice:** Before each session, remind yourself: "My job is to help the patient find her own reasons for change, not to convince her of mine." Review MI spirit principles (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation) before each session.

---

### 2. **Replace Directives with Open Questions**
- **Action:** Stop using closed, directive questions ("Are you willing to commit?") and replace them with open questions that invite the patient's perspective and evoke her own motivation.
- **Example:** 
  - Instead of: "Do you eat fast food more than twice a week?"
  - Say: "Tell me about your eating patterns. What does a typical week look like for you?"
  - Instead of: "Are you going to cut out fast food completely starting today?"
  - Say: "If you were to make one change to your eating habits, what would feel most doable for you?"
- **Practice:** Write out 5-10 open questions related to the patient's health goals before each session. Practice asking them aloud. Record yourself and listen for directive language creeping back in.

---

### 3. **Validate and Reflect Sustain Talk Rather Than Confronting It**
- **Action:** When the patient expresses barriers or reasons not to change, use double-sided reflections that validate both the barrier and the motivation for change. This softens resistance rather than hardening it.
- **Example:**
  - Patient: "With the kids and everything, sometimes fast food is just the easiest option."
  - Current response: "Look, you really need to stop making excuses."
  - MI response: "So on one hand, fast food is practical when you're managing a lot with the kids. On the other hand, you care about your health and want to model healthy choices for them. That's a real tension. How do you think about that?"
- **Practice:** When you hear sustain talk, pause and ask yourself: "What is the patient's underlying concern here?" Reflect that concern back before exploring the change side.

---

### 4. **Amplify and Reinforce Change Talk**
- **Action:** When the patient expresses motivation, desire, ability, or commitment to change, use reflective listening to amplify and deepen that language. Ask follow-up questions that expand on the change talk.
- **Example:**
  - Patient: "I want to be healthier for my kids."
  - Current response: "You really need to cut down on fast food and get moving regularly."
  - MI response: "Your kids are really important to you, and you want to be there for them long-term. Tell me more about what being healthier would mean for your family."
- **Practice:** After each patient statement, ask yourself: "Is this change talk or sustain talk?" If it's change talk, reflect it back and ask an open question to deepen it. If it's sustain talk, use a double-sided reflection.

---

### 5. **Respect Autonomy and Collaborate on Goal-Setting**
- **Action:** Stop telling the patient what she "must" do. Instead, acknowledge her expertise about her own life and collaborate on realistic goals that she can own.
- **Example:**
  - Instead of: "You must cut out fast food completely starting today. There's no room for negotiation."
  - Say: "You know your life better than I do. You're managing kids, job searching, and trying to stay healthy. What do you think would be a realistic first step? What feels doable to you?"
- **Practice:** In each session, use language like "What do you think?", "What feels realistic to you?", "How would you approach this?", "What's your sense of what might work?" This positions the patient as the expert on her own life.

---

### 6. **Use Affirmations to Build Confidence and Recognize Strengths**
- **Action:** Explicitly recognize and affirm the patient's strengths, efforts, and resilience. The patient is already managing a lot; acknowledge that rather than focusing only on what she's not doing.
- **Example:**
  - "You're managing kids, job searching, and still trying to stay healthy. That takes real strength."
  - "You're not smoking, you're thinking about your kids' health, and you're willing to make changes. Those are real positives."
  - "You're being honest about what you can realistically do. That kind of self-awareness is important for lasting change."
- **Practice:** At the beginning and end of each session, identify at least two genuine strengths or efforts to affirm. Write them down and practice saying them naturally.

---

### 7. **Explore Barriers Collaboratively Rather Than Dismissing Them**
- **Action:** When the patient mentions barriers (childcare, fatigue, overwhelm), treat these as legitimate constraints to be problem-solved together, not as excuses to be dismissed.
- **Example:**
  - Patient: "It's hard to keep up with that when there's so much else to deal with."
  - Current response: "Don't tell me it's tough—you have to make it happen."
  - MI response: "It sounds like you're juggling a lot. Let's think about this together. What's the biggest barrier right now? And what resources or support might help you work around that?"
- **Practice:** When you hear a barrier, pause and ask: "What is the patient really telling me? What do they need?" Respond with curiosity and collaboration, not dismissal.

---

### 8. **Establish Realistic, Incremental Goals Rather Than All-or-Nothing Demands**
- **Action:** Work with the patient to identify small, achievable first steps rather than demanding complete behavior change immediately. This builds confidence and momentum.
- **Example:**
  - Instead of: "You must cut out fast food completely starting today."
  - Say: "What if we started with one change? Maybe reducing fast food from, say, 3-4 times a week to 2 times a week? Or finding one healthier meal option you could substitute? What feels like a realistic first step?"
- **Practice:** For each patient goal, ask: "Is this achievable in the next week? Can the patient succeed at this?" If not, break it into smaller steps.

---

### 9. **Shift from Expert Role to Collaborative Partner**
- **Action:** Explicitly position yourself as a collaborator, not an expert who knows what's best. Share information when relevant, but frame it as one input among many, not as the final word.
- **Example:**
  - Instead of: "You have to get at least 30 minutes of exercise every day. That's essential."
  - Say: "Research shows that regular movement is important for health. You mentioned playing with your kids and walking. What if we thought about how to build on that? What would feel sustainable for you?"
- **Practice:** Use phrases like "I wonder...", "What's your sense of...?", "You know your life better than I do...", "Let's think about this together..." These communicate partnership.

### 10. **Develop Genuine Empathic Listening Skills**
- **Action:** Practice reflective listening that demonstrates accurate understanding of the patient's perspective, emotions, and constraints. This is the foundation of MI.
- **Example:**
  - Patient: "With the kids and everything, sometimes fast food is just the easiest option. I didn't keep track because, honestly, it's overwhelming on top of everything else."
  - MI response: "So you're managing a lot—kids, job searching, trying to stay healthy. Fast food feels like a practical solution when you're overwhelmed. And keeping track of everything feels like one more thing that would push you over the edge. That makes sense."
- **Practice:** Record yourself in sessions. Listen back and count how many reflections you make vs. how many directives you give. Aim for a 3:1 ratio of reflections to directives. Practice reflective listening in non-clinical settings (conversations with friends, family) to build the skill.

### 11. **Recognize and Address Your Own Frustration**
- **Action:** The psychiatrist's repeated ultimatums and confrontations suggest frustration with the patient's ambivalence. Recognize that ambivalence is normal and expected. Your job is to work with it skillfully, not to eliminate it through pressure.
- **Example:** When you feel frustrated ("Why won't she just commit?"), pause and remind yourself: "Ambivalence is normal. My job is to evoke her own motivation, not to impose mine. Pressure will backfire."
- **Practice:** Reflect on your own beliefs about behavior change. Do you believe people change through external pressure or through internal motivation? If the latter, how does that change your approach?

### 12. **Seek Supervision and Training in Motivational Interviewing**
- **Action:** This session demonstrates a fundamental misalignment with MI principles. Seek formal MI training, supervision, or consultation to develop competence in this evidence-based approach.
- **Example:** Enroll in an MI training workshop, seek a clinical supervisor trained in MI, or join an MI consultation group where you can review recordings of your sessions and receive feedback.
