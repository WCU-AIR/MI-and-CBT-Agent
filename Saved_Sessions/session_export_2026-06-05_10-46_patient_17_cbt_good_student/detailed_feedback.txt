
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

The therapist demonstrates solid foundational CBT skills: collaborative agenda-setting, guided discovery, accurate reflection of thought-emotion-behavior cycles, and warm interpersonal presence. These skills would be appropriate for a patient with depression, anxiety, or other non-psychotic conditions. However, the therapist lacks competency in:

1. **Bipolar disorder treatment** (no mention of medication, no assessment of manic symptoms, no relapse prevention)
2. **Psychosis management** (no assessment of delusions, no understanding of limited efficacy of cognitive therapy for psychotic symptoms)
3. **Acute vs. maintenance phase treatment** (no distinction between acute stabilization and longer-term therapy)
4. **Behavioral interventions for mania** (no sleep hygiene, activity regulation, or behavioral deactivation)

**Most Critical Changes Needed Immediately:**

1. **Integrate medication management** into the treatment conceptualization and explicitly discuss the role of pharmacotherapy
2. **Assess and address psychotic features** directly, recognizing that delusions require antipsychotic medication and that cognitive therapy alone is insufficient
3. **Assess critical manic symptoms** (sleep, psychomotor activity, racing thoughts) and intervene appropriately
4. **Implement homework review** in every session without exception
5. **Develop a comprehensive treatment plan** that distinguishes acute and maintenance phases and coordinates psychotherapy with pharmacotherapy

**Whether Session Qualifies as Evidence-Based CBT and Likelihood of Producing Therapeutic Change:**

This session does **not** qualify as evidence-based CBT for bipolar I disorder with psychotic features. While the techniques applied (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experimentation) are evidence-based for depression and anxiety, they are not the primary interventions for acute mania with psychosis. The evidence-based approach for this presentation is:

1. **Pharmacological stabilization** (mood stabilizer + antipsychotic) as the foundation
2. **Psychoeducation** about bipolar disorder and medication adherence
3. **Behavioral interventions** (sleep hygiene, activity regulation, stress management)
4. **Cognitive therapy** for residual symptoms *after* acute mania is stabilized

By focusing exclusively on cognitive restructuring without addressing medication, psychotic symptoms, or manic behavioral patterns, the therapist is unlikely to produce meaningful therapeutic change. The patient's core symptoms (grandiosity, urgency, racing thoughts, decreased need for sleep) are unlikely to resolve through cognitive work alone. The homework (pausing and breathing) may provide modest benefit in reducing impulsive behavior, but without pharmacological management and behavioral interventions, the underlying manic episode will likely persist or escalate.

**Risk Assessment:**

This approach carries moderate to significant risk of harm through:
- Delayed pharmacological intervention
- Failure to monitor critical manic symptoms
- Inadequate relapse prevention
- Potential escalation of manic symptoms if the patient believes cognitive work is sufficient and deprioritizes medication

**Recommendation:**

The therapist should immediately seek consultation with a bipolar disorder specialist or supervisor, pursue specialized training in bipolar disorder treatment, and restructure the treatment approach to align with evidence-based guidelines. The current approach, while well-intentioned and demonstrating good foundational skills, is fundamentally misaligned with the patient's clinical needs.
