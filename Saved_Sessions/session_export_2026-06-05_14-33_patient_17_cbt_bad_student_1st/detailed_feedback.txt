
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda

## Recommendations

### 1. **URGENT: Recognize and Respond to Psychiatric Emergency**
- **Action:** Immediately refer Mark for psychiatric evaluation and medication management. Assess for need for hospitalization given severe sleep deprivation, grandiose delusions, poor insight, and history of previous hospitalization.
- **Example:** "Mark, I'm concerned about your safety and well-being. The symptoms you're describing—needing very little sleep, feeling like you have a special mission, racing thoughts—suggest you need to see a psychiatrist right away. - **Practice:** Develop protocols for recognizing psychiatric emergencies (acute mania, psychosis, severe depression with suicidality) and immediate referral pathways. Never attempt to treat acute mania with psychotherapy alone.

### 2. **Establish Clear Agenda and Treatment Goals**
- **Action:** At the beginning of each session, collaboratively establish an agenda with specific, measurable goals. Prioritize issues based on clinical urgency and patient input.
- **Example:** "Mark, I want to make sure we use our time well today. I'm hearing several things: you're sleeping very little, you're having thoughts about a special mission, and your mind is racing. What feels most important to address today? And I want to be honest—some of what you're describing concerns me medically, so we may need to involve a psychiatrist. Let's talk about that."
- **Practice:** Use a written agenda at the start of each session. Practice prioritizing issues based on clinical urgency (safety, psychiatric symptoms) vs. patient preference. Learn to balance collaboration with clinical judgment about what needs to be addressed.

### 3. **Eliminate Excessive Self-Disclosure**
- **Action:** Dramatically reduce personal anecdotes. Self-disclosure should be rare, strategic, and clearly in service of the patient's treatment—not for the therapist's comfort or connection.
- **Example:** Instead of "I remember when I was super stressed about exams," say: "Many people in your situation find it helpful to... Let's explore what might work for you."
- **Practice:** Record sessions and count instances of self-disclosure. Aim for zero to minimal personal stories. When tempted to share a personal anecdote, ask: "Is this in service of this patient's treatment, or am I making this about me?" If the latter, don't share it.

### 4. **Apply Specific CBT Techniques Appropriately**
- **Action:** Learn and apply evidence-based CBT techniques matched to the patient's condition and current stability level. For acute mania, focus on psychoeducation and behavioral structure; for stabilized bipolar disorder, add cognitive work.
- **Example:** "Let's create a sleep log together to track your sleep each night. We'll also talk about what bipolar disorder is and how sleep affects your mood. Once you're more stable on medication, we can work on examining some of your thoughts more carefully."
- **Practice:** Study specific CBT protocols for bipolar disorder. Practice thought records, behavioral activation, psychoeducation, and reality testing. Understand which techniques are appropriate at which stages of illness (acute vs. maintenance).

### 5. **Systematically Address Delusions Through Gentle Reality Testing**
- **Action:** When delusions are present, gently explore them through Socratic questioning rather than direct confrontation or avoidance. Help the patient examine evidence and consider alternative explanations.
- **Example:** "I hear that you feel like you have a special mission from God. That's clearly very real and important to you. Can we explore this together? What evidence do you have for this belief? What would someone who doesn't believe this say? How do you explain the fact that others don't see you this way? What would need to happen for you to question this belief?"
- **Practice:** Study motivational interviewing and gentle reality testing techniques. Practice with role-plays. Understand that reality testing in acute psychosis requires careful timing and should only be done once the patient is somewhat stabilized on medication.

### 6. **Focus on Key Symptoms: Sleep Deprivation in Bipolar Disorder**
- **Action:** Recognize that severely decreased need for sleep is a medical emergency in bipolar disorder. Systematically assess and address this symptom with urgency.
- **Example:** "You mentioned you're sleeping only a few hours a night and don't feel like you need more sleep. In bipolar disorder, this is actually a critical symptom we need to address immediately. Lack of sleep can make your mood symptoms worse and can be dangerous. Let's talk about what's preventing you from sleeping and what we can do about it. This might require medication adjustment."
- **Practice:** Study the relationship between sleep and bipolar disorder. Learn to assess sleep systematically (hours per night, quality, subjective need for sleep). Understand that behavioral sleep interventions alone are often insufficient in acute mania—medication is usually necessary.

### 7. **Assess and Address Poor Insight (Anosognosia)**
- **Action:** Recognize when patients lack awareness of their illness and gently work to build insight without being confrontational. This is a critical target in acute mania.
- **Example:** "Mark, I notice you said the lack of sleep doesn't bother you and feels like part of who you are right now. I want to gently point out that this thinking—not being concerned about severe sleep deprivation—is actually part of the illness. When people are in a manic episode, they often don't see it as a problem. That's part of the condition. Let's talk about what's happening in your brain and body right now."
- **Practice:** Study psychoeducation about bipolar disorder and anosognosia. Practice gentle, non-confrontational ways of introducing the idea that the patient's perception might be affected by their illness. Use analogies (e.g., "It's like the illness is wearing glasses that make everything look different").

### 8. **Elicit and Respond to Patient Feedback**
- **Action:** Regularly ask for feedback about whether interventions are helpful and adjust approach based on patient input. When a patient says something isn't working, take that seriously and problem-solve together.
- **Example:** "You mentioned that thinking positive doesn't seem to match what you're experiencing. That's really important feedback. Let's talk about what might actually be helpful for you. What have you found helpful in the past? What do you think might help right now?"
- **Practice:** Build feedback elicitation into every session. Ask: "Is this making sense?" "Is this helpful?" "What would be more helpful?" When feedback is negative, don't repeat the same intervention—collaborate on finding alternatives.

### 9. **Assign Specific, Detailed Homework with Clear Rationale**
- **Action:** Assign between-session tasks that are specific, measurable, and clearly connected to treatment goals. Provide written instructions and plan to review at the next session.
- **Example:** "I'd like you to keep a sleep log this week. Write down what time you go to bed, what time you wake up, and how many hours you slept. Also notice what you're thinking about when you can't sleep. Bring this to our next session so we can look at the patterns together. This will help us understand what's happening with your sleep."
- **Practice:** Practice writing clear homework assignments. Include: (1) specific task, (2) rationale, (3) written instructions, (4) discussion of obstacles, (5) explicit commitment from patient, (6) planned review date.

### 10. **Develop Collaborative Treatment Planning**
- **Action:** Work as a true partner with the patient. Solicit input on goals, treatment approach, and strategies. Position the patient as an active agent in their own recovery.
- **Example:** "Mark, I want us to work together on this. You know your experience better than anyone. What do you think would help? What have you tried before? What are you willing to try? Let's figure this out together."
- **Practice:** Practice collaborative language. Replace "You should..." with "What do you think about...?" Replace directives with questions. Genuinely listen to patient input and incorporate it into treatment planning.

### 11. **Understand the Limits of Psychotherapy in Acute Mania**
- **Action:** Recognize that acute mania with psychosis requires psychiatric medication management as the primary intervention. Psychotherapy alone is insufficient and potentially harmful. Know when to refer and when to coordinate care with psychiatry.
- **Example:** "Mark, I want to be clear about something. What you're experiencing—the racing thoughts, the feeling that you have a special mission, the lack of need for sleep—these are symptoms of a medical condition called bipolar disorder. While talking with me can be helpful, you need to see a psychiatrist who can prescribe medication to help stabilize your mood. That's the most important thing right now. I'll work with your psychiatrist to support your treatment."
- **Practice:** Study bipolar disorder treatment guidelines. Understand the role of mood stabilizers, antipsychotics, and other medications. Learn to recognize when psychotherapy alone is inadequate. Develop collaborative relationships with psychiatrists for coordinated care.

### 12. **Maintain Appropriate Professional Boundaries**
- **Action:** Keep the focus on the patient's experience and needs. Avoid making the session about the therapist's experiences or feelings. Maintain clear professional boundaries.
- **Example:** Instead of sharing personal stories, say: "Many people find it helpful to..." or "Research shows that..." or "Let's explore what might work for you."
- **Practice:** Reflect on why you're tempted to share personal stories. Often it's to create connection or normalize the patient's experience—but this can be done without self-disclosure. Practice alternative ways of creating connection and normalizing that keep the focus on the patient.
