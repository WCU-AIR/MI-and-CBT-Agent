### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The psychiatrist demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance. Judgment and disapproval permeate the session through statements like "Stress is no excuse to keep smoking" and "You really need to take this seriously." When the patient proposes small, realistic commitments (cutting back one cigarette daily), the psychiatrist responds with dismissal rather than validation: "That's a start, but honestly, just cutting back by one cigarette a day won't make much difference." There is no warmth, affirmation of the patient's worth, or normalization of her struggle managing work, school, and single parenthood. Acceptance is entirely conditional on compliance with the psychiatrist's prescriptions.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Empathy is virtually absent. The psychiatrist opens with directives rather than exploring the patient's experience. Pseudo-reflections are immediately negated by "but," signaling the psychiatrist's agenda overrides understanding. When the patient expresses fear of failure ("I don't want to fail or set myself up for more stress"), the psychiatrist does not explore this vulnerability; instead, pivots to demands. The patient's core emotional experience—that smoking serves as her primary stress management tool—is never genuinely reflected or explored. The psychiatrist reframes concerns as "excuses" rather than attempting to understand their validity. There is no effort to grasp the function her coping behaviors serve or the emotional nuance of her ambivalence.

### Direction

The same rigid directive is repeated throughout with minimal variation and no exploration of the patient's goals, values, or readiness. When the patient proposes alternative goals (cutting back one cigarette, adding 10-minute walks), these are dismissed as insufficient rather than explored as potential stepping stones. There is one brief moment of strategic direction ("What small changes do you feel confident trying?"), but this is immediately abandoned when the psychiatrist reverts to the rigid agenda. Direction lacks strategic progression and adaptation based on patient input.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The session demonstrates an authoritarian, expert-driven stance fundamentally antithetical to collaboration. Language patterns reveal power imbalance: "You really need to," "You must," "you have to," "No exceptions." The psychiatrist never asks what the patient thinks would work or what her priorities are. When the patient offers input (proposing to cut back by one cigarette or add 10-minute walks), these contributions are dismissed rather than explored collaboratively. The psychiatrist fails to seek the patient's perspective on barriers, strengths, or readiness. Most tellingly, when the patient explicitly states "Can we work with that?," the psychiatrist does not honor this request but instead resets and reiterates the same demands, signaling that the patient's input does not matter.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy is actively undermined throughout. Language eliminates choice: "You really need to," "you must," "No exceptions." When the patient expresses her actual readiness level ("I'm not ready to make big changes all at once"), the psychiatrist pressures rather than honors this self-assessment. When the patient articulates her own plan ("cutting back by one cigarette feels like something I could handle"), the psychiatrist contradicts her self-knowledge: "You have to be stronger than the stress and just stop." This directly tells the patient her judgment about her own capacity is wrong. Shame and guilt are used as motivators ("Are you ready to stop making excuses?"), which further undermines autonomy by suggesting her choices are morally deficient. The patient's right to choose is treated as an obstacle rather than a resource.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** The session is entirely prescriptive with virtually no evocation of the patient's own motivations, values, or resources. The psychiatrist never asks about reasons for change, what matters to the patient, or what she has tried before that worked. Instead, the psychiatrist tells the patient why she should change: "Just think about the risks if you don't change now," "If you don't quit now, you're risking serious health problems." This is imposition, not evocation. The one evocative question ("What small changes do you feel confident trying?") is immediately negated by dismissal of the patient's answer, sending the message that the psychiatrist does not genuinely want to understand her perspective. Multiple instances of patient change talk ("I'm willing to try maybe going down to fewer smokes," "I want to be healthier") are neither recognized nor amplified.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Multiple instances of patient change talk are either ignored or actively undermined. The patient offers: "I'm willing to try maybe going down to fewer smokes this week," "I want to be healthier, I do," "Small steps that don't mess up my routine—that's what I can commit to," and "I'm open to talking about ways I can make smaller changes." Rather than recognizing and amplifying this language, the psychiatrist minimizes it: "That's a start, but...won't make much difference," "That's barely anything." In MI, these moments should strengthen motivation; instead, they are contradicted, which likely decreases the patient's confidence and commitment. There is no strategic evocation of additional change talk through open questions designed to elicit reasons for change, confidence, or commitment.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** The psychiatrist's approach to sustain talk is primarily confrontational rather than skillfully softening resistance. When the patient expresses sustain talk ("Smoking helps me manage stress," "Two drinks at night isn't that much"), the psychiatrist argues against her position rather than using double-sided reflections or strategic reframing. Fear and shame are used as tactics: "If you don't quit now, you're risking serious health problems," "Why didn't you try cutting down before?" These increase defensiveness rather than soften resistance. However, the psychiatrist does occasionally redirect toward change ("What small changes do you feel confident trying?"), which represents a brief moment of strategic skill. This moment is quickly abandoned when the psychiatrist dismisses the patient's answer, undermining any benefit from the redirection.

### Strengths
- The psychiatrist demonstrates genuine concern for the patient's health and wellbeing.
- Clear focus is maintained on relevant health behaviors (smoking and exercise).
- One brief moment of MI-consistent practice: asking an open, evocative question ("What small changes do you feel confident trying this week?").
- Occasional attempts to redirect away from sustain talk.

---

### Critical Areas for Improvement

**1. Fundamental Shift from Expert to Collaborative Stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
The psychiatrist operates from an authoritarian position that directly contradicts MI principles. Replace directives ("You must," "You need to") with genuine curiosity and open questions. Explore the patient's perspective before introducing clinical concerns.

**2. Develop Genuine Empathy and Reflective Listening**
Practice reflections that capture the patient's emotional experience and the function her behavior serves. Use complex reflections without immediately negating them with "but" and the psychiatrist's agenda. When the patient says "Smoking helps me manage stress," reflect: "So smoking is one of the few ways you've found to take care of yourself when everything feels overwhelming."

**3. Recognize and Amplify Change Talk**
The patient offered multiple instances of change talk that were dismissed or ignored. When the patient says "I'm willing to try maybe going down to fewer smokes," respond with: "So you're thinking about cutting back. What makes you think you could pull that off?" rather than "That won't make much difference."

**4. Support Autonomy and Honor Patient Self-Assessment**
Affirm the patient's right to choose and her expertise about her own situation. When she proposes a goal or timeline, explore it collaboratively rather than dismissing it as insufficient. Ask: "You know your life better than I do. What makes you think that's doable?"

**5. Use Double-Sided Reflections for Ambivalence**
When the patient expresses sustain talk, acknowledge both sides of her ambivalence rather than arguing against her position. Example: "On one hand, smoking helps you manage stress, and on the other hand, you're concerned about your health and being there for your son. Both matter to you."

**6. Establish True Partnership**
Use inclusive language ("we," "together"), seek the patient's input on decisions, and honor her perspective. Replace "Are you ready to commit to this plan?" with "Let's think together about what might work for you. What are your thoughts?"

---

### Impact on Patient Engagement and Change

This session is likely to **decrease intrinsic motivation** and **increase resistance**. The patient entered with openness ("I'm willing to try...") and realistic self-assessment. The psychiatrist's dismissal of her efforts and demands for more have solidified her resistance. By session end, the patient has retreated to "maybe I need to think it through more"—a clear sign of decreased engagement. The psychiatrist has communicated that the patient's judgment is wrong, her autonomy does not matter, and the psychiatrist's agenda supersedes her readiness. This damages the therapeutic relationship and decreases the likelihood of sustained behavior change.

---

### Recommendation

**Formal MI training and clinical supervision are strongly recommended.** This session represents significant departure from MI principles. The current approach is not only ineffective—it is potentially harmful, as it undermines the patient's self-efficacy and autonomy. A collaborative, empathic, evocative approach grounded in MI principles would be far more likely to engage the patient's intrinsic motivation and support meaningful, sustained change.
