
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance—the foundational elements of acceptance in MI. The psychiatrist repeatedly communicates disapproval and judgment throughout the interaction, most starkly in statements like "You really need to take this seriously. Stress is no excuse to keep smoking" and "Can you at least promise to try cutting back on smoking this week? That's the only way you'll start moving in the right direction." These phrases carry implicit judgment that Irina's current coping mechanisms are morally deficient and that her stress is an invalid reason for her behavior.

The psychiatrist also fails to normalize Irina's struggle or affirm her worth as a person managing genuine competing demands. When Irina expresses realistic concerns about her capacity ("I'm already juggling work, school, and my son"), the psychiatrist responds with dismissal rather than validation: "You really need to quit smoking completely...you must exercise at least 30 minutes daily—that's the minimum to see any health benefits." This language ("must," "have to," "no exceptions") conveys conditional regard—acceptance only if Irina complies with the psychiatrist's prescriptions. There is no warmth, no genuine curiosity about her experience, and no affirmation of her efforts to manage an objectively difficult life situation.

Most damaging is the repeated pattern of invalidation when Irina offers small, realistic commitments. When she proposes "cutting back by one cigarette a day," the psychiatrist responds: "That's a start, but honestly, just cutting back by one cigarette a day won't make much difference...cutting back slowly just prolongs the problem." This directly communicates that Irina's genuine effort is inadequate and unworthy of support. The psychiatrist later doubles down: "Cutting back by just one cigarette a day and a couple of 10-minute walks? That's barely anything." This is the opposite of acceptance—it is conditional regard tied to compliance with the psychiatrist's agenda.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance. Judgment, dismissal of Irina's concerns, and conditional regard permeate the session. There is no affirmation of her worth, no normalization of her struggle, and no genuine warmth—only pressure and disapproval.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy in MI requires accurate reflective listening, genuine effort to understand the client's perspective, and complex reflections that capture emotional nuance and deepen insight. This session contains almost none of these elements.

The psychiatrist opens with a directive statement ("we need to talk about your smoking and exercise habits") rather than exploring Irina's experience or concerns. When Irina expresses feeling overwhelmed by competing demands, the psychiatrist responds with "I understand you're feeling overwhelmed, but you really need to quit smoking completely"—a pseudo-reflection immediately negated by "but," which signals the psychiatrist's agenda overrides understanding. This is not empathy; it is performative acknowledgment followed by dismissal.

Throughout the session, the psychiatrist never reflects back Irina's core emotional experience: that smoking and alcohol serve as her primary stress management tools in a genuinely demanding life, that she feels guilty about this, that she fears failure if pushed too hard, and that she is ambivalent about change. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly reframes her concerns as "excuses" ("Stress is no excuse to keep smoking," "You can't keep making excuses"). This is the opposite of empathic understanding—it is judgment disguised as motivation.

The one moment where the psychiatrist offers a genuine reflection ("It sounds like smoking helps you manage stress, and that cutting back aggressively hasn't felt doable before") is immediately undermined by the follow-up question, which frames small steps as insufficient rather than exploring Irina's readiness and self-efficacy.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal empathy. Reflective listening is absent or superficial, the psychiatrist's agenda consistently overrides understanding, and there is no genuine effort to grasp Irina's emotional experience or the function her coping behaviors serve.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI involves maintaining strategic focus on a specific target behavior while remaining client-centered and collaborative. This session has clear direction—but it is imposed rather than collaboratively developed, and it is rigid rather than strategic.

The psychiatrist establishes a narrow, non-negotiable agenda from the opening: quit smoking completely and exercise 30 minutes daily. This direction is stated as fact, not as a collaborative exploration of goals. The psychiatrist repeats this same directive throughout the session with minimal variation: "Are you ready to quit smoking completely?" "You really need to quit smoking altogether," "You must exercise at least 30 minutes daily," "quit smoking completely and exercise daily starting now," "quit smoking completely and exercise at least 30 minutes daily. No exceptions."

The problem is not that the psychiatrist has a clear focus—that is appropriate. The problem is that the direction is imposed without exploring Irina's own goals, values, or readiness. MI direction should be strategic and progressive, meaning it evolves based on client input and moves toward change in a way the client can own. Here, the psychiatrist's direction is static and authoritarian. When Irina proposes alternative goals (cutting back by one cigarette, adding 10-minute walks), the psychiatrist dismisses them as insufficient rather than exploring whether these might be strategic stepping stones toward larger change.

There is one brief moment where the psychiatrist asks an open question aligned with MI direction: "What small changes do you feel confident trying this week that could fit into your life without adding stress?" This represents a shift toward client-centered direction. However, this is immediately abandoned when the psychiatrist reverts to the rigid agenda: "I see you're trying to be reasonable, but honestly, small steps like that won't get you far...Anything less is just procrastination."

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains clear focus on smoking and exercise, but the direction is imposed, rigid, and non-negotiable rather than collaboratively developed or strategically progressive. There is minimal adaptation based on client input.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration in MI requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have valuable knowledge. This session demonstrates an authoritarian, expert-driven stance that is fundamentally anti-collaborative.

The psychiatrist consistently positions herself as the authority who knows what is best and frames Irina as someone who needs to be corrected or convinced. The session is entirely psychiatrist-driven: psychiatrist proposes, Irina responds, psychiatrist dismisses or demands more.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates no genuine collaboration. The stance is entirely expert-driven and authoritarian, with the psychiatrist telling Irina what to do rather than exploring her perspective or co-constructing solutions.

### 5. Autonomy Support
### **Score: 1/5**

## Recommendations

### 1. **Shift from Expert to Collaborative Stance (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**

**Action:** Fundamentally reframe your role from "expert who tells the client what to do" to "collaborative partner who explores the client's perspective." This requires using open questions, genuine curiosity, and shared decision-making rather than directives.

**Example:** Instead of "You really need to quit smoking completely," try: "I'm curious about your smoking. What's your sense of how it's affecting your life right now? What concerns you most about it, if anything?" Or: "You mentioned smoking helps you manage stress. Tell me more about that. What would it be like to manage stress without smoking?"

**Practice:** Before your next session, write out 5-10 open questions you could ask to understand the client's perspective on their behavior, their readiness for change, and their own ideas about what might work. Practice asking these questions without immediately following up with your own agenda. Listen to the client's full response before speaking.

### 2. **Develop Genuine Empathy and Reflective Listening Skills**

**Action:** Practice reflective listening that accurately captures the client's emotional experience and the function their behavior serves. Use complex reflections that deepen understanding rather than simple reflections followed by "but" and your agenda.

**Example:** When Irina says "Smoking helps me manage stress," instead of "I understand you're feeling overwhelmed, but you really need to quit," try: "So smoking is one of the few ways you've found to take care of yourself when everything else feels overwhelming. **Practice:** In your next session, aim for at least 5 reflections that capture the client's emotional experience or the function their behavior serves. Do not follow these reflections with "but" or your own agenda. Simply reflect and pause, allowing the client to elaborate.

### 3. **Recognize and Amplify Change Talk**

**Action:** Train yourself to identify instances when the client expresses desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment to change. When you hear change talk, reflect it back in a way that strengthens it, and ask follow-up questions to evoke more.

**Example:** When Irina says "I'm willing to try maybe going down to fewer smokes this week," instead of "That's a start, but honestly, just cutting back by one cigarette a day won't make much difference," try: "So you're thinking about cutting back to fewer smokes this week. **Practice:** In your next session, identify every instance of change talk and write it down. ### 4. **Support Autonomy and Client Self-Assessment**

**Action:** Explicitly affirm the client's right to choose and their expertise about their own situation. When the client proposes a goal or timeline, explore it collaboratively rather than dismissing it as insufficient.

**Practice:** In your next session, when the client proposes a goal or timeline, pause and ask yourself: "Is this something the client can own? Does this feel doable to them?" ### 5. **Use Double-Sided Reflections to Address Ambivalence**

**Action:** When the client expresses sustain talk (reasons not to change), acknowledge both sides of their ambivalence rather than arguing against their position. This softens resistance and creates space for the client to explore their own reasons for change.

**Example:** When Irina says "Smoking helps me manage stress," instead of "Stress is no excuse to keep smoking," try: "On one hand, smoking is one of the few ways you've found to manage the stress you're under, and on the other hand, you're concerned about how it might affect your health and your ability to be there for your son.
