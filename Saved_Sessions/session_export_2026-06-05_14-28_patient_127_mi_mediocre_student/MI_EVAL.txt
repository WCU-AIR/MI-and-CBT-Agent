### Collaboration

All solutions generated by clinician: "Have you tried going to bed earlier?" "Have you thought about relaxation techniques?" "Maybe you could set small goals like drinking less during the week." When patient expresses skepticism ("Seems a bit over the top"), clinician overrides rather than explores. No genuine invitation for patient to co-construct problem or solution. ### Softening Sustain Talk

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.4/5**

**Assessment:** This session represents a **fundamental departure from motivational interviewing principles** and demonstrates **pre-MI or non-MI practice**. While basic politeness and some reflective listening are present, the core spirit of MI—partnership, evocation, autonomy support, and acceptance—is absent throughout.

**Key Deficits:**
- Expert-driven, directive approach rather than collaborative partnership
- Advice-giving and persuasion rather than evocation of intrinsic motivation
- Overriding of patient autonomy through prescriptive language and subtle pressure
- Minimal cultivation of change talk; failure to amplify patient's own reasons for change
- Confrontational responses to sustain talk that increase rather than soften resistance
- Shallow, factual reflections lacking emotional depth and meaning-making
- Imposed agenda rather than collaborative negotiation of focus

**Impact on Patient Engagement:**
Patient's motivation for change has **decreased throughout the session**. Evidence: Initial low concern ("wouldn't say it's a big problem") → mid-session strengthened resistance ("doesn't seem worth the hassle") → explicit statements of low motivation ("I'm not making any promises," "I'm not sure I really feel motivated to change"). Clinician's directive approach has triggered **reactance**—natural resistance to perceived pressure—rather than evoking intrinsic motivation.

**Likelihood of Sustained Behavior Change:**
**Low.** Patient leaves session with compliance language ("I'll think about what you said") rather than genuine commitment. Without authentic motivation or partnership, patient unlikely to follow through on suggested changes.

**Critical Recommendations for Improvement:**
1. **Shift from advice-giving to evocation** — Stop generating solutions; ask open-ended questions that draw out patient's own ideas and motivations
2. **Establish genuine collaboration** — Invite patient to co-create agenda and solutions; position patient as expert on their own life
3. **Support autonomy** — Respect patient's right to choose; eliminate prescriptive language ("should," "would help")
4. **Cultivate change talk** — Listen for and amplify patient's own reasons for change through strategic questioning
5. **Develop complex, empathic reflections** — Move beyond factual summaries to reflections capturing meaning, emotion, and underlying perspective
6. **Seek formal MI training** — Engage with MI-trained supervisor for feedback on recordings; practice OARS technique until natural
7. **Study MI spirit in depth** — Understand MI as collaborative, evocative approach, not as technique overlay on directive counseling

**Recommendation:** Clinician requires **significant restructuring of approach** and **formal MI training with ongoing supervision** to align practice with evidence-based MI principles and effectively support patient motivation for sustainable change.
