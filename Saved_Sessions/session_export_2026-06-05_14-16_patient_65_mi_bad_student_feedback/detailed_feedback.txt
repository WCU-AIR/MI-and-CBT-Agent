
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a complete absence of unconditional positive regard and contains multiple instances of explicit judgment and disapproval. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels Jordan's behavior as "selfish" ("Your hesitation is selfish and could have serious consequences"), uses shaming language ("stop being so indecisive," "quit downplaying this"), and frames his ambivalence as a character flaw rather than a normal part of decision-making. Most critically, the psychiatrist shows no warmth, normalizing of struggle, or affirmation of Jordan's worth as a person.

The psychiatrist's repeated use of imperatives ("You must," "You have to," "You need to") combined with threats ("If you don't follow it, you'll suffer the consequences") creates an adversarial rather than supportive environment. When Jordan expresses his need to feel autonomous in his decision-making ("I need to feel like I'm making this choice, not being told to"), the psychiatrist responds by doubling down on control and dismissal ("Alright, enough of the hesitation... This is not up for debate"). There is no attempt to validate Jordan's concerns, normalize his ambivalence about vaccination, or affirm his underlying values (protecting his grandparents). The repeated, verbatim recycling of the same confrontational opening statement across multiple turns suggests the psychiatrist is not listening to or adapting to Jordan's actual responses—a fundamental violation of acceptance.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates zero acceptance. Judgment, shaming, and control dominate the interaction. No affirmation, warmth, or unconditional positive regard is present.

---

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective. This session contains virtually no reflective listening and demonstrates a consistent failure to understand or acknowledge Jordan's actual experience. Jordan explicitly states multiple times that his hesitation is not about fear of needles, side effects, or lack of understanding—it's about not feeling personally vulnerable and needing autonomy in decision-making. The psychiatrist ignores these clarifications entirely and continues to ask, "Are you afraid of needles or side effects?" even after Jordan has directly answered this question.

More critically, when Jordan reveals genuine change talk—"I do care about my family," "Maybe I will get the shot this week," "I'm thinking about it more than before"—the psychiatrist does not reflect, validate, or build upon these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist dismisses them as "hesitation" and "overthinking." Jordan's emerging motivation to protect his grandparents is never explored, deepened, or affirmed. A single complex reflection like, "So you're torn between not feeling like you personally need it, but recognizing that getting the shot could really matter for your grandparents' safety—that's a meaningful tension," would demonstrate empathy. None occurs.

The psychiatrist also fails to recognize or respond to Jordan's explicit emotional need: "I need to feel like I'm making this choice, not being told to." This is a window into Jordan's values and autonomy concerns, yet it receives no empathic response—only further pressure.

**Summary:**

No genuine reflective listening or perspective-taking occurs. The psychiatrist repeatedly misses or ignores Jordan's actual concerns and emerging change talk, demonstrating a fundamental failure of empathic attunement.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

While the psychiatrist does maintain a clear target behavior (flu vaccination), the approach to direction is fundamentally misaligned with MI principles. In MI, direction should be maintained through strategic questioning, collaborative exploration, and progressive clarification—not through repeated commands and threats. The psychiatrist does maintain focus on the flu vaccine (not wandering aimlessly), which prevents a score of 1, but the execution is so heavy-handed and coercive that it actively undermines the stated goal.

The psychiatrist's repeated use of the identical opening monologue across multiple turns ("Hello! I'm here to help you get on track with your health...") suggests a scripted, inflexible approach rather than strategic guidance. True direction in MI would involve asking questions like, "What would need to happen for you to feel confident about getting the vaccine?" or "What matters most to you about protecting your grandparents?" Instead, the psychiatrist issues directives: "You need to schedule your flu shot appointment this week—no delays, no excuses."

The psychiatrist does attempt to connect the behavior to consequences (protecting grandparents), which shows some strategic thinking about direction. However, this is undermined by the coercive framing ("Your grandparents' health depends on you not being careless") rather than collaborative exploration of Jordan's own values and motivations.

**Summary:**

Direction is maintained but through coercion and commands rather than strategic MI-consistent guidance. The approach is rigid and fails to adapt to client responses.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners with shared decision-making and joint problem-solving. This session demonstrates an authoritarian, expert-driven stance throughout. The psychiatrist opens with "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do," establishing a hierarchical power dynamic from the start. The language is consistently unilateral: "You must," "You have to," "You need to," "This is non-negotiable," "This is not up for debate."

When Jordan attempts to assert his perspective or express ambivalence, the psychiatrist shuts down collaboration: "Alright, enough of the hesitation" and "Listen, you can't keep dragging your feet on this." These responses explicitly reject Jordan's input and signal that his thoughts are not welcome in the decision-making process. True collaboration would involve statements like, "Let's think through this together" or "What would help you feel more confident about this decision?"

The psychiatrist also fails to seek Jordan's input on barriers, preferences, or solutions. Instead of asking, "What would make this feel more manageable for you?" the psychiatrist prescribes: "Just try to stop cold turkey—it's the best way" (in the smoking example) and "Just go to the clinic and get it—no delays" (for vaccination). There is no co-construction of a plan, no exploration of Jordan's ideas, and no genuine partnership.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains complete control and explicitly rejects collaboration. The stance is authoritarian throughout, with no genuine power-sharing or joint decision-making.

### 5. Autonomy Support

## Recommendations

### 1. **Fundamental Shift from Expert-Directive to Collaborative-Evocative Stance**
- **Action:** Reframe your role from "expert who tells the client what to do" to "collaborative partner who helps the client explore their own motivations and values."
- **Example:** Instead of "You must get the flu shot," say: "I'm wondering what your thoughts are about getting the flu vaccine. What concerns do you have, and what might be reasons to consider it?"
- **Practice:** Before each client interaction, remind yourself: "My job is to ask, not tell. My job is to evoke, not impose. My job is to understand, not judge." Record yourself in practice sessions and count how many times you use directive language ("you must," "you have to," "you need to") vs. evocative language ("I'm curious," "tell me more," "what do you think").

---

### 2. **Develop Genuine Reflective Listening Skills**
- **Action:** Practice complex reflections that capture both content and emotion, and that demonstrate accurate understanding of the client's perspective.
- **Example:** When Jordan says "I'm young and healthy, so I don't think I really need the flu shot," reflect: "So from your perspective, because you're in good health, the flu vaccine feels less relevant to you personally—even though you recognize it could matter for your grandparents."
- **Practice:** In supervision or role-play, after each client statement, pause and generate 2-3 possible reflections before responding. Focus on reflections that go beyond parroting back facts (simple reflections) to reflections that capture meaning, emotion, and ambivalence (complex reflections). Ask yourself: "What is the client really saying beneath the words?"

---

### 3. **Recognize and Amplify Change Talk**
- **Action:** Train yourself to identify all instances of change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment, taking steps) and respond with follow-up questions that deepen and amplify this language.
- **Example:** When Jordan says "Maybe I will get the shot this week," respond with: "That's interesting—tell me more about what's making you consider that. What would getting the shot this week mean to you?"
- **Practice:** Review recordings of your sessions and highlight every instance of client change talk. For each instance, write down what you said in response. Did you amplify it, ignore it, or undermine it? Commit to amplifying at least 80% of change talk instances in your next session.

---

### 4. **Respect and Actively Support Client Autonomy**
- **Action:** Explicitly affirm the client's right to choose, even when you have a strong clinical preference for a particular behavior. Use language that emphasizes client ownership and control.
- **Example:** "This is ultimately your decision. I can share what I think is important for your health, but you're the expert on what's right for you. What are your thoughts?"
- **Practice:** In every session, include at least one explicit autonomy-supportive statement. Practice phrases like: "You get to decide," "What feels right to you?" "I respect whatever you choose," "You know yourself best." Notice how clients respond when autonomy is genuinely supported vs. when it's undermined.

---

### 5. **Eliminate Shame, Guilt, and Confrontation**
- **Action:** Remove judgmental language, accusations, and shame-based appeals from your clinical repertoire. Replace them with curiosity, acceptance, and collaborative exploration.
- **Example:** Instead of "Your hesitation is selfish and could have serious consequences," say: "I'm hearing that you care about your grandparents and also that you're not quite ready to commit to the vaccine. Help me understand what's making this decision difficult for you."
- **Practice:** Before sessions, identify potential shame-based language you might use and generate alternative, non-judgmental responses. In sessions, if you notice yourself becoming frustrated or judgmental, pause and take a breath. Ask yourself: "What is the client's perspective here? What might be reasonable about their hesitation?" This pause creates space for empathy.

---

### 6. **Use Open-Ended Questions to Evoke Rather Than Leading Questions to Pressure**
- **Action:** Replace rhetorical, leading questions ("Why haven't you gotten it yet?" "Are you ready now?") with genuine open-ended questions designed to elicit the client's own thinking.
- **Example:** Instead of "Why haven't you gotten the flu shot yet?" ask: "What's your sense of what would need to happen for you to feel ready to get the vaccine?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, commit to asking at least 5 open-ended questions that begin with "What," "How," or "Tell me about..." Avoid yes/no questions and questions that are really statements in disguise ("Don't you think you should get the vaccine?").

---

### 7. **Connect to Client Values Rather Than Imposing Your Own**
- **Action:** Explore what matters most to the client and help them see how their behavior aligns with or conflicts with their own values. Don't impose your values as the "right" ones.
- **Example:** Jordan values protecting his grandparents. Rather than saying "You must get the vaccine," explore: "You mentioned your grandparents are important to you and that they might struggle with the flu. How do you see the vaccine fitting into your goal of keeping them safe?"
- **Practice:** In each session, identify at least one client value and ask follow-up questions that help the client explore how their current behavior aligns with that value. This creates intrinsic motivation rather than external pressure.

---

### 8. **Adapt Your Approach Based on Client Responses**
- **Action:** Listen carefully to what the client is saying and adjust your approach accordingly. If a client says "I need to feel like I'm making this choice," that is critical information—respond to it, don't ignore it.
- **Example:** When Jordan says "I need to feel like I'm making this choice, not being told to," the appropriate response is: "I hear you. This needs to feel like your decision, not something I'm pushing you into. Let's talk about what would help you feel confident making this choice for yourself."
- **Practice:** After each client statement, ask yourself: "What is the client telling me about what they need? Am I responding to that, or am I just following my script?" Commit to flexibility and responsiveness rather than rigid adherence to a predetermined agenda.

---

### 9. **Develop Skill in Handling Ambivalence Without Confrontation**
- **Action:** Learn to use double-sided reflections and strategic responses that acknowledge both sides of the client's ambivalence without pushing them toward your preferred outcome.
- **Example:** "So you see yourself as healthy and not at high personal risk, and at the same time, you care deeply about your grandparents and recognize they could be seriously affected by the flu. That's a real tension you're sitting with."
- **Practice:** In role-play or supervision, practice responding to sustain talk with reflections rather than arguments. The goal is not to convince the client, but to help them explore their own ambivalence and move toward their own resolution.

---

### 10. **Seek Supervision and Training in Motivational Interviewing**
- **Action:** Recognize that this session represents a fundamental departure from evidence-based MI practice. Seek formal MI training, supervision, and feedback to develop competence.
- **Example:** Enroll in a certified MI training program, participate in ongoing supervision with MI-trained supervisors, and use session recordings to identify areas for improvement.
- **Practice:** Commit to at least 20 hours of formal MI training and ongoing supervision. Record your sessions and review them with a supervisor who can provide specific, behavioral feedback on your use of MI techniques.
