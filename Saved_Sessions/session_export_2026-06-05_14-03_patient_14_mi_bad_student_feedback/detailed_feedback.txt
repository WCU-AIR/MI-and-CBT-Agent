
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Fundamental Shift from Expert to Collaborative Stance**
- **Action:** Reframe your role from "expert who tells the patient what to do" to "partner who helps the patient explore her own motivations and solutions."
- **Example:** Instead of opening with "We're going to talk about your smoking habits, and I'll be giving you some advice on how to quit," try: "I'd like to understand more about your smoking and what it means to you. What brings you in today? What are your thoughts about your smoking?"
- **Practice:** Before each session, remind yourself: "My job is to draw out the patient's own motivation, not to impose mine." Record yourself in practice sessions and listen for how often you use directive language ("you need to," "you should," "you must") versus collaborative language ("What do you think?" "How could we work together?" "What feels possible to you?").

### 2. **Develop Genuine Reflective Listening Skills**
- **Action:** Use reflective listening to demonstrate that you understand the patient's perspective from her internal frame of reference. Move beyond simple reflections to complex reflections that capture emotional nuance and deeper meaning.
- **Example:** When the patient says "It's not that easy for me. Smoking is kind of the only thing that feels steady when everything else is falling apart," reflect: "So right now, smoking feels like your anchor—the one thing that's stable when everything else feels chaotic. And the idea of giving that up feels like losing that stability, which is scary." This demonstrates deep understanding and validates her experience.
- **Practice:** In supervision or role-play, practice reflecting back what you hear without immediately offering advice or correction. Aim for at least 3-4 reflections before asking a new question. Record yourself and count the ratio of reflections to questions and directives.

### 3. **Evoke Rather Than Impose Motivation for Change**
- **Action:** Ask open-ended questions designed to draw out the patient's own reasons for change, concerns about smoking, values, and ideas about what might work. Build on the patient's own suggestions rather than dismissing them.
- **Example:** When the patient says "if I could take small steps instead of having to quit all at once, maybe that would be better," respond with: "That's a really good insight. Tell me more about what small steps might look like for you. What would feel manageable?" Then explore her ideas rather than pivoting to your own recommendations.
- **Practice:** Develop a set of open-ended evocation questions and practice using them: "What concerns you most about your smoking?" "What would be the benefits of smoking less?" "What have you tried in the past when you've made changes?" "What would give you confidence that you could do this?" "What's one small step you might be willing to try?" Use these questions to draw out change talk rather than imposing directives.

### 4. **Support Autonomy and Client Ownership**
- **Action:** Consistently communicate that the patient has the right to choose and that change is ultimately her decision. Emphasize her ownership of the process.
- **Example:** Instead of "You must commit fully; otherwise, you'll keep smoking and risk your health," try: "This is ultimately your decision. I can share what I know about quitting, but you're the expert on what will work for you. What do you think might be possible?"
- **Practice:** In every session, use language that emphasizes choice: "You get to decide," "It's up to you," "What feels right to you?" Avoid language that implies the patient has no choice: "You need to," "You have to," "You must." Track how often you use autonomy-supporting language versus controlling language.

### 5. **Soften Sustain Talk Through Empathic Exploration Rather Than Confrontation**
- **Action:** When the patient expresses reasons for not changing, respond with empathy and curiosity rather than confrontation. Use double-sided reflections and strategic reframing to explore the sustain talk without amplifying it.
- **Example:** When the patient says "I'm not sure if I could stick with them. It feels like if I don't do everything perfectly, I'd just be setting myself up to fail," respond with: "So you're worried that if you try and don't do it perfectly, you'll feel worse about yourself. That's a real concern. Tell me more about what 'perfect' would look like for you. Is there a way to try that doesn't have to be all-or-nothing?"
- **Practice:** In role-play, practice responding to sustain talk with curiosity and empathy rather than confrontation. Use reflections that acknowledge the patient's concerns while gently exploring alternatives. Avoid saying "but" or "however" after reflections, as this negates the reflection.

### 6. **Explore Ambivalence and Help the Patient Develop Her Own Change Plan**
- **Action:** Recognize that the patient is in contemplation—she sees both sides of the issue. Help her explore both sides and develop her own change plan based on what feels manageable to her.
- **Example:** "It sounds like part of you recognizes that smoking isn't helping you long-term, and part of you feels like it's the only thing that helps you get through stressful days right now. Both of those things are true. What would need to happen for you to feel more ready to make a change? What would feel like a manageable first step?"
- **Practice:** Use the "decisional balance" technique to explore both sides of ambivalence. Ask: "What are the good things about smoking for you?" and "What are the concerns you have about smoking?" Then ask: "What would be the benefits of smoking less or quitting?" and "What would be the challenges?" This helps the patient develop her own reasons for change.

### 7. **Validate the Patient's Concerns and Normalize Struggle**
- **Action:** Communicate genuine acceptance and warmth. Validate that quitting is hard, that stress management is real, and that the patient's concerns are understandable.
- **Example:** "Quitting smoking is one of the hardest things people do. You're dealing with a lot right now—the baby, the house, all the stress. It makes sense that you're using smoking to cope. That doesn't make you weak or bad; it makes you human. And it also means that if you do decide to make a change, we need to think about what other ways you can manage stress."
- **Practice:** Before each session, remind yourself of the patient's humanity and struggle. Practice statements that communicate acceptance: "That makes sense," "I understand why you feel that way," "That's a real concern," "You're dealing with a lot." These statements communicate that you see the patient as a whole person, not just as someone with a problem to fix.

### 8. **Collaborate on Goal-Setting and Treatment Planning**
- **Action:** Rather than imposing a goal (quit completely), explore what goal feels right to the patient. Build a treatment plan together based on her ideas and readiness.
- **Example:** "So you mentioned that taking small steps instead of quitting all at once might feel more manageable. I'm curious—what would a small step look like for you? Would it be reducing the number of cigarettes? Identifying one stressful situation where you wouldn't smoke? Something else? Let's figure out what might work for you."
- **Practice:** In supervision, practice collaborative goal-setting. Instead of telling the patient what the goal should be, ask: "What would you like to work on?" "What feels possible to you right now?" "What would be a meaningful change for you?" Then build the treatment plan around the patient's goals and ideas.

### 9. **Recognize and Reinforce Change Talk**
- **Action:** When the patient expresses change talk—desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment to change—recognize it and reinforce it. Ask follow-up questions that deepen and strengthen change talk.
- **Example:** When the patient says "I guess I should try cutting down," respond with: "Tell me more about that. What makes you think cutting down might be a good idea?" or "What would be the benefits of smoking less?" This reinforces the change talk and helps the patient develop her own reasons for change.
- **Practice:** In role-play, practice identifying change talk and responding with reinforcement. Use follow-up questions that deepen change talk: "Tell me more," "What else?" "How would that help?" "What would that mean for you?" Track how often you reinforce change talk versus how often you miss opportunities to do so.

### 10. **Receive Training and Supervision in Motivational Interviewing**
- **Action:** Seek formal MI training and ongoing supervision. This session demonstrates significant gaps in MI competence that require structured learning and practice.
- **Example:** Enroll in a certified MI training program (e.g., through the Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers). Participate in regular supervision where you record sessions and receive feedback on your MI skills.
- **Practice:** After training, practice MI skills in role-play with feedback. Record your sessions and review them with a supervisor. Focus on developing the core MI skills: open-ended questions, affirmations, reflective listening, and summaries (OARS). Track your progress over time.
