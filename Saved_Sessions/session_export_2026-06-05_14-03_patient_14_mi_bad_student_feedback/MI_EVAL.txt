### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no unconditional positive regard. Opening statement establishes judgmental, prescriptive stance ("We're going to give you advice on how to quit"). Patient's disclosure that smoking helps with stress is met with immediate correction rather than validation. Repeated use of disapproving language ("You really have to take this seriously," "You must commit fully") conveys moral judgment. Single moment of brief acceptance is immediately undermined by return to directive, pressuring language. No genuine affirmation of patient's worth or struggle evident throughout session.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal empathic effort demonstrated. Patient clearly articulates her experience—smoking as primary coping mechanism, quitting feels overwhelming and impossible, fear of failure, multiple life stressors (baby, house, family)—yet psychiatrist dismisses rather than reflects understanding. Single reflective statement ("It sounds like smoking is a way you cope with stress") is superficial and immediately followed by directive question. Patient's invitations for empathic exploration ("It just feels like too much," "It's not that easy for me") are met with correction, not curiosity. No open-ended questions designed to deepen understanding of patient's internal experience. Complex reflections capturing emotional nuance entirely absent.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains clear focus on smoking cessation as target behavior; however, direction is maintained through rigid prescription rather than strategic, collaborative exploration. Goals stated in absolute terms ("cut down to zero cigarettes," "quit completely") without inviting patient to clarify her own goals or explore achievable direction. When patient indicates she is "not ready to quit completely," psychiatrist does not explore ambivalence or help articulate patient's own change goals. Direction is imposed rather than co-constructed. More MI-consistent approach would involve exploring patient's reasons for change, values regarding health, and what small steps might feel manageable.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist operates from authoritarian, expert-driven stance throughout. Opening statement immediately establishes hierarchical, one-directional relationship ("I'll be giving you some advice on how to quit"). Consistent use of controlling language ("you really should," "you need to," "you have to," "you must") positions clinician as sole expert with answers. Patient's input solicited only for information-gathering or to be corrected. When patient offers collaborative suggestion ("if I could take small steps instead of having to quit all at once, maybe that would be better"), psychiatrist does not build on this. No joint problem-solving. Patient's ideas not sought, valued, or incorporated into treatment plan. Power held entirely by clinician.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively suppresses autonomy through directive pressure and control. Repeated statements communicate patient has no choice: "you really should," "You need to cut down to zero," "You must commit fully," "Are you ready to quit completely now?" When patient expresses hesitation or states "I'm not ready to quit completely," psychiatrist treats this as resistance to overcome rather than respecting it as valid information about patient's autonomy and readiness. No acknowledgment of patient's right to make own decisions. No language emphasizing "this is your choice" or "you're the expert on what will work for you." Approach likely triggers reactance; patient's responses show increasing defensiveness and withdrawal ("I'm not even sure I'd stick with them," "I don't want to bother them," "I'm not ready to quit completely right now").

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no genuine evocation. Relies entirely on telling and prescribing rather than drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources. Multiple missed opportunities: Patient's statement "sometimes it helps me calm down" met with correction, not exploration; patient's own idea "if I could take small steps...maybe that would be better" not built upon; patient's fear "It just feels too big and scary" not explored for underlying concerns. No open-ended questions designed to elicit patient's reasons for change, confidence, ability, or commitment. Questions asked are closed and compliance-focused ("Do you smoke every day?" "Have you tried any quitting methods?" "Are you ready to quit completely now?"). Patient's minimal change talk not reinforced or explored. Psychiatrist creates no space for patient's own change talk to emerge.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role and maintains power throughout interaction. Opening statement positions clinician as expert with answers, patient as recipient of advice. Unilateral decisions made throughout ("you need to cut down to zero," "you should try nicotine patches," "you must commit fully"). When patient offers own wisdom ("if I could take small steps...maybe that would be better"), this is not honored as valuable input; instead, psychiatrist prescribes different approach and demands commitment. Patient's concerns about patches dismissed rather than explored as valid information about what might work for her. No collaborative language present ("You know your life better than I do," "You're the expert on what will help," "Let's figure this out together"). Session structure remains unchanged regardless of patient's contributions. Patient's wisdom and expertise about her own life not valued or incorporated.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist does not cultivate change talk; instead, discourages it through pressure and creates environment where patient becomes increasingly defensive and withdrawn. Patient offers fragile change talk ("I guess I should try cutting down," "if I could take small steps...maybe that would be better"), yet psychiatrist immediately pivots to demands for complete cessation and prescriptions for patches, sending message that patient's own ideas and motivations are insufficient. No strategic questioning designed to elicit patient's reasons for change, confidence, ability, or commitment. Questions asked invite sustain talk and defensiveness, not change talk ("Why didn't you try quitting completely before?" "Why haven't you started using them yet?"). By session end, patient's change talk has diminished and sustain talk increased ("I'm not ready to quit completely right now. It just feels too big and scary...I don't want to set myself up for failure"). Psychiatrist's approach has moved patient further from change, not closer to it.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist confronts sustain talk directly rather than softening it through empathic exploration. Patient expresses significant sustain talk throughout ("It's not that easy for me," "Smoking is the only thing that feels steady when everything else is falling apart," "I'm not sure if I could stick with them," "I'm not ready to quit completely," "It just feels too big and scary," "I don't want to set myself up for failure"). Rather than softening through empathic reflection and strategic reframing, psychiatrist confronts: "you really need to stop using smoking as a crutch," "you must commit fully; otherwise, you'll keep smoking and risk your health," "Are you ready to quit completely now?" Confrontational responses harden rather than soften sustain talk; patient becomes more entrenched in reasons for not changing. Skillful MI response would explore sustain talk with curiosity ("Tell me more about what feels most overwhelming") rather than confronting it. Psychiatrist's approach amplifies patient's resistance and defensiveness rather than reducing it.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.1/5**

### Strengths:
- Maintains consistent focus on smoking cessation as treatment goal
- Attempts to gather baseline information about smoking frequency and quantity
- Demonstrates awareness that reflection is part of good practice (one moment of reflection present, though not sustained)

### Critical Deficiencies:

This session represents a **fundamental departure from motivational interviewing principles** across nearly every dimension. The psychiatrist operates from an expert, authoritarian stance that is antithetical to MI spirit. Rather than evoking the patient's own motivation for change, the clinician imposes directives and applies pressure. Rather than supporting autonomy, the clinician attempts to control behavior. Rather than exploring the patient's perspective, the clinician corrects and dismisses it.

**Most Significant Areas for Improvement:**

1. **Absence of genuine collaboration and partnership** — Patient treated as someone who needs to be told what to do, not as partner in problem-solving. This approach triggers reactance and defensiveness, moving patient further from change rather than closer to it.

2. **Lack of evocation** — Patient's own ideas, motivations, and resources never explored. When patient offers insight ("if I could take small steps...maybe that would be better"), this is not built upon; instead, psychiatrist prescribes different approach and demands commitment.

3. **Confrontational approach to sustain talk** — Amplifies rather than softens patient's resistance. By session end, patient more entrenched in reasons for not changing: "I'm not ready to quit completely right now. It just feels too big and scary...I don't want to set myself up for failure."

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change:

This session is likely to **decrease the patient's intrinsic motivation for change** and increase defensiveness. Patient enters with some ambivalence—recognizes smoking is not ideal but feels overwhelmed by prospect of quitting. Rather than exploring this ambivalence and helping patient move toward change, psychiatrist applies pressure and demands, triggering reactance.

By session end, patient has become more defensive and withdrawn. She explicitly states she is "not ready to quit completely" and fears "setting myself up for failure." Psychiatrist's approach has not moved patient closer to change; it has moved her further away. Patient is unlikely to follow through on recommendations or return for follow-up care with this clinician.

### Recommendation:

**This psychiatrist requires comprehensive motivational interviewing training and ongoing clinical supervision before continuing to provide smoking cessation counseling.** The current approach is likely to be ineffective and potentially harmful to patient outcomes. Formal MI training (e.g., through Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers) and regular supervision with recorded session review are essential.
