## Summary of Clinical Findings

### Strengths
- Clear, warm opening establishes non-threatening initial rapport
- Consistent focus on target behavior (alcohol use and peer pressure) without significant topic drift
- Concrete action plan emerges by session end (talk to coach, have responses ready, stay busy)
- Client remains engaged throughout without significant therapeutic rupture
- Appropriate suggestion to involve coach as trusted figure in Lukas's life

### Critical Areas for Improvement

This session represents a **fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles** and instead reflects a traditional medical model characterized by expert advice-giving and prescription. The psychiatrist violates core MI tenets across nearly every domain:

- **Absence of genuine collaboration and partnership**: Psychiatrist unilaterally decides what matters and what Lukas should do; Lukas remains passive recipient of advice
- **Compromised empathy and acceptance**: Failure to affirm Lukas's strengths, normalize his developmentally appropriate struggle, or demonstrate genuine understanding of his internal conflict
- **Prescriptive language dominates**: Repeated "should" and "maybe you should" conveys judgment and undermines autonomy support
- **Minimal evocation**: Clinician tells rather than asks; imposes solutions rather than helping Lukas discover his own motivations and resources
- **Weak change talk cultivation**: While some emerges naturally, clinician fails to strategically amplify or strengthen Lukas's own language about change
- **Sustain talk not skillfully addressed**: Clinician acknowledges barriers but doesn't use MI techniques to help Lukas work through ambivalence
- **No exploration of values or goals**: Clinician never deeply explores what matters to Lukas or helps him connect current choices to his own values

### Impact on Client Engagement and Likelihood of Change

While Lukas remains superficially engaged and agrees to recommendations, this session is unlikely to generate genuine, intrinsic motivation for change. Lukas's change talk is not deepened; instead, clinician imposes external reasons for change (grades, sports, scholarship). This external motivation is fragile and unlikely to sustain behavior change when peer pressure intensifies. The directive stance may inadvertently reinforce ambivalence or create reactance—Lukas may feel controlled rather than supported, potentially leading to resistance or shame about seeking help (which he already expressed concern about). By session end, Lukas has a plan but not genuine ownership of it; he's following clinician's advice rather than pursuing his own goals. This is unlikely to produce lasting change.

## Recommendations for Improvement

**HIGHEST PRIORITY: Shift from Expert to Collaborative Stance**
- Fundamentally reframe role from expert with answers to collaborative partner
- Practice asking open questions that genuinely invite Lukas's perspective, ideas, and solutions
- Replace prescriptive language with autonomy-supportive language emphasizing choice and agency
- Eliminate "should" language; replace with "What do you think?" "Tell me more about that" "How do you see this?"

**Develop Genuine Empathy Through Reflective Listening**
- When Lukas expresses ambivalence, emotion, or concern, pause and reflect back what you hear—not just facts, but feeling and internal conflict
- Use complex reflections capturing multiple dimensions of experience
- Practice reflecting emotional content and ambivalence before offering any advice

**Evoke Rather Than Impose Motivations for Change**
- Stop telling Lukas why he should change; ask questions helping him discover his own reasons
- Explore his values, goals, and what matters to him; help him connect these to current choices
- Use evocative questions: "What matters most to you?" "Tell me about your goals" "What kind of friend do you want to be?" "What would it look like if you felt confident in this situation?"

**Affirm Strengths and Normalize Experience**
- Actively identify and affirm Lukas's strengths, values, and positive qualities
- Normalize his struggle as developmentally appropriate rather than pathologizing it
- Identify at least 2-3 genuine strengths at beginning of each session; weave into reflections throughout

**Support Autonomy Rather Than Prescribe**
- Eliminate prescriptive language; replace with autonomy-supportive language
- Offer options rather than prescriptions: "You could... or you could... What feels right to you?"
- Communicate respect for Lukas's agency and decision-making capacity

**Cultivate Change Talk Strategically**
- When Lukas expresses desire, ability, reasons, or commitment to change, pause and deepen it
- Ask follow-up questions helping him elaborate and strengthen his own change talk
- Use scaling questions strategically: "On a scale of 1-10, how important is it to you to stay focused on your goals? What would move it from a 7 to an 8?"

**Use Double-Sided Reflections to Address Ambivalence**
- When Lukas expresses sustain talk, use double-sided reflections validating both sides of ambivalence
- Formula: "On one hand... and on the other hand..."
- This validates ambivalence and often naturally leads to exploring change side more deeply

**Ask Permission Before Offering Advice**
- If offering advice or suggestions, ask permission first: "I have a thought about this—would it be helpful to hear it?"
- This respects autonomy and frames advice as collaborative rather than prescriptive

**Explore Values and Goals Deeply**
- Don't assume you know what matters to Lukas; ask open questions about his values and future
- Help him articulate these clearly; connect current choices to these values
- Questions: "What does being a good athlete mean to you?" "What are you proud of?" "What kind of person do you want to be?" "If you imagine yourself in 5 years, what does that look like?"

**Reduce Closed Questions; Increase Open Questions**
- Audit question patterns; closed questions gather facts but don't evoke motivation
- Replace with open questions inviting exploration
- Aim for ratio of at least 3 open questions for every 1 closed question

**Build True Partnership Through Shared Decision-Making**
- Actively involve Lukas in deciding what to focus on, how to approach problems, and what next steps to take
- Make decisions together, not for him
- Explicitly invite Lukas's input on agenda, approach, and plan

**Receive Supervision on MI Fidelity**
- Seek regular supervision from MI-trained clinician
- Have sessions recorded and reviewed for fidelity to MI principles
- Commit to monthly supervision focused on MI fidelity for at least 6 months—gold standard for developing MI competence

## Conclusion

This session does **not embody the spirit of Motivational Interviewing**. MI is fundamentally about partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation—none of which are demonstrated here. The psychiatrist's directive, prescriptive stance may actually undermine Lukas's intrinsic motivation and create reactance or shame. To develop genuine MI competence, this clinician must fundamentally shift from expert role to collaborative partner, from telling to asking, from imposing to evoking, and from prescribing to supporting autonomy. This requires significant retraining and ongoing supervision focused on MI fidelity. Without this shift, the psychiatrist will continue to deliver advice-giving rather than motivational interviewing, which limits effectiveness with ambivalent clients like Lukas who need support in discovering their own reasons for change.
