# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal acceptance demonstrated. While opening contains surface warmth ("I'm glad you could come in today"), the clinician quickly adopts a problem-focused, deficit-oriented stance. No explicit affirmations of client worth or normalization of struggle present. Language is consistently prescriptive ("Maybe you should try...") rather than affirming. Missed opportunity to validate client's stated value (family stability) and acknowledge the legitimacy of reentry stress. Absence of unconditional positive regard undermines genuine acceptance.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflective listening present but shallow and emotionally disconnected. Reflections are factual summaries ("So you've been feeling stressed and having trouble sleeping") rather than emotionally accurate understanding. Critical missed opportunities: when client states "trying to keep things together and not mess up again," clinician reduces to "mostly home stress," stripping emotional weight of fear and pressure. When client expresses overwhelm ("feels like a lot"), clinician offers reassurance rather than validating the experience. When client discloses loneliness ("no one really gets it"), clinician offers conditional suggestion rather than acknowledging pain. Empathy remains transactional and surface-level.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Session maintains reasonably clear focus on presenting concerns (stress, sleep, family stability) with logical progression from problem identification to planning. However, direction is clinician-driven rather than collaboratively established. Psychiatrist identifies targets (sleep improvement, stress reduction, support-seeking) and guides client toward predetermined solutions without exploring what direction client believes would be most helpful. Most powerful motivational anchor (family stability) remains underdeveloped. Direction present but lacks strategic depth and client-centeredness.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Collaboration is minimal throughout. Clinician consistently positions self as expert who diagnoses problems and prescribes solutions, while client is positioned as passive recipient of advice. Evidence: "Maybe you should try some relaxation...Do you think you could do that?" (advice framed as question); "Maybe you should start with something small, like trying a relaxation exercise" (prescribes specific intervention without exploration); "I think starting with relaxation and then maybe talking to someone could be good steps" (constructs plan unilaterally). True collaboration would involve asking what client thinks might help before offering suggestions, genuinely considering client input, and co-constructing plan. Power held by clinician; client input minimal and reactive.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Autonomy nominally acknowledged but undermined by consistent prescriptive language and subtle pressure. Statement "It's up to you how you want to take these steps" comes after session of directive guidance, undermining authenticity. Prescriptive language throughout: "Maybe you should try..." (should implies obligation); "It would probably help if you made some changes..." (frames change as necessary); "I think it would probably help..." (clinician judgment presented as fact). When client expresses doubt ("Not sure if it'd really make a difference"), clinician responds with persuasion rather than supporting autonomy to decide. Genuine autonomy support absent; compliance gently encouraged instead.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Evocation minimal and poorly executed. One genuine moment occurs when clinician asks "What would be good about getting better sleep and less stress?" Client responds with powerful change talk: desire ("it'd be good"), ability ("more energy"), reasons ("keep a job or be there for my family"), and values ("good life with them"). However, clinician fails to amplify or deepen this critical statement. Response is brief acknowledgment ("That makes sense...is important") that does not explore or strengthen change talk. Missed opportunity to ask "Tell me more about what that looks like" or "How important is that to you?" Session then pivots to confidence scaling and advice-giving. Clinician relies primarily on telling rather than asking; most questions are closed or leading. True evocation would involve sustained open questions exploring client's own motivations and resources.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Partnership—understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside within client—is largely absent. Clinician assumes expert role throughout: diagnoses problems, prescribes solutions (relaxation, sleep aids, support groups, counseling), provides rationale, and constructs plan unilaterally. Client contributions minimal and reactive; client responds to clinician questions but does not substantially shape session direction or content. Client's ideas, experiences, and wisdom about own life not sought or incorporated. True partnership would involve asking what client already knows about stress and sleep, exploring what he's tried before, inviting his ideas about solutions before offering suggestions, and genuinely considering his input. Session communicates: "I am expert; you are patient. I will tell you what to do." This is not partnership.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Change talk elicited once but inadequately cultivated or reinforced. When asked "What would be good about getting better sleep and less stress?" client provides strong change talk across multiple dimensions (desire, ability, reasons, values). This represents excellent intrinsic motivation rooted in deepest values (family). However, clinician's response is minimal ("That makes sense, wanting more energy to be there for your family is important"), acknowledging but not amplifying or deepening. After this moment, clinician does not strategically evoke additional change talk. Session pivots to confidence scaling and advice-giving, missing opportunities to strengthen intrinsic motivation. Clinician never asks follow-up questions that would deepen change talk: "Tell me more about what being there for your family means to you?" or "How would your life be different if you had more energy?" One powerful moment of change talk abandoned rather than cultivated.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Sustain talk not reinforced, which is appropriate. However, responses often avoidant (offering solutions) rather than skillfully exploring or reframing ambivalence. When client expresses doubt ("Not sure if it'd really make a difference"), clinician responds with open question ("Maybe there are some reasons why you'd want to try"), which successfully evokes change talk—adequate response. When client expresses overwhelm ("feels like a lot"), clinician responds with reassurance and advice rather than exploring underlying concern—weak response that bypasses ambivalence. When client expresses reluctance about talking ("no one really gets it"), clinician offers mild reframe rather than first validating reluctance—adequate but not sophisticated. Clinician demonstrates adequate but not sophisticated management of sustain talk. Could more effectively use double-sided reflections or strategic questioning to soften sustain talk while maintaining collaboration.

## Summary of Clinical Findings

### Strengths
- Clear, welcoming opening establishes basic rapport
- Identification of presenting concerns (stress, sleep, family values)
- One moment of genuine evocation successfully elicits powerful change talk rooted in client values
- Logical session structure with coherent flow from problem identification to planning
- Non-confrontational, non-judgmental stance maintained throughout

### Critical Areas for Improvement
- **Expert-driven rather than collaborative:** Clinician prescribes solutions and constructs plan unilaterally, positioning self as authority rather than partner
- **Minimal empathy and emotional attunement:** Reflections are factual summaries; clinician misses opportunities to validate client's pressure, fear, and isolation
- **Inadequate cultivation of change talk:** One powerful moment of change talk acknowledged but not deepened or reinforced; clinician does not strategically evoke additional change talk
- **Lack of acceptance and affirmation:** No explicit affirmation of client worth, normalization of struggle, or validation of ambivalence as understandable
- **Insufficient autonomy support:** Prescriptive language and subtle pressure undermine genuine autonomy support despite nominal acknowledgment of choice
- **Limited exploration of ambivalence:** When client expresses doubt or hesitation, clinician responds with reassurance or advice rather than curious exploration
- **Closed and leading questions:** Most questions are closed or leading, limiting client opportunity to explore own thinking

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change Motivation
Client's final statement ("Yeah, I guess I will. Thanks.") reflects compliance and obligation rather than genuine commitment or ownership. Session unlikely to evoke sustained, intrinsically motivated change. For client with history of incarceration navigating reentry, this represents missed opportunity to strengthen internal motivation and sense of agency—both critical for sustained behavior change and reduced recidivism risk.

### MI Competency Assessment
Clinician demonstrates emerging MI skills (basic reflective listening, one moment of genuine evocation, non-confrontational stance) but lacks strategic skillfulness required for competent MI practice. Fundamental shift needed from telling to asking, from prescribing to exploring, from expert to partner.

---

## Recommendations for Skill Development

1. **Shift from expert-driven to collaborative partnership:** Ask client what he thinks might help before offering suggestions; genuinely consider his ideas and allow them to shape plan
2. **Deepen and amplify change talk:** When client articulates change talk, pause and explore further rather than moving quickly to next topic
3. **Develop genuine empathy through emotional attunement:** Move beyond factual reflections to emotionally accurate reflections capturing feeling and meaning beneath content
4. **Explicitly affirm client worth and normalize struggle:** Regularly communicate unconditional positive regard, especially important for clients with incarceration history
5. **Support genuine autonomy:** Replace "should" language with autonomy-supportive language; explicitly affirm client's right to choose
6. **Explore ambivalence with curiosity:** Respond to doubt and hesitation with curious exploration rather than reassurance or persuasion
7. **Connect client values to behavioral change:** Explicitly explore discrepancy between stated values (family, stability) and current behaviors (stress, poor sleep, isolation)
8. **Use open questions strategically:** Replace closed and leading questions with genuine open questions; aim for 70% open questions
9. **Assess confidence strategically:** Use confidence scaling after exploring ambivalence and evoking change talk; follow with exploration of what would increase confidence
10. **Pursue formal MI training and supervision:** Seek MINT-certified training and engage in regular supervision focused on MI-specific competencies
