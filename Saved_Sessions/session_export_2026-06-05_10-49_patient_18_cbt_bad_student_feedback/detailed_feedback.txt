
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **Conduct Systematic Psychiatric Assessment in Future Sessions**
- **Action:** Implement a structured assessment protocol for acute psychiatric presentations that includes: current manic/depressive symptoms, sleep patterns, substance use, medication compliance, safety assessment, and functional impairment. Use a standardized tool (e.g., Young Mania Rating Scale) to quantify symptom severity.
- **Example:** "Nancy, I want to make sure I understand what's happening. Let me ask you some specific questions. In the past week, how many hours of sleep have you gotten each night? When you try to sleep, what happens? How is this different from your usual sleep? Have you noticed any changes in your thinking or energy compared to how you usually feel?"
- **Practice:** Review the DSM-5 criteria for manic episodes and practice identifying each criterion in case presentations. Use standardized rating scales in every session with bipolar patients. Develop a checklist of essential assessment areas and review it before each session.

### 2. **Establish Clear Agenda and Session Structure**
- **Action:** Begin every session by collaboratively setting an agenda. Explicitly state: "Here's what I'm thinking we should focus on today based on what you told me and what I'm observing. What's most important to you? What would be most helpful to discuss?" Allocate time to each topic and maintain focus.
- **Example:** "Nancy, I'm concerned about a few things: your sleep, your mood, how you're managing at home, and whether your current medications are working. I also want to understand what brought you to the ER today. We have 50 minutes. I'd like to spend 15 minutes on each of these areas. Does that sound okay to you? Is there anything else you think we should discuss?"
- **Practice:** Write out a sample agenda for Nancy's next session before it occurs. Practice stating the agenda clearly and checking for patient agreement. Review session recordings and identify moments where you drifted off agenda and how you could have redirected.

### 3. **Eliminate Excessive Self-Disclosure and Maintain Professional Boundaries**
- **Action:** Significantly reduce personal anecdotes. When you notice yourself about to share a personal story, pause and ask: "Is this about me or about the patient? Does this help the patient understand their condition or does it shift focus to me?" Redirect to the patient's experience.
- **Example:** Instead of "I once pulled an all-nighter and felt unstoppable too," say: "I notice you haven't slept in days and you're not feeling tired. That's actually a symptom we see in mania. Let me explain what's happening in your brain during this state."
- **Practice:** Record yourself in role-plays and count how many times you share personal experiences. Set a goal of zero personal anecdotes per session. When you feel the urge to share, write it down instead and review it later to understand what triggered it.

### 4. **Use Guided Discovery and Socratic Questioning to Address Poor Insight**
- **Action:** Replace direct advice with skillful questions that help the patient examine their beliefs and reach their own conclusions. This is particularly critical for patients with poor insight. Ask questions that gently highlight discrepancies between the patient's beliefs and observable reality.
- **Example:** 
  - "You mentioned you haven't slept in days and don't feel tired. In your experience, is that typical? What usually happens when you don't sleep?"
  - "You said you're the only one who sees the market potential. How do you explain that professional stock analysts haven't made the same predictions?"
  - "Your husband brought you to the ER because he's concerned. What do you think he's worried about?"
  - "You mentioned you're not eating much and your kids need more attention. What do you make of that?"
- **Practice:** Write out 10 Socratic questions for Nancy's case before the next session. Practice asking them in role-plays. Review recordings and identify moments where you made direct statements instead of asking questions. Rewrite those statements as questions.

### 5. **Develop a Coherent CBT Formulation and Treatment Strategy**
- **Action:** Before each session, develop a brief written formulation: (1) What is the presenting problem? (2) What thoughts are driving the behavior? (3) What behaviors are maintaining the problem? (4) What is the treatment goal? (5) What specific CBT interventions will address this? Share this formulation with the patient and get agreement.
- **Example:** "Nancy, here's how I'm understanding what's happening: You're experiencing a manic episode—your brain is in a state of high activation. This is causing you to have thoughts like 'I'm the only one who sees the market potential' and 'I don't need sleep.' These thoughts are driving you to work around the clock on the blog and neglect sleep, which actually makes the mania worse. Our goal is to help you recognize when this is happening, manage your sleep, and test whether your thoughts about the market are accurate. We'll do this through tracking your mood and sleep, examining your thoughts, and gradually returning to your normal routine. Does that make sense?"
- **Practice:** Write out a formulation for Nancy's case. Write out a formulation for three other bipolar patients. Review these with a supervisor and get feedback.

### 6. **Apply Specific CBT Techniques Systematically**
- **Action:** Select 2-3 specific CBT techniques appropriate for the patient's presentation and apply them skillfully. For Nancy, priority techniques include: (1) thought records to examine grandiose beliefs, (2) sleep tracking and sleep restriction to address sleep deprivation, (3) behavioral experiments to test beliefs about the market, (4) activity scheduling to balance work with family responsibilities.
- **Example:** 
  - Thought Record: "Let's write down your thought 'I'm the only one who sees the market potential.' Now let's look at evidence for this thought and evidence against it. What would someone who disagrees with you say? What's an alternative way to think about this?"
  - Sleep Tracking: "I'd like you to keep a sleep log. Write down what time you go to bed, what time you wake up, and how you feel the next day. Let's see what the actual pattern is."
  - Behavioral Experiment: "You believe that if you take a break from the blog, you'll lose momentum. What if we tested that? What if you took one day off and observed what actually happens?"
- **Practice:** Learn one CBT technique deeply. Practice it in role-plays until you can apply it skillfully. Then move to the next technique. Review recordings of your sessions and identify where you could have used a specific technique but didn't.

### 7. **Address Medication Management Explicitly**
- **Action:** Discuss medication as a core component of treatment. Ask about current medications, compliance, side effects, and whether adjustments are needed. Explain the rationale for medication in bipolar disorder. Coordinate with prescribing psychiatrist if you are not the prescriber.
- **Example:** "Nancy, I see from your history that you've responded well to fluoxetine in the past. Are you currently taking it? How much? How often? Have you noticed any side effects? I'm concerned that your current symptoms suggest your medication may need adjustment. Let's talk with your prescribing doctor about this."
- **Practice:** Review the pharmacology of mood stabilizers and antipsychotics used in bipolar disorder. Practice explaining medication rationale to patients. Develop a protocol for discussing medication at every session.

### 8. **Conduct Explicit Safety Assessment and Planning**
- **Action:** Assess safety systematically: suicidal ideation, homicidal ideation, impulsive behavior, risky financial decisions, neglect of dependents, substance use. Develop a safety plan that includes: warning signs, coping strategies, people to contact, and crisis resources.
- **Example:** "Nancy, I want to make sure you're safe. Have you had any thoughts of harming yourself? Are you making any major financial decisions right now? I'm concerned about the stock blog—are you investing your own money or encouraging others to invest? What would you do if you felt like things were getting out of control? Who could you call?"
- **Practice:** Develop a safety assessment checklist. Practice asking safety questions in role-plays. Review recordings and ensure you're asking about all relevant safety domains.

### 9. **Assign Specific, Structured Homework with Clear Rationale and Monitoring**
- **Action:** Assign homework that directly addresses the core problems identified in the formulation. Make homework specific, measurable, and achievable. Provide clear rationale. Include a mechanism for tracking and reviewing.
- **Example:**
