### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates almost no evocation, relying entirely on telling and prescribing. Questions asked are rhetorical and accusatory rather than exploratory: "Why haven't you done that already?" "Why didn't you start checking your blood sugar daily?" "Why haven't you made quitting a priority yet?" None invite Marcus to explore own reasons for change, values, self-efficacy, or resources. All designed to elicit compliance or expose failure. True evocative questions would explore motivation, confidence, and barriers. Only final exchanges include genuine evocation: "What part of your treatment plan feels most manageable for you to start with?" and "What kind of support do you think would help you the most?" Represents virtually complete absence of evocative skill until final exchanges.

### Softening Sustain Talk

**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates poor skill in softening sustain talk, using confrontation and pressure that likely strengthen client resistance. Marcus provides sustain talk throughout ("It's just overwhelming," "I'm not sure I'm doing it right," "It's hard to keep up," "I haven't really been consistent," "It's kind of intimidating," "If I rush into it without feeling ready, I might just end up giving up altogether"). Psychiatrist's response is confrontational: "Don't make excuses," "You have to stop being so hesitant," "You have to stop making excuses," "It's not about confidence; it's about willpower," "There's no room for hesitation here." Research shows confrontation increases resistance and decreases motivation. Skillful response would use double-sided reflections normalizing ambivalence. Only final exchanges show appropriate softening: "It sounds like managing your blood sugar and making lifestyle changes feels overwhelming right now, and that's completely understandable." Represents fundamental MI failure in majority of session.
