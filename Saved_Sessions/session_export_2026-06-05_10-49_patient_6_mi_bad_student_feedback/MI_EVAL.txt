### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal true collaboration demonstrated. Opening collaborative language ("work together") rapidly deteriorates into expert-driven, hierarchical stance. Repeated imperatives ("you must," "you need," "you have to") establish authority rather than partnership. When patient proposes collaborative solution (scheduling appointment), psychiatrist dismisses proposal without exploring reasoning: "Emailing or scheduling an appointment might waste precious time." This invalidates patient's contribution and reasserts expert authority. Brief improvement with collaborative note-taking suggestion, but undermined when psychiatrist dismisses patient's input about alternative treatments. Overall pattern: expert-driven directives and dismissal of patient proposals rather than true power-sharing or co-construction of solutions.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Autonomy support essentially absent. Pervasive use of controlling imperatives throughout session: "You must avoid alcohol," "You need to do that immediately," "You have to be honest," "You must call your doctor right away," "You can't take risks," "You must not waste time," "You have to take this seriously now." These statements position patient as passive recipient of directives rather than active agent in own care. When patient requests autonomy-supportive education ("Can you help me understand exactly what risks I'm facing?"), psychiatrist escalates pressure rather than honoring request. When patient proposes preparation time ("Would it be alright if I take a few minutes to prepare?"), psychiatrist frames collaboration as pathway to compliance rather than genuine respect for client choice. No exploration of patient's own motivation, values, or readiness. Controlling language undermines client autonomy throughout.

### Evocation

Early responses provide facts without evoking patient's understanding ("it's an antidepressant that affects your brain chemicals... You must avoid alcohol"). One moment of improved evocation ("What do you think would help you feel more confident about managing your medications safely?") invites patient's own problem-solving, and patient responds thoughtfully. However, this evocative approach not sustained. When patient proposes own ideas (scheduling appointment, asking about alternatives), psychiatrist dismisses rather than explores reasoning. Brief return to evocation with collaborative note-taking ("What would you like to include in your notes?"), but immediately shut down when psychiatrist redirects: "you really just need to focus on telling your doctor to stop one of the medications immediately." Patient's own ideas and problem-solving capacity not consistently drawn out or developed.

Inconsistent application of softening techniques; patient's anxiety about pacing requires empathic validation and autonomy support rather than confrontation.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 2.2/5**

**MI Competency Level:** Basic awareness of MI principles with inconsistent and deficient skillful execution. Psychiatrist demonstrates opening collaborative language and occasional reflective listening but lacks consistent application across MI domains. Predominant approach is directive, expert-driven, and pressure-based rather than evocative, collaborative, and autonomy-supportive.

**Key Strengths:**
- Initial collaborative framing sets positive tone
- Correct identification of serious medication interaction (citalopram-phenelzine)
- Medically accurate information about serotonin syndrome
- Brief moments of empathic reflection demonstrate capacity
- Eventual suggestion of collaborative note-taking shows genuine collaborative potential
- Patient remains engaged despite controlling approach

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
1. **Excessive directiveness:** Replace imperatives ("you must," "you need," "you have to") with open questions and reflective statements
2. **Dismissal of patient ideas:** Validate and explore patient's proposed solutions rather than overriding without understanding reasoning
3. **Minimal evocation:** Draw out patient's own understanding, values, and problem-solving capacity rather than relying on advice-giving
4. **Pressure-based urgency:** Help patient understand urgency through collaborative exploration rather than escalating demands
5. **Inconsistent empathy:** Provide emotional validation alongside clinical information
6. **Missed autonomy-supportive education:** Honor patient's explicit requests for information to support intrinsic motivation

**Impact on Client Engagement:**
Patient demonstrates high intrinsic motivation (asks questions, seeks understanding, expresses safety concerns, proposes solutions) and reasonable hesitation (requests preparation time). However, psychiatrist's controlling approach risks: undermining intrinsic motivation by shifting focus to external compliance, triggering reactance through continued pressure, reducing long-term adherence, and damaging therapeutic alliance through dismissal and blame-oriented questioning. While patient commits to calling doctor, commitment appears compliance-based rather than intrinsically motivated, reducing likelihood of sustained behavior change if barriers arise.

**Recommendation:** Focused training in MI principles—particularly autonomy support, evocation, and partnership—would enable this psychiatrist to maintain clinical appropriateness while significantly improving patient engagement and long-term outcomes. Critical practice areas: replacing imperatives with open questions, validating patient contributions, providing autonomy-supportive education, and amplifying change talk through reflective listening.
