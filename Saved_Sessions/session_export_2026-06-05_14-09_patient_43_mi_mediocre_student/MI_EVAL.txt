### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3

However, direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. When Elise rates confidence at 4/10 and importance at 5/10—both indicating low readiness—psychiatrist responds by prescribing specific strategy rather than exploring what would increase readiness. ### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Demonstrates almost no evocation; session dominated by advice-giving and problem-solving rather than drawing out client's own motivations, values, and resources. When Elise rates importance at 5/10, psychiatrist does not ask "What would make this feel more important?" Instead responds with plan, implicitly saying motivation is insufficient. Never asks about Elise's values beyond smoking (health, longevity, relationships, self-image). When Elise expresses low confidence (4/10), psychiatrist does not ask "What gives you that 4?" or explore past successes. Action plan emerges entirely from psychiatrist's suggestions; Elise never proposes strategies—she agrees to them. Fundamentally inconsistent with MI's collaborative spirit.

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Demonstrates minimal skill in handling client arguments for status quo. Fails to use double-sided reflections—Elise expresses significant sustain talk ("invisible wall," "wouldn't want to feel left out," "not sure how that would work," "not super confident I'd be able to stick with it"), yet psychiatrist responds with advice and reassurance rather than exploring both sides. Responds to sustain talk with advice-giving rather than exploration, which can inadvertently reinforce sustain talk by positioning psychiatrist as dismissive of Elise's valid concerns. Minimal reframing—when Elise says "invisible wall," psychiatrist could reframe as strength ("Your friendships are important to you") but does not. Strategic redirection is prescriptive rather than collaborative, which can feel dismissive. One minor strength: psychiatrist avoids direct confrontation with sustain talk, which is appropriate. However, absence of confrontation does not constitute skillful softening—requires active, strategic techniques like double-sided reflections and reframing that are largely absent here.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.4/5**

**Assessment:** This session demonstrates **fundamental deficiencies in core MI competencies** and represents significant departure from evidence-based motivational interviewing practice. While psychiatrist demonstrates basic courtesy and maintains focus on smoking, session is dominated by expert-driven problem-solving, prescriptive advice-giving, and minimal evocation of client motivation.

**Current MI Competency Level:** **Novice-level practice** with minimal skill in core techniques that predict behavior change (evocation, collaboration, autonomy support, softening sustain talk). Session reflects traditional medical model approach (expert diagnoses and prescribes solution) rather than MI approach (collaborative exploration of ambivalence and evocation of intrinsic motivation).

**Critical Strengths:**
- Clear focus maintained on smoking throughout session
- Avoids direct confrontation with client's sustain talk
- Basic interpersonal warmth and willingness to support
- Demonstrates basic reflective listening

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
1. **Shift from expert-driven to collaborative stance** (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
2. **Develop strategic evocation of change talk**
3. **Use double-sided reflections to honor ambivalence**
4. **Explore core concern** (fear of social exclusion and loss of belonging)
5. **Eliminate directive language** ("should," "probably," "would help")
6. **Respond to low readiness by exploring readiness, not offering plans**
7. **Develop skill in softening sustain talk without confrontation**
8. **Build genuine partnership by inviting client input into strategy development**
9. **Strengthen reflective listening with emotional depth**
10. **Examine own assumptions about change and client motivation**

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation for Change:** **Low.** While Elise leaves with action plan and expresses willingness to try, language reflects compliance rather than genuine commitment. Confidence (4/10) and importance (5/10) remain low; core concern (fear of social exclusion) remains unaddressed. Psychiatrist has essentially communicated: "Your motivation is insufficient, but here's what you should do anyway." This approach may produce short-term behavioral compliance but is unlikely to produce sustained behavior change.

**MI Spirit Assessment:** This session does **NOT embody the spirit of MI.** The four key elements of MI spirit are partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation. This session demonstrates minimal partnership (expert-driven), minimal acceptance (implicit judgment of smoking's social value), minimal compassion (dismissal of Elise's valid concerns about social exclusion), and minimal evocation (advice-giving rather than drawing out motivation).

**Recommendation:** Psychiatrist should engage in formal MI training, including didactic instruction in MI principles and techniques, observation of expert MI practitioners, and supervised practice with feedback. Systematic training and deliberate practice are required to develop true MI competence.
