# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal unconditional positive regard and adopts a problem-focused stance rather than affirming client worth. While opening is warm, the session quickly shifts to what the client is doing wrong. No explicit validation of struggle, normalization of barriers, or affirmation of Marcus's resilience as a dedicated father and hardworking employee. Barriers (work stress, family obligations, financial constraints) are treated as problems to solve rather than understandable human realities deserving compassion. Client's worth is not affirmed independent of behavior change.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Reflective listening is predominantly surface-level and factual, missing emotional depth and client meaning. Simple reflections like "So, you're having trouble sticking with weight loss efforts, right?" add no new understanding. Complex reflections capturing ambivalence, fear of disappointment, loss, or emerging hope are absent. When Marcus expresses nostalgia about dancing or fear of disappointing family, these emotionally rich moments are acknowledged factually but not deepened. Emerging hope is met with caution rather than celebration. True empathy requires understanding client meaning—this is minimally demonstrated.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains general focus on physical activity and weight management throughout, preventing aimless conversation. However, direction becomes increasingly directive rather than collaborative. The psychiatrist moves from open exploration to specific prescriptions ("Maybe you should try setting reminders," "Maybe you should tell your family," "It would probably help if you keep track"). Direction is psychiatrist-imposed rather than client-chosen. No exploration of why Marcus wants to change or what change would mean to him. Session lacks inquiry into Marcus's own vision of change.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist adopts expert-driven stance that minimizes true joint problem-solving. Frequent directive language positions psychiatrist as expert and Marcus as recipient of advice: "Maybe you should try," "It would probably help if," "Maybe you could start by picking." True collaboration would involve asking Marcus's ideas first ("How do you think you could fit this in?") rather than proposing solutions and asking him to react. Power remains with psychiatrist throughout. Phrases like "I'm here to support you" position psychiatrist as helper and Marcus as helped, rather than equal partners. Pseudo-collaboration present but genuine co-construction absent.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist frequently undermines autonomy through directive language and implicit pressure. Pervasive use of "should" language ("Maybe you should try," "Maybe you should tell your family") prescribes behavior and implies obligation. Statement "Keep in mind, it's your choice how and when to do these things, but I think you're on the right track" appears autonomy-supportive but is immediately undermined by "but I think you're on the right track," communicating psychiatrist's judgment of what is "right." Overall tone communicates psychiatrist knows what Marcus should do rather than trusting Marcus's own wisdom. Risk of undermining intrinsic motivation and creating reactance over time.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist relies heavily on telling rather than asking, significantly limiting evocation of client's own motivations and resources. Approximately 8-10 questions asked, but most are closed or leading questions that don't evoke Marcus's thinking. Open-ended evocative questions are rare (1-2 instances). When Marcus mentions dancing, psychiatrist acknowledges factually rather than evoking: "What was it about dancing that you loved?" or "What would it mean to dance again?" are not asked. When Marcus expresses hope, psychiatrist cautions about ups and downs rather than evoking: "What's giving you that hope?" Session is psychiatrist-driven rather than client-centered in idea generation.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role throughout, positioning themselves as knowledgeable guide and Marcus as recipient of guidance. Expertise and wisdom about change are positioned with psychiatrist, not with client. Prescriptive statements dominate: "Maybe you should try setting reminders," "It would probably help if you keep track," "Just remember, it's normal to have ups and downs." True partnership would involve asking Marcus what he thinks will work and treating his ideas as equally valid. Power dynamics favor psychiatrist; Marcus's contributions shape session minimally while psychiatrist's agenda shapes it substantially. Client's expertise about his own life is underutilized.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Marcus produces substantial change talk (10+ instances): "Maybe smaller changes could work," "A goal for a couple walks a week sounds doable," "Starting small sounds better," "I could give that a shot," "I'm kind of feeling a bit hopeful," "Maybe this time it won't be one of those things I quit." Psychiatrist acknowledges some change talk ("That's good to hear you're feeling hopeful"; "Great, it sounds like you're open to trying some small steps") but fails to strategically amplify it. No follow-up questions deepening commitment or confidence. When Marcus expresses hope, psychiatrist responds with caution rather than exploring it. Missed opportunities to strengthen emerging motivation through strategic questioning and reflection are frequent.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist does not effectively soften sustain talk. Seven instances of sustain talk present: "Every time I try, I end up gaining it back," "I can't stick with it," "I can't afford gym," "I don't want to disappoint them," "I'm not sure if it'll make a difference," "I'm worried about fitting it in," "I'm not sure how to keep it going." Psychiatrist addresses sustain talk minimally (1-2 times) without skillfulness MI requires. Double-sided reflections absent. Reframing absent. When Marcus expresses doubt ("I'm not sure if it'll actually make a difference this time"), psychiatrist ignores doubt and pivots to closed question rather than exploring ambivalence. Prescriptive approach may inadvertently reinforce sustain talk by validating barriers without exploring client resourcefulness.

---

### Overall Assessment

**Summary Score: 2.3/5 – Below-Average MI Competence**

**Strengths:**
- Maintains general focus on physical activity; session doesn't wander
- Acknowledges some change talk and recognizes client strengths (walking, dancing, family support)
- Warm opening tone establishes basic rapport
- Avoids confrontation; maintains supportive demeanor
- Client ends with increased hope and commitment despite MI deficiencies

**Critical Deficiencies:**
- Expert-driven stance dominates; prescriptive solutions replace evocation
- Minimal open-ended questions; mostly closed/leading questions
- Surface-level reflections; complex reflections capturing emotion and ambivalence absent
- Pervasive "should" language undermines autonomy and collaboration
- Sustain talk not softened; change talk not strategically amplified
- Acceptance and affirmation minimal; barriers not normalized
- Pseudo-collaboration; psychiatrist presents solutions and asks client to react

**MI Spirit Assessment:** Session does not fully embody MI spirit. While compassion and non-confrontation are present, core elements of partnership, evocation, and autonomy support are lacking. This is supportive counseling rather than true motivational interviewing. Significant training needed to develop authentic MI competence.

**Likelihood of Sustained Change:** Moderate to low. While Marcus leaves with concrete plan and increased hope, psychiatrist's failure to evoke intrinsic motivation, address ambivalence, and support autonomy suggests change may be fragile. Marcus may follow plan initially but is at risk for same pattern of abandonment experienced before. Prescriptive approach creates compliance rather than genuine commitment.

**Priority Recommendations:**
1. **Shift from expert to evocative stance** – Replace prescriptive statements with open-ended questions
2. **Develop complex reflections** capturing emotional nuance and deeper meaning
3. **Strategically amplify change talk** through follow-up questions and exploration
4. **Support autonomy** by replacing "should" language with choice-supporting language
5. **Use double-sided reflections** to soften sustain talk without confrontation
