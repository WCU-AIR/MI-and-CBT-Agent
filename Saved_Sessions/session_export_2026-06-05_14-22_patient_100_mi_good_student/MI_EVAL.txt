However, acceptance is significantly undermined by failure to explicitly address or normalize the alcohol screening referral. ### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 3

However, empathy lacks depth and consistency. Missed emotional nuance when Matteo says "sounds like a lot when you say [chuckles]"—a moment of genuine self-reflection about drinking frequency receives no follow-up. No acknowledgment of Matteo's likely experience of being referred for alcohol discussion. Empathy is present but superficial.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** **Critical deficiency.** Psychiatrist was referred Matteo specifically due to alcohol screening concerns, yet entire session focuses exclusively on ankle care. Opening question ("what would you most like to focus on?") is collaborative but inadvertently allows session to drift from clinical referral reason. No strategic direction toward identified concern. Represents either clinical avoidance or fundamental misunderstanding of session purpose. Psychiatrist should have acknowledged both concerns: "I know your ankle is a priority, and I also want to make sure we touch on something that came up in your screening about alcohol use." Fundamentally misdirected away from actual clinical concern.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates strong collaborative stance with shared decision-making throughout. Opens with client input, seeks permission before sharing information ("Would it be okay if I shared...?"), and invites Matteo's perspective on helpful steps. Uses inclusive language ("we can talk about," "let's think about") creating partnership rather than hierarchy. Responsive to client concerns and validates hesitations. However, collaboration is undermined by fundamental direction problem: psychiatrist allows session to be entirely client-directed away from clinical concern. True collaboration requires balancing client autonomy with clinical responsibility—negotiating agenda to include both ankle care AND alcohol discussion rather than passively accepting avoidance.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist consistently respects Matteo's autonomy and freedom of choice. Uses explicit choice language ("Would it be okay if...?"), frames suggestions as options rather than directives, and respects hesitation about exercises without pressure. Confidence scaling is used to explore self-assessment, not to shame or pressure. Acknowledges difficulty without judgment. However, autonomy support is compromised by avoidance of alcohol discussion. By not addressing referral reason, psychiatrist implicitly communicates that Matteo can avoid discussing alcohol entirely. More autonomy-supportive approach would explicitly offer the conversation: "We can focus on your ankle today, and I also want to check in about your drinking—you get to decide how much we talk about that."

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** **Severe deficiency.** Evocation—drawing out client's own motivations, values, and resources—is cornerstone of MI and is minimally demonstrated. Regarding ankle care: some evocation occurs ("What do you wish you could do differently?"), but could be deeper. Regarding alcohol use (actual clinical concern): **zero evocation.** Psychiatrist never asks how Matteo feels about drinking patterns, what he notices about alcohol in his social life, or whether he's thought about changing. **Most critically: spontaneous change talk is completely missed.** When Matteo says "sounds like a lot when you say [chuckles]," this is a moment of self-reflection and incipient change talk. Psychiatrist does not reflect it back, ask follow-up questions, or reinforce it. This represents fundamental failure in MI technique—the clinician's primary job is to evoke and amplify change talk.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Partnership—conveying that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within client—is superficially present but fundamentally compromised. Psychiatrist makes unilateral clinical decision to focus entire session on ankle care despite being referred specifically for alcohol screening. This is expert-driven decision that overrides clinical partnership. True partner would say: "I notice you're focused on your ankle, which makes sense. I also want to make sure we address the alcohol screening that came up—how do you want to handle that?" By avoiding alcohol discussion, psychiatrist implicitly positions himself as expert who decides what's important. No co-construction of change agenda. Information delivery is largely expert-driven advice-giving rather than true co-construction of solutions. Session reflects implicit expert stance rather than equality in therapeutic relationship.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** **Most severe deficiency.** Cultivating change talk—strategically evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change—is central to MI effectiveness. Psychiatrist demonstrates almost no cultivation of change talk, particularly around clinical concern. Regarding alcohol use: **zero change talk elicited.** Psychiatrist never asks about Matteo's perspective on drinking, concerns about it, or motivation to change. **Spontaneous change talk completely missed.** When Matteo says "sounds like a lot when you say [chuckles]," psychiatrist does not: reflect it back to amplify, ask follow-up questions to deepen, reinforce with affirmation, or use as springboard for exploration. Instead, moves on without acknowledgment. Regarding ankle care: some change talk elicited but not strategically amplified or reinforced. No follow-up like "What else?" or "Tell me more." No exploration of what would strengthen commitment. Fundamental failure in MI technique.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates inconsistent application of this skill. Regarding ankle care: skillfully handles Matteo's hesitation about exercises using double-sided reflection ("comfortable with icing...but unsure about exercises because you want to avoid harm"). Validates concern while maintaining distinction between what he's willing to do and what he's hesitant about. Could have gone further with reframing. Regarding alcohol use: **no softening of sustain talk** because no sustain talk is explicitly addressed. Matteo's framing of drinking as purely social ("Wednesdays with friends") is sustain talk—reason to maintain current patterns. Psychiatrist does not reflect it back with curiosity, explore discrepancy, gently challenge minimization, or use double-sided reflection. Instead, avoids topic entirely. Skill is present but inconsistently applied and absent where most needed.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 2.8/5**

**Session Rating: Below Average – Significant Clinical Deficiencies**

---

### Key Strengths:
- ✓ Warm, validating tone creating safe therapeutic environment
- ✓ Collaborative language inviting partnership
- ✓ Consistent autonomy support and respect for hesitation
- ✓ Accurate reflective listening of content and emotion
- ✓ Gentle handling of client concerns without pressure
- ✓ Practical problem-solving (identifying manageable first steps)

### Critical Areas for Improvement:
- ✗ **Avoidance of clinical referral reason** – entire session focuses on ankle care despite alcohol screening referral
- ✗ **Failure to evoke change talk** – minimal evocation, particularly around alcohol use
- ✗ **Missed spontaneous change talk** – "sounds like a lot" moment completely unaddressed
- ✗ **Lack of strategic direction** – collaboration used to avoid rather than address clinical issue
- ✗ **Superficial empathy** – accurate reflections lack emotional depth and nuance
- ✗ **No context linking** – never explores whether alcohol involved in injury or affects recovery
- ✗ **Weak change talk cultivation** – even around ankle care, change talk not strategically amplified

---

### Clinical Impact Assessment:

**Likelihood of evoking client motivation for change regarding alcohol use: LOW**

This session will likely **reduce rather than increase** motivation to address drinking by:
1. Signaling that alcohol use is not a clinical priority
2. Missing critical window of ambivalence when Matteo questioned his own drinking frequency
3. Failing to build any motivation for change
4. Creating expectation that alcohol won't be discussed in future sessions
5. Potentially reinforcing minimization and avoidance

---

### Recommendations for Clinician Development:

**Priority 1 (Highest):** Directly address referral reason while maintaining collaboration
- Practice opening statements that acknowledge both presenting concern and screening result
- Develop language that balances clinical responsibility with client autonomy

**Priority 2:** Recognize and amplify spontaneous change talk
- Record and review sessions to identify moments of self-reflection
- Practice deepening these moments with follow-up questions

**Priority 3:** Develop complex, emotion-capturing reflections
- Move beyond surface-level reflections to capture underlying meaning
- Use double-sided reflections to hold ambivalence

**Priority 4:** Evoke and explore ambivalence around alcohol use directly
- Develop open-ended questions about drinking that feel natural and non-judgmental
- Practice curious, collaborative tone rather than interrogative approach

**Priority 5:** Maintain clinical direction while remaining collaborative
- Practice negotiating session agenda to include both concerns
- Develop language that asserts clinical responsibility while respecting autonomy

---

**Recommended Training Focus:** Strategic direction maintenance, spontaneous change talk recognition and amplification, and addressing clinical avoidance patterns.
