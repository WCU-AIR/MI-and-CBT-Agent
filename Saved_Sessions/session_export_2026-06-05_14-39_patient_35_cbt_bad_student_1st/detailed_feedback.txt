
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

### 1. Agenda
### **Score: 0/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made no attempt to establish a collaborative agenda at any point during this session. A proper CBT agenda would involve: (1) identifying specific target problems to address, (2) prioritizing which issues to focus on given time constraints, (3) establishing measurable session goals, and (4) obtaining patient agreement on the plan. None of these occurred.

The session drifted aimlessly from topic to topic—fatigue, anhedonia, sleep disturbance, motor symptoms, outdoor activity—without any explicit prioritization or structure. The therapist never asked, "What would be most helpful to focus on today?" or "Of these concerns, which one would you like to tackle first?" or "How much time do you think we need for each of these issues?" The patient raised multiple significant problems (sleep-onset insomnia, REM sleep behavior disorder with violent movements, social withdrawal, concentration difficulties, weight loss), yet the therapist never helped organize these into a coherent treatment plan.

This is particularly problematic given Mr. Anderson's complex medical presentation. A proper agenda would have distinguished between: (1) symptoms directly attributable to Parkinson's disease (motor symptoms, some fatigue), (2) depressive symptoms requiring intervention (anhedonia, concentration difficulties, insomnia), and (3) the REM sleep behavior disorder requiring specialized management. Without agenda-setting, the session lacks direction and accountability.

**Summary:**

The therapist completely failed to establish a collaborative agenda. No target problems were prioritized, no session goals were set, and no structure was imposed on the session flow. This is a fundamental CBT failure.

### 2. Feedback
### **Score: 1/6**

**Explanation:**

The therapist made minimal attempts to elicit feedback and did not systematically check for patient understanding throughout the session. Proper feedback elicitation in CBT involves: (1) regularly asking "Does that make sense?" or "How does that land with you?", (2) checking whether the patient understands the therapist's reasoning, (3) soliciting reactions to suggestions, (4) adjusting approach based on patient responses, and (5) summarizing key points and asking for patient input at session end.

The therapist offered numerous suggestions (breathing exercises, focusing on positive thoughts, getting outside, drinking water, listening to music) but never checked whether the patient understood the rationale, agreed with the suggestions, or found them helpful. When the patient expressed doubt ("it just doesn't stick," "hard to hold onto hope"), the therapist did not pause to explore this resistance or adjust the approach. The patient's statement "it's hard to feel that hope when the progress seems so slow or even in reverse" was a critical moment requiring feedback elicitation—the therapist should have asked, "What would help you feel more hopeful?" or "Does this approach feel realistic to you?" Instead, the therapist simply repeated generic reassurance.

At no point did the therapist ask, "What was most helpful about our conversation today?" or "Is there anything I said that didn't make sense?" or "How are you feeling about the suggestions we discussed?" The session ended abruptly without any summary or feedback check.

**Summary:**

The therapist rarely elicited feedback and never systematically checked patient understanding or satisfaction. This represents a critical gap in collaborative practice and prevents the therapist from knowing whether interventions are landing or whether the patient is disengaging.

### 3. Understanding
### **Score: 2/6**

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness

## Recommendations

### 1. **Establish and Maintain Session Agenda (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** At the beginning of every session, explicitly collaborate with the patient to set an agenda. Ask: "What would be most helpful to focus on today?" "Of these concerns, which one feels most important to address?" "How much time do you think we need for each?" Write the agenda down and refer to it throughout the session.
- **Example:** "George, I know you're dealing with fatigue, sleep problems, difficulty concentrating, and some loss of interest in activities you used to enjoy. These are all important. Given our time today, I'm thinking we should focus on [X] first because [reason]. Does that sound right to you? Anything you'd like to add or change?"
- **Practice:** Role-play agenda-setting with a supervisor or colleague. Practice with 5-10 different patients to develop fluency. Record yourself and review for clarity and collaboration.

### 2. **Apply Guided Discovery Systematically (HIGHEST PRIORITY)**
- **Action:** Replace advice-giving with skillful questioning. When the patient expresses a concern or belief, ask questions that help him examine it rather than telling him what to think. Use the Socratic method: ask about evidence, alternatives, implications, and conclusions.
- **Example:** Instead of "Just think positive," ask: "When you think about the future, what specifically worries you? What evidence do you have for that worry? Are there any times when things have gone better than you expected? What would need to happen for you to feel more hopeful?"
- **Practice:** Study Socratic questioning techniques. Practice with a supervisor, recording yourself and reviewing for quality of questions. Aim for 80% of your responses to be questions rather than statements.

### 3. **Develop a Coherent Treatment Strategy Based on CBT Conceptualization**
- **Action:** After gathering information, explicitly state your understanding of how the patient's problems developed and are maintained. Connect this to specific interventions. For Mr. Anderson: "Here's how I understand what's happening: Your Parkinson's disease has made it harder to do the activities you used to enjoy, so you've been staying home more. Staying home means less activity and less social contact, which actually makes depression worse. Additionally, when you think about what you've lost, it's hard to feel hopeful. And poor sleep makes everything harder. So here's what I think will help: We'll work on gradually increasing your activity, even in small ways. We'll look at some of the thoughts about loss that keep you stuck. And we'll work on your sleep. Does that make sense?"
- **Example:** Write out your conceptualization for each patient and share it with them. Explain how each intervention addresses a specific part of the problem.
- **Practice:** For each patient, write a one-paragraph conceptualization before the session. Review it with a supervisor. Revise based on feedback.

### 4. **Eliminate Excessive Self-Disclosure; Maintain Professional Boundaries**
- **Action:** Limit personal anecdotes to rare, brief moments when they directly illustrate a clinical point. Do not use personal stories to normalize the patient's experience or suggest that your experience is similar to theirs. Maintain professional language and tone appropriate for psychiatric evaluation.
- **Example:** Instead of "I remember when I had exam stress and just telling myself it would pass helped me," say: "Many people find that when they're going through difficult times, reminding themselves that situations change can be helpful. I'm wondering if that might work for you, or if something else might be more useful."
- **Practice:** Record yourself and count personal anecdotes. Aim for zero per session. If you include one, ensure it directly illustrates a clinical point and is brief (1-2 sentences).

### 5. **Implement Systematic Behavioral Activation with Structure and Monitoring**
- **Action:** Don't just suggest "get outside." Instead: (1) Identify valued activities the patient can realistically do, (2) Break them into small, manageable steps, (3) Schedule them specifically, (4) Have the patient predict barriers and problem-solve, (5) Establish how to monitor mood before and after, (6) Plan to review results at the next session.
- **Example:** "You mentioned you used to enjoy walks with your wife. Given your motor symptoms, let's think about a modified version. Write down what you notice. We'll look at this together next time to see if it helps your mood."
- **Practice:** Develop behavioral activation plans for 5-10 patients. Write them out in detail. Review with a supervisor for specificity and feasibility.

### 6. **Implement Formal Homework Assignment with Clear Rationale and Review Plan**
- **Action:** At the end of every session, assign specific, concrete homework. Explain the rationale. Write it down. Discuss potential barriers. Confirm the patient's commitment. At the next session, review the homework first and discuss what the patient learned.
- **Example:** "This week, I'd like you to keep a sleep log. Write down what time you go to bed, when you fall asleep, how many times you wake up, and what you're thinking about when you can't sleep. - **Action:** Explicitly map which symptoms belong to which condition. Understand that depression in the context of medical illness requires different intervention than primary depression. Recognize that REM sleep behavior disorder is a separate condition requiring specialized management (potentially medication adjustment, safety measures, sleep study).
- **Example:** "George, let me make sure I understand what's going on. Some of what you're experiencing—the tremor, the shuffling gait, some of the fatigue—that's your Parkinson's disease.
