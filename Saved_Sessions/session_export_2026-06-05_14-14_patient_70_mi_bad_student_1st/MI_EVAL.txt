### Acceptance

**Comments:** Session demonstrates near-complete absence of unconditional positive regard. Psychiatrist uses explicit judgment, blame, and shame throughout ("you have to stop making excuses," "you must have not tried hard enough," "you're just choosing to ruin your health"). Patient's emotional experience is repeatedly dismissed ("There's no room for feelings or excuses"). No affirmation of patient's worth, no normalization of addiction struggle, and no genuine warmth. Fundamentally rejecting stance antithetical to MI spirit.

### Empathy

When patient states "it's just like the same thing every day" and "feeling like I'm being told what to do makes me wanna check out more," psychiatrist dismisses as "excuses" rather than exploring. ### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains consistent focus on smoking cessation target behavior but uses coercive, prescriptive approach rather than collaborative guidance. Imposes single solution without exploring patient's ideas, resources, or readiness ("there's no other way," "you have to just stop completely"). Fails to clarify patient's own direction or values. No exploration of what patient wants for his life, what smoking costs him, or what quitting might enable. Direction without collaboration becomes control.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Entirely devoid of genuine collaboration. Psychiatrist establishes expert-driven, hierarchical stance from opening ("I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do"). Makes unilateral decisions about what patient "has to" do without joint problem-solving or seeking patient input. Directly contradicts patient's feedback ("no one's yelling at you" when patient explicitly states it feels like yelling). Repeated imperatives ("you have to," "you need to," "you must") create power imbalance where psychiatrist's will is imposed rather than negotiated. No invitation for patient to contribute ideas or share decision-making.

### Autonomy Support

"Will you quit smoking today or not?"). Uses guilt and shame to pressure compliance ("If you don't, you're just choosing to keep harming yourself"). Patient explicitly states resistance to control ("I'm not gonna just flip a switch 'cause you say so," "just being told what to do turns me off from the whole thing"), yet psychiatrist intensifies pressure rather than adjusting. No acknowledgment of patient's right to choose or affirmation of autonomy.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Entirely absent. Psychiatrist tells rather than asks, imposes rather than explores. Uses closed, factual questions for information-gathering ("Do you smoke every day?") but never follows with reflections demonstrating understanding. Critical missed opportunities: when patient says "I've tried quitting before but it didn't stick," psychiatrist responds "you must have not tried hard enough" rather than exploring experience. When patient says "It's way more complicated than that," psychiatrist dismisses rather than asking "Help me understand what makes it complicated?" Limited change talk offered by patient ("I get the damage part," "I don't wanna be sick," "I'm not saying I don't want to quit") is missed, dismissed, or contradicted rather than amplified.

Patient's explicit feedback that current approach is counterproductive ("just being told what to do turns me off from the whole thing") is ignored and intensified rather than reshaping session. ## Overall Session Summary

### **Overall MI Competency Score: 1.0/5**

---

### Key Findings

**Strengths:**
- Maintains consistent focus on target behavior (smoking cessation)
- Asks factual questions to gather information about smoking patterns
- Expresses concern about health consequences (though communicated counterproductively)

**Critical Deficiencies:**
This session represents a **fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles** and demonstrates a confrontational, expert-driven approach that is **actively counterproductive** to evoking intrinsic motivation for change.

---

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change

**Trajectory:** Patient enters with ambivalence (acknowledging damage but hesitating to commit). By session end, patient has moved toward **active resistance and disengagement** ("I'm not promising anything just 'cause you say so," "maybe I'm just stuck").

**Mechanism:** Confrontational approach increases **reactance** (psychological resistance to perceived control) rather than evoking intrinsic motivation. Patient's repeated statements about monotony and feeling unheard suggest prior exposure to this approach without success. Psychiatrist's response is to intensify same ineffective approach.

**Most Critical Issue:** Session ends with psychiatrist restarting from beginning with identical language ("I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do"), suggesting no reflection on what is not working. This is likely to severely damage therapeutic relationship and patient's willingness to engage in future treatment.

---

### Primary Recommendations

1. **Shift from expert-driven to collaborative partnership** (FOUNDATIONAL)
   - Replace "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do" with collaborative language inviting patient's perspective
   - Position patient as expert on his own life

2. **Eliminate shame, blame, and guilt as motivational tools**
   - Replace confrontational statements with acceptance and curiosity
   - Recognize that shame increases reactance and decreases intrinsic motivation

3. **Develop genuine reflective listening skills**
   - After patient speaks, reflect back emotion, meaning, and perspective
   - Use complex reflections that deepen insight
   - Aim for 2:1 ratio of reflections to questions

4. **Respond to patient feedback by adjusting approach**
   - When patient provides explicit feedback that something is not working, treat as critical information
   - Adjust immediately rather than intensifying same approach

5. **Evoke rather than impose motivation for change**
   - Replace directives with open questions drawing out patient's own reasons, values, and ideas
   - Ask rather than tell

6. **Amplify and reinforce change talk**
   - When patient offers change talk, pause and explore it
   - Reflect back and ask follow-up questions helping patient elaborate on own reasons for change

7. **Soften sustain talk through double-sided reflections**
   - Acknowledge both sides of ambivalence without confrontation
   - Avoid arguing against patient's resistance

8. **Support autonomy rather than pressuring compliance**
   - Replace pressure and demands with genuine autonomy support
   - Affirm patient's right to choose and ownership of change process

9. **Demonstrate genuine acceptance and unconditional positive regard**
   - Communicate patient's inherent worth regardless of smoking choices
   - Normalize struggle with addiction
   - Express genuine warmth and support

10. **Seek formal MI training and ongoing consultation**
    - Enroll in 2-day MI training workshop through Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers
    - Obtain ongoing consultation with skilled MI trainer reviewing session recordings
    - Develop genuine competence before continuing behavior change counseling

---

### Clinical Conclusion

**Current MI Competency:** Minimal (1/5). Session does not embody MI spirit (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation) and is likely to **decrease** patient's motivation for change and damage therapeutic relationship.

**Likelihood of Evoking Client Motivation:** Currently **very low**. Patient has moved from ambivalence to active resistance. Confrontational approach has increased reactance rather than evoking intrinsic motivation.

**Recommendation:** Psychiatrist should seek formal MI training and ongoing consultation with MI trainer before continuing to see patients for behavior change counseling. Current approach is inconsistent with evidence-based practice and likely to harm patient outcomes.
