### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains clear focus on alcohol cessation, which prevents aimless conversation. However, direction is achieved through coercion and ultimatums rather than strategic, collaborative exploration. Relevant factual questions asked ("How many drinks?" "Do you drink every day?") but answers used as ammunition for pressure rather than to deepen understanding. Critical failure: never explores Kevin's own goals, values, or readiness for change. ### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist operates from explicit expert-authority stance throughout. Opening statement establishes unilateral positioning: "I'm here to help you get on track... we need to talk about what you're doing wrong and how you must change it." Unilateral declarations dominate ("You really need to," "You have to," "You must"). When Kevin offers compromise ("Maybe I could try cutting back first?"), psychiatrist responds with rejection: "No, you're not getting it." Kevin's explicit requests for collaboration ("Can't we look at smaller steps?") are denied. No joint problem-solving. Kevin's expertise about his own life, values, and capacity for change completely disregarded. Power is asserted, not shared.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively undermines autonomy through coercive language and pressure tactics. Repeated ultimatums ("Are you going to quit today or not?") frame Kevin's choice as binary compliance or failure, not genuine autonomy. Explicitly denies Kevin's autonomy: "This is not about feeling ready or not—it's about doing what's necessary. You don't get to negotiate." Dismisses Kevin's self-assessment of readiness ("No more delays, no more 'I'm not ready'"). Uses conditional language implying autonomy contingent on compliance ("If you really cared about your health, you would quit immediately"). Treats Kevin as object to be fixed rather than autonomous agent. Kevin's autonomy assertions ("I'm not ready," "that feels like too much too soon") trigger escalated pressure rather than respect. Approach violates core MI principle and likely increases psychological reactance.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1/5
**Comments:** Psychiatrist imposes reasons for change rather than evoking Kevin's own motivations. Kevin produces change talk ("I'm embarrassed," "it was really stupid," "I'm not proud of what happened") that could be evoked and amplified; instead, it is ignored. Psychiatrist substitutes external reasons ("serious health problems," "worse injuries," "serious diseases") without asking Kevin to articulate his own. Never asks open-ended evocative questions: "What concerns you most?" "What would be different if you changed?" "What matters to you about your health?" "How do you see this affecting your future?" When Kevin mentions stress and socializing as functions of drinking, opportunity to explore values is missed; instead dismissed as "excuses." Kevin's resources and strengths (willingness to attend, self-awareness, openness to considering change) never evoked or recognized. Complete failure of evocation principle.

### Partnership

**Comments:** Psychiatrist actively assumes and asserts expert authority throughout. Positions himself as authority on what Kevin is "doing wrong" and how he "must" change. Makes unilateral declarations: "You really need to," "You have to," "You must." Kevin positioned as someone who "doesn't get it" and needs repeated instruction. No genuine curiosity about Kevin's perspective. Questions asked to gather information for confrontation, not to understand Kevin's wisdom. Kevin's repeated requests for collaboration denied. Systematically rejects partnership. ## Overall Summary

### Strengths
- **Maintains topical focus:** Conversation consistently focused on alcohol use rather than drifting to unrelated topics.
- **Asks relevant factual questions:** Questions about quantity and frequency gather basic information about Kevin's drinking pattern.
- **Acknowledges presenting problem:** Recognizes Kevin's drinking as issue requiring attention.

**Note:** These are minimal baseline clinical skills, not MI-specific competencies.

---

### Critical Areas for Improvement

**Fundamental MI Spirit Violations:**
Psychiatrist operates from confrontational, authoritarian stance directly contradicting MI's foundational principles. Repeated judgmental language, ultimatums, and dismissal of Kevin's perspective create adversarial relationship rather than collaborative partnership. Approach likely to increase resistance and decrease intrinsic motivation for change.

**Absence of Core MI Skills:**
Reflective listening entirely absent. Questions asked but understanding of responses not demonstrated. Complex reflections capturing emotional nuance, ambivalence, or deeper meaning never occur. Kevin's change talk ignored; sustain talk met with confrontation rather than skillful softening. Evocation completely absent.

**Autonomy Undermining:**
Systematic undermining of Kevin's autonomy through coercive language, pressure tactics, and denial of right to self-determination. Violates core MI principle and likely triggers psychological reactance.

**Failure to Engage with Ambivalence:**
Kevin's explicit ambivalence treated as character deficiency rather than normal human experience. Validated exploration with curiosity absent; instead, pressure applied for immediate resolution.

**Repetitive, Non-Strategic Approach:**
Same directive repeated 8+ times without variation or strategic deepening. Suggests no clinical plan for engaging Kevin or evoking motivation. True MI involves strategic progression; this represents stagnation.

---

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change

**Likely to DECREASE Kevin's intrinsic motivation for change.** Kevin's repeated statements ("this kind of talk just makes me want to shut down," "you're not really listening," "I'm just not there yet") indicate he experiences interaction as rejecting and pressuring rather than supportive. Research demonstrates confrontational, authoritarian approaches less effective than collaborative, evocative MI. Kevin in contemplation stage—precisely where MI most effective. This approach likely pushes Kevin toward precontemplation (denial, defensiveness) rather than toward preparation or action. Repeated pressure without movement toward Kevin's goals creates power struggle. Final statement "I'm just not there yet" suggests disengagement rather than movement toward change; psychiatrist's response (repeating same demand) will likely further entrench resistance.

### Overall MI Competency Assessment

**Pre-Competent MI Practice**

While basic clinical skills present (maintaining focus, asking factual questions), MI-specific competencies largely absent. Approach more consistent with confrontational counseling or directive advice-giving than Motivational Interviewing.

### Recommendation

**Psychiatrist should seek formal MI training and supervision before continuing MI sessions.** Current approach risks harm by increasing client resistance and decreasing motivation for change. With dedicated training and deliberate practice, significant improvement is possible. Recommend: (1) Formal MI training workshop; (2) MI-trained supervision; (3) Session recording and review using MITI (Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity) scale; (4) Deliberate skill-building practice focusing on reflective listening, evocation, and autonomy support; (5) Elimination of judgmental language and confrontational tactics; (6) Fundamental shift from expert-authority to collaborative partnership.
