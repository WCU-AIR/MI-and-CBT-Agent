
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

Odd numbers (1, 3, 5) represent intermediate ratings between descriptors. Ratings are based on the therapist’s skill level while accounting for patient difficulty.

## Recommendations

### 1. **Develop Comprehensive Case Formulation Addressing Both Diagnoses**
- **Action**: In the next session, explicitly develop a shared case formulation that integrates both separation anxiety disorder and provisional tic disorder. Create a visual diagram showing how Ethan's catastrophic thoughts about parental safety trigger anxiety, which exacerbates tics, which creates secondary anxiety about the tics, which maintains avoidance and safety behaviors.

- **Practice**: Study the relationship between anxiety and tics in the literature. Practice drawing the formulation diagram with other patients. Discuss with supervisor how to present complex formulations to children in age-appropriate language.

### 2. **Systematically Map and Target Avoidance Hierarchy**
- **Action**: In the next session, collaboratively develop an avoidance hierarchy with Ethan, listing situations from least to most anxiety-provoking: (1) Being alone in room with door open, (2) Being alone in room with door closed, (3) Being alone in another room, (4) Going to school without parent, (5) Attending a playdate without parent, (6) Attending a sleepover. Then prioritize which situations to target first, starting with least anxiety-provoking.
- **Example**: "Let's make a list of times when you feel worried about being away from your parents. We'll start with the easiest situations and work our way up to the harder ones. This way, you can practice and build confidence step by step."
- **Practice**: Develop avoidance hierarchies for 3-5 anxiety patients. Practice explaining the rationale for graduated exposure to patients and parents.

### 3. **Identify and Address Core Beliefs**
- **Action**: Use guided discovery to help Ethan identify underlying core beliefs. Ask questions like: "What would it mean if something bad happened to your parents? What would that say about you? - **Example**: If Ethan's core belief is "I'm responsible for my parents' safety," use guided discovery: "Is it possible for a 9-year-old to prevent bad things from happening to adults? - **Practice**: Study core beliefs in childhood anxiety. Practice identifying core beliefs through guided discovery with other patients. Develop interventions to challenge core beliefs (behavioral experiments, thought records, metaphors).

### 4. **Systematically Plan Safety Behavior Reduction**
- **Action**: In the next session, explicitly discuss the door jamb ritual and other safety behaviors. Explain that while they provide temporary relief, they keep the anxiety cycle going. - **Example**: "The door jamb ritual helps you feel safe for a little while, but it's like a trap. Your brain thinks 'I need to do this to be safe,' so the worry keeps coming back. If we gradually stop doing it, your brain will learn that you're safe without it, and the worry will get smaller."
- **Practice**: Study the role of safety behaviors in maintaining anxiety. Practice explaining safety behavior reduction to patients. Develop specific plans for reducing safety behaviors in other patients.

### 5. **Involve Parents in Treatment Planning**
- **Action**: Schedule a session with Ethan and his parents to discuss the treatment plan, explain the anxiety cycle, and discuss parental accommodation (how parents may inadvertently reinforce anxiety by allowing Ethan to stay close, checking on him frequently, reassuring him excessively). Develop a parental involvement plan: (1) Parents allow Ethan to practice being alone without checking on him, (2) Parents do not provide excessive reassurance about safety, (3) Parents model calm behavior and realistic thinking about danger, (4) Parents encourage independence and valued activities.
- **Example**: "Mrs. Miller, when Ethan feels anxious and comes to you for reassurance, it helps him feel better temporarily. But it also teaches his brain that he needs you to feel safe. To help Ethan get better, we need you to gently encourage him to practice being alone and to resist the urge to reassure him constantly. This is hard, but it's what will help him most."
- **Practice**: Study parental accommodation in childhood anxiety. Practice explaining accommodation and its role in maintaining anxiety to parents. Develop specific parental involvement plans for other patients.

### 6. **Apply Tic-Specific Interventions (Habit Reversal Training)**
- **Action**: In a future session, assess Ethan's tics in detail: frequency, triggers, premonitory urges (does he feel an urge before the tic?), and impact. - **Example**: "Ethan, I notice you blink a lot, especially when you're worried. Before you blink, do you feel something in your eyes? Like an urge or a feeling? - **Practice**: Study habit reversal training for tics. Practice assessing tics and identifying premonitory urges. Practice teaching competing responses. Consult with a tic specialist or supervisor for guidance.

### 7. **Provide Psychoeducation About Anxiety Cycle and CBT Model**
- **Action**: In the next session, provide explicit psychoeducation about how anxiety works and how CBT helps. Use a visual diagram to show: (1) Trigger/situation, (2) Scary thought, (3) Anxiety feeling, (4) Safety behavior/avoidance, (5) Temporary relief but worry comes back stronger. Explain that CBT works by: (1) Identifying scary thoughts, (2) Testing whether they're true, (3) Practicing being in scary situations without safety behaviors, (4) Letting anxiety decrease naturally.
- **Example**: "Ethan, anxiety is like a smoke alarm in your brain that goes off when it thinks there's danger. Sometimes the alarm is helpful—it warns us about real danger. But sometimes the alarm goes off even when there's no real danger, like when you're thinking about your parents. When the alarm goes off, you want to do something to make it stop—like stay close to your parents or rub the door jamb. That helps for a little while, but then the alarm goes off again. In CBT, we teach your brain that there's no real danger, so the alarm stops going off. We do this by practicing being in situations that trigger the alarm and letting it go off without doing anything to stop it. Eventually, your brain learns there's no danger and the alarm stops."
- **Practice**: Develop age-appropriate psychoeducation materials for children with anxiety. Practice explaining the anxiety cycle and CBT model to other patients. Use diagrams, metaphors, and examples that resonate with children.

### 8. **Conduct In-Session Behavioral Experiments and Exposures**
- **Action**: In future sessions, conduct in-session behavioral experiments to test Ethan's predictions.
