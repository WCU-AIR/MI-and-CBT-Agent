### Acceptance

**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates consistent unconditional positive regard and nonjudgmental stance throughout the session. Frequent affirmations of client resilience and insight are evident ("That sense of being stuck in a dead end, yet still wanting to find a way forward, really shows your resilience"). Minor opportunity exists to affirm client's inherent worth independent of coping efforts or performance.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 5
**Comments:** Exemplary empathic understanding demonstrated throughout. Psychiatrist uses complex, accurate reflections capturing both content and emotional nuance. Sophisticated double-sided reflections skillfully address ambivalence ("It sounds like you really want to feel more in control...At the same time, there's a part of you that's skeptical..."). Clinician accurately names unstated emotions (frustration, exhaustion, courage) and demonstrates genuine perspective-taking regarding client's need for simplicity and manageability.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains appropriate strategic focus on anxiety management during photography work with progressive clarification toward concrete action planning. Direction is client-centered rather than imposed, with questions inviting client to articulate her own goals. Minor opportunities exist to establish measurable success markers ("How will you know if these reminders are helping?") and to explore whether anxiety management is truly the client's priority versus other competing concerns.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong collaborative stance demonstrated throughout, positioning client as equal partner in problem-solving. Opening question establishes agenda-setting collaboration. Psychiatrist builds on client's ideas (focusing on camera) rather than imposing solutions. Inclusive language ("How about we work together") reinforces partnership. Minor opportunity to invite client's input on session structure itself and to co-construct meaning more dialogically rather than through lengthy clinician reflections.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Consistent autonomy support with option-focused language ("If you were to take one small step...") rather than prescriptive directives. Psychiatrist normalizes possibility of not succeeding and affirms client's right to define success on her own terms. Minor opportunities exist to explicitly affirm client's right to choose not to change and to invite her to identify potential barriers she might face, supporting her autonomy in anticipating challenges.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong evocation demonstrated through strategic questions drawing out client's own motivations and resources. Powerful use of past success exploration ("Can you tell me about a time when you successfully managed...?") elicits client's own coping strategies (focusing on camera). Clinician builds on self-discovered strengths. Opportunities exist to evoke deeper values-based motivation ("What does being a photographer mean to you?") and to invite client to articulate her own reasons for change more directly rather than through clinician reflections.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Strong partnership stance with inclusive language and respect for client's expertise about her own experience. Psychiatrist validates client's insight and builds directly on her ideas. Opening question establishes collaborative agenda-setting. Minor opportunities to co-construct meaning more dialogically through shorter reflections followed by open questions, and to explicitly invite client feedback on whether session direction feels right to her.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 4
**Comments:** Skillful cultivation of change talk through reflections and strategic questions about past successes and commitment. Psychiatrist amplifies client's desire for change and reinforces ability talk ("That's a really important strength"). Commitment talk is reinforced through acknowledgment of willingness to try. Opportunities exist to invite client to articulate her own change talk more directly ("What makes you think this could work for you?") and to explore confidence in implementation and problem-solving around initial setbacks.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Adequate but inconsistent handling of sustain talk. Psychiatrist uses skillful double-sided reflections acknowledging both hope and doubt without reinforcing skepticism. However, limited strategic reframing of underlying concerns occurs. When client expresses fear about change, psychiatrist acknowledges but does not strategically reframe (e.g., "What's scarier—facing feelings with a plan, or staying stuck?"). Minimal anticipation of obstacles or problem-solving around potential barriers to strategy implementation. Underlying sustain talk about perfectionism and self-criticism is affirmed but not directly addressed.

## Overall Session Summary

**Overall Score: 4.1/5**

**Key Strengths:**
- Exemplary empathic understanding with consistently accurate, complex reflections capturing emotional nuance and ambivalence
- Strong collaborative stance with frequent invitations for client input and co-construction of solutions
- Skillful evocation of past successes and client-generated strategies
- Consistent unconditional positive regard and affirmation of client resilience
- Appropriate strategic direction toward concrete action planning while maintaining client-centeredness
- Strong autonomy support with option-focused language and respect for client choice
- Effective cultivation of change talk through reflections and strategic questions

**Areas for Development:**
- Limited explicit affirmation of client's inherent worth independent of coping efforts
- Insufficient exploration of whether anxiety management is truly the client's priority
- Lengthy clinician reflections that sometimes shift balance toward expert interpretation rather than co-construction
- Minimal direct evocation of client's own reasons for change, values, or identity-based motivation
- Inadequate strategic reframing or exploration of sustain talk and underlying concerns
- Limited anticipation of obstacles or problem-solving around potential barriers
- No explicit establishment of measurable success markers
- Insufficient invitation for client feedback on session direction

**Clinical Impact:**

This is a solid, competent MI session demonstrating genuine therapeutic presence and strong foundational skills. The client leaves with a concrete, collaboratively-developed action plan (phone reminders + focused attention on camera), increasing ownership and follow-through likelihood. Client confidence increased from implicit uncertainty to explicit willingness to try (4/5). However, the session could have generated deeper intrinsic motivation by connecting anxiety management to client's identity as a photographer and core values. The lack of obstacle anticipation may result in lower follow-through if barriers emerge.
