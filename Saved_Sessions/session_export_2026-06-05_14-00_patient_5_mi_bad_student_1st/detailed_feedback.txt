
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a near-complete absence of unconditional positive regard and acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly uses language that is overtly judgmental and dismissive of Aizada's legitimate concerns. Within the first exchange, the clinician frames Aizada's health anxiety as "making excuses" ("You're just making excuses. Pain or no pain..."), which directly communicates disapproval of her perspective and invalidates her experience. This pattern intensifies throughout the session with statements like "You're just overthinking it," "You need to stop feeling sorry for yourself," and "Enough hesitation"—all of which communicate contempt rather than respect for the client's autonomy and emotional reality.

Most critically, the psychiatrist never acknowledges Aizada's legitimate fear about missed diagnosis, her years of suffering, or the real difficulty of managing chronic pain. When Aizada expresses fear of cancer (a reasonable concern given her mother-in-law's history and four years of unexplained symptoms), the clinician dismisses this as something to "stop worrying about" rather than exploring it with curiosity or validating the emotional burden. There is no affirmation of Aizada's strengths (her willingness to engage, her self-awareness about stress patterns, her caution about medication safety). The tone throughout is authoritarian and punitive—the psychiatrist acts as if Aizada is deliberately non-compliant rather than ambivalent and frightened.

The repeated use of "you must," "you have to," and "you need to" creates a coercive atmosphere antithetical to MI spirit. Aizada's repeated requests for more testing and her hesitation about medication are treated as character flaws ("not taking this seriously," "not being committed") rather than understandable responses to uncertainty and fear.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance of Aizada's perspective, fears, or autonomy. Judgmental language, dismissal of concerns, and absence of affirmation create an adversarial rather than supportive relationship, fundamentally violating MI principles of unconditional positive regard.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate understanding of the client's perspective and emotional experience. This session contains almost no evidence of genuine empathic engagement. The psychiatrist never reflects back Aizada's emotional state, never demonstrates curiosity about her experience, and never attempts to understand the world from her viewpoint.

When Aizada says, "The pain is real, and I don't usually take medicines unless a doctor says it's safe," the psychiatrist responds with "You're just overthinking it" rather than exploring why medication safety matters to her or what past experiences might inform her caution. When Aizada reveals that stress patterns correlate with symptom fluctuation ("When my son got married, I was too busy to notice the pain"), the psychiatrist dismisses this as "just a distraction from the real problem" rather than exploring this important insight with curiosity.

Most tellingly, the psychiatrist never reflects Aizada's core fear: that her symptoms might represent serious organic disease. This fear appears repeatedly in her speech ("What if it's cancer, like my mother-in-law?"), yet the clinician never acknowledges it, explores it, or validates the emotional weight it carries. A empathic response might be: "It sounds like you're carrying a lot of fear that something serious might be missed, and that fear is making it hard to focus on other things right now."

Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly tells Aizada what she should feel ("You need to stop being scared") and what she should think ("Stress isn't the cause here"), demonstrating a fundamental failure to understand her perspective. The clinician also misses Aizada's subtle change talk—her acknowledgment that stress does correlate with symptoms—and instead frames this as "making excuses," showing a failure to recognize and build upon her own emerging insights.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal empathic understanding. There are no complex reflections, no exploration of emotional nuance, and no genuine attempt to see the situation through Aizada's eyes. Dismissal and redirection replace empathic listening.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

While the psychiatrist does maintain a clear focus on lifestyle modification and medication management, this direction is pursued in a way that violates MI principles. Direction in MI should involve collaborative clarification of goals while maintaining client-centeredness; here, direction becomes rigid prescription.

The psychiatrist does consistently redirect toward diet, exercise, hydration, and medication—these are legitimate health targets. However, the approach is entirely top-down. The clinician never asks Aizada what her goals are, what she wants to work toward, or how she envisions improvement. Instead, the psychiatrist imposes a predetermined agenda: "You must start doing these things immediately."

Critically, the psychiatrist ignores Aizada's stated priority—understanding the cause of her symptoms through testing—and instead insists on lifestyle interventions as prerequisites. This is directionally misaligned with the client's actual concerns. A more skillful approach would acknowledge her testing concerns while gently exploring whether lifestyle changes might coexist with further investigation, rather than positioning them as competing priorities.

The direction also lacks strategic progression. The psychiatrist repeats the same demands (take over-the-counter medications daily) without adapting when Aizada expresses hesitation. There is no attempt to understand the barriers to her compliance or to collaboratively problem-solve around them. The session ends with a yes/no ultimatum ("Are you going to start taking over-the-counter painkillers every day, yes or no?"), which is directive but not collaborative.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist maintains focus on health behaviors but pursues this direction through prescription rather than collaboration. Goals are imposed rather than co-constructed, and the client's stated priorities are overridden rather than integrated.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have valuable knowledge. This session demonstrates the opposite: a rigid expert-driven, hierarchical stance where the psychiatrist assumes complete authority over what is "right" and what Aizada should do.

The opening statement sets the tone: "I'm here to help you get on track with your health, so I'm going to tell you what you need to do today." This immediately establishes a unilateral, expert-driven dynamic. The psychiatrist then proceeds to interrogate Aizada about smoking, diet, exercise, and hydration without ever asking what she thinks might help, what she's already tried, or what barriers she faces.

When Aizada attempts to redirect the conversation toward her actual concern (the persistent GI symptoms and need for testing), the psychiatrist dismisses this and continues with the predetermined agenda. There is no negotiation, no shared decision-making, and no incorporation of Aizada's input into the session structure or goals.

Most damaging to collaboration is the psychiatrist's repeated framing of Aizada as the problem: "You're just making excuses," "You're just overthinking it," "You need to stop feeling sorry for yourself." This language positions the psychiatrist as judge and Aizada as defendant, not as partners working together. True collaboration would involve statements like: "I hear that you're worried about missing a serious diagnosis. That's important. Let's think together about how we can address both your need for clarity and your physical wellbeing."

The psychiatrist also never seeks Aizada's perspective on what might be causing her symptoms, what she's already tried, or what she believes would help. Her knowledge of her own body, her stress patterns, and her medical history are treated as irrelevant. The session is entirely clinician-driven.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist operates in a completely hierarchical, expert-driven mode with no genuine collaboration. The client's input is neither sought nor valued, and the session structure is entirely clinician-imposed.

### 5. Autonomy Support
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Autonomy support requires respecting the client's right to choose and emphasizing their ownership of the change process. This session is saturated with language that undermines autonomy and attempts to control Aizada's behavior through pressure and prescription.
