# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

### Acceptance

**Comments:** Near-complete absence of unconditional positive regard. Psychiatrist repeatedly uses judgmental language ("making excuses," "overthinking," "feeling sorry for yourself") that invalidates client's legitimate concerns. No affirmation of client's strengths, willingness to engage, or self-awareness about stress patterns. Tone is authoritarian and punitive rather than supportive. Client's fears about missed diagnosis are dismissed rather than explored with curiosity.

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal empathic understanding demonstrated. No complex reflections or genuine attempt to see situation through client's perspective. When client expresses medication safety concerns, psychiatrist responds with dismissal rather than exploration. Client's core fear (cancer diagnosis) appears repeatedly but is never acknowledged or validated. Psychiatrist tells client what she should feel rather than understanding her emotional experience. Emerging insights about stress-symptom connections are contradicted rather than explored.

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Psychiatrist maintains clear focus on lifestyle modification and medication management, but pursues direction through rigid prescription rather than collaboration. Client's stated priority (diagnostic testing) is overridden rather than integrated. No attempt to understand barriers to compliance or collaboratively problem-solve. Session ends with yes/no ultimatum rather than collaborative exploration. Direction lacks strategic adaptation when client expresses hesitation.

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Rigid expert-driven, hierarchical stance with no genuine power-sharing. Opening statement ("I'm going to tell you what you need to do today") immediately establishes unilateral dynamic. Client's input is neither sought nor valued. When client attempts to redirect toward actual concern (persistent GI symptoms), psychiatrist dismisses this and continues with predetermined agenda. Language positions psychiatrist as judge and client as defendant. No negotiation or shared decision-making evident.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Session saturated with language that undermines autonomy: "You must," "You have to," "You need to." Client's hesitation about medication is framed as character flaw rather than legitimate choice. Session ends with false binary ultimatum ("yes or no?") rather than autonomy-supportive exploration. No acknowledgment of client's right to decline medication, request testing, or proceed at own pace. Coercive atmosphere designed to shame client into compliance violates fundamental MI principle of respecting client choice.

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no evocation of client's own motivations, values, or resources. Client provides important change talk ("When my son got married, I was too busy to notice the pain"; "It gets worse when my husband is away") but psychiatrist dismisses these as "making excuses" rather than exploring them. Emerging self-discovery about stress-symptom connections is contradicted rather than developed. No questions designed to elicit client's own reasons for change. Client's resilience, self-awareness, and caution are never acknowledged or built upon.

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist operates entirely in expert role with no genuine power-sharing or acknowledgment of client's expertise about her own experience. Client's four years of living with symptoms, observations about stress patterns, and medical history are treated as irrelevant. When client expresses concern about medication masking serious symptoms, psychiatrist dismisses reasoning without exploration. Language reinforces power imbalance ("You have to," "Why haven't you?"). No invitation to partnership or shared language evident throughout session.

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal change talk produced, and what emerges is actively suppressed rather than cultivated. Client expresses desire ("I want to feel better"), willingness ("I'm willing to try some lifestyle changes"), and conditional commitment ("I'm willing to try medication if it's safe"). Psychiatrist responds with demands and pressure rather than exploring or amplifying these statements. When client acknowledges stress patterns, psychiatrist dismisses as "making excuses." Session ends with client stating "I'm not ready to commit fully"—opposite of MI goal. Change talk has been suppressed rather than developed.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Poor handling of sustain talk primarily through confrontation rather than skillful redirection. Client's resistance to psychological framing, preference for medical investigation, and fear of serious disease are met with shame and dismissal ("You're just making excuses," "You're just overthinking it") rather than skillful softening. One attempt at minimization ("Painkillers are standard") is done dismissively rather than collaboratively. Session ends with ultimatum rather than exploration of underlying concerns. No genuine attempt to understand or reframe sustain talk collaboratively.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Current MI Competency Level:** Pre-MI (Below Basic Competence)

**Overall Session Score: 1.1/5**

**Key Findings:**

This session represents a **fundamental departure from Motivational Interviewing principles and spirit**. The psychiatrist operates in a directive, authoritarian, judgmental mode that is antithetical to MI. Across all nine competency domains, performance ranges from minimal to non-existent.

**Critical Deficiencies:**

1. **Absence of acceptance and empathy** — Repeated judgmental language and dismissal of legitimate fears create adversarial rather than supportive relationship
2. **Coercive rather than collaborative approach** — Imperatives and ultimatums replace genuine partnership and shared decision-making
3. **Suppression rather than evocation of motivation** — Emerging insights are dismissed; change talk is minimized; sustain talk is confronted
4. **Violation of autonomy** — Client's hesitation treated as character flaw; no genuine support for client choice or ownership of change process
5. **Misalignment with client priorities** — Client's stated concern (diagnostic testing) is overridden; resistance is predictable result

**Impact on Client Engagement:**

This session is **likely to decrease motivation for change and damage therapeutic relationship**. By session end, client explicitly states "I'm not ready to commit fully"—opposite of intended outcome. Psychiatrist's judgmental, coercive approach has reinforced client defensiveness and conviction that concerns are not being taken seriously.

**Immediate Recommendations:**

- **Urgent referral for formal MI training** (e.g., through MINT—Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers)
- **Supervised practice with feedback** on MI fidelity using standardized measures (e.g., MITI)
- **Fundamental shift required** from expert-directive to collaborative-evocative stance before continuing clinical work with this approach
- **Do not continue current practice pattern** without training and supervision—current approach is likely to harm client outcomes and increase dropout rates

**Likelihood of Evoking Genuine Client Motivation:** **Very Low** — Current approach actively decreases motivation and increases resistance.
