Stress management introduced as target behavior without exploring whether this is Ahmed's concern or priority. ### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Minimal collaboration; operates entirely from expert-driven, hierarchical stance. Clinician positions himself as authority on what Ahmed needs while Ahmed's input is solicited but not genuinely incorporated. Unilateral problem identification: assumes stress management is issue without asking Ahmed's concerns. Rhetorical questions disguised as collaboration ("Do you think your work schedule might be making it harder to take care of your health?") function as leading statements; Ahmed's answers don't shape session direction. Dismisses Ahmed's input: when Ahmed says interventions won't help, clinician responds "But maybe you should still try." No joint problem-solving occurs. Power imbalance maintained throughout with phrases like "It would probably help if you tried" and "It's important to." Fundamentally hierarchical and expert-driven; opposite of MI collaboration.

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Actively undermines client autonomy through repeated pressure toward predetermined interventions while minimizing expressed preferences. Prescriptive language throughout: "It would probably help if you tried" (appears 3 times), "It's important to keep an eye on your health," "Maybe you should consider trying." Directly contradicts Ahmed's stated autonomy: when Ahmed says "I'll pass on that for now," clinician responds "But maybe you should consider trying a walk." No genuine choice offered; Ahmed never asked "What would you be willing to try?" or "What feels realistic for you?" Frames compliance as obviously beneficial ("It might help you feel better without taking too much energy"), implying Ahmed's reluctance is irrational. Closing statement ("Keep up the good work") frames Ahmed's current functioning as aligned with clinician's recommendations, subtly pressuring compliance. Ahmed's response ("I'll think about trying some of that stuff, but can't promise much") reflects low autonomy support—verbal compliance without genuine commitment.

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no evocation occurs; cornerstone MI principle absent. Clinician never asks: "What brings you in today?" "What matters most to you about your health?" "What have you tried before that helped?" "What would need to change for you to feel differently?" "What are your own ideas about managing stress?" Instead, clinician imposes motivations: "It would probably help if you tried... It's good to take care of yourself... It's important to keep an eye on your health." When Ahmed produces minimal change talk ("I'll notice if things get worse"), clinician doesn't amplify or explore it. When Ahmed expresses sustain talk ("I'm okay with how things are," "I don't think it'll happen"), clinician doesn't skillfully explore ambivalence—simply reiterates advice. Ahmed's own reasons for change, values, self-efficacy, and intrinsic motivation never explored. Entire session operates on assumption that clinician's reasons for change are sufficient, violating fundamental MI principle that motivation resides within client.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall MI Competency Score: 1.4/5**

### Strengths
- Warm, respectful opening tone establishes initial rapport
- Consistent focus on health and stress management throughout session
- Some reflective listening attempts present
- Non-hostile, respectful demeanor maintained throughout

### Critical Deficiencies
This session demonstrates **fundamental misalignment with Motivational Interviewing principles** across nearly all domains. Core deficiency: **expert-driven, advice-giving model** rather than **evocative, collaborative model**.

**Primary Problems:**
1. **Unilateral problem identification** — Assumes stress management is issue without exploring Ahmed's actual concerns
2. **Advice-giving without evocation** — Prescribes interventions without exploring Ahmed's motivations, barriers, or preferences
3. **Dismissal of client autonomy** — Overrides Ahmed's stated preferences ("I'll pass on that for now") with "But maybe you should..."
4. **Contradiction of self-assessment** — Implies Ahmed's self-knowledge ("I feel fine") is insufficient
5. **No genuine collaboration** — Ahmed's input solicited but not incorporated into decision-making
6. **Minimal change talk cultivation** — Ahmed produces almost no change language; sustain talk never skillfully explored

### Impact on Client Engagement
**Likely to decrease Ahmed's intrinsic motivation for change.** Ahmed's closing response ("I'll think about trying some of that stuff, but can't promise much") reflects **psychological reactance**—verbal compliance without genuine commitment. When autonomy is threatened, people resist change even when beneficial. Research demonstrates advice-giving without evocation produces minimal sustained behavior change, particularly when client doesn't perceive problem.

### Recommendations (Priority Order)
1. **Establish collaborative agenda-setting** — Begin by genuinely exploring client's concerns, not clinician's assumptions
2. **Develop genuine empathic reflections** — Move beyond factual restatement to complex reflections capturing emotional nuance
3. **Explore ambivalence rather than prescribe** — Use curiosity and double-sided reflections instead of contradiction
4. **Affirm client strengths and resilience** — Build motivation on foundation of existing competence
5. **Ask permission before offering advice** — Respect autonomy; increase receptiveness through collaborative approach
6. **Evoke change talk systematically** — Use open-ended questions to draw out client's own reasons for change
7. **Recognize and respect precontemplation** — Ahmed is clearly not considering change; adjust approach accordingly
8. **Soften sustain talk with curiosity** — Respond to resistance with exploration, not repetition of advice
9. **Develop collaborative goal-setting** — Move from clinician-imposed to jointly constructed goals
10. **Avoid subtle pathologizing language** — Replace phrases implying hidden problems with curious, collaborative language

### Conclusion
Psychiatrist is operating at **pre-MI level**, using supportive counseling without strategic evocation and collaboration defining MI. **Formal MI training strongly recommended**, with focus on spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation) and core techniques (open-ended questions, affirmations, reflective listening, summarization). Session recording and review with attention to MI competency domains will accelerate skill development.
