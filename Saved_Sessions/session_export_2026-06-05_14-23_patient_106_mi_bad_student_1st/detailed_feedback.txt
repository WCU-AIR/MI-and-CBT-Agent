
# Motivational Interviewing Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Motivational Interviewing (MI) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent motivational interviewers. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Doctor/Counselor:** [response]
- **Patient/Client:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (1-5)
- **1:** Poor - Significant deficiencies, inconsistent with MI spirit
- **2:** Below Average - Some MI elements but major gaps in execution
- **3:** Adequate - Basic MI competence with notable room for improvement
- **4:** Good - Solid MI practice with minor areas to strengthen
- **5:** Excellent - Exemplary MI demonstration, skillful and consistent

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based MI principles and spirit
- Scores of 4-5 reserved for truly skillful MI practice
- Basic reflective listening alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be fair but rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (9 Categories)

### 1. Acceptance
Unconditional positive regard, nonjudgmental stance, affirmation of client worth, normalizing struggle, genuine warmth and support

### 2. Empathy
Accurate reflective listening, understanding client perspective, capturing emotional nuance, complex reflections that deepen insight

### 3. Direction
Strategic guidance toward change goals, maintaining focus, progressive clarification of direction while remaining client-centered

### 4. Collaboration
Joint problem-solving, shared decision-making, seeking client input, co-constructing solutions, teamwork in exploration

### 5. Autonomy Support
Respecting client's right to choose, avoiding advice-giving or prescription, emphasizing client ownership and self-efficacy

### 6. Evocation
Drawing out client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources; asking rather than telling; facilitating self-discovery

### 7. Partnership
Maintaining collaborative stance, sharing power, co-creating process, avoiding expert role, using inclusive language, true equality in relationship

### 8. Cultivating Change Talk
Evoking and reinforcing client statements about desire, ability, reasons, need, and commitment to change; strategically responding to strengthen motivation

### 9. Softening Sustain Talk
Skillfully handling client arguments for status quo; using double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic responses to minimize sustain talk without confrontation


## Score Requirements Examples

**For 5/5:** Multiple instances (4+) of skillful technique use, strategic amplification of change talk, deep complex reflections capturing emotion and meaning, consistent demonstration throughout

**For 4/5:** Frequent instances (3+) of technique, solid execution with minor missed opportunities, generally consistent application

**For 3/5:** Some instances (2) of technique, basic competence but lacks depth or consistency, notable gaps in skillfulness

**For 2/5:** Minimal instances (1 or fewer), technique present but poorly executed, significant deficiencies

**For 1/5:** Technique absent or used in ways inconsistent with MI spirit

## Before Scoring - Ask Yourself:
1. Does this session embody the spirit of MI (partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation)?
2. What MI-specific techniques were skillfully used?
3. How well did the counselor evoke vs. impose motivation?
4. Would this conversation increase client's intrinsic motivation to change?

## For Each Criterion - Identify:
1. What was done well (with specific transcript examples)
2. What was missing or poorly executed (specific MI techniques)
3. How frequently and skillfully the technique was demonstrated
4. Impact on client's change talk and engagement

## MI Spirit Check
Evaluate whether the session demonstrates:
- **Partnership** - Collaboration vs. confrontation
- **Acceptance** - Absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation
- **Compassion** - Prioritizing client's welfare and needs
- **Evocation** - Drawing out vs. installing motivation

## Consistency Check
After generating all scores, verify:
- Overall score equals the average of all 9 criterion scores
- Low scores (1-2) have serious critiques with specific examples of what was done wrong
- High scores (4-5) are justified by multiple instances of skillful MI practice
- Explanations clearly support the scores assigned
- Recommendations directly address all identified deficiencies
- Feedback is constructive but rigorous

---

**Remember:** This must evaluate true MI practice, not just supportive counseling. Be fair but critical. The goal is developing excellent motivational interviewers who can effectively evoke client motivation for change.

Motivational Interviewing Feedback Criteria (5-Point Likert Scale)

Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)
------------------------------------
1. Acceptance: Extent to which the counselor communicates unconditional positive regard for the client.
   1 = Judgmental, disapproving, labeling
   2 = Inconsistent respect, occasional judgment
   3 = Neutral stance, basic respect
   4 = Generally respectful and supportive
   5 = Consistently respectful, warm, supportive

2. Empathy: Extent to which the counselor understands and/or makes an effort to accurately understand the client’s perspective.
   1 = Little effort to understand, minimal reflective listening
   2 = Limited perspective-taking, mostly factual focus
   3 = Adequate but inconsistent effort to understand
   4 = Good reflective listening and perspective-taking
   5 = Highly accurate, consistent understanding and reflections

3. Direction – Degree to which the counselor maintains appropriate focus on a specific target behavior or concerns directly tied to it.
1 = No clear focus; session wanders aimlessly
2 = Minimal focus; frequent topic drift
3 = Occasional focus on target behavior but inconsistent
4 = Generally keeps discussion directed toward target behavior
5 = Consistently maintains clear focus and guides toward change

---

4. Collaboration – Extent to which the clinician behaves as if the interview is occurring between two equal partners, both of whom have knowledge that might be useful in the problem under consideration.
1 = Authoritarian or expert-driven stance
2 = Limited collaboration; frequent direction by counselor
3 = Partial collaboration; uneven power-sharing
4 = Strong collaborative stance most of the time
5 = Fully collaborative; client’s ideas shape session direction

---

5. Autonomy Support – Extent to which clinician supports and actively fosters client perception of choice as opposed to attempting to control the client’s behavior or choices.
1 = Directive or pressuring; minimizes choice
2 = Occasionally acknowledges choice but inconsistently
3 = Neutral regarding client autonomy
4 = Regularly affirms client freedom to choose
5 = Consistently reinforces client ownership of change process

---

6. Evocation – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that motivation for change, and the ability to move toward that change, reside mostly within the client and therefore focuses efforts to elicit and expand it within the therapeutic interaction.
1 = Provides reasons for change without exploring client view
2 = Limited interest in client motivations
3 = Some evocation but superficial
4 = Frequently draws out client’s own reasons for change
5 = Consistently elicits and develops client change talk throughout session

---

7. Partnership – Extent to which the clinician conveys an understanding that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly within the client

1 = Clinician actively assumes the expert role for the majority of the interaction with the client. Collaboration or partnership is absent.
2 = Clinician superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate.
3 = Clinician incorporates client’s contributions but does so in a lukewarm or erratic fashion.
4 = Clinician fosters collaboration and power sharing so that client’s contributions impact the session in ways that they otherwise would not.
5 = Clinician actively fosters and encourages power sharing in the interaction in such a way that client’s contributions substantially influence the nature of the session.

---

8. Cultivating Change Talk – Extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change.
1 = No effort to evoke change talk
2 = Rare or weak efforts
3 = Occasional evocation of change language
4 = Frequent evocation of change talk
5 = Consistent, skillful evocation throughout

---

9. Softening Sustain Talk – Extent that the clinician avoids a focus on the reasons against changing or for maintaining the status quo.
1 = Reinforces sustain talk
2 = Rarely shifts focus away from sustain talk
3 = Occasionally redirects toward change
4 = Often redirects effectively
5 = Consistently pivots client language toward change

Evaluation Guidance
-------------------
- All domains are rated on a 1–5 Likert scale.
- Ratings should reflect overall quality and consistency of behavior.
- Provide qualitative feedback with examples of effective and ineffective moments.
- Focus on both counselor skillfulness and client speech as indicators of MI process quality.

## Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

### 1. Acceptance
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

This session demonstrates a profound absence of unconditional positive regard and contains multiple instances of explicit judgment and criticism. The psychiatrist labels Naomi's hesitation as "weakness" ("Feeling scared or stuck is just a weakness you need to overcome"), directly contradicting MI's foundational principle of acceptance. Rather than normalizing her struggle with anxiety and ambivalence, the clinician pathologizes her reluctance and frames it as a character flaw.

The psychiatrist explicitly dismisses Naomi's emotional experience: "No, you really have to do it now. You can't keep using drinking as a crutch—it's making your anxiety worse, whether you feel it or not." This statement invalidates her lived experience and suggests her perception is unreliable. When Naomi expresses fear and uncertainty, the response is "No, you have to stop making excuses," which communicates disapproval rather than acceptance of her current state. The phrase "There's no excuse for not doing this" is overtly judgmental and contradicts MI's stance that clients are doing the best they can with their current resources.

Most critically, the psychiatrist never affirms Naomi's strengths, acknowledges her willingness to engage, or communicates genuine warmth. There is no normalization of her anxiety or validation of how difficult change feels. The repeated confrontational stance ("are you going to quit drinking today or not?") creates an adversarial dynamic rather than a supportive one. Naomi's response—"I don't like feeling pressured like this"—directly reflects the absence of acceptance in the room.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates virtually no acceptance throughout this session. Judgment, criticism, and dismissal of the client's experience are pervasive, creating a fundamentally non-MI environment that actively undermines therapeutic alliance.

### 2. Empathy
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Empathy requires accurate reflective listening and genuine effort to understand the client's perspective from their internal frame of reference. This session contains almost no reflective listening and demonstrates a consistent failure to understand or validate Naomi's experience.

When Naomi expresses concern about stopping drinking completely ("Maybe if I had some help figuring out other ways to cope first, then I could think about it"), the psychiatrist responds with prescription and pressure rather than reflection: "No, you really have to do it now." There is no attempt to understand *why* she feels unprepared, what her fears are, or what support she needs. A empathic response might have been: "It sounds like you're worried that stopping drinking all at once without a plan might backfire. Help me understand what you're most concerned about."

When Naomi explicitly states, "I don't like feeling pressured like this. I mean, it's not that I'm making excuses. I really do want to feel better," the psychiatrist responds with further pressure and accusation rather than reflection. An empathic response would acknowledge her stated desire for change while exploring her ambivalence: "You genuinely want to feel better, and at the same time, you're worried that quitting drinking completely right now might not work for you. Tell me more about that concern."

The psychiatrist never reflects Naomi's emotional state (fear, overwhelm, feeling unheard) or demonstrates understanding of her anxiety as the primary presenting concern. Instead, the focus remains on drinking as a behavioral target, ignoring Naomi's repeated attempts to redirect toward her anxiety and her need for support in developing coping strategies first.

Most tellingly, when Naomi says, "It feels like you're not listening to how scared or stuck I am," the psychiatrist does not pause to reflect or validate this feedback. Instead, the response continues with more pressure and judgment.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal empathy. There are virtually no reflective listening statements, no accurate understanding of Naomi's perspective, and no validation of her emotional experience. The session is characterized by prescription and pressure rather than understanding.

### 3. Direction
### **Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**

Direction in MI involves maintaining strategic focus on a target behavior while remaining client-centered. This session has a clear target (reducing alcohol consumption), but the approach is so rigid and confrontational that it actively undermines the collaborative exploration of that target.

The psychiatrist does maintain focus on alcohol use across multiple exchanges, which prevents complete aimlessness. However, the direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed. The psychiatrist never explores Naomi's own goals or asks what she wants to work on first. When Naomi repeatedly attempts to redirect focus toward her anxiety—"What I'm more worried about is, well, this anxiety thing"—the psychiatrist ignores this and continues with the predetermined agenda (smoking, drinking).

A more strategically sound approach would have been to explore Naomi's priorities: "I hear that anxiety is the main thing bothering you right now. Let's start there. Help me understand how it's affecting your life." This would establish direction collaboratively while honoring the client's stated concerns.

The psychiatrist's direction is also counterproductive. By insisting on alcohol cessation without exploring its function (anxiety management), the clinician misses an opportunity to understand the behavior's purpose and work collaboratively on alternative coping strategies. Naomi explicitly states: "It's just, when I do drink, it's kind of a way to relax sometimes, you know?" A strategically directed MI approach would explore this function and help develop alternatives, rather than simply demanding cessation.

The repeated yes/no questions ("are you going to quit drinking completely starting today or not?") create a false binary that doesn't allow for nuanced exploration of ambivalence or collaborative problem-solving.

**Summary:**

While the session maintains some focus on a target behavior, the direction is imposed rather than collaboratively developed, and it actively contradicts the client's stated priorities. The approach is rigid and counterproductive to meaningful behavior change.

### 4. Collaboration
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Collaboration requires treating the interview as occurring between two equal partners, with both parties contributing knowledge and perspectives. This session demonstrates an almost complete absence of collaboration and instead exemplifies an authoritarian, expert-driven model.

The opening statement sets the tone: "I'm here to help you get your health on track today, so we're going to focus on what you need to change." The use of "we're going to focus" masks a unilateral decision—the psychiatrist has already determined what needs to change (drinking) without consulting Naomi's priorities or concerns.

Throughout the session, the psychiatrist makes pronouncements rather than inviting input: "You really need to cut back," "You must cut back to no more than one drink," "You have to be strong and quit drinking now." These are directives, not collaborative explorations. There is no "What do you think?" or "How do you see this?" or "What would work for you?"

When Naomi expresses ambivalence or resistance, the psychiatrist responds with further directives rather than collaborative problem-solving. For example, when Naomi says she's not ready to stop drinking completely, a collaborative response might be: "I hear that stopping completely feels like too much right now. Let's think together about what might be a realistic first step that you'd be willing to try." Instead, the psychiatrist responds: "No, you really have to do it now."

The psychiatrist explicitly rejects Naomi's input about her own readiness: "No, you have to take this seriously. Feeling scared or stuck is just a weakness you need to overcome." This dismisses her self-knowledge and positions the psychiatrist as the sole authority on what she needs.

There is no shared decision-making, no co-construction of goals, and no genuine invitation for Naomi's perspective to shape the session. The power dynamic is entirely one-directional.

**Summary:**

The session is fundamentally authoritarian rather than collaborative. The psychiatrist makes unilateral decisions about treatment targets and prescribes solutions without genuine partnership with the client.

### 5. Autonomy Support
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Autonomy support requires respecting the client's right to choose and emphasizing their ownership of the change process. This session actively undermines autonomy through coercion, pressure, and the removal of choice.

The psychiatrist repeatedly frames change as non-negotiable: "You have to stop drinking completely," "You must cut back," "You have to take this seriously," "You have to be strong and quit drinking now." These statements eliminate choice and position compliance as mandatory rather than volitional.

When Naomi expresses her current inability to commit to complete abstinence, the psychiatrist responds with pressure rather than autonomy support: "No, you really have to do it now." This directly contradicts MI's principle that clients have the right to choose their own path, even if that path differs from the clinician's recommendation.

Most egregiously, the psychiatrist frames Naomi's hesitation as a character defect: "Feeling scared or stuck is just a weakness you need to overcome. You have to be strong and quit drinking now if you want to get better." This is coercive language that pressures compliance through shame and threat ("your anxiety will spiral out of control and you'll regret it later").

A autonomy-supportive approach would sound like: "This is your decision to make. I can share what research suggests about alcohol and anxiety, but ultimately, you get to decide what feels right for you and when you're ready to make changes. What are you thinking?"

Naomi's response—"I can't just quit drinking today—not like this. I want to get better, but I need more support and understanding first"—directly reflects the absence of autonomy support. She is explicitly stating her need for collaborative support rather than coercion, and this need goes unmet.

**Summary:**

The psychiatrist systematically undermines client autonomy through coercive language, pressure, and the removal of choice. The session communicates that compliance is mandatory rather than volitional.

### 6. Evocation
### **Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**

Evocation is the process of drawing out the client's own motivations, values, ideas, and resources rather than imposing them. This session demonstrates virtually no evocation and instead relies entirely on the clinician telling the client what to do.

Naomi provides multiple opportunities for evocation. She states: "I really do want to feel better and have less anxiety" and "I want to get better." These are clear expressions of desire for change. ## Recommendations

### 1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance from the Outset**
- **Action:** Begin sessions by asking the client what brought them in and what they want to work on, rather than imposing a predetermined agenda.
- **Example:** "Naomi, thanks for coming in today. I'd like to understand what's most important to you right now. What brought you here, and what would be most helpful to focus on?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, spend the first 10 minutes asking open-ended questions about the client's priorities before introducing any treatment recommendations. Practice listening without planning your response.

---

### 2. **Replace Directives with Reflective Listening**
- **Action:** When a client expresses ambivalence or resistance, reflect their statement back to them rather than arguing or prescribing.
- **Example:** Instead of "No, you really have to do it now," try: "It sounds like stopping drinking completely right now feels overwhelming, especially when you're already dealing with so much anxiety. Help me understand what you're most worried about."
- **Practice:** Record yourself in sessions and count how many times you use "you have to," "you must," or "you need to." Aim to replace each of these with a reflective listening statement. Practice with a colleague or supervisor.

---

### 3. **Validate Client Experience Rather Than Dismissing It**
- **Action:** When a client describes their experience, accept it as their truth rather than correcting or invalidating it.
- **Example:** Instead of "Drinking is clearly a problem, even if you don't want to admit it," try: "You mentioned that sometimes drinking helps you relax, but other times it makes you feel more stressed the next day. That's an important observation about how it affects you."
- **Practice:** In your next three sessions, identify moments where you're tempted to correct or dismiss the client's experience. Pause and instead ask a curious question: "Tell me more about that" or "How does that show up for you?"

---

### 4. **Explore the Function of Behavior Before Prescribing Change**
- **Action:** Understand why the client engages in the behavior (what need it meets) before recommending cessation. This allows for collaborative problem-solving around alternatives.
- **Example:** "You mentioned that drinking helps you relax sometimes. What is it about relaxing that's important to you right now? And what happens when you try to relax without drinking?"
- **Practice:** For any behavior you want to address, ask yourself: "What need does this behavior meet for the client?" Then ask the client this question directly before recommending change.

---

### 5. **Recognize and Amplify Change Talk**
- **Action:** Listen for statements indicating desire, ability, reasons, or commitment to change. When you hear them, pause and explore them further rather than moving past them.
- **Example:** When Naomi says "I really do want to feel better and have less anxiety," respond with: "You genuinely want to feel better. What would be different in your life if your anxiety improved? What would that make possible for you?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, identify every instance of change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment). Write them down. For each one, ask a follow-up question designed to deepen and strengthen that change talk.

---

### 6. **Soften Resistance Through Empathic Reflection, Not Confrontation**
- **Action:** When a client expresses resistance or sustain talk, respond with empathic reflection and exploration rather than argument or pressure.
- **Example:** Instead of "No, you have to stop making excuses," try: "On one hand, you can see how reducing drinking might help your anxiety. On the other hand, you're worried that quitting completely without a plan might feel too hard or isolating. Both of those concerns make sense. What would need to happen for you to feel ready?"
- **Practice:** Role-play with a colleague. Have them express resistance to a change goal. Practice responding with double-sided reflections and open-ended questions rather than confrontation. Record yourself and listen for any argumentative tone.

---

### 7. **Explicitly Respect Client Autonomy**
- **Action:** Communicate that the client has the right to choose their own path, even if it differs from your recommendation. Frame your role as providing information and support, not control.
- **Example:** "This is your decision to make. I can share what research suggests about alcohol and anxiety, but ultimately, you get to decide what feels right for you and when you're ready to make changes. What are you thinking?"
- **Practice:** In your next session, use the phrase "This is your decision" or "You get to choose" at least three times. Notice how the client responds to this autonomy support.

---

### 8. **Attend to Feedback About the Therapeutic Relationship**
- **Action:** When a client gives you feedback about how they're experiencing the session (e.g., "I don't like feeling pressured"), pause and respond with genuine curiosity and reflection rather than defensiveness.
- **Example:** When Naomi says "It feels like you're not listening to how scared or stuck I am," respond with: "I hear you saying that you don't feel heard right now, and that's important. I want to understand what you're experiencing. Tell me more about what you need from me."
- **Practice:** In your next session, if a client gives you any feedback about the relationship, pause and reflect it back. Ask what they need. Make a genuine effort to adjust your approach based on their feedback.

---

### 9. **Prioritize the Client's Stated Concerns**
- **Action:** When a client identifies a primary concern, honor that concern by exploring it first, rather than imposing your own agenda.
- **Example:** When Naomi repeatedly mentions anxiety as her primary concern, respond with: "I hear that anxiety is what's most affecting your life right now. Let's start there. Tell me more about how it shows up for you and how it's impacting your work and relationships."
- **Practice:** In your next session, ask the client "What's most important to you to work on?" and then structure the session around their answer, not your predetermined agenda.

---

- **Action:** Seek formal training in Motivational Interviewing (e.g., through MINT-certified trainers) and regular clinical supervision focused on MI competence.
- **Example:** Attend a 2-day MI workshop, then participate in monthly supervision where sessions are recorded and reviewed for MI fidelity.
- **Practice:** Use the MITI (Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity) coding system to assess your own sessions. Identify your lowest-scoring domains and focus deliberate practice on those areas.
