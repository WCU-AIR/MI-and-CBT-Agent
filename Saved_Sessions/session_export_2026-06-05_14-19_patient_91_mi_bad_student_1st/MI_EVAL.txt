Repeatedly uses dismissive language ("You really need to stop making excuses" used twice), pathologizes client's attempts to communicate lived experience. Psychiatrist clearly identifies goal but pursues it through repetitive, escalating pressure rather than strategic exploration. Repeats demand "You really must use your inhaler every day without fail" without modification or exploration of barriers. Direction present but poorly executed through rigid repetition rather than strategic guidance.

### Collaboration

**Comments:** Entirely authoritarian and expert-driven. Opening statement explicitly rejects collaboration: "I'm going to tell you exactly what you need to do to improve." Proceeds with interrogative yes/no questions providing no space for client input to shape conversation. When client attempts to introduce relevant information (financial stress, family stress, dog attachment), psychiatrist dismisses it: "No, the dog is not the issue here." Zero shared decision-making. Zero seeking of client input. Psychiatrist assumes exclusive expert role. Creates adversarial rather than collaborative dynamic.

Uses controlling language throughout: "You really must," "You have to," "You need to," "Using your inhaler daily is not optional," "No, you don't get to decide when you're ready," "No, you don't get to decide what's simple or not." These statements communicate client has no choice and preferences are irrelevant. Communicates "Your answer must be yes, or you are failing." Strips away client's right to choose. Controlling approach likely increases client resistance rather than supporting autonomous decision-making.

Psychiatrist tells client what he "must" do, what will happen if he doesn't comply ("you'll end up in the hospital"), and what is/isn't important ("the dog isn't the problem here; your asthma is"). Zero curiosity about client's own reasons for change, values, ideas, or resources. Client provides change talk that could be evoked and amplified ("I know my asthma's a problem," "I want to get better," "I'm not saying no forever," "I'm willing to think about using it more") but psychiatrist does not acknowledge, explore, or reinforce any of it. Instead focuses on client's ambivalence and sustain talk, treating it as resistance to overcome through pressure. No open-ended questions about motivation, values, or confidence. Client provides multiple instances of change talk that could be amplified: "I know my asthma's a problem" (recognition of problem), "I want to get better" (desire for change), "I'm willing to think about using it more" (willingness), "I'm not saying no forever" (openness to future change). ### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Attempts to soften sustain talk through fear-based motivation ("you'll end up in the hospital") but primarily uses confrontation and argument, which likely strengthens sustain talk. Client provides sustain talk throughout ("It's a bit of an inconvenience," "Finances is a problem," "The dog has been there before my wife," "Everything feels all over the place," "It's not that simple to just flip a switch," "I'm already overwhelmed"). Psychiatrist's response is confrontational and escalating rather than using double-sided reflections or reframing. Argues directly against client's concerns: "No, the dog is not the issue here. Your asthma is the problem." When client says "it's not that simple," psychiatrist responds: "No, you don't get to decide what's simple or not." This is argumentative and dismissive, typically strengthening sustain talk. Fear-based motivation can sometimes redirect sustain talk but often backfires by increasing defensiveness. More skillful approach would involve double-sided reflections, reframing, and strategic redirection. Partially effective but not skillfully executed and violates MI spirit.

## Overall Clinical Summary

**Overall Session Score: 1.1/5**

**MI Competency Assessment:** Minimal MI competency demonstrated. Session characterized by directive advice-giving, confrontation, dismissal of client perspective, and coercive yes/no questioning—all antithetical to MI spirit and associated with worse outcomes.

**Key Strengths:**
- Clear identification of target behavior (daily inhaler use)
- Persistence on focus regarding asthma management
- Recognition of health urgency

**Critical Areas for Improvement:**
1. Fundamental reframing from expert/authority to partner/collaborator
2. Development of reflective listening skills, especially complex reflections
3. Replacement of closed yes/no questions with open-ended exploration
4. Exploration and validation of real barriers rather than dismissal
5. Evocation of client's own motivation rather than demanding compliance
6. Autonomy-supportive language replacing controlling language
7. Addressing ambivalence with double-sided reflections, not confrontation
8. Focus on intrinsic motivation rather than fear-based motivation
9. Collaborative problem-solving skills development

**Impact on Client Engagement:** This session is likely to **decrease** Marcus's motivation for change and **damage** the therapeutic relationship. By session end, client is more defensive, more focused on justifying position, and less likely to engage in collaborative problem-solving. Psychiatrist has communicated that client's concerns, autonomy, and perspective don't matter. This violates core MI principle of evoking intrinsic motivation and is associated with worse health outcomes.

**Recommendation:** Clinician should not continue conducting MI sessions without formal training and supervision. Current approach likely causes harm by damaging therapeutic relationship and decreasing client motivation for change. Immediate enrollment in formal MI training (e.g., through MINT—Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers) strongly recommended, followed by ongoing clinical supervision.
