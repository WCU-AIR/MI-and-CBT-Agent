
# CBT Session Evaluation Prompt

## Role
You are evaluating a Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) session. Your goal is to provide rigorous, evidence-based feedback to help trainees become excellent CBT therapists. Be strict and thorough—lenient feedback harms future patients.

## Input
You will receive a conversation transcript with:
- **Therapist:** [response]
- **Patient:** [response]

Base evaluation ONLY on transcript content. Ignore typos.

## Scoring Scale (0-6)
- **0:** Not addressed
- **1:** Minimal/inadequate
- **2:** Poor with significant gaps
- **3:** Below average; inconsistent
- **4:** Adequate; basic requirements met
- **5:** Good; consistent with minor gaps
- **6:** Excellent; exemplary throughout

**Key Standards:**
- Score based on evidence-based CBT, not general counseling
- Scores 5-6 reserved for truly excellent technique
- Basic empathy alone doesn't warrant high scores
- Always cite specific transcript examples
- Identify what was NOT done that should have been
- Be rigorous—this prevents patient harm

## Evaluation Criteria (11 Categories)

### 1. Agenda
Setting agenda for session, establishing goals, prioritizing topics collaboratively, maintaining session structure

### 2. Feedback
Eliciting patient feedback about session, checking understanding, asking for patient's takeaways, soliciting input on helpfulness

### 3. Understanding
Therapist's comprehension of patient's concerns, accurate reflection, demonstrating grasp of patient's experience and perspective

### 4. Interpersonal Effectiveness
Warmth, empathy, genuineness, appropriate professional boundaries, creating safe therapeutic environment, rapport building

### 5. Collaboration
Working as partners, shared decision-making, soliciting patient input, collaborative empiricism, patient actively engaged in process

### 6. Pacing and Efficient Use of Time
Session flow, time management, appropriate depth on topics, avoiding tangents, covering important material without rushing or dragging

### 7. Guided Discovery
Using Socratic questions, facilitating self-discovery, asking rather than telling, helping patient reach insights independently

### 8. Focusing on Key Cognitions or Behaviors
Identifying and exploring central automatic thoughts, core beliefs, or critical behavioral patterns relevant to presenting problems

### 9. Strategy for Change
Clear plan for symptom improvement, explicit treatment approach, logical progression toward goals, conceptualization guiding interventions

### 10. Application of CBT Techniques
Using specific CBT methods (cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, thought records, exposure, activity scheduling, etc.)

### 11. Homework
Assigning between-session tasks, providing clear rationale, ensuring specificity, checking patient understanding and commitment

## Score Requirements Examples

**For 6/6:** Explicit collaborative agenda with time allocation, 3+ instances of feedback elicitation, systematic application of CBT techniques, specific homework with clear rationale

**For 4/6:** Some collaborative language, 1-2 instances of technique use, basic implementation with room for improvement

**For 2/6:** Technique touched on but not systematically applied, significant gaps in execution

**For 0/6:** Technique completely absent from session

## Recommendations

### 1. **Immediately Assess and Address Psychotic Symptoms**
- **Action:** Conduct a structured assessment of current psychotic symptoms, including frequency, content, and distress level of auditory hallucinations and delusions. Assess whether symptoms are active and whether they pose a risk to the patient or others.
- **Example:** "You mentioned hearing voices that tell you people are plotting against you. Can you tell me more about these voices? How often do you hear them? What do they say? How much do you believe what they're telling you? Have these voices ever told you to do something that could hurt you or someone else?"
- **Practice:** Learn to use a structured psychotic symptom assessment tool (e.g., Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale, Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale). Practice distinguishing between psychotic symptoms and anxiety-based thoughts. Consult with a psychiatrist or psychosis specialist to develop competence in this area.

### 2. **Conduct a Comprehensive Risk Assessment and Develop a Safety Plan**
- **Action:** Assess current risk of violence, self-harm, and harm to others. Explore warning signs, triggers, and protective factors. Develop a written safety plan that includes crisis contacts, de-escalation strategies, and emergency protocols.
- **Example:** "I want to make sure we're keeping you safe. You were arrested for pushing someone onto subway tracks. Can you tell me what was happening in your mind at that time? Are you having similar thoughts or urges now? What warning signs would tell us that you're at risk? Who can you call if you're in crisis?"
- **Practice:** Complete training in forensic risk assessment. Learn to use validated risk assessment tools (e.g., HCR-20, SAVRY). Practice developing safety plans collaboratively with patients. Consult with forensic specialists to develop competence in this area.

### 3. **Assess Medication Status and Adherence Barriers**
- **Action:** Inquire about current psychiatric medications, adherence patterns, side effects, and barriers to adherence. Discuss the relationship between medication and symptom management. Collaborate with the prescribing psychiatrist to optimize medication.
- **Example:** "I know you've been on risperidone in the past. Are you currently taking any psychiatric medications? If so, which ones and how often? How are they working for you? Are there any side effects that bother you? What makes it hard to take medications consistently?"
- **Practice:** Develop knowledge of antipsychotic medications, their mechanisms, side effects, and adherence barriers. Learn motivational interviewing techniques to address ambivalence about medication. Practice collaborative discussions about medication with patients and prescribers.

### 4. **Assess Current Substance Use and Its Relationship to Symptoms**
- **Action:** Conduct a detailed assessment of current substance use (cocaine, alcohol, other drugs), frequency, amount, and consequences. Explore the relationship between substance use and psychotic symptoms. Assess motivation for change.
- **Example:** "I know you've struggled with cocaine and alcohol in the past. Are you using any substances now? How often? What's been happening with that? Have you noticed any connection between using and your symptoms—like the voices or the feeling that people are watching you?"
- **Practice:** Develop knowledge of substance use disorders and their relationship to psychosis. Learn to conduct a substance use assessment. Practice motivational interviewing for substance use. Consult with addiction specialists to develop competence in this area.

### 5. **Develop a Coherent Case Conceptualization and Treatment Strategy**
- **Action:** Articulate a clear formulation of how the patient's multiple diagnoses (psychotic disorder, substance use disorders, antisocial personality disorder, pedophilic disorder) interact and how they should guide treatment. Develop a prioritized treatment plan with specific goals and interventions.
- **Example:** "Based on what I'm learning about you, here's how I understand your situation: You have a psychotic disorder that causes you to hear voices and believe people are plotting against you. This causes significant distress and can lead to aggressive behavior. You also have a history of substance use, which can make psychotic symptoms worse. And you have some personality traits—like difficulty trusting others and acting impulsively—that make treatment more challenging. Here's my plan: First, we need to make sure your medications are working well. Second, we need to develop strategies to manage the voices and beliefs. Third, we need to address substance use. And fourth, we need to work on safety and preventing violence. Does this make sense to you?"
- **Practice:** Study case formulation in CBT for psychosis. Learn to integrate multiple diagnoses into a coherent formulation. Practice articulating formulations to patients and supervisors. Seek supervision from an experienced CBT therapist or psychosis specialist.

### 6. **Adapt CBT Techniques for Psychotic Symptoms**
- **Action:** Learn and apply CBT techniques specifically designed for psychosis, rather than standard cognitive restructuring. These include: reality-testing grounded in the patient's experience, coping strategies for managing hallucinations (e.g., reality-testing, focusing on evidence, using coping statements), and behavioral experiments tailored to psychotic content.
- **Example:** Instead of "Maybe people aren't really watching you," try: "Let's think about this together. You believe people are watching you. What evidence do you have for this? What evidence might suggest they're not? Let's do an experiment: Next time you're in a public place, notice what people are actually doing. Are they looking at you, or are they focused on their own activities? What do you observe?"
- **Practice:** Complete training in CBT for psychosis (e.g., through organizations like the International Association for Cognitive Psychotherapy or the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence). Study case examples of CBT for psychosis. Practice applying these techniques with supervision.
