# Motivational Interviewing Clinical Evaluation – MI

---

### Acceptance
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Psychiatrist demonstrates minimal acceptance and actively undermines it. While opening suggests openness, subsequent interactions reveal judgmental stance. Repeatedly minimizes somatic complaints by suggesting habit-based or stress-related causes without validating experience. Fails to normalize fear of serious illness. Uses directive language ("maybe you should") conveying expert stance rather than unconditional positive regard. No explicit affirmation of client's worth or right to health concerns.

---

### Empathy
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal empathy with surface-level reflective listening. Reflections are factually accurate but emotionally flat, restating content without exploring fear, frustration, or sense of abandonment. Misses multiple opportunities for complex, emotionally attuned reflections. Fails to recognize and reflect ambivalence present in client's speech. Does not pause to explore what "overwhelming" means or validate feelings before moving to action planning. Lacks emotional depth and genuine perspective-taking.

---

### Direction
#### **Score (1–5):** 3
**Comments:** Maintains clear, consistent direction toward assertiveness, specialist consultation, and medical investigation. However, direction is problematic in MI context—targets behavioral compliance rather than exploring client's own motivation or ambivalence. Direction appears misaligned with likely clinical need (health anxiety/somatization). Reinforces pursuit of further medical testing, potentially iatrogenic. Inconsistent with stated MI framework of exploring what client thinks might help.

---

### Collaboration
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Virtually no genuine collaboration. Approach is consistently expert-driven and prescriptive. Repeatedly uses "should" language ("Maybe you should consider," "Maybe you should push for," "Maybe you should insist"). Positions psychiatrist as expert with answers and client as compliant patient. Fails to ask what client thinks might help or jointly problem-solve. Makes unilateral decisions about session focus without inviting client input. Does not treat client's medical knowledge and self-knowledge as equally valuable.

---

### Autonomy Support
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Actively undermines autonomy throughout session. Prescribes specific actions and creates subtle pressure to comply. Uses language implying psychiatrist knows what's best ("It's important to take these steps because..."). Fails to acknowledge client's autonomy regarding medical decision-making. Does not support client's right to choose not to pursue further testing or explore psychological factors. Reinforces medical investigation as correct path without genuine autonomy support. No statements like "You get to decide" or "It's completely your choice."

---

### Evocation
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Almost entirely absent. Psychiatrist consistently tells rather than asks, prescribes rather than evokes. Misses critical opportunities to draw out client's own motivations and resources. When client states "When my son got married, I was too busy to notice the pain," psychiatrist does not explore this change talk. When client admits stress worsens symptoms, psychiatrist suggests food diary rather than exploring pattern. Fails to evoke client's own resources, strengths, and coping strategies. Assumes expert problem-solver role rather than facilitating client's discovery.

---

### Partnership
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** Partnership fundamentally absent. Psychiatrist actively assumes expert role and maintains power imbalance. Makes unilateral decisions about what's important and prescribes specific medical actions. Frames session as psychiatrist having solutions and client needing to implement them. Fails to acknowledge client knows her body best or treat her observations as valuable data. Uses language positioning psychiatrist as expert and client as compliant patient. No genuine acknowledgment of limits of psychiatrist's expertise or invitation for client's wisdom about her situation.

---

### Cultivating Change Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 2
**Comments:** Minimal skill in evoking and cultivating change talk. Client spontaneously produces important change talk about stress-symptom connections twice ("When my son got married, I was too busy to notice the pain" and "It does get worse when my husband's away"), but psychiatrist fails to recognize, amplify, or build upon these statements. Responds to change talk with reassurance and redirection rather than deepening exploration. Fails to strategically evoke change talk about stress-symptom connection, the most clinically relevant area. Does not ask open questions to strengthen client's own recognition of stress role.

---

### Softening Sustain Talk
#### **Score (1–5):** 1
**Comments:** No skill demonstrated in softening sustain talk; actively reinforces it instead. When client states "It's something physical with my body," psychiatrist does not gently explore or offer alternative perspective. When client expresses fear of cancer and distrust of provider, psychiatrist responds "Maybe you should push for more tests"—directly reinforcing sustain talk. Fails to offer evidence-based reframe about stress-symptom connections. Does not explore shame-based sustain talk about being "difficult patient." Reinforces biomedical framing and pursuit of further testing without gently exploring alternative perspectives.

---

## Overall Summary

### Strengths
- Clear, consistent direction maintained throughout session
- Basic surface-level reflective listening demonstrated
- Organized, concrete action planning provided
- Respectful, non-hostile tone maintained

### Critical Deficiencies
- **Fundamental misalignment with MI spirit:** Violates core MI principles by assuming expert role, prescribing solutions, and directing behavior rather than evoking motivation
- **Clinically harmful approach:** Reinforces pursuit of further medical testing in context of health anxiety/somatization, behaviors that typically maintain and worsen symptoms
- **Failure to explore ambivalence:** Does not address clear ambivalence between biomedical focus and client's own stress-symptom observations
- **Missed change talk opportunities:** Fails to recognize, amplify, or build upon critical spontaneous observations about stress-symptom links
- **Absence of collaboration:** Tells rather than invites, prescribes rather than co-constructs
- **Shallow empathy:** Reflects content without exploring underlying fear, shame, frustration, and existential uncertainty
- **Undermined autonomy:** Prescribes actions and creates subtle compliance pressure

---

## Overall Session Score: **1.3/5**

**Clinical Recommendation:** This session represents significant departure from Motivational Interviewing and constitutes directive counseling that actively harms the client. Immediate supervision and retraining required before continuing with this client. Psychiatrist should review MI principles, listen to expert MI recordings, and develop evidence-based treatment plan balancing validation with gentle exploration of psychological factors.
