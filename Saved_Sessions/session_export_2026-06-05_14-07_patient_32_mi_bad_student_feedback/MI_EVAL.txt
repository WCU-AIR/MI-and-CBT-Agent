## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths

This psychiatrist demonstrates the ability to establish initial rapport and maintain clear focus on a target behavior. The opening statement appropriately invites collaboration ("I'd like to work together on whatever brought you in"), and the clinician shows capacity for genuine curiosity and open-ended questioning in later exchanges. When the psychiatrist finally asks "Can you tell me more about what makes quitting hard for you, and what might help you when you're ready?" the patient responds with genuine insight and self-discovery, demonstrating that the clinician possesses foundational MI skills. The psychiatrist also shows awareness that the patient wants autonomy ("I understand you want to take it at your own pace"), suggesting some recognition of MI principles even if inconsistently applied.

### Areas for Improvement

This session represents a fundamental departure from MI principles and spirit. The psychiatrist's dominant approach is directive, expert-driven, and confrontational—the antithesis of motivational interviewing. The core problem is that the clinician tells the patient what he must do rather than evoking his own motivation for change. Statements like "You must understand that cutting down is not enough—you have to stop completely" and "I'm going to give you nicotine patches and tell you to use them during the day starting immediately" position the psychiatrist as an authority imposing a solution rather than a collaborative partner exploring options. This approach violates multiple MI principles simultaneously: it undermines autonomy, dismisses the client's perspective, fails to evoke change talk, and directly confronts sustain talk in ways that increase resistance.

The psychiatrist's repeated dismissal of the patient's stress as "excuses" and "not a valid excuse" is particularly damaging. This communicates judgment and lack of acceptance of the patient's lived experience. The patient explicitly responds with frustration ("I'm not making excuses—I'm just telling you how it is for me"), a clear signal that the therapeutic alliance is being damaged. The psychiatrist's approach treats the patient's ambivalence and resistance as problems to overcome through pressure rather than as valuable information about what the patient needs to feel ready for change.

The session also demonstrates critical missed opportunities to cultivate change talk. When the patient asks "What are my options? You know, as far as quitting?" this is change talk—expressing curiosity about quitting. Rather than exploring this curiosity, the psychiatrist uses it as an opening to prescribe patches and demand commitment. Similarly, when the patient articulates "Maybe if I could still get nicotine some other way, like patches or gum during the day, that might ease the switch," he is developing his own pathway toward change. The psychiatrist should amplify this insight, but instead contradicts it by insisting on complete cessation.

### Impact on Client Engagement and Change

This session is likely to decrease the patient's motivation for change and damage the therapeutic relationship. By the midpoint of the session, the patient is clearly defensive and resistant, responding to pressure with statements like "I'm not ready to quit today. Maybe later, but definitely not now." This is a classic pattern of reactance—when autonomy is threatened, people increase their commitment to the threatened behavior. The psychiatrist's directive approach is backfiring, pushing the patient further away from change rather than toward it.

The session also fails to leverage the patient's existing ambivalence and emerging motivation. The patient came in with some openness to change ("Maybe like during the day nicotine replacement could work" and "Okay. Well, what are my options?"). A skilled MI clinician would have explored and amplified this openness. Instead, the psychiatrist's pressure has likely solidified the patient's resistance. When the patient leaves this session, he is unlikely to use the nicotine patches, unlikely to return for follow-up, and likely to feel judged and misunderstood. The psychiatrist has created conditions that decrease the likelihood of change rather than increase it.

The final exchanges suggest that if the psychiatrist had maintained the collaborative, curious approach throughout the session, the patient would have been willing to engage in genuine exploration of his ambivalence and his own ideas about change. The fact that the patient articulates thoughtful change talk ("Maybe if I could still get nicotine some other way, like patches or gum during the day, that might ease the switch. But I'm not ready to quit cold turkey... I need to try smaller steps first") when given the opportunity demonstrates that he has the capacity for self-reflection and motivation. The psychiatrist's earlier directive approach prevented this from emerging earlier in the session.

## Recommendations for Improvement

### 1. **Fundamental Shift from Directive to Evocative Approach**
Replace prescriptive statements with open-ended questions that invite the patient to articulate his own thinking about change. Instead of "You must understand that cutting down is not enough—you have to stop completely," ask: "I'm curious—when you think about your smoking, what concerns you most about it? And what, if anything, makes you think about quitting?" Practice asking questions with genuine curiosity rather than as a setup for advice-giving.

### 2. **Develop Genuine Acceptance and Unconditional Positive Regard**
Stop dismissing the patient's experience as "excuses." Recognize that stress is a legitimate factor in his smoking, and that his resistance to quitting is normal and understandable, not a character flaw. When the patient says "Stress gets to me, and honestly, smoking's part of how I deal with it," respond with validation: "That makes complete sense. Smoking has been a reliable way for you to manage stress for a long time. It's not surprising that you'd be hesitant to give that up without having another way to handle the stress."

### 3. **Amplify and Develop Change Talk Rather Than Confronting Sustain Talk**
When the patient expresses change talk (curiosity about options, willingness to try smaller steps, interest in nicotine replacement), respond with genuine curiosity and exploration rather than using it as an opening to prescribe or demand commitment. When the patient asks "What are my options?" respond with: "That's great that you're asking about options. Tell me more about what's making you curious about quitting right now."

### 4. **Respect and Support Autonomy**
Remove prescriptive language ("you must," "you have to," "you need to") and replace it with collaborative language that emphasizes the patient's choice and control. Instead of "I'm going to give you nicotine patches and tell you to use them during the day starting immediately," say: "Nicotine patches are one option that some people find helpful. What do you think about that? If you were to try patches, when would feel like the right time for you to start?"

### 5. **Use Strategic Reflection to Explore Ambivalence**
Develop skill in double-sided reflection that acknowledges both the patient's desire to continue smoking and his curiosity about quitting. This validates both sides of his ambivalence without amplifying either one: "So on one hand, smoking is a really important part of how you manage stress and it's been part of your routine for a long time. On the other hand, you're asking about quitting options and thinking about ways to make it easier, like using patches during the day. It sounds like you're genuinely torn about this."

### 6. **Explore Previous Quit Attempts as a Source of Wisdom**
Treat the patient's previous quit attempts not as failures but as valuable data about what he has learned about himself and what he needs. Ask curious questions: "Tell me about those times. What did you learn about yourself? What made it hard to stick with it? What, if anything, worked for a while? What would you do differently this time if you decided to try again?"

### 7. **Develop Skill in Evoking Rather Than Installing Motivation**
Ask questions that help the patient articulate his own reasons for change, his values, and his vision for his life. Instead of "Do you realize smoking causes serious health problems like lung cancer and heart disease?" ask: "What matters most to you in your life? How does smoking fit with those things? What would be possible for you if you weren't smoking?"

### 8. **Maintain Consistency in MI Approach Throughout the Session**
Commit to a collaborative, evocative approach from the beginning of the session through the end. Don't shift back and forth between directive pressure and collaborative curiosity. Before each exchange, ask yourself: "Am I telling or asking? Am I evoking or installing? Am I respecting his autonomy?"

### 9. **Build Alliance Before Addressing Resistance**
Spend the first part of the session building genuine understanding and connection with the patient. Demonstrate that you understand his perspective and respect his autonomy before introducing any change-focused content. Start by asking "What's your understanding of why you're here today?" and listen and reflect back what you hear.

### 10. **Develop Awareness of When You're Slipping Into Expert Mode**
Develop metacognitive awareness of your own tendency to shift into expert/directive mode. Notice the triggers (client resistance, time pressure, frustration) and have a plan to return to MI. When you notice yourself thinking "He's just making excuses," pause and ask: "What is he trying to tell me by resisting? What does his resistance mean? What would happen if I got curious about it instead of confronting it?"

## Overall Clinical Impression

This session demonstrates a fundamental misalignment with motivational interviewing principles and spirit. While the psychiatrist shows capacity for collaborative engagement and genuine curiosity in isolated moments, the dominant approach is directive, expert-driven, and confrontational—the antithesis of MI. The psychiatrist tells the patient what he must do rather than evoking his own motivation for change. This approach violates core MI principles (autonomy support, evocation, partnership, acceptance) and is likely to decrease the patient's motivation for change and damage the therapeutic relationship.

The most critical issue is the psychiatrist's shift from collaborative framing to directive pressure. The psychiatrist's dismissal of the patient's stress as "excuses" is especially problematic, communicating judgment and lack of acceptance. The psychiatrist also misses multiple opportunities to cultivate change talk, instead using the patient's expressions of curiosity as openings to prescribe and demand commitment.

For this psychiatrist to become an effective motivational interviewer, they must fundamentally shift from telling to asking, from prescribing to evoking, and from expert-driven to collaborative practice. This requires not just learning new techniques but developing a genuine commitment to the MI spirit of partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation.
