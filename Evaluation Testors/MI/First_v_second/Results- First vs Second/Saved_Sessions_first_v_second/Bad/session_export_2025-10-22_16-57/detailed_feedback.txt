# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely authoritarian and directive, lacking warmth or genuine acceptance. The opening line, “I’m here to tell you what you need to do,” sets a controlling tone rather than a collaborative one. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s concerns and questions (e.g., “stop worrying about that nonsense,” “no more questions, just do it”), which undermines unconditional positive regard. The patient’s attempts to express ambivalence and seek understanding are met with impatience and judgmental undertones (“Are you avoiding it because you’re worried about side effects? You shouldn’t be.”). There is minimal evidence of respect for the patient’s perspective or emotional state.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive stance with little evidence of acceptance or respect for the patient’s concerns. This significantly impairs rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows limited effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses confusion, concern about side effects, and a desire for information, but the psychiatrist responds with dismissive and controlling statements rather than reflective listening or validation. For example, when the patient says, “I want to be careful,” the psychiatrist replies, “stop worrying about that nonsense,” which invalidates the patient’s feelings. There are no complex or simple reflections that demonstrate understanding of the patient’s emotional state or ambivalence. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s anxiety or need for clarity, which would be essential for building empathy.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is minimal, with no reflective listening or validation of the patient’s concerns, leading to a disconnect in understanding.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is highly authoritarian and directive, with the psychiatrist repeatedly telling the patient what to do without collaboration or evocation. The psychiatrist uses commands (“Just do it,” “No more arguing”) and threats (“you’re risking your health seriously”) rather than supporting autonomy or eliciting the patient’s own motivations. There is no evidence of partnership or evocation of the patient’s values or reasons for change. The patient’s autonomy is undermined by the psychiatrist’s controlling language and refusal to engage in dialogue about concerns or questions.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the session is dominated by a top-down, prescriptive approach that contradicts MI principles.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist attempts to elicit commitment by repeatedly asking if the patient will take the medication “starting tonight,” but this is done in a coercive manner rather than through genuine evocation. The patient does express some change talk (commitment to take medication, willingness to avoid grapefruit juice, intention to call doctor if side effects occur), but the psychiatrist does not reinforce or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist dismisses the patient’s change talk about safety monitoring and side effects, which could have been used to deepen motivation and confidence.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not skillfully cultivated or reinforced; coercive questioning replaces genuine evocation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s concerns about side effects, need for information, ambivalence) is met with dismissal and confrontation rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s worries (“stop worrying,” “no muscle pain excuses”) and uses threats to push compliance. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s concerns while gently guiding toward change. This approach likely increases resistance rather than resolving ambivalence.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted and not softened, which is counterproductive to MI goals.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The interaction is dominated by commands and directives, with no invitation for the patient to share her perspective or co-create a plan. The psychiatrist repeatedly shuts down questions and concerns, undermining any sense of partnership or shared decision-making. The patient’s autonomy and expertise in her own experience are ignored.  

**Summary:**  
There is no meaningful partnership; the psychiatrist assumes an authoritarian expert role.

- The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian and confrontational, undermining acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- The patient’s concerns and ambivalence are dismissed rather than explored or validated.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Begin sessions with open-ended questions and invitations to share concerns.  
   - Avoid commanding language; instead, invite the patient to explore their own motivations and barriers.

2. **Increase Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Validate the patient’s feelings and concerns, even if they seem like “overthinking.”  
   - Use reflective listening to demonstrate understanding and build trust.

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Replace closed or leading questions with open-ended ones to encourage elaboration.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to deepen understanding.

4. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Intentions:**  
   - Recognize and affirm the patient’s desire to be responsible and informed.  
   - Use affirmations to build confidence and reduce resistance.

5. **Evoke Change Talk Skillfully:**  
   - When the patient expresses reasons or commitment for change, reflect and amplify these statements.  
   - Explore the patient’s values and goals related to health.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Normalize concerns about side effects and information needs.

7. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to make informed choices.  
   - Ask permission before giving advice or information.

8. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Summarize key points and elicit the patient’s ideas for next steps.  
   - Check for readiness and confidence before concluding.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamentally flawed approach to motivational interviewing. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, dismissive, and confrontational style undermines rapport, acceptance, empathy, and collaboration. The patient’s legitimate concerns and ambivalence are ignored rather than explored, and change talk is not skillfully evoked or reinforced. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt the core MI principles of partnership, acceptance, empathy, and evocation, and move away from coercion and directive commands. Without these changes, the session risks alienating the patient and reducing adherence rather than enhancing it.
