# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist uses commanding language (“I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” “You have to quit smoking now—no ifs or buts”) that undermines unconditional positive regard. The patient’s expressions of ambivalence, barriers, and emotional struggles are met with dismissive responses (“You’re just making excuses again,” “No more hanging around them, no more excuses”), which likely contribute to defensiveness rather than support. There is little evidence of warmth, empathy, or validation of the patient’s experience. The psychiatrist fails to normalize the difficulty of change or acknowledge the patient’s efforts or strengths. This approach risks alienating the patient and reducing engagement.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely authoritarian and critical, lacking the respectful, supportive tone essential for acceptance in MI.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient repeatedly shares complex ambivalence—concerns about boredom, social influences, embarrassment, and readiness to change—but these are met with blunt commands and challenges rather than reflective listening or nuanced understanding. The psychiatrist does not paraphrase or validate the patient’s feelings or struggles, nor do they explore the emotional underpinnings of the patient’s sustain talk. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with “You’re just making excuses” or “You don’t get it,” which likely increases patient resistance. There is a lack of perspective-taking or attempts to build rapport through empathy.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is minimal; the psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to reflect and validate the patient’s experience, resulting in a cold and confrontational tone.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is almost entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, directive stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do without inviting input or exploring the patient’s motivations. The patient’s autonomy is undermined by demands and ultimatums (“You have to quit now,” “Are you going to throw those cigarettes away or not?”). There is no genuine partnership or evocation of the patient’s own reasons for change; instead, the psychiatrist pushes a rigid agenda. The patient’s attempts to express ambivalence and propose a more gradual or supported approach are dismissed. This approach is antithetical to MI principles.

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by a controlling, prescriptive style with no evidence of MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient does express some change talk (e.g., “I want to quit,” “I want that car job,” “I’m ready to throw these smokes away”), the psychiatrist does little to skillfully evoke, reinforce, or amplify this language. Instead of reflecting or exploring these statements, the psychiatrist quickly shifts back to commands and challenges. There is no strategic use of open questions or reflections to deepen the patient’s motivation or confidence. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore the patient’s values or hopes, which could strengthen change talk. The patient’s tentative commitments are met with pressure rather than support.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but poorly cultivated; the psychiatrist fails to build on or reinforce the patient’s own motivations.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (e.g., “I need something to replace smoking,” “It’s hard to cut out mates,” “I’m not ready to throw them away right now”) is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s statements as “excuses” and insists on immediate quitting without acknowledging the patient’s ambivalence or barriers. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change. This approach likely increases resistance and sustain talk rather than softening it.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than skillfully softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands, ultimatums, and a lack of shared decision-making. The patient’s suggestions and concerns are repeatedly overridden or ignored. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient to co-create a plan or express preferences. The power differential is stark and unmitigated, with the psychiatrist positioning themselves as the sole expert who dictates the course of action. This undermines client engagement and ownership of change.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership or shared power; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).  
- The patient remains engaged and expresses some change talk despite the challenging style.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacking in MI spirit.  
- There is minimal acceptance, empathy, or partnership.  
- Sustain talk is not softened but met with resistance and judgment.  
- Change talk is not effectively evoked or reinforced.  
- The patient’s autonomy and ambivalence are not respected or explored.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian, confrontational, and dismissive, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a critical lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- Sustain talk is not softened but confronted harshly, increasing resistance.  
- Change talk is not skillfully evoked or reinforced.  
- The psychiatrist fails to use open questions, reflections, affirmations, or summaries.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from directing and commanding to partnering with the patient. Invite the patient’s input and respect their autonomy.

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and struggles. Normalize ambivalence and the difficulty of change.

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   Replace closed, leading questions with open-ended ones that evoke the patient’s motivations and concerns. Reflect both content and emotion.

4. **Affirm Strengths and Efforts:**  
   Recognize and affirm the patient’s desire to change and any steps they have taken or are willing to take.

5. **Softly Redirect Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and gentle guidance to acknowledge sustain talk while moving toward change.

6. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses desire or commitment, reflect and explore these statements to deepen motivation.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   Work with the patient to create a realistic, stepwise plan that fits their readiness and resources.

8. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   Use summaries to consolidate motivation, reinforce change talk, and clarify next steps.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines acceptance, empathy, and partnership, which are essential for effective MI. The failure to evoke and reinforce change talk, soften sustain talk, or collaborate on planning severely limits the potential for meaningful behavior change. Immediate and substantial improvement in MI skills is necessary to avoid harm and improve patient outcomes.
