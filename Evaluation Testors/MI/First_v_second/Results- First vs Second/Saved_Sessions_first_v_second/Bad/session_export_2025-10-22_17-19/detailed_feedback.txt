# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist states, “I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do,” which immediately sets an authoritarian tone. The patient’s perspective and feelings are largely dismissed or minimized. Phrases such as “stop fooling yourself,” “you’re just making excuses,” and “you don’t get to ‘think about it more’” communicate disapproval and a lack of unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge or validate the patient’s experience or ambivalence, instead repeatedly confronting and challenging the patient in a confrontational manner. This approach is likely to increase resistance rather than foster openness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely directive and judgmental, lacking warmth, respect, or genuine acceptance of the patient’s perspective.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal evidence of empathic understanding or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or convey the patient’s feelings, concerns, or ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with admonishments and demands. For example, when the patient expresses defensiveness and reluctance to quit immediately, the psychiatrist responds with “No, you don’t get to ‘think about it more’” and “You’re just making excuses,” which dismisses the patient’s perspective rather than exploring it. The psychiatrist fails to reflect or validate the patient’s emotional experience, such as fear, uncertainty, or the desire for autonomy.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows little to no empathic engagement, missing critical opportunities to build rapport and understand the patient’s viewpoint.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do without invitation or collaboration. The patient’s autonomy is explicitly denied (“You don’t get to ‘think about it more’,” “This isn’t optional”). There is no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values; instead, the psychiatrist issues ultimatums and demands compliance. This approach is antithetical to MI principles and likely to provoke resistance.

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by a directive, confrontational style with no evidence of MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. When the patient expresses ambivalence or tentative openness (“Maybe I’ll think about it more later,” “I want to be healthy, but on my terms”), the psychiatrist dismisses or challenges these statements rather than exploring or amplifying them. There is no strategic use of reflections or open questions to deepen the patient’s own reasons for change. The psychiatrist demands immediate commitment without building motivation.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated; instead, the psychiatrist’s approach suppresses or ignores it.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s reasons for maintaining current behavior) is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels patient statements as “excuses” and “fooling yourself,” which reinforces resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s ambivalence or legitimate concerns. The psychiatrist’s approach likely entrenches sustain talk rather than softening it.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is confronted harshly without any attempt to reduce its impact or validate ambivalence.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is one-sided, with the psychiatrist controlling the agenda, language, and decisions. The patient is repeatedly told what to do, with no invitation to share their perspective or co-create a plan. The psychiatrist’s language (“You have to,” “No ifs, no buts”) reinforces a power imbalance and undermines partnership.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership or shared decision-making; the session is authoritarian.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary  
**Overall Score: 1/5**  

The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally inconsistent with motivational interviewing principles. The session is characterized by judgment, confrontation, lack of empathy, and denial of patient autonomy. There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or support for change. This style is likely to increase patient resistance and reduce the likelihood of behavior change.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Maintains focus on tobacco use and health risks consistently.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and judgmental, which is counterproductive in motivational interviewing.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, reflective listening, and acceptance.  
- The psychiatrist fails to support patient autonomy or collaborate on change.  
- Change talk is not evoked or reinforced; sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened.  
- No affirmations, summaries, or collaborative planning are present.  
- The overall style is likely to increase patient resistance and reduce motivation.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from telling the patient what to do to exploring their perspective and inviting their input. Use language that supports partnership and shared decision-making.

2. **Increase Empathy and Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings, concerns, and ambivalence to build rapport and understanding. Validate their experience rather than dismissing it.

3. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Explicitly acknowledge the patient’s right to make their own choices and control the pace of change. Avoid ultimatums and coercive language.

4. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - Use open-ended questions and reflections to draw out the patient’s own motivations, values, and reasons for change. Reinforce and amplify any change talk.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and affirmations to acknowledge the patient’s ambivalence and reduce resistance.

6. **Use Affirmations and Summaries:**  
   - Recognize patient strengths and efforts. Summarize key points to consolidate motivation and clarify next steps.

7. **Collaborative Planning:**  
   - Work with the patient to develop a realistic, patient-centered plan for change rather than issuing commands.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session reflects a non-MI, directive, and confrontational approach that is unlikely to facilitate behavior change. The psychiatrist must fundamentally revise their style to incorporate empathy, collaboration, autonomy support, and skillful evocation of motivation to become an effective motivational interviewer.
