# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation – Asthma Management Session with Maria Alvarez

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently demonstrates a judgmental, authoritarian, and directive stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“Today we’re going to get straight to the point... I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do”—the psychiatrist dismisses the patient’s autonomy and perspective. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s concerns and emotions are repeatedly ignored or invalidated, and the psychiatrist uses language that is confrontational (“You have to stop doubting,” “Enough with the stalling,” “This isn’t optional”). This approach clearly undermines acceptance and creates resistance rather than rapport.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate respect or acceptance, instead adopting a harsh, directive, and dismissive tone that alienates the patient.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no evidence of empathic understanding or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or validate the patient’s fears, skepticism, or time constraints. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts or overrides the patient’s expressions of concern with commands and admonishments. For example, when the patient expresses worry about inhaler side effects, the psychiatrist responds with “You have to stop doubting” and “You need to just start using it,” rather than exploring or acknowledging the patient’s perspective. The patient’s frustration and defensiveness escalate, indicating a lack of empathic attunement.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows no genuine effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, missing critical opportunities to build rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely devoid of the MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly issuing ultimatums and demands (“You have to make time,” “This isn’t optional,” “Stop making excuses”). There is no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values, no support for autonomy, and no partnership. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s subtle ambivalence and instead pushes for compliance. This approach is antithetical to MI principles and likely to increase resistance.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is directive, coercive, and controlling, completely lacking the collaborative and autonomy-supportive spirit essential to MI.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce any change talk. The patient offers minimal implicit change talk (e.g., “I want to be healthier,” “Maybe later”), but the psychiatrist does not explore or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with commands and threats, which likely suppresses any emerging motivation. There are no open-ended questions or reflections designed to elicit the patient’s own reasons for change or confidence. The psychiatrist’s approach is more likely to provoke sustain talk and resistance than to cultivate change talk.  

**Summary:**  
No effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk; missed opportunities to explore and amplify patient motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften or skillfully respond to sustain talk. The patient expresses sustain talk frequently—skepticism about inhalers, concerns about side effects, time constraints, and frustration—but the psychiatrist responds with confrontation, dismissal, and pressure. There are no double-sided reflections, no normalization of ambivalence, and no attempts to validate the patient’s concerns while gently guiding toward change. Instead, the psychiatrist escalates conflict and resistance.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and pressure rather than softening or strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist repeatedly asserts control, issues ultimatums, and demands compliance. The patient is repeatedly told what to do without invitation or collaboration. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s requests for information and understanding, and does not negotiate or co-create a plan. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.  

**Summary:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with no genuine partnership or client-centered collaboration.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Begin sessions with warmth and respect. Avoid commands and ultimatums.  
   - Validate the patient’s feelings and concerns, even if you disagree.

2. **Practice Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Use complex reflections to explore ambivalence and underlying fears.

3. **Support Autonomy and Collaboration:**  
   - Invite the patient to share their perspective and preferences.  
   - Use language that emphasizes choice and control (“What do you think would work best for you?”).

4. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions to explore reasons, desires, and confidence for change.  
   - Amplify any statements favoring change.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Normalize concerns and fears as part of the change process.

6. **Avoid Confrontation and Pressure:**  
   - Refrain from issuing ultimatums or threats.  
   - Recognize that pushing too hard often increases resistance.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Summarize key points and check for understanding.  
   - Co-create achievable next steps that respect the patient’s readiness.
