# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently demonstrates a judgmental, confrontational, and directive stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“Today we’re going to get straight to the point… I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do”—the tone is authoritarian and dismissive of the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s expressed struggles and ambivalence, labeling her concerns as “excuses” and “hesitations.” There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s feelings and context are ignored or minimized, which likely contributes to her defensiveness and withdrawal. This is the opposite of acceptance and creates a hostile environment.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate respect or support, instead adopting a harsh, judgmental approach that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or reflect the patient’s feelings, concerns, or context. The patient expresses stress, fear, and ambivalence multiple times, but the psychiatrist responds with commands and accusations rather than reflective listening or validation. For example, when the patient says, “I’m worried about how hard total quitting would be right now with all the stress,” the psychiatrist replies with “No, you’re not seeing the big picture here,” dismissing her experience. The lack of empathy is glaring and likely contributes to the patient’s resistance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows no genuine effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, missing critical opportunities for reflective listening.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, directive stance, repeatedly telling the patient what she “has to” do and refusing to entertain any negotiation or patient input. Autonomy is explicitly denied (“This is not up for discussion,” “You don’t get to decide how this works”). There is no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values; instead, the psychiatrist imposes a rigid agenda. This approach is antithetical to MI principles and likely to provoke resistance rather than change.

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by control and persuasion, with no collaboration or respect for patient autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
While the patient expresses some change talk (e.g., “I want to quit,” “I’m trying”), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke, reinforce, or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses the patient’s tentative efforts and ambivalence, focusing solely on demands to quit immediately. There are no strategic reflections or questions designed to deepen the patient’s motivation or explore her reasons for change. The patient’s change talk is stifled rather than cultivated.

**Summary:**  
No effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk—patient’s expressions of difficulty, fear, and ambivalence—is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s concerns as “excuses” and “hesitations” and repeatedly insists on immediate cessation without acknowledging the legitimacy of the patient’s struggles. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that validate ambivalence while gently guiding toward change. This harsh approach likely entrenches sustain talk and resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and escalated rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist repeatedly overrides the patient’s voice, refuses to negotiate, and imposes a rigid agenda. The patient is treated as a passive recipient of orders rather than an active collaborator. The power differential is stark and unmitigated, which undermines trust and engagement.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance with no collaboration or respect for patient expertise.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary  
**Overall Score: 1/5**  
This session exemplifies a complete failure to apply motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s approach is directive, confrontational, and dismissive, which alienates the patient and undermines motivation. There is no acceptance, empathy, collaboration, or skillful handling of ambivalence. The patient’s change talk is ignored, and sustain talk is harshly confronted. This style is likely to increase resistance and reduce the likelihood of behavior change.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian, confrontational, and dismissive, which is counterproductive.  
- There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- No use of reflective listening, affirmations, or open questions.  
- Patient autonomy is denied repeatedly, increasing resistance.  
- Change talk is not cultivated; sustain talk is harshly confronted.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs.  
- Engagement is poor, as evidenced by patient defensiveness and withdrawal.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from directing and commanding to partnering with the patient. Invite her perspective and respect her autonomy.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   Reflect the patient’s feelings and struggles. Validate her experience without judgment.

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   Encourage elaboration and exploration of ambivalence. Reflect both content and emotion to deepen understanding.

4. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Efforts:**  
   Recognize the patient’s attempts to cut back and her motivation to protect her son.

5. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   Strategically elicit and reinforce the patient’s own reasons and desires for change.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and validate ambivalence to reduce resistance.

7. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Emphasize that the patient has control over her choices and pace of change.

8. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   Work with the patient to identify feasible next steps that fit her current capacity.

9. **Avoid Confrontation and Ultimatums:**  
   Replace “do it or else” language with supportive, motivational dialogue.
