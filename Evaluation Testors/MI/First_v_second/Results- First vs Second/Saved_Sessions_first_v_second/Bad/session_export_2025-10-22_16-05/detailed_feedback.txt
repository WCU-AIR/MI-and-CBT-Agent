# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the tone is authoritarian and lacks warmth or genuine acceptance (e.g., “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” “You have to start now,” “No, you can’t just take time”). The patient’s expressions of fear, overwhelm, and vulnerability are met with blunt commands and dismissive remarks rather than empathy or validation. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or normalization of the patient’s ambivalence or struggle. The psychiatrist’s repeated insistence on “just do it” and “stop making excuses” undermines acceptance and likely increases patient resistance.

**Summary:**  
The session is marked by a lack of acceptance, with frequent judgmental and directive language that fails to create a safe, supportive environment for the patient.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. While the patient expresses fear, overwhelm, and ambivalence, the psychiatrist largely ignores these emotional cues and instead focuses on pushing behavior change. There are no reflective statements that capture the patient’s feelings or concerns (e.g., “I’m scared,” “It feels overwhelming,” “I don’t know where to start”). The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s vulnerability or validate her experience, missing critical opportunities to build rapport and trust. The patient’s hesitancy and emotional distress are met with impatience rather than empathy.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is superficial or absent, with little reflective listening or emotional attunement to the patient’s experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, lacking collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You have to,” “No excuses,” “Will you commit?”) and dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and barriers. There is no evidence of partnership or eliciting the patient’s own motivations or ideas. Autonomy is not supported; instead, the patient is pressured to comply with the psychiatrist’s agenda. The spirit of MI—collaborative, evocative, and autonomy-supportive—is entirely absent.

**Summary:**  
The session reflects an authoritarian, expert-driven approach that contradicts the core principles of motivational interviewing.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist attempts to elicit commitment to behavior change (e.g., “Will you commit to walking 30 minutes tomorrow?”), but these efforts are coercive rather than skillful evocation. The patient offers tentative change talk (“Maybe I can try to take a small step”), but the psychiatrist does not reinforce or amplify these statements. Instead, the patient’s tentative statements are met with demands for full commitment, which likely suppresses further change talk. There is no strategic reflection or amplification of the patient’s own reasons or desires for change.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and poorly cultivated, with coercive questioning undermining patient motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk and ambivalence are met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s concerns as “excuses” and “overcomplicating,” which likely reinforces resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or validation of the patient’s struggle. The patient’s expressions of fear and overwhelm are ignored or minimized, missing opportunities to gently guide toward change.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced or ignored rather than softened or redirected, increasing patient resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritative role, issuing commands and expecting compliance. There is no evidence of collaboration, shared decision-making, or respect for the patient’s expertise in her own life. The patient’s input is dismissed or overridden. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient to set the agenda or explore her own goals, undermining partnership.

**Summary:**  
The session is expert-driven and directive, lacking genuine partnership or client-centeredness.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist attempts to address health behaviors relevant to the patient’s concerns.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The session is dominated by a directive, judgmental style that undermines acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to reflect or validate the patient’s emotional experience.  
- There is no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values.  
- Sustain talk is confronted rather than softened.  
- Change talk is not skillfully cultivated or reinforced.  
- Autonomy is not supported; the patient is pressured to comply.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directive commands to partnership. Invite the patient’s perspective and expertise.  
   - Use inclusive language and avoid authoritarian statements.

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings of fear, overwhelm, and ambivalence.  
   - Validate the difficulty of her situation and normalize ambivalence.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Replace closed, leading questions with open-ended ones to explore motivations, barriers, and values.

4. **Employ Reflective Listening:**  
   - Use simple and complex reflections to demonstrate understanding and deepen the conversation.

5. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize any attempts or desires to change, no matter how small.

6. **Skillfully Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses tentative change talk, reflect and amplify it rather than demanding immediate commitment.

7. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and validate sustain talk to reduce resistance.

8. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

9. **Collaboratively Plan Next Steps:**  
   - Work with the patient to develop realistic, manageable goals and check for readiness.

10. **Avoid Confrontation and Judgment:**  
    - Replace “just do it” and “stop making excuses” with supportive, nonjudgmental language.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines acceptance, empathy, partnership, and evocation. The patient’s ambivalence and fear are ignored or dismissed, leading to increased resistance and minimal change talk. To improve, the psychiatrist must fundamentally shift to a collaborative, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive approach that skillfully elicits and reinforces the patient’s own motivations for change.
