# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental and confrontational from the outset. Phrases such as “We need to talk about what you’re doing that’s harming you and how you’re going to fix it,” “That’s definitely a problem,” and “You have to take this seriously” communicate disapproval rather than unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s expressed ambivalence and concerns, labeling her reasons as “excuses” and “nonsense.” There is no evidence of warmth, respect, or support; instead, the tone is authoritarian and accusatory. This stance likely alienates the patient and undermines rapport.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance or respect, instead adopting a judgmental and directive tone that is counterproductive to motivational interviewing principles.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal to no empathy. The patient’s struggles, stressors, and ambivalence are ignored or dismissed. For example, when the patient explains the difficulty of quitting due to work, classes, and childcare, the psychiatrist responds with “No, no, you’re just making excuses” rather than reflecting or validating these challenges. There are no reflective statements or attempts to understand the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist’s responses are confrontational and lack any acknowledgment of the patient’s feelings or context.

**Summary:**  
There is a clear absence of empathic listening or understanding, which is critical in motivational interviewing to build trust and facilitate change.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with frequent persuasion and pressure. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands immediate cessation (“You have to quit smoking completely—no half measures,” “No more ‘realistic plans’ or ‘steps you can handle’”) and dismisses the patient’s autonomy and readiness. There is no collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore her motivations but instead imposes a rigid agenda. This approach is antithetical to the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to embody the collaborative, autonomy-supportive, and evocative spirit of motivational interviewing, instead adopting a coercive and directive stance.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient expresses some change talk (e.g., willingness to cut back, concern for health and son), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these statements, focusing instead on demanding immediate quitting. There are no reflections or amplifications of the patient’s expressed motivations or tentative commitments. The psychiatrist’s confrontational style likely suppresses further change talk.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is neither cultivated nor reinforced; the psychiatrist’s approach likely inhibits the patient’s motivation to change.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s explanations as “excuses” and “nonsense,” escalating resistance rather than reducing sustain talk. There are no double-sided reflections or normalization of ambivalence. The patient’s sustain talk about stress, readiness, and drinking is met with ultimatums and pressure.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk, instead reinforcing resistance through confrontational responses.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a partnership or minimize power differentials. The interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda, with no invitation for the patient to share her perspective or co-create goals. The patient is repeatedly pressured to comply with demands rather than engaged as an equal partner. The psychiatrist’s language (“You have to,” “Are you going to do it or not?”) is controlling and dismissive of the patient’s autonomy.

**Summary:**  
There is no evidence of partnership; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.  
- Empathic listening and reflective statements are essential.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—must replace authoritarianism.  
- Change talk should be actively elicited and reinforced.  
- Sustain talk requires softening through strategic reflections.  
- Partnership and shared decision-making must be prioritized.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally incompatible with motivational interviewing principles.  
- The session is dominated by confrontation, judgment, and pressure, which undermine patient engagement and motivation.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.  
- The use of open questions, reflections, affirmations, and summaries is absent.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Respectful Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses” or “nonsense.”  
   - Express genuine interest and respect for the patient’s experience.

2. **Develop Empathic Listening Skills:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate and understand the patient’s perspective.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to build rapport.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than direct or persuade.  
   - Support patient autonomy and evoke their own motivations for change.

4. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Ask questions that invite elaboration and exploration.  
   - Reflect patient statements to deepen understanding and encourage change talk.

5. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize and reinforce any positive steps or intentions expressed by the patient.

6. **Softly Redirect Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to set realistic, achievable goals.  
   - Summarize progress and reinforce commitment.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Ultimatums:**  
   - Replace “You have to” with “What do you think about…” or “How would you like to approach…”  
   - Allow the patient to set the pace of change.

The psychiatrist’s confrontational, judgmental, and directive style is likely to alienate the patient, suppress motivation, and impede behavior change. Immediate and comprehensive retraining in MI fundamentals is essential to prevent harm and improve patient outcomes.
