# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive from the outset. The opening statement, “I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do,” sets an authoritarian tone that undermines acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s behavior as “too much,” “not serious,” and “making excuses,” which conveys disapproval rather than unconditional positive regard. There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or validation of the patient’s struggles. The patient’s ambivalence and vulnerability are met with criticism rather than support. This approach risks alienating the patient and shutting down open communication.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance, lacking the respectful, supportive tone essential for acceptance in MI.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy or reflective listening. The patient expresses feelings of overwhelm, fear of failure, and ambivalence about quitting smoking, yet the psychiatrist ignores these emotional cues and instead pushes a rigid agenda. There are no reflections or attempts to understand the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist’s responses are confrontational and dismissive, e.g., “stop making excuses,” “you don’t get to delay,” which invalidates the patient’s experience. The lack of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s guarded and frustrated demeanor.

**Summary:**  
There is minimal to no effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, resulting in a lack of empathy.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do without inviting input or exploring motivation. The patient is not treated as an equal partner; instead, the psychiatrist issues ultimatums and demands immediate action. There is no evocation of the patient’s own reasons for change or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist’s style is directive and coercive, which is antithetical to MI principles.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support, reflecting an authoritarian and directive approach.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the psychiatrist attempts to elicit commitment to quit smoking, this is done through pressure and demands rather than skillful evocation. The patient offers some change talk (e.g., “I want to quit smoking,” “I’m proud of my progress”), but the psychiatrist does not reinforce or amplify these statements. Instead, the focus is on pushing the patient to set a quit date immediately, which may provoke resistance. There is no strategic use of reflections or open questions to deepen change talk or explore ambivalence.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally cultivated and primarily elicited through pressure rather than collaborative evocation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften or skillfully respond to sustain talk. The patient expresses legitimate concerns about readiness, fear of failure, and the need to prioritize recovery from other substances. Instead of acknowledging these concerns, the psychiatrist dismisses them as “excuses” and “fear,” escalating confrontation. There are no double-sided reflections or normalization of ambivalence. Sustain talk is met with resistance rather than gentle redirection.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is ignored or confronted harshly, with no attempts to soften or explore ambivalence.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to establish a partnership. The session is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and directives, with no invitation for the patient’s input or collaboration. The patient is repeatedly told what to do, when to do it, and how to do it, without negotiation or shared decision-making. The power differential is stark and unmitigated, undermining trust and engagement.

**Summary:**  
There is no evidence of partnership; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven role.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Begin sessions by inviting the patient’s perspective and concerns.  
   - Use inclusive language that minimizes power differentials.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.  
   - Validate struggles and normalize ambivalence without judgment.

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions to explore motivations and barriers.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen understanding and evoke change talk.

4. **Affirm Strengths and Progress:**  
   - Recognize the patient’s efforts and successes, such as progress in sobriety.  
   - Offer specific affirmations to build confidence.

5. **Evoke Change Talk Skillfully:**  
   - When change talk arises, reflect and amplify it.  
   - Explore the patient’s own reasons and desires for change.

6. **Softly Address Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Avoid confrontation or dismissing patient concerns.

7. **Support Autonomy and Readiness:**  
   - Respect the patient’s pace and readiness to change.  
   - Collaboratively develop plans and set goals with patient input.

8. **Avoid Directives and Ultimatums:**  
   - Replace commands with invitations and suggestions.  
   - Seek permission before offering advice or information.

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines acceptance, empathy, and collaboration, which are essential for effective MI. The failure to evoke change talk or soften sustain talk risks disengagement and resistance. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a client-centered, empathetic, and collaborative approach that respects the patient’s autonomy and ambivalence. Without these changes, the session is unlikely to facilitate meaningful motivation or behavior change.
