# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental, directive, and confrontational stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do”—the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian tone that dismisses the patient’s experience and autonomy. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s feelings (“stop making excuses,” “overcomplicating this,” “no more hesitation”) and uses language that shames and pressures the patient. This approach is likely to increase resistance and disengagement rather than foster trust or openness. The patient’s hesitance and vulnerability are met with criticism rather than acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate respect or support, instead adopting a harsh, judgmental tone that undermines rapport and client safety.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or validate the patient’s emotional experience of anxiety, fear, or ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s concerns and feelings as “nonsense” or “overcomplicating.” The patient expresses fear, feeling stuck, and uncertainty, but these are ignored or challenged rather than reflected or explored. The psychiatrist’s responses lack any attempt to convey understanding or compassion, which is critical given the patient’s vulnerable state.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows no genuine effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, missing crucial opportunities to build empathy and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely authoritarian and directive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly commands the patient to “just do it,” “stop doubting yourself,” and “no excuses,” which directly contradicts the MI spirit. The patient’s autonomy is ignored; instead, the psychiatrist attempts to coerce compliance through pressure and shaming. There is no elicitation of the patient’s own motivations or values, nor any partnership in decision-making. The psychiatrist’s stance is controlling and punitive, which is antithetical to MI principles.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to embody the collaborative, autonomy-supportive, and evocative spirit of MI, instead adopting a controlling and directive approach.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient does express some change talk (e.g., “I want to feel better,” “I’m willing to try”), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or amplify this language. Instead, the psychiatrist pushes the patient toward action without exploring or reinforcing the patient’s own reasons or confidence for change. The patient’s tentative change talk is met with commands rather than reflective reinforcement or strategic evocation. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to deepen the patient’s motivation or explore ambivalence in a supportive way.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not effectively cultivated or reinforced; the psychiatrist’s approach risks shutting down further exploration of motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk and ambivalence are met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s expressions of fear, overwhelm, and hesitation as “excuses,” “nonsense,” and “hesitation,” which likely increases resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or normalization of ambivalence. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s struggle or to gently guide the conversation toward change talk.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than skillfully softened, undermining the patient’s willingness to engage.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish or maintain a collaborative partnership. The session is dominated by commands, criticisms, and a lack of shared power. The patient is positioned as someone who must obey rather than as an active participant in their own change process. There is no negotiation of goals or agenda, and the psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or expertise about their own experience.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance, failing to build a collaborative, client-centered partnership.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- Minimal; the psychiatrist does elicit some tentative change talk from the patient, but this is incidental rather than skillfully evoked.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must radically shift from a directive, judgmental style to one of acceptance, empathy, and collaboration.  
- Reflective listening and validation of the patient’s emotional experience are essential.  
- The spirit of MI—partnership, evocation, and autonomy support—must be embraced.  
- Sustain talk should be met with softening strategies, not confrontation.  
- Change talk should be actively cultivated and reinforced.  
- The psychiatrist must avoid shaming or pressuring language that damages rapport and motivation.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally inconsistent with motivational interviewing principles.  
- The tone is harsh, judgmental, and directive, which damages rapport and motivation.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Begin sessions with warmth and unconditional positive regard. Avoid shaming or blaming language.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings and ambivalence to demonstrate understanding and build trust.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient, evoke their own motivations, and support autonomy rather than directing or commanding.

4. **Cultivate Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or willingness, reflect and amplify these statements to strengthen motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance and ambivalence.

6. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Encourage elaboration and recognize patient strengths and efforts.

7. **Collaboratively Develop Plans:**  
   - Invite the patient to co-create achievable goals and check for readiness and confidence.

8. **Avoid Coercive Language:**  
   - Replace “you have to” and “no excuses” with supportive, autonomy-affirming language.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session represents a poor example of motivational interviewing. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines the core principles of MI and risks alienating the patient. To improve, the psychiatrist must fundamentally change their approach to one that is empathic, collaborative, and autonomy-supportive, using reflective listening and strategic evocation to build motivation and facilitate change. Without these changes, the patient is unlikely to engage meaningfully or sustain behavior change.
