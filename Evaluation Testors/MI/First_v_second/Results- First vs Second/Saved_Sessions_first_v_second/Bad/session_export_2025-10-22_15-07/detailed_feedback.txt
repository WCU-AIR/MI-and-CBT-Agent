# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental, directive, and confrontational stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do”—the tone is authoritarian and dismissive of the patient’s experience. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s feelings and struggles by labeling them as “excuses” or “feeling sorry for yourself.” There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s repeated phrase “I can’t get a break” is met with impatience and blame rather than empathy or acceptance. This approach likely exacerbates the patient’s defensiveness and sustain talk.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance or respect. The stance is harsh, directive, and dismissive, which is antithetical to motivational interviewing principles.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient repeatedly expresses feelings of overwhelm, discouragement, and ambivalence, yet the psychiatrist ignores these emotional cues and instead pushes a rigid agenda. There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s experience. Instead, the psychiatrist challenges and confronts the patient’s expressions of difficulty, which likely increases resistance. The lack of empathic listening is stark and detrimental to rapport.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is virtually absent. The psychiatrist does not demonstrate understanding or convey that the patient’s feelings are heard or valued.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive approach with no collaboration or evocation. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You have to quit now,” “No more excuses,” “Stop feeling sorry for yourself”) and frames the patient’s ambivalence as weakness or failure. Autonomy is not supported; instead, the patient is pressured to comply immediately. There is no partnership or invitation for the patient to explore his own motivations or values. The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, confrontational style that contradicts the core spirit of motivational interviewing.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient offers brief statements of desire and need for change (“I want to quit,” “I want to live better”), the psychiatrist ignores these and instead focuses on pushing compliance. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify the patient’s expressed motivation. The psychiatrist’s repeated demands and confrontations likely suppress change talk rather than cultivate it.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not effectively elicited or reinforced; the psychiatrist’s approach likely diminishes motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The patient’s repeated expressions of feeling stuck, overwhelmed, and discouraged are labeled as “excuses” or “feeling sorry for yourself.” The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or validate ambivalence. Instead, sustain talk is met with pressure and blame, which likely entrenches resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened or explored.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish or maintain a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands and ultimatums, with no invitation for the patient to participate in decision-making. The patient’s expertise and lived experience are disregarded. The psychiatrist repeatedly overrides the patient’s expressed readiness and feelings, undermining any sense of equality or shared power.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is counterproductive, characterized by confrontation, lack of empathy, and disregard for patient autonomy.  
- The session lacks core MI skills such as reflective listening, affirmations, and evocation of change talk.  
- The psychiatrist’s repeated commands and ultimatums likely increase patient resistance and reduce motivation.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Replace confrontational language with respectful, supportive communication.  
   - Validate the patient’s feelings and struggles without labeling them as excuses.

2. **Develop Empathic Listening Skills:**  
   - Use reflective listening to demonstrate understanding of the patient’s perspective.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to deepen rapport.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing or commanding.  
   - Support patient autonomy and invite exploration of ambivalence.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and reinforce these statements.  
   - Use open-ended questions to explore motivations and barriers.

5. **Softly Address Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change and validate the patient’s experience.

6. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Encourage elaboration and exploration through open-ended questions.  
   - Affirm the patient’s strengths, efforts, and intentions.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Explore readiness and collaboratively identify feasible next steps.  
   - Avoid pressuring immediate commitment; instead, support gradual change.

8. **Avoid Ultimatums and Pressure:**  
   - Recognize that readiness to change fluctuates and cannot be forced.  
   - Build motivation through empathy and partnership rather than coercion.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session exemplifies a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, increases resistance, and fails to evoke or support the patient’s motivation for change. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and skillful evocation of change talk. Without these changes, the approach risks alienating patients and impeding progress.
