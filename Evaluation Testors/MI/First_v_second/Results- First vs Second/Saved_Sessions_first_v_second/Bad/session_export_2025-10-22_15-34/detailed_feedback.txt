# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist states, “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do to quit,” which immediately establishes an authoritarian tone rather than a collaborative one. The patient’s expressions of frustration and resignation are met with admonishments such as “You really need to stop making excuses,” and “You have to stop feeling sorry for yourself,” which are clearly disapproving and invalidating. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth; instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly blames and pressures the patient, which likely increases resistance. The lack of empathy and acceptance is stark and pervasive.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental and directive, lacking any genuine acceptance or respect for the patient’s perspective or ambivalence.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate understanding or reflective listening. The patient’s expressions of frustration (“It’s just like the same thing every day,” “Feels like no point if I’m just gonna fail anyway”) are ignored or dismissed rather than explored or validated. The psychiatrist does not attempt to paraphrase or reflect the patient’s feelings or perspective, instead responding with commands and accusations (“You have to take this seriously,” “No more ‘whatever’ attitude”). There is no attempt to understand the patient’s emotional state or the underlying reasons for ambivalence. The patient’s flat affect and resignation are met with impatience rather than empathy.

**Summary:**  
There is a complete absence of empathic engagement or reflective listening, which undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely authoritarian and directive. The psychiatrist repeatedly tells the patient what to do, uses threatening language (“you’re just digging your own grave”), and insists on non-negotiable compliance (“Do you understand that this is non-negotiable?”). There is no collaboration, no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values, and no support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore ambivalence; instead, the patient is positioned as someone who must obey orders. This is the antithesis of the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks any collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support, reflecting an authoritarian and confrontational style.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not evoke or reinforce change talk. When the patient expresses any tentative statements about quitting or reducing smoking, these are ignored or met with skepticism and pressure rather than encouragement. The psychiatrist does not ask open-ended questions to explore reasons for change, nor does he amplify or reflect any change talk. Instead, the focus is on pushing the patient to commit immediately, which the patient resists. The patient’s minimal change talk is not nurtured or deepened.

**Summary:**  
No effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses any emerging motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s statements as “excuses” and “whatever attitude,” and repeatedly insists the patient “stop” these behaviors. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s ambivalence or difficulty. The patient’s sustain talk is met with threats and ultimatums, which likely increase resistance and disengagement.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than skillfully softened or reframed.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a partnership. The session is dominated by commands, ultimatums, and a lack of invitation for patient input. The psychiatrist repeatedly tells the patient what to do and insists on compliance without negotiation. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s expertise in his own experience. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership or collaboration; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance.

---

- Embrace the MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- None identified. The session is dominated by confrontational, directive, and judgmental behaviors.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist must fundamentally change approach to align with MI principles.  
- Develop skills in acceptance, empathy, and reflective listening.  
- Use open-ended questions to explore patient’s perspective and ambivalence.  
- Avoid confrontation and instead soften sustain talk with validation and double-sided reflections.  
- Evoke and reinforce change talk by exploring patient’s own motivations and values.  
- Support autonomy by inviting patient input and choice.  
- Collaboratively develop plans and summarize progress.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Replace commands and ultimatums with invitations to explore the patient’s experience and feelings.

2. **Practice Reflective Listening:**  
   Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and build rapport.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   Encourage the patient to elaborate on ambivalence, barriers, and motivations.

4. **Validate Ambivalence and Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge the patient’s struggle without reinforcing resistance.

5. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   Ask about the patient’s own reasons for change, hopes, and values related to quitting.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Emphasize that the patient has control over decisions and pace of change.

7. **Collaboratively Plan:**  
   Develop quit plans together, ensuring patient buy-in and realistic goals.

8. **Avoid Judgment and Confrontation:**  
   Replace blaming language with supportive, nonjudgmental communication.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session reflects a fundamentally flawed approach to motivational interviewing. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s motivation, and likely increases resistance. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation of the patient’s own motivations and autonomy. Without these changes, the patient is unlikely to engage meaningfully or make progress toward quitting smoking.
