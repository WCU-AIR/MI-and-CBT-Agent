# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and authoritarian stance throughout the session. From the very first utterance, the psychiatrist states, “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” which immediately sets a directive, controlling tone. The patient’s frustration and resistance are met with repeated admonishments and accusations of making excuses or being stubborn (“No, no, you’re just making excuses,” “You have to stop being so stubborn”). There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s feelings or struggles and instead repeatedly invalidates her perspective. This approach likely exacerbates resistance rather than fostering openness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental and directive, lacking any genuine acceptance or respect for the patient’s autonomy or ambivalence.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective or emotional state. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with commands and accusations. For example, when the patient expresses feeling overwhelmed and needing to find her own reasons and timing, the psychiatrist responds with “No, you don’t get to ‘not be sure’” and “Enough with the hesitation,” which dismisses the patient’s feelings outright. The patient’s subtle change talk and ambivalence are ignored or met with confrontation rather than reflective listening.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to convey understanding or validate the patient’s experience, missing critical opportunities to build rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding immediate compliance. There is no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values, no support for autonomy, and no partnership. The psychiatrist’s language is coercive (“You have to commit now,” “No more excuses”), which is antithetical to MI principles.

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by directive commands and persuasion, with no evidence of MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient offers subtle change talk (e.g., “I want to feel better,” “Maybe someday”), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these statements. Instead of amplifying or exploring these motivations, the psychiatrist pushes for immediate action, which the patient resists. There are no reflective statements or open questions designed to deepen the patient’s own reasons for change.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but neglected and not cultivated by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient as “stubborn” and accuses her of “making excuses,” which likely entrenches sustain talk and resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s ambivalence or the difficulty of change.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and escalated rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation, issuing commands and demands without inviting the patient’s input or perspective. The patient is repeatedly pressured to comply immediately, with no negotiation or shared decision-making. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, lacking any collaborative partnership.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must abandon the authoritarian, directive approach.  
- Develop genuine acceptance and empathy for the patient’s ambivalence and resistance.  
- Embrace the spirit of MI by collaborating, evoking the patient’s own motivations, and supporting autonomy.  
- Learn to skillfully evoke and reinforce change talk while softening sustain talk.  
- Build a true partnership with the patient rather than issuing commands.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and judgmental, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and instead escalates sustain talk.  
- No use of reflective listening, affirmations, or open questions to engage the patient.  
- Planning and summarizing are absent.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and resistance are met with pressure and dismissal, likely increasing resistance.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Avoid telling the patient what to do. Instead, invite her perspective and work together to explore ambivalence.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings and struggles. Validate her experience without judgment.

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflective Listening:**  
   - Encourage elaboration and deeper exploration of motivations and barriers.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and explore these statements to strengthen motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and affirm ambivalence to reduce resistance.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize that the patient has control over her choices and timing.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - When the patient is ready, work together to create achievable steps rather than demanding immediate action.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Judgment:**  
   - Replace commands and accusations with curiosity and support.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session reflects a fundamental misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, increases resistance, and fails to engage the patient in meaningful change talk or planning. Immediate and substantial retraining in MI spirit, techniques, and patient-centered communication is essential to prevent harm and improve clinical outcomes.
