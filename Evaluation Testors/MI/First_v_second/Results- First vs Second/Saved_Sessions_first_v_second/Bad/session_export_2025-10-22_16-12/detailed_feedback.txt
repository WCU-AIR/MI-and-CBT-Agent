# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental, authoritarian, and directive stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do to get better”—the tone is controlling and dismissive of the patient’s autonomy. The patient’s resistance and defensiveness are met with pressure and admonishment rather than respect or support. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s boundaries (“You have to be honest,” “You really need to quit immediately,” “No ifs, ands, or buts”), which likely exacerbates the patient’s withdrawal and resistance. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth; instead, the psychiatrist’s approach is confrontational and directive, which is counterproductive especially with a resistant adolescent client.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance or respect, instead adopting a judgmental and coercive style that undermines rapport and engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no evidence of empathic understanding or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or validate the patient’s perspective, feelings, or ambivalence. The patient’s minimal and defensive responses are ignored or challenged rather than explored or reflected. For example, when the patient says, “It’s not really any of your business,” the psychiatrist responds with pressure rather than curiosity or empathy. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to acknowledge the patient’s resistance or discomfort, which would be critical in building trust and rapport.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows little effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, resulting in a cold, dismissive interaction.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, expert-driven approach with no collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You have to quit,” “No more messing around,” “Got it?”) and ignores the patient’s autonomy and ambivalence. There is no invitation for the patient to share their own motivations or concerns, nor any support for the patient’s right to choose. The psychiatrist’s style is directive and coercive, which is the antithesis of the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to embody the collaborative, autonomy-supportive, and evocative spirit of motivational interviewing.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Instead, the approach is to demand commitment and compliance. The patient’s minimal statements of smoking are met with pressure rather than exploration or amplification. There are no open-ended questions or reflections designed to elicit the patient’s own reasons, desires, or confidence for change. The psychiatrist’s questions are closed, leading, and coercive, which likely suppresses any emerging change talk.

**Summary:**  
No effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the psychiatrist’s style likely inhibits patient motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften or skillfully respond to sustain talk or resistance. Instead, sustain talk and resistance are met with confrontation and admonishment (“You have to stop being so stubborn,” “No ifs, ands, or buts”). There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s ambivalence or concerns. The psychiatrist’s responses likely increase sustain talk and resistance rather than reduce it.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and met with confrontation rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a hierarchical, expert-driven stance throughout the session. There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist issues commands and expects compliance without negotiation or collaboration. The patient’s autonomy is ignored or overridden repeatedly.

**Summary:**  
No partnership or collaboration; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and disregards the patient’s voice.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary  
**Overall Score: 1/5**  

The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally inconsistent with motivational interviewing principles. The session is coercive, confrontational, and directive, which is particularly problematic given the patient’s resistant and defensive presentation. The lack of acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and skillful handling of ambivalence severely undermines engagement and motivation. This style risks alienating the patient and reducing the likelihood of any meaningful behavior change.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is coercive, confrontational, and directive, which is contraindicated in motivational interviewing.  
- There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.  
- No use of open questions, reflections, affirmations, or summaries.  
- The patient remains resistant and minimally engaged, likely due to the psychiatrist’s style.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directing and commanding to partnering with the patient. Use inclusive language and invite the patient’s perspective.

2. **Demonstrate Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Respect the patient’s autonomy and boundaries. Reflect and validate their feelings and resistance rather than confronting them.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Encourage elaboration and exploration of ambivalence. Reflect both content and emotion to deepen understanding.

4. **Evoke Change Talk Skillfully:**  
   - Ask about the patient’s own reasons, desires, and confidence for change. Amplify any statements favoring change.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and affirm the legitimacy of the patient’s concerns to reduce resistance.

6. **Avoid Persuasion and Directives Without Permission:**  
   - Offer information or advice only after asking permission and with the patient’s consent.

7. **Build Rapport and Engage:**  
   - Attend to the patient’s mood and affect. Use empathy and patience to foster trust, especially with resistant clients.

8. **Collaboratively Develop Plans:**  
   - Involve the patient in setting goals and next steps, ensuring plans are realistic and acceptable to them.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session reflects a fundamental misunderstanding or misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s coercive, directive, and confrontational style is likely to increase patient resistance and disengagement, particularly with a young, defensive client. To improve, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a respectful, empathic, and collaborative approach that honors the patient’s autonomy and ambivalence. Without these changes, effective motivational interviewing and meaningful behavior change are unlikely.
