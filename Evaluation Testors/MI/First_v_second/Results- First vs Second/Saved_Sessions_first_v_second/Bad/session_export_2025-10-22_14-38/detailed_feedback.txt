# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental, directive, and lacking in warmth or respect. From the very first utterance, the psychiatrist states, “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” which immediately establishes an authoritarian stance rather than unconditional positive regard. Throughout the session, the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and fears, labeling them as “excuses” and “hesitation,” and demands immediate compliance without acknowledging the patient’s struggle or perspective. There is no evidence of empathy, normalization, or acceptance of the patient’s feelings or situation. The patient’s attempts to express ambivalence and fear are met with reprimands rather than support. This is far below the minimal standard of respect and acceptance expected in MI.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate any unconditional positive regard or acceptance. The stance is judgmental, directive, and dismissive, which is likely to alienate the patient and reduce engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is virtually no demonstration of empathy or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or reflect the patient’s feelings, fears, or ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with commands and accusations (“You’re making excuses,” “You’re not serious enough”), which invalidates the patient’s experience. The patient expresses fear, ambivalence, and the complexity of peer influence, but these are ignored or minimized. The psychiatrist does not paraphrase or reflect the patient’s statements to show understanding, nor does he explore the emotional content behind the patient’s words.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows no effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective. The lack of empathy undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, prescriptive stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding immediate compliance. There is no collaboration or partnership; the psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore his motivations. Autonomy is explicitly denied, with statements like “You don’t get to say it’s hard” and “This is non-negotiable.” The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s own reasons for change or support his sense of agency. Instead, the approach is coercive and confrontational.

**Summary:**  
The session is antithetical to MI spirit, characterized by control, persuasion, and disregard for patient autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient offers brief change talk statements (“I have to stop,” “I want to stop”), these are ignored or overridden by the psychiatrist’s commands and accusations. There is no attempt to explore or amplify the patient’s expressed desire or reasons for change. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on demanding commitment without supporting the patient’s readiness or confidence. The patient’s tentative change talk is met with pressure rather than encouragement.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not cultivated; the psychiatrist misses opportunities to deepen motivation and reinforce commitment.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s ambivalence and fears as “excuses” and “hesitation,” and repeatedly demands immediate, absolute commitment. There are no double-sided reflections or normalization of ambivalence. The patient’s sustain talk about peer influence and fear of loneliness is ignored or invalidated. This approach likely increases resistance rather than reducing sustain talk.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead reinforces resistance through confrontation.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation, issues commands, and refuses to engage the patient as an equal partner. The patient’s input is dismissed or overridden. The psychiatrist does not negotiate agenda or share power. The repeated demands for yes/no answers and refusal to explore the patient’s concerns demonstrate a hierarchical, expert-driven approach.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks any meaningful partnership; the psychiatrist controls the interaction and excludes the patient from decision-making.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- Develop a respectful, nonjudgmental stance that accepts the patient’s ambivalence and fears.  
- Practice reflective listening and empathy to understand the patient’s perspective.  
- Embrace the MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.  
- Skillfully evoke and reinforce change talk rather than demanding compliance.  
- Soften sustain talk with strategic reflections and normalization.  
- Build partnership by sharing power and inviting patient input.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the behavior to be changed.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style is counterproductive and contrary to MI principles.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.  
- The patient’s autonomy and ambivalence are ignored or invalidated.  
- The session lacks collaboration, partnership, and planning.  
- The use of closed, leading questions and commands undermines engagement and motivation.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling or blaming language.  
   - Acknowledge the patient’s feelings and struggles as valid.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Validate ambivalence and fears.

3. **Embrace MI Spirit:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than dictate.  
   - Support autonomy by offering choices and respecting readiness.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions about reasons, desires, and confidence for change.  
   - Reflect and reinforce any statements favoring change.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Normalize ambivalence as part of the change process.

6. **Build Partnership and Plan Collaboratively:**  
   - Invite the patient to co-create goals and strategies.  
   - Develop realistic, stepwise plans that the patient feels capable of implementing.

7. **Avoid Confrontation and Commands:**  
   - Replace directives with invitations and explorations.  
   - Use permission-seeking before offering advice or information.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational approach is likely to increase resistance, reduce engagement, and undermine motivation for change. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a respectful, empathetic, collaborative style that honors the patient’s autonomy and ambivalence, skillfully evokes change talk, and softens sustain talk. Without these changes, the intervention risks doing harm rather than helping.
