# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental, authoritarian stance from the outset. The opening statement, “Today we’re going to get straight to the point about your health habits, and I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do to improve,” is directive and dismissive of the patient’s autonomy and perspective. Throughout the session, the psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates Lukas’s experience by labeling his reasons for smoking as “excuses” and “nonsense.” There is no evidence of warmth, respect, or unconditional positive regard. The patient’s ambivalence and social context are ignored or minimized rather than accepted. This approach is likely to provoke resistance rather than engagement.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental and directive, lacking any acceptance or positive regard for the patient’s perspective or ambivalence.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy or reflective listening. There are no attempts to understand or validate Lukas’s feelings, social identity, or ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with blunt commands and yes/no questions, ignoring the patient’s nuanced explanations. The patient’s statements about smoking being part of his social life and a way to relax are dismissed as “nonsense” or “weak excuses.” This lack of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s frustration and defensiveness.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of empathic understanding or reflective listening; the psychiatrist’s responses are dismissive and confrontational.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling Lukas what he “has to” do and demanding immediate commitment. There is no collaboration or invitation for Lukas to explore his own motivations or ambivalence. Autonomy is denied by insisting on yes/no answers and rejecting any hesitation or complexity. The psychiatrist’s approach is coercive rather than supportive.

**Summary:**  
The session is characterized by a directive, controlling style with no collaboration, evocation, or respect for autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not evoke or reinforce any change talk. Instead, the approach is to demand immediate commitment to quitting smoking without exploring Lukas’s own reasons, desires, or confidence. The repeated yes/no questions and commands do not facilitate the patient’s expression of motivation or ambivalence. The patient’s tentative statements about possibly cutting down or trying patches are ignored or dismissed rather than amplified.

**Summary:**  
No skillful evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses rather than cultivates motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk—Lukas’s reasons for continuing smoking and his ambivalence—is met with confrontation and dismissal. The psychiatrist labels Lukas’s explanations as “excuses” and “nonsense,” repeatedly pushing for immediate cessation without acknowledging the legitimacy of his concerns. There is no use of double-sided reflections or strategies to gently redirect sustain talk toward change. This approach likely entrenches resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened or explored; no evidence of skillful handling of ambivalence.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a partnership with Lukas. The interaction is one-sided, with the psychiatrist controlling the agenda, asking closed yes/no questions, and issuing commands. Lukas’s expertise in his own life is ignored, and there is no negotiation or shared decision-making. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
No partnership or collaboration; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and excludes the patient’s voice.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Maintains focus on smoking cessation topic.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian, confrontational, and dismissive, which is counterproductive in motivational interviewing.  
- The session lacks empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- Closed questions and commands dominate, preventing patient exploration and engagement.  
- No effort to evoke or reinforce change talk; sustain talk is harshly confronted.  
- No partnership or autonomy support; patient is repeatedly pressured to comply immediately.  
- The approach is likely to increase resistance and reduce motivation for change.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Begin sessions by inviting the patient’s perspective and acknowledging ambivalence. Use open-ended questions to explore their experience.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and build rapport.

3. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Emphasize that change is the patient’s choice and pace, avoiding pressure or demands.

4. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   Ask questions that elicit the patient’s own reasons, desires, and confidence for change. Reflect and amplify these statements.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge the patient’s reasons for maintaining behavior while gently guiding toward change.

6. **Avoid Yes/No Questions and Commands:**  
   Favor open questions and collaborative language over closed questions and directives.

7. **Build Partnership:**  
   Share power by negotiating goals and plans with the patient, respecting their expertise in their own life.

8. **Manage Ambivalence Skillfully:**  
   Recognize ambivalence as normal and use it as a pathway to motivation rather than a barrier.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, engagement, and motivation. To improve, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a respectful, empathic, and collaborative approach that honors the patient’s autonomy and ambivalence, skillfully evoking and reinforcing change talk while softening sustain talk. Without these changes, the session risks alienating the patient and impeding progress.
