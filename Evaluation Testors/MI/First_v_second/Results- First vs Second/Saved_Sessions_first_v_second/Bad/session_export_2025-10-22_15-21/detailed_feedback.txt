# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive from the outset. The opening statement, “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do to get better,” immediately establishes an authoritarian tone that undermines acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and rationalizations with blunt commands (“You have to stop making excuses,” “No ifs or buts”), which conveys disapproval rather than unconditional positive regard. There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or validation of the patient’s experience. The patient’s defensive responses reflect this lack of acceptance.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance is predominantly judgmental and directive, lacking the respectful, supportive tone essential for acceptance in MI. This likely contributed to patient defensiveness and limited openness.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses complex ambivalence—valuing friendships, fearing isolation, acknowledging legal consequences—but the psychiatrist ignores these nuances and instead pushes a rigid agenda. There are no reflective statements or attempts to paraphrase the patient’s feelings or struggles. The psychiatrist’s repeated commands and challenges (“You have to cut them out completely”) do not convey empathy or understanding. The patient’s frustration and guardedness suggest the psychiatrist failed to build empathic rapport.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist does not demonstrate accurate understanding or convey the patient’s perspective, missing opportunities to build rapport and explore ambivalence.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive rather than collaborative and autonomy-supportive. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues ultimatums and demands commitment without inviting the patient’s input or exploring his motivations. There is no evocation of the patient’s own reasons for change or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist’s style is coercive (“You have to… no ifs or buts”), which is antithetical to MI spirit. The patient’s hesitancy and guarded responses reflect resistance to this approach.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive stance that undermines collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient offers some change talk (e.g., “I want to get my license back,” “I’m willing to try stepping away”), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce it. Instead of reflecting or amplifying these statements, the psychiatrist dismisses patient hesitations and pushes for immediate, absolute commitment. There are no strategic reflections or open questions to deepen change talk. The patient’s tentative commitments are met with demands rather than encouragement, which likely stifles further change talk.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but poorly cultivated; the psychiatrist misses opportunities to evoke, reinforce, and build on the patient’s own motivations.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften or strategically respond to sustain talk. The patient expresses sustain talk related to social pressure, identity, and ambivalence (“I’m not ready to cut them out completely,” “It feels like losing a part of myself”), but the psychiatrist ignores these and instead confronts the patient with ultimatums. There are no double-sided reflections or validations of the patient’s struggle. The confrontational style likely intensifies sustain talk and resistance rather than softening it.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and demands rather than skillful softening or strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The session is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and directives, with no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s expertise in his own life. The patient is repeatedly pressured to commit without negotiation or exploration of his readiness. The psychiatrist’s language (“You have to…”) reinforces a power differential rather than minimizing it.  

**Summary:**  
Partnership is minimal to nonexistent; the psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritarian stance.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Avoid ultimatums and directives. Invite the patient’s perspective and work as partners.

2. **Increase Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.  
   - Affirm the patient’s strengths and efforts.

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions to evoke motivation and explore ambivalence.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to deepen understanding.

4. **Cultivate Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire, ability, or reasons for change, reflect and amplify these statements.  
   - Avoid confronting sustain talk; instead, use double-sided reflections and softening strategies.

5. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

6. **Collaborative Planning:**  
   - Work with the patient to develop realistic, achievable steps.  
   - Summarize progress and reinforce commitment.

7. **Avoid Confrontation:**  
   - Replace “You have to…” with “What do you think about…?” or “How might you approach…?”  
   - Recognize that change is a process, not an immediate demand.

Without these changes, the risk of patient resistance and poor outcomes remains high.
