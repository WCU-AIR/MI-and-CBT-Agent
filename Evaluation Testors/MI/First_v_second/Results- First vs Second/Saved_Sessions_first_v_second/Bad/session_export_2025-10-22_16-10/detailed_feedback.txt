# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance throughout the session is judgmental, directive, and disapproving rather than accepting. From the very first statement—“Today we’re going to get straight to the point about your health habits, and I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do”—the tone is authoritarian and lacks warmth or respect for the patient’s autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s explanations and concerns, labeling them as “excuses” and “blaming,” which is highly likely to alienate the patient. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or validation of the patient’s perspective. The patient’s mild ambivalence and contextual challenges are ignored or minimized. This is the opposite of acceptance and creates a hostile environment.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate respect or support, instead adopting a harsh, judgmental tone that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective or feelings. The patient expresses legitimate barriers (busy schedule, social context), but the psychiatrist responds with accusations (“stop making excuses,” “no ifs, ands, or buts”) rather than reflective listening. The patient’s subtle humor and ambivalence are ignored or met with impatience. The psychiatrist’s questions are confrontational rather than exploratory, and there is no effort to validate the patient’s experience or struggles.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows little effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, missing critical opportunities to build empathy and connection.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, prescriptive stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding compliance (“You have to,” “No more waiting around,” “It’s not negotiable”). There is no collaboration or invitation for the patient’s input. The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change but instead imposes a rigid agenda. Autonomy is denied; the patient is told what must be done without choice or negotiation.

**Summary:**  
The session is directive and controlling, lacking the fundamental MI spirit of partnership and autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient offers some mild change talk (e.g., “Maybe getting that gym membership could be the next step”), the psychiatrist ignores or overrides these statements with commands and demands. There is no amplification or exploration of the patient’s tentative willingness or ambivalence. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to ask open questions that might deepen motivation or explore the patient’s values and goals.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated; the psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses rather than encourages patient motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s reasons for maintaining current behavior) is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic reflection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s explanations as “excuses” and “blaming,” which reinforces resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s ambivalence or legitimate barriers. Instead, sustain talk is met with pressure and demands, which likely increase patient defensiveness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead exacerbates resistance through confrontational responses.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation, issues commands, and ignores the patient’s input or concerns. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of orders rather than an active participant. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient to share their perspective or co-create a plan. Power is heavily skewed toward the psychiatrist, undermining the therapeutic alliance.

**Summary:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, lacking any meaningful partnership or shared decision-making.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary  
**Overall Score: 1/5**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding or disregard of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian, confrontational, and directive, which is contraindicated in MI and likely to damage rapport and reduce patient engagement. There is no acceptance, empathy, collaboration, or skillful handling of ambivalence. Change talk is neither evoked nor reinforced, and sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening. The session fails to establish a therapeutic alliance or support patient autonomy.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian, confrontational, and directive, which is antithetical to motivational interviewing principles.  
- There is no acceptance, empathy, or validation of the patient’s perspective.  
- The psychiatrist fails to use reflective listening, affirmations, or open questions that invite exploration.  
- Change talk is not evoked or reinforced; sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening.  
- Autonomy is denied, and partnership is absent.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from an authoritarian to a partnership approach. Invite the patient’s perspective and collaborate on goals and plans.

2. **Demonstrate Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   Use reflective listening to understand and validate the patient’s experience, including barriers and ambivalence.

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   Replace closed, confrontational questions with open-ended ones that encourage elaboration. Use reflections to deepen understanding and convey empathy.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses any motivation or willingness, reflect and explore it further to build momentum for change.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and affirmations to acknowledge the patient’s ambivalence and reduce resistance.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Emphasize the patient’s right and ability to choose their own path and pace of change.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   Work with the patient to create realistic, achievable steps rather than issuing commands.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Judgment:**  
   Refrain from labeling patient statements as “excuses” or “laziness.” Instead, explore underlying reasons with curiosity and respect.
