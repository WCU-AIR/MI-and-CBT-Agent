# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach throughout the session is judgmental, confrontational, and directive rather than accepting. From the very first statement—“I’m here to get straight to the point today”—the tone is authoritarian and lacks warmth or respect. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s concerns as “excuses” and dismisses her fears and ambivalence (e.g., “You’re just making excuses again,” “Stop overthinking this,” “No more delays or excuses”). There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or validation of the patient’s experience. The patient’s fears about withdrawal, weight gain, and financial concerns are minimized or outright rejected rather than explored or normalized. This approach is likely to increase resistance and disengagement.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate acceptance, instead adopting a harsh, judgmental stance that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses complex fears and ambivalence, yet the psychiatrist responds with blunt commands and dismissive remarks rather than reflective listening. For example, when the patient shares concerns about weight gain, cost, and withdrawal, the psychiatrist responds with “You have to stop overthinking this” and “You have to take this seriously,” which invalidates the patient’s feelings. There are no reflective statements that capture the patient’s emotional experience or ambivalence. The psychiatrist does not attempt to explore or validate the patient’s fears, nor does he acknowledge the difficulty of change from the patient’s viewpoint.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is virtually absent; the psychiatrist does not demonstrate understanding or convey that the patient’s perspective is heard or valued.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is completely lacking. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, directive style, repeatedly telling the patient what she “must” do and demanding immediate commitment. There is no collaboration or partnership; the psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore her motivations. Autonomy is not supported; instead, the patient is pressured and coerced (“No more hesitation,” “This isn’t optional”). The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s own reasons for change or support her ambivalence; rather, he dismisses sustain talk and pushes for compliance.

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by a controlling, prescriptive approach that contradicts the fundamental principles of MI.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient does express some change talk (e.g., “I know I’ve got to stop,” “The doctor’s warning made me rethink everything”), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce this language. Instead of amplifying the patient’s motivations, the psychiatrist ignores or overrides them with commands and criticism. There are no strategic reflections or questions designed to deepen change talk or explore the patient’s reasons, desires, or confidence. The patient’s tentative statements about readiness and willingness are met with pressure rather than encouragement.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not effectively cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s sustain talk as “excuses” and “hesitation,” failing to soften or skillfully respond to it. Instead of using double-sided reflections or validating ambivalence, the psychiatrist confronts and challenges sustain talk aggressively (“You have to stop being scared,” “No more waiting”). This approach likely increases resistance and defensiveness. The patient’s fears about withdrawal, weight gain, and social support are real and legitimate but are dismissed rather than explored or normalized.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation, issuing commands and demands without inviting the patient’s perspective or negotiating goals. The patient’s input is ignored or overridden. The psychiatrist does not share power or work as an equal partner; instead, he adopts an expert-driven, authoritarian stance. The patient’s ambivalence and fears are not treated as valid contributions to the conversation.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks partnership; the psychiatrist controls the agenda and decision-making entirely.

- There is no collaboration or autonomy support; the patient is pressured rather than engaged.

### Strengths

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian, confrontational, and dismissive, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to use reflective listening, affirmations, or open questions.  
- Change talk is not cultivated or reinforced.

- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient concerns as “excuses.”  
   - Validate and normalize ambivalence and fears.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy Through Reflective Listening:**  
   - Use reflections to capture both content and emotion.  
   - Reflect ambivalence and fears to show understanding.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Support autonomy and evoke the patient’s own motivations.

4. **Use Open-Ended Questions to Explore Ambivalence:**  
   - Invite the patient to elaborate on fears and reasons for change.

5. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Reflect and reinforce statements indicating desire, ability, reasons, and need for change.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and validate the difficulty of change.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Realistic Plan:**  
   - Help the patient set achievable goals and identify supports.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Pressure:**  
   - Recognize that pushing too hard can increase resistance.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, empathy, and collaboration. The patient’s ambivalence and fears are dismissed rather than explored, and change talk is not effectively cultivated. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, empathetic, and collaborative approach that supports the patient’s autonomy and gently guides her toward change. Without these changes, the risk of patient disengagement and failure to change behavior is high.
