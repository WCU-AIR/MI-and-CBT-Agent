# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental, authoritarian stance throughout the session. From the outset, the tone is directive and lacks warmth or respect for the patient’s perspective. Phrases such as “I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do,” “You have to be active, no excuses,” and “Stop making excuses” communicate disapproval and disregard for the patient’s autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s concerns and feelings, failing to convey unconditional positive regard or acceptance. This approach likely alienates the patient and undermines rapport.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive, with no evidence of acceptance or positive regard.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective or feelings. The patient expresses difficulty balancing school and health habits, feelings of overwhelm, and a desire to start small, but the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead insists on rigid compliance. The lack of reflective listening or validation of the patient’s experience is stark. The psychiatrist’s responses are confrontational rather than understanding.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is essentially absent; the psychiatrist fails to acknowledge or validate the patient’s experience or ambivalence.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is completely lacking. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly issuing commands and ultimatums. There is no collaboration or invitation for the patient’s input; instead, the psychiatrist demands compliance and dismisses the patient’s attempts to negotiate or express ambivalence. Autonomy is not supported; the patient is pressured to “just do what I’m telling you” with no room for choice or self-direction.

**Summary:**  
The session is antithetical to MI spirit, characterized by coercion and disregard for patient autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. When the patient tentatively expresses willingness to “start small” or acknowledges the importance of health, these statements are ignored or dismissed. Instead of amplifying or exploring these change talk statements, the psychiatrist presses for immediate, full commitment. This approach likely suppresses further change talk and increases resistance.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated; the psychiatrist’s confrontational style likely inhibits patient motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s resistance, ambivalence, or reasons for maintaining current behavior) is met with confrontation and demands rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s concerns as “excuses” and refuses to acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s barriers or feelings. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and escalated rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation, sets rigid expectations, and ignores the patient’s input. The patient’s attempts to express concerns or negotiate are dismissed. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
No partnership is established; the psychiatrist assumes full control and authority.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.  
- Empathy and reflective listening are essential to understand and validate the patient’s perspective.  
- The spirit of MI requires collaboration and autonomy support, not coercion.  
- Change talk should be evoked and reinforced, not suppressed.  
- Sustain talk must be skillfully softened, not confronted.  
- Partnership and shared decision-making are critical for engagement and motivation.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and partnership.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from authoritarian commands to a partnership approach that respects the patient’s autonomy and expertise in their own life.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   Reflect the patient’s statements and feelings to demonstrate understanding and build rapport.

3. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses any motivation or willingness, explore and reinforce these statements to build momentum.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalization to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.

5. **Ask Open-Ended Questions:**  
   Encourage elaboration and exploration rather than yes/no answers.

6. **Avoid Ultimatums and Demands:**  
   Recognize that change is a process and support gradual steps rather than insisting on immediate full compliance.

7. **Collaboratively Develop Plans:**  
   Involve the patient in setting realistic, personalized goals that fit their life context.

8. **Provide Affirmations:**  
   Recognize and affirm patient strengths, efforts, and intentions to encourage engagement.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive style undermines rapport, suppresses motivation, and alienates the patient. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically change their approach to one grounded in empathy, collaboration, and respect for patient autonomy. Without these changes, the likelihood of meaningful behavior change is minimal, and the therapeutic relationship is at risk.
