# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance is largely judgmental and directive from the outset. The opening statement, “Today we’re going to get straight to the point… I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do,” sets a controlling and authoritarian tone rather than one of acceptance or collaboration. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s efforts with statements like “Why didn’t you just stop altogether if you know it’s bad for you?” and “You have to stop making excuses,” which convey disapproval and a lack of unconditional positive regard. There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or validation of the patient’s struggle. The patient’s ambivalence and progress are met with skepticism rather than support.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive approach, lacking the fundamental acceptance necessary for effective motivational interviewing.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence and acknowledges progress, yet the psychiatrist dismisses these with blunt commands and challenges. For example, when the patient explains the difficulty of quitting cold turkey and the role of routine and stress, the psychiatrist responds with “Look, you can’t just say ‘I’m trying’ and expect things to change,” rather than reflecting or exploring these feelings. There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s experience or emotional state. The psychiatrist’s questions are primarily interrogative and accusatory, not empathic.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist fails to demonstrate reflective listening or perspective-taking, missing opportunities to connect with the patient’s ambivalence and emotional reality.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with frequent persuasion and little collaboration. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You have to pick a quit date now,” “No more cutting down slowly,” “No excuses”) and challenges the patient’s statements as “making excuses.” There is no evidence of evocation of the patient’s own motivations or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore their values or reasons for change beyond insisting on compliance. The patient is positioned as someone who must obey rather than as an active partner in change.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the session is dominated by a controlling, prescriptive approach that undermines collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**  
While the patient offers some change talk (e.g., “I want that too,” “I’m trying here—I cut back,” “I’m committed”), the psychiatrist does little to reinforce or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist challenges or dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and progress, which risks suppressing further change talk. The psychiatrist does eventually accept a quit date proposed by the patient, but only after persistent pressure and insistence. There is no strategic use of reflections or affirmations to deepen or sustain the patient’s motivation.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not effectively cultivated; the psychiatrist’s confrontational style likely inhibits the patient’s willingness to elaborate on motivations.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk and ambivalence are met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s explanations as “excuses” and “procrastination,” and repeatedly demands immediate, uncompromising action. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. The patient’s concerns about readiness and fear of failure are ignored or invalidated, which likely increases resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced or harshly challenged rather than softened; the psychiatrist misses critical opportunities to reduce resistance and support ambivalence resolution.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritarian role throughout the session. The patient is not invited to share their agenda or collaborate on goals. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues ultimatums and demands compliance, minimizing the patient’s autonomy and expertise in their own experience. The only moment of apparent collaboration is when the patient proposes a quit date, but even this is framed as grudging acceptance rather than genuine partnership.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is minimal to nonexistent; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and fails to engage the patient as an equal collaborator.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on tobacco cessation and attempts to set a quit date.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian, confrontational, and directive.  
- There is a pervasive lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- Change talk is not cultivated; sustain talk is harshly challenged rather than softened.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is absent.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).  
- The patient remains engaged and expresses some change talk despite the challenging style.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is overly directive, confrontational, and lacking in acceptance and empathy.  
- There is a failure to build partnership or support autonomy.  
- Change talk is not effectively evoked or reinforced.  
- Sustain talk is harshly challenged rather than softened, increasing resistance.  
- The psychiatrist does not use reflections, affirmations, or summaries to deepen motivation or engagement.  
- The session lacks the collaborative, evocative spirit essential to motivational interviewing.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Avoid issuing commands or ultimatums. Invite the patient’s perspective and preferences.  
   - Use inclusive language that emphasizes partnership and shared decision-making.

2. **Increase Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Validate the patient’s efforts and struggles.  
   - Use reflective listening to demonstrate understanding of ambivalence and emotional experience.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Ask questions that invite elaboration and exploration of motivations and barriers.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to deepen engagement.

4. **Cultivate Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or commitment, reinforce and amplify these statements.  
   - Explore reasons, ability, and confidence to change.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change and reduce resistance.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.  
   - Avoid pressuring the patient to set immediate quit dates; instead, collaboratively explore readiness.

7. **Use Affirmations and Summaries:**  
   - Recognize and affirm patient strengths and progress.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and clarify next steps.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines acceptance, empathy, and partnership. Change talk is not cultivated, and sustain talk is harshly challenged, increasing resistance. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, empathic approach that supports autonomy and skillfully evokes and reinforces the patient’s own motivations for change. Without these fundamental shifts, the session risks alienating the patient and impeding progress toward smoking cessation.
