# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental, confrontational, and directive stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“I’m here to help you fix the problem… I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do”—the tone is authoritarian and dismissive of the patient’s perspective. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or respect for the patient’s autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s behavior as “excuses” and “not taking it seriously,” which is likely to increase resistance rather than foster openness. The patient’s defensiveness and resistance are met with escalating pressure rather than acceptance or empathy.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance or respect, instead adopting a harsh, directive approach that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is virtually no demonstration of empathy or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, feelings, or ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with commands and accusations (“You’re just making excuses,” “You have to quit immediately”), ignoring the patient’s expressed concerns about social life and readiness. The patient’s statements about valuing social connections and feeling that quitting “cold turkey” is unrealistic are dismissed rather than explored or validated. This lack of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s increasing defensiveness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective, missing critical opportunities to build rapport and explore ambivalence.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely devoid of the MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding compliance (“Just say yes and do it,” “No exceptions”). There is no evidence of partnership or eliciting the patient’s own motivations or values. Autonomy is explicitly denied, with the psychiatrist insisting on immediate behavior change without negotiation or exploration. This approach is antithetical to MI principles and likely to increase resistance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to embody the MI spirit, instead using coercion and directive commands that undermine collaboration and autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not evoke or reinforce any change talk. The patient offers minimal change talk spontaneously (e.g., acknowledging presence at the party), but the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these moments rather than exploring or amplifying them. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on pressing the patient to commit to immediate cessation without eliciting the patient’s own reasons, desires, or confidence for change. This results in no meaningful evocation of change talk and no movement toward motivation.

**Summary:**  
No skillful evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the psychiatrist misses opportunities to draw out the patient’s own motivations.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly confronts and challenges sustain talk with accusations and demands, rather than softening or exploring it. The patient’s sustain talk—minimizing the problem, valuing social life, denying responsibility—is met with blunt commands and labeling as “excuses.” There are no double-sided reflections, no normalization of ambivalence, and no attempts to gently redirect sustain talk toward change. This approach likely entrenches resistance and defensiveness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk, instead reinforcing resistance through confrontational responses.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish or maintain a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and commands, with no invitation for the patient’s input or shared decision-making. The patient is repeatedly told what to do, with no acknowledgment of their expertise or lived experience. The power differential is stark and unmitigated, undermining any sense of equality or mutual respect.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Begin sessions by expressing respect and curiosity about the patient’s perspective. Avoid telling the patient what to do; instead, invite their input.

2. **Use Open Questions and Reflective Listening:**  
   Ask open-ended questions to explore the patient’s thoughts and feelings. Reflect back what the patient says to demonstrate understanding and build rapport.

3. **Demonstrate Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   Validate the patient’s feelings and experiences, even if you disagree with their choices. Avoid judgmental language.

4. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses any desire, ability, reason, or need for change, explore and amplify these statements.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalization to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Emphasize that the patient has the right and power to make their own choices, and that your role is to support them.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   Work with the patient to set realistic, achievable goals based on their readiness and motivation.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Commands:**  
   Replace “you have to” and “just do it” with invitations and collaborative language.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational approach alienates the patient, fails to build rapport, and does not facilitate motivation or change. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation of the patient’s own motivations. Without these changes, the risk of harm and disengagement is high.
