# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive stance from the outset. The opening statement, “I’m here to help you fix this drinking problem,” is authoritative and lacks warmth or respect for the patient’s autonomy. The repeated insistence on “you need to cut down immediately” and “you really must take this seriously” conveys disapproval rather than acceptance. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s feelings or ambivalence and instead pushes a rigid agenda. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or normalization of the patient’s struggle.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive, undermining acceptance and rapport-building.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence and the role of alcohol in relaxation and mood regulation, but the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead challenges the patient with “Why haven’t you done that already?” and “You really must take this seriously.” There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s experience or emotions. The tone is confrontational rather than empathic.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is largely absent; the psychiatrist fails to listen or reflect the patient’s feelings and perspective.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with the psychiatrist repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding immediate compliance. There is no collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s expressed concerns and readiness, insisting on a strict plan without negotiation. This approach is antithetical to the MI spirit.  

**Summary:**  
The session lacks collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support; it is rigid and controlling.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the psychiatrist attempts to elicit commitment to change (“Are you going to commit to one glass a night starting immediately?”), this is done in a coercive manner rather than through skillful evocation. The patient does express some change talk (willingness to cut back, desire to feel better), but the psychiatrist does not reinforce or amplify these statements. Instead, the patient’s ambivalence is met with confrontation, which risks increasing resistance rather than fostering change talk.  

**Summary:**  
Some attempt to elicit change talk is present but is undermined by confrontational style and lack of reinforcement.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften or acknowledge sustain talk. Instead, sustain talk (patient’s ambivalence, need for gradual change) is met with dismissal and pressure (“You have to stop rationalizing,” “No, that won’t work”). There are no double-sided reflections or normalization of ambivalence. The approach likely exacerbates sustain talk and resistance.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is ignored or confronted harshly, with no attempt to soften or explore ambivalence.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritarian role throughout, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and refusing to negotiate or collaborate. The patient’s input is dismissed, and the psychiatrist insists on a non-negotiable plan. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s expertise in her own life.  

**Summary:**  
No partnership or collaboration; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and excludes the patient from decision-making.

---

### **Overall Global Ratings Summary**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is rigid, confrontational, and directive, lacking the core elements of motivational interviewing: acceptance, empathy, collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support. The patient’s ambivalence and change talk are met with pressure and dismissal, likely increasing resistance and reducing motivation. This session falls far short of MI standards.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (alcohol use).  
- The patient expresses some change talk and tentative goals.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly confrontational, judgmental, and directive, undermining rapport and motivation.  
- There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- No reflective listening or affirmations are used.  
- Open questions are absent; the psychiatrist relies on closed, leading questions.  
- Sustain talk is not softened but confronted, increasing resistance.  
- Autonomy is ignored; the patient’s readiness and preferences are dismissed.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs.  
- Attempts to evoke change talk are coercive rather than skillful.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling or blaming language.  
   - Express genuine respect and positive regard for the patient’s experience and ambivalence.

2. **Enhance Empathy and Reflective Listening:**  
   - Use reflections to validate and explore the patient’s feelings and motivations.  
   - Reflect sustain talk and change talk to deepen understanding.

3. **Support Autonomy and Partnership:**  
   - Invite the patient to share her perspective and preferences.  
   - Collaboratively set goals and plans that fit the patient’s readiness.

4. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Encourage elaboration and exploration rather than yes/no answers.

5. **Skillfully Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Reinforce statements favoring change.  
   - Explore the patient’s values and reasons for change.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.

7. **Collaborative Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Summarize key points and change talk to reinforce motivation.  
   - Develop a realistic, patient-centered plan with clear next steps.

8. **Avoid Coercion and Pressure:**  
   - Frame suggestions as options rather than demands.  
   - Respect the patient’s pace and readiness for change.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental approach undermines rapport, empathy, and patient motivation. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, accepting, and autonomy-supportive style that skillfully evokes and reinforces the patient’s own motivations for change. Without these changes, the risk of patient resistance and treatment failure is high.
