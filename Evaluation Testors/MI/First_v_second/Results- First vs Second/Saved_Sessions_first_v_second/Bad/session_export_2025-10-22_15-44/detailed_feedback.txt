# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental, directive, and confrontational stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“Today we’re going to get straight to the point... I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do”—the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian tone that dismisses the patient’s autonomy and perspective. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or respect for the patient’s experience or ambivalence. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s feelings and choices, using language such as “You don’t get to decide,” “No excuses,” and “You have to quit now,” which is coercive and dismissive. This approach likely increases patient resistance and defensiveness rather than fostering openness.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate acceptance, instead adopting a harsh, judgmental, and directive style that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no evidence of empathic understanding or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand the patient’s perspective or emotional state beyond a superficial level. The patient expresses ambivalence, frustration, and a need for autonomy, but the psychiatrist ignores these cues and responds with repeated commands and challenges. The psychiatrist never reflects or validates the patient’s feelings, struggles, or rationale. For example, when the patient expresses that quitting “feels like too much right now” or that “it’s something I do on my own terms,” the psychiatrist dismisses these statements rather than exploring or acknowledging them. This lack of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s resistance and disengagement.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows no genuine effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, missing critical opportunities for empathic connection.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do without invitation or collaboration. The patient’s autonomy is explicitly denied (“You don’t get to decide,” “No more excuses”), and the psychiatrist uses ultimatums and pressure tactics. There is no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values; instead, the psychiatrist imposes a rigid agenda. This approach is antithetical to MI principles and likely to provoke resistance rather than motivation.  

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by a directive, controlling approach with no collaboration, evocation, or respect for autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient occasionally offers tentative change talk (e.g., “I’m open to cutting back,” “I want to get there”), the psychiatrist ignores these openings and instead focuses on commands and demands. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify the patient’s own reasons or desires for change. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s ambivalence and readiness, which likely suppresses further change talk.  

**Summary:**  
No effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk; missed opportunities to build motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften or skillfully respond to sustain talk. The patient expresses sustain talk through minimizing use, emphasizing control, and expressing reluctance to quit immediately. Rather than acknowledging or exploring these statements, the psychiatrist confronts and challenges them aggressively. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. This confrontational style likely entrenches sustain talk and resistance.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist repeatedly asserts control and authority, telling the patient what to do and when to do it. The patient’s expertise in his own life is ignored, and the psychiatrist does not invite collaboration or input. The repeated ultimatums and demands create a power imbalance that undermines partnership.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to establish a collaborative, equal partnership; the interaction is authoritarian and directive.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from telling and directing to partnering and inviting.  
   - Respect the patient’s autonomy and expertise in their own life.

2. **Develop Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Use reflective listening to understand and validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.  
   - Avoid judgmental or confrontational language.

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions to explore the patient’s perspective and motivations.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to deepen understanding.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and reinforce these statements.  
   - Explore the patient’s values and goals related to health.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change and validate the patient’s experience.

6. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Instead of demanding a quit date, explore readiness and collaboratively set achievable goals.  
   - Support gradual steps if needed, respecting the patient’s pace.

7. **Avoid Ultimatums and Pressure:**  
   - Recognize that pressure often increases resistance.  
   - Foster intrinsic motivation rather than compliance.

Without these changes, the likelihood of effective intervention and positive patient outcomes is minimal.
