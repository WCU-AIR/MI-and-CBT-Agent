# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly demonstrates a judgmental, directive, and confrontational stance rather than unconditional positive regard. From the outset, the psychiatrist invalidates Maria’s emotional experience by telling her to “stop thinking about suicide” and that “it’s not the answer,” which dismisses her pain and struggle. The tone throughout is authoritarian and blaming, with statements like “You have to stop feeling sorry for yourself” and “You must take responsibility here.” There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or validation of Maria’s suffering. The psychiatrist fails to normalize or acknowledge the difficulty of Maria’s experience, instead repeatedly implying fault and weakness. This approach is likely to increase resistance and disengagement rather than foster trust.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is harsh, directive, and lacking in acceptance. This is a critical failure in MI, especially with a vulnerable client presenting with suicidality.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect Maria’s perspective. There are no reflective statements that capture Maria’s emotional pain, ambivalence, or fears. Instead, the psychiatrist interrupts or overrides Maria’s expressions of distress with commands and accusations. For example, when Maria says she feels overwhelmed and scared, the psychiatrist responds with “You have to stop feeling sorry for yourself,” which is dismissive and invalidating. The psychiatrist does not attempt to paraphrase or explore Maria’s feelings, nor does he acknowledge the complexity of her experience. This lack of empathy undermines rapport and safety.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is virtually absent. The psychiatrist fails to listen reflectively or convey understanding of Maria’s emotional state.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is completely missing. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling Maria what she “has to” do and demanding compliance. There is no evidence of partnership or eliciting Maria’s own motivations or values. Autonomy is undermined by commands and threats rather than supported. The psychiatrist does not evoke Maria’s reasons for change or explore her ambivalence in a respectful way. Instead, the interaction is coercive and confrontational, which is contraindicated in MI and especially harmful with suicidal clients.

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by directive, controlling behavior with no collaboration or evocation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. When Maria tentatively expresses desire to try or hope for relief, the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these statements, focusing instead on compliance and responsibility. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify Maria’s change talk or explore her motivations. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to deepen Maria’s expressions of hope or willingness to try. Instead, the focus remains on adherence to treatment as an obligation, which likely stifles change talk.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated or reinforced; the psychiatrist’s responses suppress client motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk—Maria’s expressions of hopelessness, fear, and ambivalence—is met with confrontation and blame rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels Maria’s feelings as “excuses” or “feeling sorry for yourself,” which reinforces sustain talk defensively. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate ambivalence while gently guiding toward change. The psychiatrist’s approach likely entrenches sustain talk and resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation with commands and demands, ignoring Maria’s attempts to express her experience and needs. The power differential is stark and unmitigated. The psychiatrist does not invite Maria’s input or collaborate on goals or plans. This authoritarian approach is antithetical to MI principles.

**Summary:**  
No partnership or collaboration; the psychiatrist assumes full control and authority.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacking in MI spirit.  
- There is a critical failure to validate and empathize with Maria’s emotional pain.  
- The psychiatrist does not use reflective listening or affirmations.  
- Change talk is not evoked or reinforced; sustain talk is harshly confronted.  
- No partnership or autonomy support is provided.  
- The style is likely to increase client resistance and disengagement, especially dangerous in a suicidal client.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Begin sessions by validating the client’s pain and struggle. Avoid blaming or shaming language.

2. **Develop Reflective Listening Skills:**  
   - Use reflections to demonstrate understanding of the client’s feelings and ambivalence.

3. **Shift from Directive Commands to Collaborative Exploration:**  
   - Use open-ended questions to explore the client’s perspective and motivations.

4. **Evoke and Reinforce Change Talk:**  
   - When the client expresses hope or desire to try, reflect and amplify these statements.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and validate ambivalence to reduce resistance.

6. **Build Partnership and Support Autonomy:**  
   - Invite the client to co-create goals and plans. Respect their right to make choices.

7. **Avoid Confrontational or Blaming Language:**  
   - Replace “stop feeling sorry for yourself” with empathetic statements acknowledging the difficulty of change.

8. **Enhance Engagement and Safety:**  
   - Prioritize building rapport and trust, especially with suicidal clients, before focusing on adherence.
