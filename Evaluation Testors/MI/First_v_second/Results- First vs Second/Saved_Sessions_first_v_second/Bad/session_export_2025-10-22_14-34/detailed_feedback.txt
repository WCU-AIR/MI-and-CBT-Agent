# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental, directive, and authoritarian from the outset. The opening statement, “Today we’re going to get straight to the point about your health habits, and I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do to improve,” immediately establishes a controlling and non-collaborative tone. This stance dismisses the patient’s autonomy and fails to communicate any unconditional positive regard. Throughout the session, the psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s perspective, uses confrontational language (“You have to stop making excuses,” “This isn’t optional”), and shows no warmth or support. There is no evidence of respect for the patient’s current stage of change or ambivalence. This is far from the acceptance expected in MI, which requires a nonjudgmental, supportive stance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is highly judgmental and directive, lacking any genuine acceptance or respect for the patient’s autonomy or perspective.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal to no empathy. The patient’s expressions of ambivalence, limitations, and personal context are ignored or dismissed. For example, when the patient explains that time is tight or that they feel fit enough, the psychiatrist responds with commands rather than reflective listening or validation. There are no reflective statements that capture the patient’s feelings or perspective. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s concerns or the complexity of behavior change, instead opting for blunt commands and admonishments. This lack of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s defensive and disengaged responses.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist does not demonstrate understanding or validation of the patient’s perspective, resulting in a cold, confrontational interaction.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do without eliciting the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change. There is no evocation of the patient’s values or goals, no partnership, and no support for autonomy. Instead, the psychiatrist issues ultimatums and demands compliance, which is antithetical to MI principles. The patient’s autonomy is repeatedly undermined, and the psychiatrist does not invite or explore the patient’s perspective beyond superficial questioning.

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by a directive, controlling approach with no evidence of MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce any change talk. When the patient tentatively offers mild change talk (e.g., “I guess I could try to drink more water”), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses it, instead doubling down on commands. There is no strategic reflection or amplification of change talk to build motivation. The psychiatrist does not ask open-ended questions designed to elicit reasons, desires, or confidence for change. The patient’s ambivalence is met with confrontation rather than exploration, which likely suppresses any emerging motivation.

**Summary:**  
No effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk; missed opportunities to build motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with direct confrontation and dismissal rather than skillful softening or reframing. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s statements with ultimatums and accusations (“You have to stop making excuses,” “This isn’t optional”), which likely increases resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s ambivalence or legitimate concerns. The psychiatrist fails to normalize sustain talk or use it as a bridge to evoke change talk.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened or redirected, increasing resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation, issuing commands and ultimatums without inviting the patient’s input or negotiating goals. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of orders rather than an active participant in their care. The psychiatrist does not share power or acknowledge the patient’s expertise in their own life. The repeated use of “You have to” and “No excuses” language reinforces a hierarchical, authoritarian dynamic.

**Summary:**  
No partnership or shared decision-making; the interaction is authoritarian and directive.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary  
**Overall Score: 1/5**  
This session exemplifies a complete failure to apply motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian, confrontational, and directive, lacking acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation. The patient’s ambivalence is met with resistance rather than exploration, and no change talk is cultivated. This style is likely to increase patient resistance and reduce motivation for change.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Begin sessions with open, nonjudgmental invitations to discuss health behaviors. Avoid directives and ultimatums.

2. **Demonstrate Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s perspective and ambivalence. Avoid confrontation.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   Ask questions that invite the patient to explore their own motivations, values, and concerns.

4. **Affirm Strengths and Efforts:**  
   Recognize and reinforce any positive behaviors or intentions the patient expresses.

5. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses any desire or ability to change, reflect and amplify these statements.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalization to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.

7. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control their own change process.

8. **Collaboratively Develop Plans:**  
   Work with the patient to set realistic, achievable goals rather than imposing demands.

9. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   Use summaries to reinforce motivation and clarify next steps.
