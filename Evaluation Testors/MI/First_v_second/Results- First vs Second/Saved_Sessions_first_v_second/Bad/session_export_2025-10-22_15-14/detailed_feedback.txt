# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist dismisses the patient’s actual concern (anxiety and avoidance) and instead imposes a rigid agenda focused on “getting on track” and “what you need to change today.” The language is harsh and critical, e.g., “You have to get serious about this,” “No more waiting for motivation,” “You have to be tougher on yourself,” and “No excuses.” This approach lacks any warmth, empathy, or unconditional positive regard. The patient’s vulnerability and anxiety are met with impatience and pressure rather than acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s experience by labeling anxiety as “excuses” and “all in your head,” which is dismissive and likely to increase resistance. There is no normalization or validation of the patient’s struggle.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive, lacking respect and support. This is a critical failure in MI practice and risks alienating the patient.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses anxiety, self-doubt, and ambivalence, but the psychiatrist ignores these emotional cues and instead pushes a rigid, willpower-based solution. There are no reflective statements that capture the patient’s feelings or struggles. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s vulnerability or the difficulty of anxiety-driven avoidance. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly challenges and confronts the patient with “You have to…” statements, which shuts down empathic connection. The patient’s hesitations and fears are met with impatience rather than understanding.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is essentially absent. The psychiatrist fails to listen reflectively or validate the patient’s emotional experience, undermining rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You have to…,” “No excuses,” “No backing out”) and dismisses the patient’s input or ambivalence. The patient is not invited to explore her own motivations or values; instead, the psychiatrist imposes a fixed plan (5 PM start time) without negotiation. Autonomy is explicitly denied (“You don’t get to pick the time”). The psychiatrist’s stance is controlling and coercive, which is antithetical to the MI spirit.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is completely absent. The session is dominated by control and persuasion rather than partnership and evocation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the psychiatrist attempts to elicit commitment to a start time, this is done through pressure and insistence rather than skillful evocation. The patient offers tentative change talk (“I want to try,” “I’ll set the alarm”), but the psychiatrist does not reinforce or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and low readiness, demanding an unrealistic level of certainty (“You have to be at least an 8 or 9”). There is no exploration of the patient’s own reasons for change or confidence. The change talk is coerced rather than cultivated.  

**Summary:**  
Some change talk is elicited, but it is forced and not skillfully nurtured or amplified.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s anxiety, fear, and ambivalence) is met with confrontation and dismissal. The psychiatrist labels anxiety as “excuses” and “all in your head,” and repeatedly challenges the patient to “stop being scared” and “just do it.” There are no double-sided reflections, no normalization of ambivalence, and no gentle redirection. Instead, sustain talk is met with increased pressure and demands, likely exacerbating resistance.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead reinforces resistance through confrontation.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The patient’s input is overridden repeatedly, and the psychiatrist insists on a fixed plan without negotiation. The patient is denied choice and control (“You don’t get to pick the time”). The psychiatrist’s language is authoritarian and controlling, creating a clear power imbalance. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s expertise in her own experience.  

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and excludes the patient from meaningful participation.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling anxiety as “excuses” or “all in your head.”  
   - Validate the patient’s struggle and normalize ambivalence.

2. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Capture the patient’s fears and doubts empathetically.

3. **Support Autonomy and Collaboration:**  
   - Invite the patient to co-create the plan and choose start times.  
   - Avoid imposing fixed plans or ultimatums.

4. **Evoke Change Talk Skillfully:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions about reasons, desires, and confidence for change.  
   - Amplify and reinforce any change talk the patient offers.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Avoid confrontation; gently guide toward change.

6. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize any attempts or willingness to try.  
   - Build confidence through specific affirmations.

7. **Summarize Progress and Plan Collaboratively:**  
   - Summarize change talk and patient statements to consolidate motivation.  
   - Develop a realistic, patient-centered plan with agreed-upon steps.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian, confrontational, and dismissive of the patient’s emotional experience and autonomy. This style is likely to increase resistance and reduce engagement. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, empathic, and autonomy-supportive stance, use reflective listening, and skillfully evoke and reinforce the patient’s own motivations for change. Without these changes, the intervention risks being ineffective or harmful.
