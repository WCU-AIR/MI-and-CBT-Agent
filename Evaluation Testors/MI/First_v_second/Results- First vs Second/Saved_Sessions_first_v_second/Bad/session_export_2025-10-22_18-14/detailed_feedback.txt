# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and confrontational from the outset. Phrases such as “We need to talk about what you’re doing that’s harming you and how you’re going to fix it,” “You really need to cut down or stop completely,” and “You have to be tougher and just do it” communicate disapproval and a lack of unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s motivations and frames the patient’s explanations as “excuses,” which undermines acceptance. There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or normalization of the patient’s struggles. The tone is authoritarian and directive rather than supportive.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to establish a respectful, nonjudgmental stance. The patient is met with criticism and pressure rather than acceptance, which likely impedes rapport and openness.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses stress, ambivalence, and a need for gradual change, but the psychiatrist dismisses these concerns repeatedly (“No, you don’t get to say it’s ‘not that simple’,” “No middle ground here”). There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s feelings or struggles. The psychiatrist does not paraphrase or summarize the patient’s emotional experience, missing opportunities to demonstrate understanding. The patient’s guarded and resistant responses suggest the lack of empathic connection.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited and inconsistent. The psychiatrist does not convey accurate understanding or emotional attunement, which likely contributes to patient resistance.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive stance with frequent persuasion and confrontation. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues ultimatums (“You have to quit completely—no ifs, ands, or buts,” “No more ‘maybe’ or ‘starting’”), ignoring the patient’s autonomy and readiness. There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation of the patient’s own motivations, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore ambivalence in a respectful way. The patient’s attempts to negotiate a middle ground or express concerns are dismissed or met with pressure.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent. The approach is coercive and controlling, undermining partnership, evocation, and autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient does express some change talk (e.g., willingness to throw out alcohol, desire to improve health), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce this language. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on commands and demands rather than eliciting the patient’s own reasons and plans for change. The patient’s tentative commitments are met with pressure rather than encouragement or amplification. There is no strategic use of reflections or open questions to deepen change talk.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not effectively cultivated or amplified. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to build motivation through supportive responses.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s ambivalence, stress-related drinking, fear of abrupt change) is met with confrontation and dismissal. The psychiatrist labels patient explanations as “excuses” and insists on immediate, total cessation without acknowledging the difficulty or legitimacy of the patient’s concerns. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. The patient’s sustain talk is likely reinforced by the confrontational style, increasing resistance.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is not softened but rather exacerbated by the psychiatrist’s harsh responses.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist dominates the conversation, issuing ultimatums and ignoring the patient’s perspective. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or negotiation. The patient’s attempts to express readiness for gradual change or need for support are overridden. The psychiatrist does not invite collaboration or acknowledge the patient’s expertise in his own life. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.  

**Summary:**  
Partnership is minimal to nonexistent. The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritarian stance.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently emphasizes the seriousness of the health risks.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is judgmental, confrontational, and directive.  
- There is a lack of empathy, acceptance, and partnership.  
- The spirit of MI is absent, with no evocation or support for autonomy.  
- Change talk is not cultivated; sustain talk is not softened.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and barriers are dismissed rather than explored.  

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths

- The patient remains engaged enough to express ambivalence and tentative change talk.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is confrontational, judgmental, and directive, undermining MI principles.  
- There is a lack of empathy, acceptance, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or amplify change talk and does not soften sustain talk.  
- The patient’s autonomy and readiness are ignored, leading to resistance.  
- The session lacks reflective listening, affirmations, and collaborative planning.  
- The psychiatrist misses opportunities to build rapport and support gradual, realistic change.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses.”  
   - Use affirmations to recognize patient strengths and efforts.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy Through Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to validate patient experience.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen understanding.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing or commanding.  
   - Support patient autonomy and evoke their own motivations for change.

4. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When patient expresses willingness or reasons for change, reflect and reinforce these statements.  
   - Use open-ended questions to explore motivations and barriers.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change and validate coping strategies.

6. **Use Open Questions and Summaries:**  
   - Increase use of open-ended questions to invite elaboration.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and clarify plans.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Realistic Plan:**  
   - Negotiate achievable steps that fit the patient’s life context.  
   - Avoid ultimatums; instead, support gradual progress and readiness.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive style undermines rapport, empathy, and patient autonomy. The lack of reflective listening, affirmations, and collaborative planning results in patient resistance and limited motivation. To improve, the psychiatrist must fundamentally shift toward a respectful, empathic, and autonomy-supportive approach that skillfully evokes and reinforces the patient’s own motivations for change.
