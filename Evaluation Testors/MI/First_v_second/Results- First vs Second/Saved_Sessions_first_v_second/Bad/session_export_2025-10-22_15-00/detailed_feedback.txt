# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a largely judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the tone is authoritative and lacks warmth or genuine positive regard (e.g., “I’m going to get straight to the point and help you fix the problem,” “You have to stop making excuses”). The patient’s expressions of stress and difficulty are met with dismissive comments (“Stress is no excuse,” “You just have to be tougher than that”), which undermines acceptance. There is no normalization of struggle or validation of the patient’s experience. The psychiatrist’s repeated insistence on “no excuses” and “you have to” language conveys disapproval rather than unconditional positive regard.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive, lacking the respectful, supportive stance essential for MI. This risks alienating the patient and reducing engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient repeatedly shares the real challenges of stress and social triggers, but the psychiatrist responds with admonishments rather than reflective listening or validation. For example, when the patient describes difficulty avoiding smoking in social situations, the psychiatrist responds with “You just have to make the effort to avoid those situations,” ignoring the patient’s contextual realities. There are no complex or emotional reflections that capture the patient’s ambivalence or struggle. The psychiatrist’s responses are mostly factual or directive, lacking warmth or attunement.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist fails to demonstrate accurate understanding or convey that understanding to the patient, missing opportunities to build rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, expert-driven style with frequent persuasion and directive commands. The psychiatrist repeatedly tells the patient what to do, uses “you have to” language, and dismisses the patient’s concerns as “excuses.” There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s ideas or feelings, nor does he explore the patient’s motivations beyond insisting on compliance. The patient’s autonomy is undermined by the rigid, controlling approach.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent. The session is directive, controlling, and lacks partnership, evocation, or autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient expresses clear change talk (e.g., “I want to quit for good this time,” “I’m ready to take the medication”), the psychiatrist does little to evoke, reinforce, or amplify this language. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on commands and warnings about failure. There are no strategic reflections or questions designed to deepen the patient’s motivation or explore reasons for change. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore the patient’s commitment or confidence, and does not reinforce the patient’s expressed readiness.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not skillfully cultivated or amplified by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk related to stress and social triggers is met with confrontation and dismissal (“Stress is no excuse,” “You just have to be tougher”). The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or validate the patient’s ambivalence. Instead, sustain talk is challenged harshly, which risks increasing resistance. There is no attempt to gently redirect or normalize the difficulty of change.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced or confronted rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an expert, authoritative stance throughout, giving instructions and commands without inviting collaboration or input. The patient’s perspective is overridden by the psychiatrist’s agenda. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or co-creation of a plan. The psychiatrist’s language (“You have to,” “No ifs or buts”) minimizes the patient’s role as an active partner.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the session is dominated by an expert-driven, directive approach.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist gathers basic diagnostic information about smoking frequency and past quit attempts.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly directive, judgmental, and lacks empathy.  
- There is no collaboration or evocation of the patient’s motivations.  
- Change talk is not cultivated; sustain talk is confronted harshly.  
- The patient’s autonomy and lived experience are disregarded.  
- The session risks disengagement and resistance due to its confrontational tone.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Basic diagnostic information is obtained.  
- The patient expresses motivation and commitment despite the psychiatrist’s style.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is overly directive, confrontational, and lacks empathy.  
- There is no use of MI techniques such as reflective listening, affirmations, or evocation.  
- The patient’s autonomy and lived experience are disregarded, risking resistance.  
- Sustain talk is confronted rather than softened, increasing potential for disengagement.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing is present.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Respectful, Nonjudgmental Stance:**  
   - Avoid language that blames or shames the patient.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of quitting and validate the patient’s experience.

2. **Increase Empathy and Reflective Listening:**  
   - Use complex reflections to capture the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.  
   - Reflect both change talk and sustain talk to demonstrate understanding.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Foster collaboration and partnership rather than issuing commands.  
   - Support patient autonomy by inviting their ideas and choices.

4. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses motivation, reflect and explore it further.  
   - Ask open-ended questions to deepen reasons for change.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Avoid dismissing or confronting sustain talk harshly.

6. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Increase the use of open-ended questions to explore motivation and barriers.  
   - Affirm patient strengths, efforts, and commitment sincerely.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to create a realistic, personalized quitting plan.  
   - Summarize key points and reinforce commitment.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines acceptance, empathy, and collaboration. The patient’s motivation is not effectively evoked or supported, and sustain talk is met with harsh dismissal. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a fundamentally different approach grounded in respect, empathy, and partnership to effectively engage and support the patient in smoking cessation.
