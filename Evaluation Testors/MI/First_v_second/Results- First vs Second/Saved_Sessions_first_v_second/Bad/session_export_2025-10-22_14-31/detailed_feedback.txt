# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist dismisses the patient’s concerns and feelings, repeatedly labeling her efforts as excuses or insufficient. For example, statements like “You have to be stricter,” “Snacking is just an excuse,” and “Stop feeling sorry for yourself” communicate disapproval and lack of unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s emotional vulnerability or validate her experience, instead adopting a confrontational tone that likely increases patient resistance. There is no evidence of warmth, support, or respect for the patient’s perspective.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive, lacking the fundamental acceptance required in motivational interviewing. This severely undermines rapport and patient engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s feelings and perspective. The patient expresses frustration, discouragement, loneliness, and fear of failure, but the psychiatrist repeatedly ignores or invalidates these emotions. For example, when the patient says, “I’m scared it’ll just make me feel worse,” the psychiatrist responds with “You have to stop feeling sorry for yourself,” which dismisses the patient’s emotional experience. There are no reflective statements or attempts to paraphrase or validate the patient’s struggles. The psychiatrist’s responses are primarily confrontational commands rather than empathic reflections.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is essentially absent. The psychiatrist fails to convey understanding or emotional attunement, which likely exacerbates patient distress and disengagement.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with the psychiatrist repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding compliance (“Are you going to do that right now or not?”). There is no collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore her motivations or values. Instead, the patient is positioned as someone who must obey instructions or face failure. This approach contradicts the core MI principles of partnership, evocation, and autonomy support.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is completely absent. The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive stance that undermines motivation and engagement.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient occasionally expresses tentative willingness (“Maybe I’ll try the app someday”), the psychiatrist does not amplify or explore these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on demanding behavior change and dismisses ambivalence or hesitation. There are no open-ended questions or reflections designed to elicit the patient’s own reasons for change or confidence. The patient’s change talk is minimal and not nurtured.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is neither effectively evoked nor reinforced. The psychiatrist misses critical opportunities to explore and strengthen the patient’s motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s expressions of ambivalence, fear, and discouragement) is met with confrontation and invalidation rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels patient concerns as “excuses” or “feeling sorry for yourself,” which likely entrenches sustain talk rather than diminishes it. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and exacerbated by the psychiatrist’s confrontational responses rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The patient is repeatedly commanded to comply with the psychiatrist’s agenda, with no negotiation or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s expressed feelings and concerns, instead imposing a rigid plan (“You have to download that app right now”). There is no evidence of minimizing power differentials or working as equals.  

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance that alienates the patient.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must develop unconditional positive regard and acceptance.  
- Empathy and reflective listening skills need substantial improvement.  
- The session must shift from directive commands to collaborative exploration.  
- The psychiatrist should learn to evoke and reinforce change talk while softening sustain talk.  
- Partnership and autonomy support are critical to build rapport and motivation.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is confrontational, judgmental, and directive, which is counterproductive in motivational interviewing.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and partnership.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Practice unconditional positive regard and avoid labeling or blaming language.  
   - Validate the patient’s feelings and struggles.

2. **Develop Empathic Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen rapport.

3. **Shift from Directive to Collaborative Approach:**  
   - Invite the patient’s perspective and preferences.  
   - Use open-ended questions to explore ambivalence and motivation.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses willingness or desire, reflect and explore these statements.  
   - Avoid confronting or dismissing sustain talk; instead, use double-sided reflections.

5. **Support Autonomy and Partnership:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s control over decisions and pace of change.  
   - Collaboratively develop plans rather than issuing commands.

6. **Manage Sustain Talk Skillfully:**  
   - Acknowledge and normalize ambivalence.  
   - Avoid reinforcing sustain talk with judgmental responses.

7. **Enhance Engagement and Rapport:**  
   - Attend to the patient’s emotional state and respond with warmth and understanding.  
   - Avoid language that increases patient distress or withdrawal.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, judgmental, and directive style undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s motivation, and exacerbates sustain talk and patient distress. To improve, the psychiatrist must fundamentally change approach to embody acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and skillful evocation of change talk. Without these changes, the patient is unlikely to engage meaningfully or achieve behavior change.
