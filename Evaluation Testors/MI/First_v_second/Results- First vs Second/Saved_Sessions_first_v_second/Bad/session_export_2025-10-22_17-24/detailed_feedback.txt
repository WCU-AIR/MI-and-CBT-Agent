# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a largely judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the tone is authoritarian and lacks warmth or genuine positive regard. For example, the opening line, “I’m here to help you get on track today,” followed by “I’ll be telling you exactly what steps you must take,” immediately sets a controlling tone rather than one of acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s perspective and feelings, labeling her concerns as “excuses” and “overcomplicating,” which undermines acceptance. There is no evidence of normalization or unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s values or struggles respectfully, which is critical in MI.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive, lacking the fundamental MI principle of acceptance. This severely limits rapport and client engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence and the complexity of balancing health with life demands, but the psychiatrist ignores these nuances and responds with blunt commands and accusations (e.g., “Are you just not disciplined enough?”). There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s feelings or struggles. The psychiatrist does not demonstrate perspective-taking or emotional attunement, which results in the patient becoming defensive and disengaged.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited to a factual inquiry style without genuine reflective listening or validation of the patient’s experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive approach with no collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly insists on compliance (“You don’t get to negotiate,” “No room for maybe”), negating the patient’s autonomy and choice. There is no evidence of partnership or eliciting the patient’s own motivations. The psychiatrist’s style is coercive rather than motivational, which is antithetical to the spirit of MI.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive stance that undermines client autonomy and collaboration.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient offers some change talk (e.g., expressing desire to be consistent with yoga, acknowledging health importance), the psychiatrist fails to evoke, reinforce, or amplify this language. Instead, the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses the patient’s nuanced statements and pushes for immediate commitment without exploring underlying motivations. There are no strategic reflections or questions to deepen change talk. The psychiatrist’s repeated demands for a yes/no answer shut down the patient’s opportunity to express ambivalence or explore readiness.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not cultivated; the psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to evoke and reinforce motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s ambivalence, concerns about overwhelm, and need for manageable steps) is met with confrontation and dismissal. The psychiatrist labels patient concerns as “excuses” and “hesitation,” reinforcing sustain talk rather than softening it. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate ambivalence while gently guiding toward change. The confrontational style likely increases resistance and sustain talk rather than reducing it.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted and reinforced, with no skillful softening or strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish or maintain a collaborative partnership. The patient is repeatedly told what to do with no negotiation or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist explicitly states, “We’re not working on anything together,” and “You don’t get to negotiate,” which clearly communicates a power imbalance and lack of partnership. The patient’s expertise and values are disregarded, and the psychiatrist dominates the interaction.  

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the interaction is authoritarian and directive, undermining client-centeredness.

- The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian, confrontational, and directive, which is antithetical to MI principles.  
- There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directive commands to partnership language. Invite the patient’s perspective and co-create goals.  

2. **Increase Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and struggles. Avoid judgmental or confrontational language.  

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Ask questions that encourage elaboration and exploration of motivations and barriers.  

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Reflect and reinforce statements that indicate desire, ability, reasons, and need for change.  

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and validate ambivalence rather than confronting or dismissing it.  

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace and nature of change.  

7. **Collaborative Planning:**  
   - Work with the patient to develop realistic, manageable steps toward goals.  

8. **Avoid Pressuring for Immediate Commitment:**  
   - Recognize readiness stages and support gradual movement toward change.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines acceptance, empathy, partnership, and evocation of change talk. The patient’s ambivalence and values are ignored or dismissed, resulting in resistance rather than motivation. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, empathetic approach that respects autonomy and skillfully navigates ambivalence to support sustainable behavior change.
