# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the tone is confrontational and authoritarian (“I’m going to get straight to the point and help you fix the problem,” “You have to take this seriously,” “No ifs, ands, or buts”). The patient’s defensiveness is met with repeated challenges and dismissals of his concerns rather than validation or understanding. There is little to no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s responses as “excuses” and demands compliance, which likely exacerbates resistance. This approach fails to create a safe, supportive environment necessary for effective MI.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive, lacking respect and support. This undermines rapport and patient engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses legitimate concerns about readiness, life responsibilities, and ambivalence, but these are met with dismissal rather than empathetic reflection. For example, when the patient explains that quitting beer cold turkey feels overwhelming, the psychiatrist responds with “No, that’s not good enough” and “You’re just making excuses again,” rather than exploring these feelings or validating the difficulty. The psychiatrist does not use reflective listening or attempt to capture the patient’s emotional experience, which would be critical to building empathy and trust.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist fails to acknowledge or validate the patient’s feelings and perspective, missing opportunities for reflective listening.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, expert-driven style with frequent commands and ultimatums (“You have to quit beer completely,” “No more excuses,” “Are you going to follow this plan or not?”). There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly ignores the patient’s expressed ambivalence and readiness, instead insisting on immediate, total compliance. This approach is antithetical to the MI spirit, which emphasizes partnership, evoking the patient’s own motivations, and respecting autonomy.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent. The psychiatrist adopts a coercive, directive stance that undermines collaboration and autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient does express some change talk (e.g., willingness to try walking, acknowledging health risks), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or amplify this language. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on commands and compliance, missing opportunities to explore the patient’s motivations or reasons for change. The patient’s tentative commitments are met with skepticism and demands for immediate total change, which likely stifles further change talk. The psychiatrist does not strategically reinforce or build on the patient’s statements that indicate readiness.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not effectively cultivated or reinforced; the psychiatrist’s approach likely inhibits further exploration of motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s ambivalence, concerns about readiness, and attachment to beer as a coping mechanism) is met with confrontation and dismissal (“You’re just making excuses,” “No middle ground here”). The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections, affirmations, or gentle redirection to soften sustain talk. Instead, sustain talk is met with ultimatums and pressure, which can increase resistance and disengagement.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened or explored, undermining the patient’s willingness to engage.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a hierarchical, expert-driven stance throughout, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding yes/no answers. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or negotiation. The patient’s input is largely ignored or dismissed, and the psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in his own life. This power imbalance is likely to alienate the patient and reduce motivation.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is minimal to nonexistent; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and fails to engage the patient as an equal collaborator.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on health goals (alcohol cessation, exercise).  
- The patient remains engaged despite the confrontational style.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance to foster acceptance.  
- Empathy and reflective listening need to be integrated to understand and validate patient experience.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, autonomy support—is absent and must be prioritized.  
- Change talk should be actively cultivated and sustain talk softened through strategic reflections.  
- Partnership must be developed through shared decision-making and respect for patient autonomy.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Supportive Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses.”  
   - Use warm, respectful language to build rapport.

2. **Increase Empathy and Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to validate patient experience.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen understanding.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than dictate.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy by acknowledging patient choice and pacing.

4. **Cultivate Change Talk and Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Reflect and amplify change talk when it arises.  
   - Use double-sided reflections and affirmations to gently explore sustain talk.

5. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions to invite elaboration.  
   - Affirm patient strengths, efforts, and values sincerely.

6. **Collaboratively Develop Plans and Summaries:**  
   - Co-create achievable goals with the patient.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and commitment.

7. **Avoid Ultimatums and Forced Compliance:**  
   - Recognize ambivalence as normal and work through it collaboratively.  
   - Allow the patient to set the pace for change.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines acceptance, empathy, and collaboration. Change talk is not cultivated, and sustain talk is harshly confronted, increasing resistance. To improve, the psychiatrist must fundamentally shift toward a respectful, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive approach that embraces the spirit and techniques of MI. Without these changes, patient engagement and motivation are unlikely to improve, risking poor health outcomes.
