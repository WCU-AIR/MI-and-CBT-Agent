# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. The opening statements such as “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do” and repeated insistence on quitting smoking immediately (“You really need to cut down immediately,” “This isn’t optional”) communicate a lack of unconditional positive regard. The patient’s resistance and frustration are met with pressure and confrontation rather than acceptance. There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or validation of the patient’s experience or feelings. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s autonomy or the difficulty of his situation, which likely exacerbates the patient’s defensiveness.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely authoritarian and directive, lacking respect for the patient’s perspective and autonomy, which undermines acceptance.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The responses are focused on delivering information and commands rather than listening or reflecting feelings. For example, the psychiatrist repeatedly asks “Why haven’t you tried quitting before?” and “Why won’t you just commit to quitting now?” without acknowledging the patient’s expressed frustration or ambivalence. The patient’s statements about side effects, fatigue with advice, and desire to focus on dental treatment are ignored or dismissed rather than explored empathetically. There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s emotional experience.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is superficial or absent; the psychiatrist fails to demonstrate understanding or convey that the patient’s feelings and experiences are heard and respected.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, expert-driven style with repeated commands and pressure to quit smoking immediately. There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly tells the patient what to do (“You have to quit,” “This isn’t optional”) and challenges the patient’s resistance aggressively. The patient’s autonomy is ignored, and the psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s perspective or engage in a partnership. The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy—is entirely absent.  

**Summary:**  
The session reflects a directive, confrontational approach that is antithetical to the spirit of motivational interviewing.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient occasionally expresses ambivalence or mild change talk (“I guess I got to really want to quit,” “Maybe part of me wants to stop”), the psychiatrist fails to reflect, amplify, or explore these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly returns to commands and questions that provoke resistance rather than eliciting the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change. There is no strategic use of open-ended questions or reflections to deepen change talk or build commitment.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is largely ignored or stifled by the psychiatrist’s confrontational style.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk and resistance are met with confrontation and pressure rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s sustain talk with “You have to quit,” “Why won’t you take this seriously?” and “This isn’t optional,” which likely increases resistance. There are no double-sided reflections, no normalization of ambivalence, and no attempts to validate the patient’s struggle. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to acknowledge the patient’s difficulties and to gently guide toward change.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and escalated rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritative stance throughout the session. There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The patient is repeatedly told what to do without invitation or collaboration. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in his own life or negotiate the agenda. The patient’s repeated requests to focus on dental treatment are ignored or overridden.  

**Summary:**  
The session lacks any meaningful partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and disregards the patient’s autonomy.

---

**Areas for Improvement:**

- There is no evocation or reinforcement of change talk.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- There is no use of reflective listening or affirmations.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and instead escalates sustain talk.  
- The patient’s autonomy and perspective are ignored, leading to increased resistance and disengagement.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs.  

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from telling the patient what to do to working with the patient as an equal partner.  
   - Invite the patient’s perspective and respect their autonomy.

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and experiences.  
   - Avoid judgmental or confrontational language.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Ask questions that invite elaboration and exploration of ambivalence.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses any desire or reason for change, reflect and explore it further.  
   - Avoid jumping to advice or commands.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Normalize ambivalence and validate the difficulty of quitting.

6. **Avoid Unsolicited Advice and Commands:**  
   - Ask permission before giving information or suggestions.  
   - Support the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to identify small, achievable steps.  
   - Summarize key points and reinforce commitment.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, increases resistance, and fails to engage the patient in meaningful dialogue about change. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive approach that skillfully evokes and reinforces the patient’s own motivations for change. Without these changes, the likelihood of effective intervention and patient engagement remains very low.
