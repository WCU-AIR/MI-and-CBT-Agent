# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently adopts a judgmental, confrontational, and directive tone throughout the session. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly accuses the patient of making excuses, procrastinating, and hiding behind fear. For example, statements like “You really need to stop making excuses,” “You’re just avoiding the real issue,” and “You can’t keep hiding behind fear and excuses” are harsh and dismissive. This approach likely increases patient resistance and disengagement. There is no normalization of struggle or validation of the patient’s experience, which is critical in motivational interviewing (MI).  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance is overtly judgmental and directive, lacking any acceptance or supportive attitude. This is a fundamental failure in MI practice.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal to no empathy. The patient expresses anxiety, fear, and ambivalence repeatedly, but the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these emotional cues. Instead of reflective listening or validating the patient’s feelings, the psychiatrist pushes a rigid agenda. For example, when the patient says, “I’m scared of failing or making things worse,” the psychiatrist responds with “You’re just making excuses to avoid responsibility,” which invalidates the patient’s emotional experience. There are no complex or simple reflections that demonstrate understanding of the patient’s perspective.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy or understanding, which is critical for building rapport and facilitating change talk.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly demanding commitment and compliance (“Are you going to commit to quitting today or not?”). There is no evidence of collaboration or evocation of the patient’s own motivations. Autonomy is explicitly denied by statements such as “There’s no other option” and “You have to quit now.” This approach is antithetical to MI principles and likely to increase resistance and reduce motivation.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is directive and coercive, lacking any collaborative or autonomy-supportive elements.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient offers multiple statements of desire, ability, and reasons for change (e.g., “I want to quit,” “I’m scared of failing,” “I want to feel better”), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these. Instead of reflecting or amplifying change talk, the psychiatrist repeatedly confronts the patient and pushes for immediate commitment. There are no strategic reflections or questions designed to deepen the patient’s motivation or confidence.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present in the patient’s speech but is not cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk. The patient expresses sustain talk related to anxiety, fear of failure, and feeling overwhelmed, but the psychiatrist responds with confrontational statements that reinforce resistance (“You’re just making excuses,” “You can’t keep blaming anxiety for everything”). There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate ambivalence while gently guiding toward change. This approach likely entrenches sustain talk rather than softening it.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than skillful softening or redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues ultimatums and demands compliance, ignoring the patient’s expressed concerns and preferences. The patient’s attempts to negotiate a more gradual approach or address anxiety first are dismissed outright. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in her own experience or invite collaboration.  

**Summary:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian stance with no partnership or client-centeredness.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary  

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.  
- Empathy and reflective listening are essential to understand and validate the patient’s experience.  
- The spirit of MI requires collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support, all absent here.  
- Change talk should be actively cultivated and sustain talk softened, not confronted.  
- Partnership and shared decision-making must replace authoritarian demands.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- None identified.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist must fundamentally change approach to align with MI principles.  
- Develop skills in reflective listening, empathy, and acceptance.  
- Use open-ended questions to explore patient’s perspective.  
- Actively evoke and reinforce change talk.  
- Soften sustain talk with double-sided reflections and validation.  
- Support patient autonomy and collaborate on planning.  
- Avoid confrontational, judgmental language that increases resistance.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental Stance:**  
   Replace confrontational language with acceptance and respect for the patient’s experience.

2. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and build rapport.

3. **Employ Open-Ended Questions:**  
   Invite the patient to explore ambivalence and motivations in her own words.

4. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and amplify these statements.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without reinforcing resistance.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

7. **Collaborate on Planning:**  
   Develop a shared, realistic plan that addresses both anxiety and smoking cessation.

8. **Avoid Ultimatums and Demands:**  
   Replace coercive language with invitations and partnership.

This session demonstrates a profound misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles.
