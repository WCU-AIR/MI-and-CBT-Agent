# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance throughout the session is largely judgmental and directive rather than accepting. From the outset, the psychiatrist uses language that implies blame and lack of understanding, such as “you really need to just make time for it,” “you have to push yourself—there’s no excuse,” and “stop making excuses.” These statements communicate disapproval rather than unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s struggles empathetically or normalize the difficulty of behavior change. There is no evidence of warmth or supportive affirmation. The patient’s hesitations and ambivalence are met with impatience and pressure rather than acceptance.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly authoritarian and critical, undermining acceptance. This risks alienating the patient and reducing engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses barriers such as fatigue, busy family life, and difficulty establishing habits, but the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses these concerns as “excuses” and insists on rigid solutions. There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s feelings or ambivalence. The psychiatrist does not paraphrase or summarize the patient’s experience, nor do they explore the emotional or practical challenges the patient faces. The tone is directive and lacks warmth or attunement.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to demonstrate understanding and to build rapport through reflective listening.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, expert-driven style. The psychiatrist repeatedly tells the patient what they “have to” do, uses commands (“No more excuses,” “You must take control”), and does not invite collaboration or autonomy. There is no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values, no support for autonomy, and no partnership. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s ambivalence and hesitations, instead pushing a rigid agenda. This approach is antithetical to the MI spirit, which emphasizes collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent. The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, directive stance that undermines motivation and engagement.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient offers some change talk (e.g., “I want to get back into it,” “Maybe I could clear a corner”), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce this language. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on pushing a predetermined plan and dismisses the patient’s ambivalence. There are no reflective or amplifying responses to change talk, nor are there strategic questions to deepen motivation or explore reasons for change. The psychiatrist’s interruptions and commands may suppress further change talk.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but poorly cultivated. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to evoke and strengthen the patient’s own motivations.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (e.g., patient’s concerns about fatigue, busy family life, difficulty maintaining habits) is met with confrontation and dismissal. The psychiatrist labels these as “excuses” and insists on “pushing through” without acknowledging the legitimacy of these barriers. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s responses likely increase resistance rather than soften sustain talk.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced rather than softened. The psychiatrist’s approach risks escalating patient defensiveness.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The patient is told what to do repeatedly, with no invitation to share power or co-create goals. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s hesitations and does not negotiate the agenda or plan. The patient’s autonomy is overridden by commands and ultimatums.  

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent. The psychiatrist dominates the interaction, undermining client-centeredness.

---

### Overall Global Ratings Summary  
The psychiatrist’s approach is directive, judgmental, and controlling, lacking the core elements of motivational interviewing. There is minimal acceptance, empathy, or partnership. Change talk is not cultivated, and sustain talk is confronted rather than softened. The session risks disengaging the patient and reducing motivation for change.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (exercise).  
- The patient remains polite and minimally engaged despite the confrontational style.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian, lacking acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- No reflective listening or affirmations are used.  
- Change talk is not skillfully evoked or reinforced.  
- Sustain talk is confronted rather than softened, increasing resistance.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs.  
- The psychiatrist fails to support autonomy or evoke the patient’s own motivations.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses.”  
   - Express unconditional positive regard and normalize ambivalence.

2. **Increase Empathy Through Reflective Listening:**  
   - Use reflections to validate the patient’s feelings and barriers.  
   - Paraphrase and summarize to demonstrate understanding.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own reasons and motivations for change.  
   - Support autonomy by emphasizing choice and control.

4. **Skillfully Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or ability, reflect and explore these statements.  
   - Ask open-ended questions that invite elaboration on motivations.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of behavior change.

6. **Use Affirmations:**  
   - Recognize and affirm patient strengths, efforts, and intentions.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Realistic Plan:**  
   - Negotiate goals and next steps with the patient’s input.  
   - Avoid ultimatums or coercion.

8. **Use Open Questions and Summaries:**  
   - Increase the use of open-ended questions to explore ambivalence and motivation.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and commitment.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, directive, and judgmental style undermines acceptance, empathy, and collaboration. Change talk is not cultivated, and sustain talk is confronted rather than softened. To improve, the psychiatrist must fundamentally shift toward a client-centered, empathetic, and evocative approach that supports autonomy and partnership. Without these changes, the session risks disengaging the patient and reducing the likelihood of meaningful behavior change.
