# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and confrontational stance throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian tone, telling the patient “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” which immediately undermines collaboration and respect. The patient’s efforts to reduce smoking are dismissed or minimized repeatedly (e.g., “cutting down is not enough,” “slow progress just isn’t good enough,” “stop making excuses”). The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s ambivalence with blunt demands and accusations (“Why didn’t you just stop already?”, “That’s on you,” “No more ‘maybe later’”). This approach is disempowering and likely to provoke resistance rather than engagement. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is harsh, directive, and lacking in acceptance. This severely damages rapport and trust, and is counterproductive to motivational interviewing principles.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy or reflective listening. The patient’s expressions of struggle, ambivalence, and progress are ignored or dismissed. For example, when the patient shares that cold turkey quitting has not worked before and that cutting down feels more manageable, the psychiatrist responds with repeated demands and accusations rather than understanding or validation. There are no reflective statements or attempts to understand the patient’s perspective or emotional experience. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s feelings of frustration, fear of failure, or desire for a sustainable approach.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is essentially absent. The psychiatrist does not listen or respond to the patient’s perspective, which undermines the therapeutic alliance.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is dominated by an authoritarian, directive style that contradicts the spirit of MI. There is no collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands a yes/no commitment to quitting cold turkey immediately, ignoring the patient’s readiness and preferences. The patient is positioned as someone who must comply or be blamed for worsening health outcomes. The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change, nor does he support the patient’s autonomy or self-efficacy.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is entirely absent. The session is coercive and controlling rather than collaborative and autonomy-supportive.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient offers multiple statements of change talk (e.g., “I cut down by two cigarettes,” “I want to quit for good,” “I’m trying to make real progress”), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these. Instead of reflecting or amplifying these statements, the psychiatrist challenges the patient’s approach and demands immediate cessation. There are no strategic reflections or questions to deepen the patient’s motivation or explore ambivalence constructively.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present in the patient’s speech but is not cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften sustain talk; rather, he confronts and challenges it aggressively. The patient’s sustain talk and ambivalence (e.g., difficulty quitting cold turkey, fear of failure, preference for gradual reduction) are met with accusations and demands. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence or validate the patient’s experience. This approach likely increases resistance and sustain talk rather than softening it.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than skillful softening or redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritarian role, telling the patient what to do and demanding compliance. There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The patient’s input is ignored or dismissed, and the psychiatrist repeatedly pressures the patient to make a binary decision. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.  

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and does not engage the patient as an equal collaborator.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.  
- Empathy and reflective listening are essential to understand and validate the patient’s experience.  
- The spirit of MI requires collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support, all missing here.  
- Change talk should be actively cultivated and sustain talk softened through skillful reflections.  
- Partnership and shared decision-making must replace authoritarian demands.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Respectful Stance:**  
   - Avoid blaming or shaming language. Recognize and affirm the patient’s efforts and struggles.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s statements to demonstrate understanding and validate feelings.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient, evoke their own motivations, and support autonomy.

4. **Cultivate Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and amplify these statements.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalize ambivalence to reduce resistance.

6. **Use Open Questions:**  
   - Invite the patient to elaborate on their thoughts and feelings rather than asking closed or leading questions.

7. **Affirm Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize the patient’s progress, commitment, and courage to change.

8. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to set realistic, achievable goals and next steps.

9. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   - Periodically summarize the patient’s statements to reinforce motivation and clarify goals.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, engagement, and motivation. There is a complete absence of empathy, acceptance, and collaboration. Change talk is ignored, sustain talk is confronted aggressively, and the patient’s autonomy is not supported. To improve, the psychiatrist must fundamentally shift to a respectful, empathetic, and collaborative approach that embraces the spirit and techniques of motivational interviewing.
