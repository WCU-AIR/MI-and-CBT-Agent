# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach throughout the session is judgmental, directive, and disapproving rather than accepting. From the outset, the psychiatrist uses language that blames and pressures the patient (“You need to stop avoiding these things,” “You really should push yourself,” “You have to take this seriously,” “No excuses”). There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s hesitance and vulnerability are met with criticism and demands rather than empathy or support. This approach likely increases the patient’s discomfort and resistance rather than fostering trust or openness.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance or respect. The tone is harsh, critical, and directive, which is counterproductive in motivational interviewing.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or reflect the patient’s feelings or perspective. Instead, the patient’s expressions of fear, overwhelm, and ambivalence are dismissed as “excuses” or “overcomplicating.” The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s emotional experience and struggles, failing to validate or explore these feelings. The patient’s hesitations and fears are met with impatience and demands rather than reflective listening or supportive inquiry.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows little effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, missing critical opportunities to build rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, lacking collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly commands the patient to “just do it,” “stop making excuses,” and “take responsibility,” which undermines the patient’s autonomy and agency. There is no invitation for the patient to explore her own motivations or values, nor is there any partnership in decision-making. The psychiatrist’s stance is controlling and coercive, which is antithetical to the spirit of motivational interviewing.  

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by a directive, confrontational style with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or respect for autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. When the patient tentatively expresses willingness (“maybe I could try,” “I guess I can try”), the psychiatrist dismisses this hesitance and pushes for immediate commitment without exploring or amplifying these statements. There is no strategic use of reflections or open questions to deepen the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change. Instead, the psychiatrist demands compliance, which likely stifles the patient’s emerging change talk.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and not reinforced; the psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses rather than cultivates motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s expressions of fear, avoidance, and ambivalence) is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s feelings as “excuses” and “overcomplicating,” and repeatedly insists the patient “stop feeling sorry for yourself.” There are no double-sided reflections or validation of the patient’s struggle. This approach likely increases resistance and sustain talk rather than reducing it.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead reinforces resistance through confrontational responses.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritarian stance, issuing commands and ultimatums without inviting the patient’s input or acknowledging her expertise in her own experience. The patient is repeatedly pressured to commit immediately, with no negotiation or shared decision-making. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.  

**Summary:**  
The session lacks partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and disregards the patient’s autonomy.

---

**Strengths:**

- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- None identified. The session is dominated by confrontational, directive, and judgmental communication.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist must fundamentally change approach to align with MI principles.  
- Develop skills in reflective listening and empathy to understand and validate patient experience.  
- Use open-ended questions to explore ambivalence and evoke change talk.  
- Affirm patient strengths and efforts to build motivation.  
- Support autonomy by inviting collaboration and shared decision-making.  
- Soften sustain talk with double-sided reflections and validation.  
- Avoid confrontational language that increases resistance.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Supportive Stance:**  
   Replace commands and criticism with acceptance and positive regard.

2. **Practice Reflective Listening:**  
   Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and build rapport.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   Encourage the patient to explore her feelings, fears, and motivations.

4. **Elicit and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses willingness, reflect and explore these statements to deepen motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and validate the difficulty of change to reduce resistance.

6. **Support Autonomy and Partnership:**  
   Invite the patient to set the agenda and make decisions collaboratively.

7. **Avoid Confrontation and Ultimatums:**  
   Replace “must” and “have to” language with invitations and options.

8. **Develop Collaborative Planning:**  
   Work with the patient to identify small, achievable steps that feel manageable.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental style undermines rapport, stifles motivation, and likely increases patient resistance. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must adopt a radically different approach centered on empathy, collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support. Without these changes, the session risks harming the therapeutic relationship and impeding patient progress.
