# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s tone throughout the session is predominantly authoritarian and directive, lacking warmth or genuine acceptance. Statements such as “You need to complete them on time, no excuses,” and “You have to get moving now or you’ll be in serious trouble,” communicate judgment and pressure rather than unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient with “Why haven’t you…?” questions that imply blame rather than understanding. There is minimal effort to normalize the patient’s ambivalence or anxiety, and no explicit affirmations of the patient’s feelings or struggles. The patient’s guarded and hesitant demeanor is met with impatience rather than empathy or acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate unconditional positive regard, instead adopting a judgmental and pressuring stance that likely increases patient defensiveness.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates limited perspective-taking and minimal reflective listening. The patient expresses mild anxiety and overwhelm (“It’s kinda overwhelming, I guess”), yet the psychiatrist does not acknowledge or explore these emotions. Instead, the psychiatrist quickly redirects to demands and instructions without validating the patient’s feelings or concerns. There are no complex or simple reflections that capture the patient’s emotional state or ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s repeated “Why haven’t you…?” questions come across as confrontational rather than empathetic. The patient’s hesitancy and guardedness are not met with understanding or supportive reflections.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is minimal; the psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to reflect and validate the patient’s emotional experience, resulting in a disconnected and directive interaction.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session lacks the core MI elements of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly issuing commands (“You have to pick a place now,” “No excuses,” “You have to take responsibility here”) and pressing the patient for compliance. There is no evidence of evoking the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change, nor is there any support for autonomy or choice. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s perspective or explore ambivalence beyond demanding explanations for inaction. The interaction is directive and coercive, which is antithetical to the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by a controlling, directive approach with no collaboration or evocation, severely undermining the MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. The patient offers minimal change talk, mostly acknowledging external requirements (“I think my case worker said I’ve gotta do, like, 40 hours”), but the psychiatrist does not build on these statements or explore the patient’s own reasons or desires. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on compliance and deadlines, missing opportunities to elicit the patient’s values, concerns, or potential benefits of completing the hours. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify change talk, nor any strategic questions to deepen motivation.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is largely ignored and not cultivated; the psychiatrist misses critical opportunities to evoke and reinforce motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is present in the patient’s expressions of avoidance, worry, and doubt (“I guess I just didn’t really pay close attention,” “I was kinda worried about asking too much,” “It just seems like a lot”), but the psychiatrist does not soften or skillfully redirect this talk. Instead, the psychiatrist confronts the patient with demands and “Why haven’t you…?” questions that likely reinforce sustain talk and resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or empathetic statements that acknowledge the patient’s ambivalence while gently guiding toward change.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening; the psychiatrist’s approach likely entrenches resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is one-sided, with the psychiatrist issuing commands and the patient responding passively. There is no invitation for the patient to share his perspective or co-create a plan. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in his own life or negotiate the agenda. The power differential is stark, with the psychiatrist adopting an authoritarian role that undermines client-centeredness.

**Summary:**  
No meaningful partnership is established; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction without collaboration or shared decision-making.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from commanding to partnering. Use inclusive language and invite Julian’s perspective.  
   - Avoid “Why haven’t you…” questions that imply blame; instead, explore barriers and ambivalence.

2. **Increase Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Validate Julian’s feelings of overwhelm and anxiety. Use reflective listening to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Normalize ambivalence and struggles with compliance.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Ask questions that invite elaboration on feelings, values, and motivations (e.g., “What concerns do you have about starting your hours?”).

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When Julian expresses any desire or reason for compliance, reflect and explore it further.  
   - Ask about what might help him feel more confident or ready.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge both the desire to comply and the difficulties experienced.  
   - Avoid confrontation; gently guide toward change.

6. **Affirm Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize any attempts Julian has made or his willingness to talk.  
   - Affirm his capacity to take steps when ready.

7. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize Julian’s control over how and when to start.  
   - Avoid ultimatums; instead, explore options collaboratively.

8. **Collaborative Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Work with Julian to develop a concrete, manageable plan.  
   - Summarize key points and commitments to reinforce motivation.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines acceptance, empathy, and collaboration, resulting in minimal patient engagement and no effective evocation of change talk. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a client-centered, autonomy-supportive approach that validates the patient’s experience, explores ambivalence, and collaboratively builds motivation and plans for change. Without these changes, the risk of patient resistance and non-compliance remains high.
