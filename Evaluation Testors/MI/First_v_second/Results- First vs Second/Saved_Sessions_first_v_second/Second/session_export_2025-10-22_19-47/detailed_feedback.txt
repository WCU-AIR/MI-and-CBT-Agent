# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental, directive, and confrontational from the outset. Statements like “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” “You really should quit those habits immediately,” and “You need to take this seriously” communicate a lack of unconditional positive regard. The patient is met with accusations and commands rather than respect or understanding. There is no evidence of warmth, support, or acceptance of the patient’s perspective or ambivalence. This approach risks alienating the patient and shutting down open communication.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate acceptance, instead adopting a harsh, authoritarian tone that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows no effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence, social pressures, and past struggles, but the psychiatrist ignores these nuances and instead pushes a rigid agenda. There are no reflective statements, no validation of the patient’s feelings or challenges, and no attempt to explore the patient’s experience empathetically. The lack of reflective listening or perspective-taking is glaring.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is absent; the psychiatrist does not acknowledge or validate the patient’s feelings or social context.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely directive and confrontational, lacking collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands and warnings without inviting the patient’s input or exploring their motivations. There is no partnership or respect for the patient’s autonomy; instead, the psychiatrist assumes an authoritarian expert role. This approach is antithetical to the spirit of motivational interviewing.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is completely missing; the psychiatrist dominates the conversation and disregards patient autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient spontaneously offers some change talk (e.g., acknowledging the DUI consequences, considering calling a cab), the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead focuses on commands and criticism. There is no strategic amplification or exploration of the patient’s own reasons or plans for change.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is neither evoked nor reinforced; the psychiatrist misses opportunities to build motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly confronts and challenges sustain talk with admonishments rather than softening or exploring ambivalence. The patient’s sustain talk about social pressures and feelings of isolation is met with dismissive and harsh responses. There is no use of double-sided reflections or normalization of ambivalence to reduce resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than skillful softening or exploration.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The session is characterized by commands, accusations, and a lack of shared decision-making. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of orders rather than an active partner in change. There is no negotiation or co-creation of goals or plans.

**Summary:**  
No partnership is evident; the psychiatrist exerts control and disregards patient input.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- Immediate need to adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.  
- Develop reflective listening and empathy skills.  
- Embrace the collaborative, autonomy-supportive spirit of MI.  
- Learn to evoke and reinforce change talk rather than confront sustain talk.  
- Build partnership and shared decision-making.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental Stance:**  
   Avoid commands, accusations, and judgmental language. Approach the patient with respect and curiosity.

2. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   Reflect patient statements to demonstrate understanding and validate feelings, especially ambivalence.

3. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   Ask open-ended questions that invite the patient to explore their own motivations and reasons for change.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance and explore ambivalence.

5. **Support Autonomy and Partnership:**  
   Collaborate with the patient to set goals and develop plans, respecting their right to choose.

6. **Seek Permission Before Advice:**  
   Ask before offering information or suggestions to maintain collaboration.

7. **Build Rapport and Engagement:**  
   Use empathy and acceptance to foster trust and openness.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s motivations, and risks increasing resistance. Immediate and substantial improvement is required in acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation skills to provide effective motivational interviewing and support meaningful behavior change.
