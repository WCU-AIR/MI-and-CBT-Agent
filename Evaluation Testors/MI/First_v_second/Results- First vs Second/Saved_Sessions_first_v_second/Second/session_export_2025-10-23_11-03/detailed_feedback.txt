# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive from the outset. The opening statement, “I’m going to tell you what you need to do,” sets a controlling tone that undermines acceptance. The repeated insistence on immediate cessation of smoking (“You really should stop immediately,” “You have to stop immediately”) conveys disapproval and pressure rather than unconditional positive regard. There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or validation of the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s current efforts or strengths, which would be essential to communicate acceptance. The patient’s responses show some discomfort and defensiveness, likely reflecting the psychiatrist’s lack of acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance, lacking the respectful, supportive tone necessary for acceptance in MI.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no evidence of empathic listening or understanding. The psychiatrist does not reflect or paraphrase the patient’s statements, nor do they attempt to explore the patient’s feelings or perspective. The patient expresses concerns about weight and diet, but the psychiatrist ignores these and pushes the smoking agenda without acknowledging the patient’s actual concerns. The tone is confrontational rather than curious or understanding. The patient’s defensive and somewhat disengaged responses indicate a lack of empathic connection.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy, missing opportunities to understand or validate the patient’s experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly commands the patient to “stop immediately” and “be strict,” without inviting the patient’s input or exploring their motivations. There is no partnership or shared decision-making; instead, the psychiatrist imposes a rigid agenda. The patient is not invited to express their own reasons for change or to set goals. Autonomy is not supported; rather, the patient is pressured and questioned in a way that could provoke resistance.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the interaction is dominated by control and persuasion rather than collaboration and evocation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Instead of eliciting the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change, the psychiatrist issues commands and questions that challenge the patient’s lack of discipline (“Why haven’t you done that already?” “Why haven’t you been more disciplined?”). This approach risks eliciting sustain talk or resistance rather than change talk. The patient does express some tentative commitment at the end, but this is not effectively evoked or amplified by the psychiatrist.

**Summary:**  
There is no effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not acknowledge or validate sustain talk or ambivalence. When the patient expresses attachment to orange juice (“I really do love having that orange juice—it’s almost like comfort”), the psychiatrist dismisses this with “No excuses” and demands strictness. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist confronts and challenges the patient’s sustain talk, which may increase resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is ignored or confronted rather than softened or explored.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands and interrogations, with no invitations for the patient to share their perspective or co-create a plan. The psychiatrist repeatedly asks “Why haven’t you done that?” in a way that feels accusatory rather than collaborative. The patient’s responses indicate discomfort and defensiveness, suggesting a poor therapeutic alliance.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks partnership; the psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritarian stance.

---

- The psychiatrist maintains focus on a health behavior (smoking cessation), which is relevant to health outcomes.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly directive, judgmental, and lacks empathy.  
- There is no evidence of MI spirit: no collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support.  
- Change talk is not cultivated; sustain talk is confronted rather than softened.  
- Partnership is absent; the patient is not engaged as an equal partner.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Begin sessions by exploring the patient’s concerns and priorities.  
   - Use open-ended questions to invite elaboration and understanding.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings and perspectives.  
   - Affirm strengths and efforts, even small ones.

3. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Avoid commands and directives; instead, invite the patient to consider change.  
   - Ask permission before giving advice or information.

4. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - Use strategic questions and reflections to draw out the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Reinforce and amplify change talk when it arises.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change and validate the patient’s experience.

6. **Collaborative Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Co-create actionable plans with the patient.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and commitment.

7. **Align Agenda with Patient Priorities:**  
   - Address the patient’s expressed concerns (weight and diet) rather than imposing unrelated topics (smoking).

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, directive style undermines acceptance, empathy, and collaboration. The patient’s concerns are ignored, and the interaction is likely to provoke resistance rather than motivation. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt the MI spirit, engage the patient as a partner, and skillfully evoke and reinforce the patient’s own motivations for change.
