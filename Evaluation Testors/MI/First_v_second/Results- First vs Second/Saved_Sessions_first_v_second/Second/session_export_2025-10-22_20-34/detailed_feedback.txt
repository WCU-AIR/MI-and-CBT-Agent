# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently adopts a judgmental and directive tone throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist dismisses the patient’s concerns and immediately moves to giving advice and commands (“you have to start moving more, no excuses,” “you must do that now,” “no more excuses”). There is no evidence of warmth, respect, or unconditional positive regard. The patient’s vulnerability and ambivalence are met with impatience and reprimands rather than support. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s feelings of overwhelm and fear, which is contrary to acceptance. This is a clear example of a judgmental and disapproving stance, warranting the lowest score.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses fear, overwhelm, and hesitation multiple times, but the psychiatrist ignores these emotional cues and instead insists on strict compliance. There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s feelings. The psychiatrist’s responses are factual and directive, lacking any genuine perspective-taking or empathic listening. For example, when the patient says, “It feels like too much change all at once,” the psychiatrist responds with “No, that’s not good enough,” rather than exploring or acknowledging the patient’s experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands and ultimatums (“You have to commit,” “No more excuses,” “Are you going to do that or not?”), which undermines the patient’s autonomy and partnership. The patient is not invited to explore her own motivations or values, nor is her expertise in her own life acknowledged. The psychiatrist’s stance is controlling and coercive, which is the antithesis of the MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient occasionally expresses desire and reasons for change, the psychiatrist ignores these or responds with pressure rather than amplification. For example, when the patient says, “Maybe I could try cutting back a little first,” the psychiatrist rejects this and demands full commitment. There is no strategic use of reflections or questions to deepen or elicit change talk. The psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses rather than cultivates motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and insistence rather than softening or redirection. The patient’s expressions of overwhelm, fear, and ambivalence are dismissed as “excuses” and met with demands to “push through.” There are no double-sided reflections or normalization of ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s responses likely increase resistance rather than reduce sustain talk.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritarian stance, repeatedly telling the patient what she “must” do and demanding yes/no answers. The patient’s input is ignored or overridden. The psychiatrist does not share power or invite the patient to co-create goals or plans. This is a clear example of a non-collaborative, directive approach.

---

### **Summary of Global Ratings:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is consistently directive, judgmental, and confrontational, lacking acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and skillful MI techniques. The patient’s ambivalence and vulnerability are met with pressure and ultimatums, which is likely to increase resistance and reduce motivation. This session falls far below acceptable standards for motivational interviewing.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, judgmental, and confrontational.  
- There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- No use of reflective listening, affirmations, or open-ended questions.  
- Change talk is not evoked or reinforced; sustain talk is confronted rather than softened.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and vulnerability are ignored or dismissed, likely reducing motivation and engagement.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Communicate unconditional positive regard and respect for the patient’s experience and feelings.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and validate ambivalence.

3. **Shift from Directive to Collaborative:**  
   - Invite the patient’s perspective, support autonomy, and co-create goals and plans.

4. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Encourage elaboration and exploration of motivations, barriers, and values.

5. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and reinforce these statements.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance.

7. **Avoid Ultimatums and Demands:**  
   - Support gradual change and acknowledge readiness, rather than insisting on immediate, strict compliance.

8. **Incorporate Affirmations:**  
   - Recognize and reinforce patient strengths, efforts, and intentions.

9. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Negotiate achievable steps and check for patient buy-in.

10. **Summarize Key Points:**  
    - Use summaries to integrate change talk and reinforce motivation.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, judgmental, and confrontational style undermines rapport, engagement, and motivation. To provide effective MI, the psychiatrist must radically shift to a respectful, empathic, and collaborative approach that skillfully evokes and supports the patient’s own motivations for change. Without these changes, the risk of patient resistance and disengagement is high, potentially causing harm rather than benefit.
