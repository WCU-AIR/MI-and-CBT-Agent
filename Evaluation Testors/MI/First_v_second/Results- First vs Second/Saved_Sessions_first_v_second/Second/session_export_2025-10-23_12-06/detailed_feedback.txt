# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Smoking Cessation Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. The repeated use of phrases such as “You’ve got to stop making excuses,” “You have to take control now,” and “You must commit today” convey disapproval and pressure rather than unconditional positive regard. The patient’s resistance and ambivalence are met with confrontation rather than acceptance. There is no evidence of warmth, validation, or normalization of the patient’s struggle. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s autonomy or the difficulty of long-term behavior change in a respectful manner. This results in a low level of acceptance, closer to judgment than support.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely confrontational and directive, lacking the respectful, nonjudgmental stance essential for acceptance in MI.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The responses focus on pushing the patient to commit rather than exploring or validating his feelings, experiences, or ambivalence. There are no reflective statements or paraphrases that demonstrate understanding of the patient’s long history, emotional struggle, or guarded attitude. The patient’s expressions of weariness, skepticism, and need for autonomy are ignored or challenged rather than empathized with. The psychiatrist’s tone and language suggest impatience and frustration rather than empathy.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to reflect and validate the patient’s perspective, resulting in a disconnect.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with frequent persuasion and pressure. The psychiatrist repeatedly insists on immediate commitment and quit dates, disregarding the patient’s autonomy and readiness. There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation of the patient’s own motivations, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist’s language (“You have to,” “No excuses,” “Just do it”) is coercive and controlling, which is antithetical to the MI spirit. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of advice rather than an active partner in change.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the session is dominated by directive commands and persuasion, undermining collaboration and autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient offers faint change talk (“I’m always open to listen,” “Maybe someday I’ll take that step”), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly challenges sustain talk and pushes for immediate commitment without exploring the patient’s own reasons or desires for change. There is no strategic amplification or reflection of change talk to deepen motivation. The focus remains on compliance rather than eliciting the patient’s intrinsic motivation.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally cultivated; the psychiatrist misses opportunities to evoke, reflect, and amplify the patient’s tentative openness.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently confronts and challenges sustain talk with admonishments and pressure (“Stop making excuses,” “You’re just stalling,” “No excuses”). There are no double-sided reflections, no normalization of ambivalence, and no attempts to gently redirect sustain talk toward change. This approach likely increases patient resistance rather than softening sustain talk. The patient’s expressions of ambivalence and guardedness are met with impatience and demands, which is counterproductive.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened; the psychiatrist’s approach likely entrenches resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritative stance throughout the session. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or negotiation of agenda. The patient is repeatedly told what to do, with ultimatums and demands rather than invitations or collaboration. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s expertise in his own life or to minimize the power differential. The repeated commands (“You must commit today,” “Just do it”) undermine partnership.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is essentially absent; the session is dominated by an authoritarian approach with no client-centered collaboration.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently addresses the smoking issue and attempts to discuss cessation methods.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks acceptance and empathy.  
- There is no evidence of MI spirit—no collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support.  
- Change talk is not cultivated; sustain talk is confronted harshly.  
- Partnership is absent; the patient is not engaged as an equal partner.  

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

## Summary of Feedback

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks acceptance and empathy.  
- No use of reflective listening or affirmations.  
- No evocation of patient motivation or support for autonomy.  
- Sustain talk is confronted harshly rather than softened.  
- Partnership and collaboration are absent.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses.”  
   - Validate the difficulty of quitting after decades of smoking.  
   - Normalize ambivalence and resistance.

2. **Increase Empathy and Reflective Listening:**  
   - Use reflections to demonstrate understanding of the patient’s perspective and feelings.  
   - Reflect ambivalence and emotional content rather than just facts.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Shift from directing and persuading to collaborating and evoking.  
   - Support patient autonomy explicitly.  
   - Avoid ultimatums and demands.

4. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses openness or tentative change talk, reflect and explore it further.  
   - Ask open-ended questions that invite elaboration on reasons for change.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Avoid confrontation that may increase resistance.

6. **Enhance Partnership:**  
   - Invite the patient to co-create the agenda and plan.  
   - Respect the patient’s expertise and control over the change process.

7. **Use Affirmations and Summaries:**  
   - Affirm patient strengths, efforts, and willingness to engage.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and clarify next steps.

8. **Avoid Pressuring for Immediate Commitment:**  
   - Recognize readiness varies and support the patient’s pace.  
   - Explore barriers and ambivalence rather than demanding quit dates.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental style undermines rapport, engagement, and motivation. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation, skillfully handling ambivalence and supporting patient autonomy. Without these changes, the approach risks alienating patients and reducing the likelihood of successful behavior change.
