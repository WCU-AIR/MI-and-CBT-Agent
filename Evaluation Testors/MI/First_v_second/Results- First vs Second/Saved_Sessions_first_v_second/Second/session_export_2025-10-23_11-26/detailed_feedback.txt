# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. The tone is authoritative and pressuring, with repeated insistence that the patient "has to quit now" and "no exceptions." There is little to no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The psychiatrist dismisses the patient’s perspective and concerns, repeatedly labeling the patient’s reasons as "justifications" or "excuses," which risks alienating the patient. The patient’s ambivalence and autonomy are not respected or validated. This approach falls short of basic respect and support expected in MI.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely confrontational and directive, lacking the fundamental acceptance and respect necessary for effective MI. This severely undermines rapport and client engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence, stress-related triggers, and a tentative plan to quit at basketball season start, but the psychiatrist ignores these nuances and instead pushes a rigid agenda. There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s feelings or struggles. The psychiatrist’s responses focus on correcting or challenging the patient rather than empathizing with their experience. This results in a lack of emotional attunement and missed opportunities to build trust.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist fails to demonstrate understanding of the patient’s ambivalence or emotional state, which impairs therapeutic alliance.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with frequent persuasion and pressure to quit immediately. There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands immediate cessation, dismisses the patient’s readiness and rationale, and uses confrontational language ("no more delays," "just decide now"). This approach contradicts the core MI principles of partnership and autonomy support. The patient is positioned as someone to be convinced rather than a partner in change.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the session is dominated by a coercive, expert-driven approach that undermines client autonomy and motivation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient offers some change talk (e.g., desire to quit at basketball season, awareness of risks), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke, reinforce, or amplify this language. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s ambivalence and readiness, which may provoke resistance rather than deepen motivation. There are no strategic reflections or open questions designed to elicit more detailed change talk or commitment language. The focus is on immediate cessation rather than exploring the patient’s own reasons and confidence.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but poorly cultivated; the psychiatrist misses opportunities to deepen and reinforce motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s minimization of risk, desire to vape socially, stress coping) is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s statements as "justifications" and "excuses," which likely increases resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s struggle or legitimate reasons for vaping. The approach risks entrenching sustain talk rather than resolving ambivalence.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened; the psychiatrist fails to manage ambivalence skillfully.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritative role, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding immediate action. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or negotiation of goals. The patient’s autonomy is overridden by the psychiatrist’s insistence on quitting "right now." The language used is directive and coercive, with no invitations for the patient to express preferences or co-create a plan. This power imbalance undermines partnership.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the session is dominated by a top-down, directive approach that marginalizes the patient’s voice.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directive commands to partnership language. Invite the patient’s perspective and preferences.  
   - Avoid confrontational or judgmental language.

2. **Enhance Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as "excuses" or "justifications."

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions to explore the patient’s motivations, concerns, and readiness.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to deepen understanding.

4. **Cultivate Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons to quit, reinforce and amplify these statements.  
   - Explore confidence and commitment language.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge the patient’s reasons for vaping while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Normalize ambivalence and validate the difficulty of quitting.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Explicitly affirm the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.  
   - Avoid pressuring for immediate quitting if the patient is not ready.

7. **Collaborative Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Work with the patient to develop a realistic, stepwise plan.  
   - Summarize key points, change talk, and next steps to reinforce motivation.

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s approach is coercive, confrontational, and directive, lacking empathy, acceptance, and collaboration. The patient’s ambivalence and autonomy are disregarded, and change talk is neither cultivated nor reinforced. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt the MI spirit, use reflective listening, open questions, and affirmations, and engage the patient as an equal partner in change. Without these fundamental shifts, the session risks alienating the patient and undermining motivation for quitting.
