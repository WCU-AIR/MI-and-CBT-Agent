# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. The repeated use of phrases such as “You really must stop now,” “You have to quit immediately,” and “You’re just making excuses” convey disapproval and pressure rather than unconditional positive regard. The patient’s expressions of pain, struggle, and ambivalence are met with admonishments rather than empathy or validation. There is no evidence of normalization or acceptance of the patient’s current difficulties. The psychiatrist fails to create a safe, supportive environment where the patient feels heard and respected.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely confrontational and directive, lacking warmth and acceptance. This undermines rapport and likely increases patient resistance.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient repeatedly mentions pain, rehab challenges, and ambivalence, but the psychiatrist responds with repeated questions about quitting and admonishments rather than reflective listening. There are no paraphrases or complex reflections that capture the patient’s emotional state or struggle. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s pain or validate the difficulty of quitting under these circumstances. The lack of empathy is evident in the dismissive tone and failure to explore the patient’s experience beyond smoking cessation.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to reflect and validate the patient’s feelings and barriers.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with frequent persuasion and pressure to quit smoking immediately. The psychiatrist repeatedly uses commands (“You have to quit now,” “Stop dragging your feet”) and challenges the patient’s reasons for not quitting. There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The patient’s ambivalence and readiness are ignored or minimized. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s perspective or explore motivations; instead, the session feels like a lecture or confrontation.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent. The session is directive, controlling, and lacks partnership or evocation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient offers some change talk (e.g., “I want to get better faster,” “I’ve tried cutting down”), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce this language. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with admonishments and questions that challenge the patient’s sustain talk without exploring or amplifying the patient’s own motivations. There is no strategic use of reflections or open questions to deepen change talk or build commitment. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore the patient’s desire, ability, or reasons for change in a supportive manner.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but poorly cultivated; the psychiatrist’s approach tends to shut down rather than evoke motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften sustain talk but rather confronts and challenges it aggressively. The patient’s expressions of pain, difficulty, and ambivalence are met with accusations of “making excuses” and pressure to “stop dragging your feet.” There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change. This confrontational style likely reinforces sustain talk and resistance rather than reducing it.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening or strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritative role, issuing commands and pressing the patient to quit immediately. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or negotiation of goals. The patient’s perspective is repeatedly overridden or dismissed. The psychiatrist does not invite collaboration or acknowledge the patient’s expertise in their own experience. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.  

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the session is dominated by an expert-driven, directive approach.

---

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly directive, confrontational, and lacking in empathy and acceptance.  
- There is no evidence of MI spirit: no collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support.  
- Change talk is not cultivated; sustain talk is confronted rather than softened.  
- Partnership is absent; the patient is not engaged as an equal partner.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directive commands to partnership. Invite the patient’s perspective and acknowledge their expertise in their own experience.

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s pain, struggle, and ambivalence. Avoid judgmental or confrontational language.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Ask questions that invite elaboration on the patient’s feelings, values, and motivations rather than yes/no or challenging questions.

4. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize and affirm any attempts or willingness to change, even if tentative.

5. **Evoke Change Talk Skillfully:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and amplify these statements to build motivation.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change.

7. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize that the patient has control over their choices and pace of change.

8. **Avoid Persuasion Without Permission:**  
   - Ask permission before giving advice or information to reduce resistance.

9. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to set achievable goals and next steps, reinforcing commitment.

10. **Summarize Progress and Motivation:**  
    - Use summaries to consolidate change talk and reinforce motivation.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental style undermines rapport, empathy, and patient engagement. There is no evidence of MI spirit, reflective listening, or skillful evocation of change talk. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, empathetic approach that supports autonomy and gently guides the patient toward change. Without these changes, the session risks increasing patient resistance and reducing the likelihood of successful behavior change.
