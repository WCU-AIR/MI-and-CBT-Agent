# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive rather than accepting. From the outset, the tone is confrontational and pressuring (“you really need to quit smoking,” “You just need to stop now,” “there’s no excuse”), which risks eliciting defensiveness rather than openness. There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s behavior as “excuses” and “hesitation,” which undermines acceptance. The patient’s expressions of struggle and ambivalence are met with dismissal rather than validation. This pattern is inconsistent with MI principles of acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate unconditional positive regard and instead adopts a critical, directive stance that likely alienates the patient.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient shares nuanced feelings about cravings, stress, and fear of failure, but the psychiatrist responds with blunt commands and challenges rather than reflective listening. For example, when the patient expresses fear of failure and the difficulty of quitting, the psychiatrist counters with “you’re just making excuses” and “you need to be stronger,” missing opportunities to explore and validate these emotions. There is no use of complex or simple reflections to demonstrate understanding.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist does not accurately or consistently convey understanding of the patient’s ambivalence or emotional experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, lacking collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“you have to stop,” “just pick a quit date now,” “no more excuses”) and challenges the patient’s hesitancy without inviting the patient’s own motivations or ideas. There is no evidence of partnership or respect for the patient’s autonomy. The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s reasons for change beyond superficial questions and does not support the patient’s self-efficacy.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive approach that contradicts MI principles.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the psychiatrist asks about prior quit attempts and use of nicotine replacement therapy, these questions are framed in a confrontational manner that risks eliciting sustain talk or resistance rather than change talk. The psychiatrist does not strategically reinforce or amplify the patient’s change talk (e.g., “I want to feel better,” “I’m trying to get healthier”). Instead, the focus is on pushing the patient to commit without exploring or deepening their motivation. There is no elicitation of commitment language beyond forced yes/no responses.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and not skillfully cultivated or reinforced.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s ambivalence and hesitation as “making excuses” or “overcomplicating,” which reinforces sustain talk and resistance rather than softening it. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change. The patient’s fears and barriers are dismissed rather than explored or normalized.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is not softened; instead, it is confronted and challenged in a way that likely increases resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The interaction is dominated by commands and demands, with no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s expertise in their own experience. The psychiatrist repeatedly pressures the patient to commit immediately without negotiating or exploring readiness. The patient’s input is minimized and overridden.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance that undermines collaboration.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directive commands to partnership and shared decision-making.  
   - Respect the patient’s autonomy and readiness to change.

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and struggles.  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses” or “overthinking.”

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Invite the patient to elaborate on their motivations, fears, and barriers.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to deepen understanding.

4. **Elicit and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reinforce and explore these statements.  
   - Avoid jumping to solutions before the patient’s motivation is clear.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of quitting and validate the patient’s experience.

6. **Collaborative Planning:**  
   - Work with the patient to set realistic goals and quit dates based on readiness.  
   - Discuss strategies for managing cravings collaboratively.

7. **Use Permission and Autonomy Support:**  
   - Ask permission before offering advice or information.  
   - Emphasize the patient’s control over the process.

8. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   - Periodically summarize the patient’s statements of motivation and ambivalence to reinforce engagement and clarify goals.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental style undermines rapport, fails to evoke or reinforce change talk, and does not support the patient’s autonomy or readiness. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, empathetic, and patient-centered approach that skillfully navigates ambivalence and fosters motivation for change. Without these changes, the risk of patient disengagement and treatment failure is high.
