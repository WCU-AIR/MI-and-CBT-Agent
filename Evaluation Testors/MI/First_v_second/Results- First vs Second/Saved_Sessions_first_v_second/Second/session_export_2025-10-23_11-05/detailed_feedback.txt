# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental, confrontational, and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist labels the patient as needing to quit smoking and drinking without exploring the patient’s perspective or acknowledging his experience. Statements such as “You must stop as soon as possible,” “You just need to be stronger,” and “No excuses” communicate disapproval and lack of unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s denials and rationalizations with accusations of dishonesty or excuses, which is highly likely to increase resistance. There is no evidence of warmth, respect, or acceptance of the patient’s current stance or feelings.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian and judgmental, lacking any genuine acceptance or respect for the patient’s autonomy or perspective.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy or reflective listening. The patient’s statements are met with challenges, accusations, and directives rather than understanding or validation. For example, when the patient denies smoking, the psychiatrist immediately questions the patient’s honesty instead of exploring the patient’s experience or feelings. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand the patient’s ambivalence or resistance, nor does he reflect or paraphrase the patient’s concerns or emotions. The tone is confrontational rather than empathic.  

**Summary:**  
There is minimal to no effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, resulting in a lack of rapport and increased defensiveness.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely directive and persuasive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You must stop,” “No excuses,” “Start immediately”) and demands commitment without inviting the patient’s input or exploring his motivations. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of orders rather than an active participant in change. The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s own reasons for change or support his autonomy; instead, the patient is pressured and confronted.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the approach is authoritarian and controlling, which is counterproductive for motivating change.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not evoke or reinforce any change talk. The patient offers minimal change talk (e.g., admitting to drinking occasionally), but the psychiatrist ignores or challenges these statements rather than amplifying or exploring them. There are no open-ended questions designed to elicit the patient’s own motivations or desires for change. The psychiatrist’s focus is on telling the patient what to do rather than drawing out the patient’s reasons or plans for change.  

**Summary:**  
No skillful evocation or reinforcement of change talk is present; opportunities to explore ambivalence and motivation are missed.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently confronts and challenges sustain talk rather than softening or exploring it. The patient’s denials, rationalizations, and resistance are met with accusations of dishonesty and excuses. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s perspective while gently guiding toward change. This confrontational style likely entrenches sustain talk rather than reducing it.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than skillful softening or strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is one-sided, with the psychiatrist dominating the conversation and issuing commands. The patient’s views and autonomy are disregarded. There is no negotiation of agenda or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist’s language is controlling and directive, reinforcing a power imbalance.  

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership or client-centered collaboration; the approach is authoritarian and directive.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance that accepts the patient’s current perspective.  
- Reflective listening and empathy are essential to build rapport and understand ambivalence.  
- The spirit of MI requires collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support rather than confrontation.  
- Change talk should be skillfully elicited and reinforced, while sustain talk should be softened, not challenged aggressively.  
- Partnership and shared decision-making must replace authoritarian commands.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- None identified.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and judgmental, which is counterproductive in motivational interviewing.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce any change talk and instead confronts sustain talk aggressively.  
- The patient’s autonomy and perspective are disregarded, damaging rapport and engagement.  
- The session lacks collaboration, partnership, and shared decision-making.  
- The psychiatrist’s questions are closed and leading, limiting patient expression.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling or blaming. Respect the patient’s current perspective and feelings.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion. Validate ambivalence and resistance as normal.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient, evoke their own motivations, and support autonomy.

4. **Elicit and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Use open-ended questions to explore reasons for change and reinforce any positive statements.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and affirm the patient’s perspective while gently guiding toward change.

6. **Enhance Partnership:**  
   - Share power, negotiate agenda, and co-create plans with the patient.

7. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Encourage elaboration and recognize patient strengths and efforts.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Direct Commands:**  
   - Replace directives with invitations and permission-seeking.

9. **Summarize and Plan Collaboratively:**  
   - Summarize key points and develop actionable steps with patient buy-in.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental style undermines rapport, engagement, and motivation. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a respectful, empathic, collaborative, and autonomy-supportive approach that skillfully evokes and reinforces the patient’s own motivations for change.
