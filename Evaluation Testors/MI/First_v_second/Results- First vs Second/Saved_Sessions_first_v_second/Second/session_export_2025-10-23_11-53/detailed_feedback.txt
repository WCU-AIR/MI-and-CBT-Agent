# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is judgmental, directive, and disapproving throughout the session. The repeated commands (“you need to quit immediately,” “stop making excuses,” “just pick a quit date”) communicate a lack of unconditional positive regard. There is no evidence of warmth, respect, or support for the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s ambivalence and resistance by labeling it as “excuses” and insisting on immediate change. This approach is likely to increase patient defensiveness rather than foster openness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate acceptance, instead adopting a confrontational and authoritarian stance that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of empathic listening or understanding of the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s guarded, humorous, and resistant responses, instead pressing with the same scripted messages. The patient’s subtle hints at complexity (“it’s not that simple for me,” “it’s complicated”) are not explored or reflected. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or validate the patient’s feelings, struggles, or ambivalence, missing all opportunities for reflective listening.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal to no effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, resulting in a one-sided, directive interaction.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely authoritarian and directive, with no collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly tells the patient what to do, uses confrontational language (“stop making excuses,” “you must start using patches”), and does not invite the patient’s input or perspective. There is no attempt to evoke the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change. Autonomy is denied by insisting on immediate quitting and setting deadlines without negotiation.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the psychiatrist adopts a controlling, expert-driven approach that is counterproductive to motivational interviewing principles.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient occasionally offers minimal change talk (e.g., “I’ve thought about quitting,” “I’ve tried cutting down”), the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead repeats commands and warnings. There is no amplification, reflection, or strategic response to encourage the patient’s own motivation. The psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses rather than cultivates change talk.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is neither effectively evoked nor reinforced; the psychiatrist misses all opportunities to build motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s resistance as “excuses” and insists on immediate action, which likely entrenches sustain talk. There are no double-sided reflections or validation of ambivalence. The patient’s guarded and humorous deflections are ignored or challenged rather than gently explored.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and intensified by the psychiatrist’s confrontational style; no attempts are made to soften or redirect it.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is expert-driven, with the psychiatrist dictating what the patient “needs” to do and repeatedly asking “why haven’t you done that yet?” There is no invitation to share power, no negotiation of goals, and no respect for the patient’s autonomy or expertise in their own life. The patient’s responses are met with impatience and dismissal.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and fails to engage the patient as an equal collaborator.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient responses as “excuses.”  
   - Show genuine respect and positive regard regardless of patient resistance.

2. **Develop Empathic Listening Skills:**  
   - Use reflective listening to understand and validate the patient’s perspective.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to build rapport.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Support autonomy and invite the patient’s input and agenda.

4. **Use Open Questions and Complex Reflections:**  
   - Ask questions that invite elaboration and exploration of ambivalence.  
   - Reflect patient statements to deepen understanding and evoke motivation.

5. **Skillfully Evoke and Reinforce Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, amplify and explore these.  
   - Avoid pushing or confronting sustain talk; instead, use double-sided reflections.

6. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to set realistic, patient-driven goals.  
   - Summarize progress and reinforce commitment.

7. **Avoid Confrontation and Persuasion:**  
   - Replace “you must” and “you need to” with invitations and suggestions.  
   - Ask permission before giving advice or information.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental style undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient, and likely increases resistance. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and skillful evocation of the patient’s own motivations for change. Without these changes, the psychiatrist risks alienating patients and reducing the effectiveness of interventions.
