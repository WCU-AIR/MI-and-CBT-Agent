# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. The opening statements such as “You really need to quit whatever’s causing you trouble” and repeated insistence on “You have to quit completely” communicate a lack of unconditional positive regard. The patient’s ambivalence and expressed concerns are dismissed or minimized, and the psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s readiness in a confrontational manner. There is no evidence of warmth, support, or acceptance of the patient’s perspective or current stage of change. The tone is authoritarian and lacks empathy or validation.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive, failing to communicate respect or acceptance of the patient’s ambivalence or autonomy.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not demonstrate reflective listening or attempt to understand the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses stress, guilt, and practical barriers to quitting, but the psychiatrist ignores these emotional cues and instead repeatedly pushes for immediate cessation. There are no reflections, paraphrases, or empathic statements that acknowledge the patient’s feelings or struggles. The psychiatrist’s responses are confrontational and dismissive, which likely increases patient resistance rather than fostering understanding.

**Summary:**  
There is a near-complete absence of empathy; the psychiatrist fails to acknowledge or validate the patient’s emotional experience or perspective.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding immediate commitment to quitting. There is no attempt to evoke the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change, nor is there support for the patient’s autonomy. The psychiatrist’s repeated “Are you ready to quit today?” questions function as ultimatums rather than invitations to explore readiness.

**Summary:**  
The session is characterized by a directive, confrontational style that contradicts the collaborative, autonomy-supportive spirit of MI.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient offers tentative change talk (e.g., willingness to cut back to one drink), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these statements, instead focusing on pushing for complete cessation. There is no amplification or exploration of the patient’s expressed desire or ability to change incrementally. The psychiatrist’s repeated demands for immediate quitting likely suppress further change talk.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is neither effectively evoked nor reinforced; the psychiatrist’s approach likely inhibits the patient’s motivation to explore change.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften or strategically respond to sustain talk. The patient expresses sustain talk related to stress relief, fear of losing coping mechanisms, and practical barriers, but the psychiatrist responds with confrontation and challenges rather than reflective or double-sided statements. Sustain talk is met with accusations of “making excuses” and pressure to quit immediately, which likely increases resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than skillful softening or redirection, undermining rapport and motivation.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and directives, with no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s expertise in her own life. The patient’s input is repeatedly overridden or dismissed. The psychiatrist’s language (“You have to…”) reinforces a power differential and undermines partnership.

**Summary:**  
There is no meaningful partnership; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance that marginalizes the patient’s voice.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance that accepts the patient’s ambivalence.  
- Reflective listening and empathic responses are essential to understand and validate the patient’s experience.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—must replace the current directive, confrontational style.  
- Change talk should be actively evoked and reinforced, while sustain talk should be skillfully softened.  
- Partnership requires genuine sharing of power and respect for the patient’s expertise.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is confrontational, directive, and lacks empathy.  
- There is a failure to build rapport or establish a collaborative partnership.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and autonomy are not respected or explored.  
- Change talk is ignored or suppressed rather than evoked and reinforced.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling or pressuring language.  
   - Validate the patient’s feelings and struggles.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion.  
   - Acknowledge ambivalence and complexity.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and values.  
   - Support autonomy and choice.

4. **Elicit and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses willingness to cut back, explore and reinforce it.  
   - Avoid dismissing incremental steps as failures.

5. **Softly Respond to Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Normalize ambivalence.

6. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Invite elaboration and exploration.  
   - Recognize patient strengths and efforts.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to identify feasible next steps.  
   - Summarize progress and reinforce commitment.

---

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive style undermines rapport, suppresses motivation, and fails to engage the patient in meaningful change talk or planning. Immediate and substantial improvement is required to align with MI best practices and to effectively support this patient’s readiness and capacity for change.
