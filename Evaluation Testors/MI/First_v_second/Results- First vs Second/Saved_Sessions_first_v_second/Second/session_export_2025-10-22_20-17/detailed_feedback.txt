# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is judgmental, directive, and lacks warmth or respect. The repeated use of commands (“You really need to quit smoking,” “You have to stop now,” “You need to take this seriously”) and accusatory questions (“Why would you risk that?” “Why wouldn’t you do that?”) convey disapproval and pressure rather than acceptance. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or validation of the patient’s perspective or circumstances. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s polite but time-pressed communication style, instead pushing a rigid agenda. This is consistent throughout the session, meeting the lowest rating criteria.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance is authoritarian and judgmental, severely undermining acceptance and rapport.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no demonstration of empathy or reflective listening. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s expressed time constraints or polite, direct communication style. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly asks confrontational questions and issues warnings without attempting to understand the patient’s perspective or emotional state. The patient’s brief, polite responses are met with further pressure rather than understanding. The psychiatrist does not paraphrase, reflect, or validate the patient’s concerns or feelings at any point.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal to no effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, resulting in a cold, unempathetic interaction.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely directive and authoritative. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands and warnings, uses confrontational questions, and does not collaborate or evoke the patient’s own motivations or concerns. Autonomy is not supported; instead, the patient is told what they “have to” do. There is no evidence of partnership, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s cues about limited time and need for brief information, instead pushing a rigid agenda.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks any elements of MI spirit; it is authoritarian and coercive.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not evoke or reinforce any change talk. The patient’s minimal statements about not currently taking both medications and intention to follow up with their doctor are not explored or amplified. Instead, the psychiatrist responds with further warnings and accusatory questions, which likely suppress change talk rather than cultivate it. There is no strategic elicitation of desire, ability, reasons, or commitment language.

**Summary:**  
No effort to evoke or reinforce change talk; patient’s minimal change talk is ignored or undermined.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk or resistance is met with confrontation and pressure rather than softening or redirection. The psychiatrist repeatedly asks “Why wouldn’t you do that?” and issues ultimatums, which likely increase resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s legitimate concerns or constraints (e.g., time pressure), instead escalating pressure.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and met with confrontation rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist dominates the interaction, issuing commands and questions that imply blame or fault. There is no evidence of collaboration or shared decision-making. The patient’s autonomy and expertise are ignored. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or preferences beyond yes/no questions, and even these are framed in a judgmental manner.

**Summary:**  
No partnership or collaboration; interaction is authoritarian and expert-driven.

---

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must abandon the authoritarian, judgmental style.  
- Develop skills in acceptance, empathy, and reflective listening.  
- Support patient autonomy and collaborate rather than command.  
- Actively evoke and reinforce change talk.  
- Skillfully soften sustain talk and ambivalence.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist correctly identifies the clinical risk of mixing citalopram and phenelzine.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian, judgmental, and confrontational, which is contraindicated in motivational interviewing.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge or adapt to the patient’s time constraints and communication style.  
- No use of reflective listening, affirmations, or evocation of change talk.  
- Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening or redirection.  
- The session lacks any collaborative planning or summarizing.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Replace commands and accusations with open-ended questions and invitations to share the patient’s perspective.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   Reflect the patient’s concerns and acknowledge their time constraints and efforts to manage medications responsibly.

3. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Use language that emphasizes the patient’s control over decisions and respects their expertise about their own life.

4. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   Paraphrase and reflect patient statements to demonstrate understanding and encourage elaboration.

5. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   Ask questions that draw out the patient’s own reasons, desires, and confidence for safe medication use.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalize ambivalence rather than confront or blame.

7. **Plan Collaboratively:**  
   Work with the patient to identify next steps that fit their schedule and preferences.

8. **Respect Time Constraints:**  
   Acknowledge the patient’s limited time and offer to provide brief information now with options for follow-up.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session is a poor example of motivational interviewing. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, judgmental, and confrontational style undermines rapport, engagement, and motivation. Without fundamental changes to approach—embracing empathy, acceptance, collaboration, and evocation—the psychiatrist risks alienating patients and reducing the effectiveness of clinical interventions. Immediate and substantial training in MI principles and techniques is essential.
