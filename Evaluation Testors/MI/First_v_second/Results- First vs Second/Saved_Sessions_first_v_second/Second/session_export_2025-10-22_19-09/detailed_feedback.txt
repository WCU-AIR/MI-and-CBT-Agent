# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a largely judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist issues commands and warnings (“You have to stop drinking immediately,” “Just put the wine away now”) without acknowledging or validating the patient’s perspective or autonomy. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s expressions of independence and ambivalence are met with resistance or dismissal rather than acceptance. The psychiatrist fails to normalize the patient’s struggle or respect her values, which is critical given the patient’s defensiveness and autonomy orientation.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly authoritarian and directive, lacking respect for the patient’s autonomy and failing to communicate acceptance. This undermines rapport and engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The responses are largely confrontational and do not demonstrate reflective listening or perspective-taking. The patient’s ambivalence, cultural values, and concerns about autonomy are ignored or challenged rather than explored empathetically. There are no complex or simple reflections that capture the patient’s feelings or thoughts. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s emotional state or the difficulty of her situation.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to validate and understand the patient’s perspective, which would be essential to build trust and facilitate change.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is almost entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what she must do without inviting her input or evoking her own motivations. Autonomy is explicitly denied (“No, you don’t get to negotiate this”), which is counterproductive given the patient’s strong value on independence. There is no evidence of partnership or evocation of the patient’s reasons for change beyond superficial questioning.  

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by directive persuasion and control, directly opposing the core MI principles.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the psychiatrist asks some questions about quitting attempts and reasons for change, these are framed in a confrontational manner that risks eliciting sustain talk or resistance rather than genuine change talk. The psychiatrist does not strategically reinforce or amplify the patient’s change talk statements (e.g., “I want to have a healthy baby,” “I’m willing to take a brochure”). Instead, the focus remains on commands and warnings. There is no elicitation of commitment language or confidence statements.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and not skillfully cultivated or reinforced.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and pressure rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s autonomy and reasons for drinking, which likely increases resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s ambivalence or legitimate concerns. The psychiatrist fails to normalize sustain talk or gently guide the patient toward change.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced or escalated by the psychiatrist’s confrontational approach.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands and demands, with no evidence of shared decision-making or power-sharing. The patient’s expertise in her own life is disregarded, and her autonomy is repeatedly undermined. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient to co-create goals or plans, instead insisting on immediate compliance.  

**Summary:**  
The session lacks partnership and client-centeredness, which are fundamental to MI.

---

### **Overall Global Ratings Summary**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly authoritarian, directive, and confrontational, which is contraindicated in motivational interviewing. There is minimal acceptance, empathy, or partnership. Change talk is poorly cultivated, and sustain talk is not softened but rather escalated. The patient’s autonomy and values are disregarded, undermining engagement and motivation.  

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (alcohol cessation).  
- The patient expresses some change talk and willingness to consider small steps.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is overly directive, confrontational, and lacks acceptance and empathy.  
- No reflective listening or affirmations are used.  
- Autonomy is denied rather than supported, undermining motivation.  
- Change talk is not skillfully evoked or reinforced.  
- Sustain talk is escalated rather than softened.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs.  
- The overall spirit of MI is absent.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directive commands to partnership. Invite the patient’s perspective and respect her autonomy.

2. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and build rapport.

3. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Values:**  
   - Recognize the patient’s desire to be a good parent and her willingness to consider change.

4. **Evoke Change Talk Skillfully:**  
   - When the patient expresses motivation, reflect and amplify these statements to deepen commitment.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and validate ambivalence rather than confronting or dismissing it.

6. **Ask Permission Before Offering Advice:**  
   - Seek consent before providing information or suggestions to respect autonomy.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to identify manageable steps and reinforce her control over the process.

8. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   - Use summaries to consolidate motivation and clarify next steps.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational approach undermines acceptance, empathy, and autonomy support, which are essential for effective MI. Without these, the patient’s ambivalence is not explored or resolved, and motivation for change is unlikely to be enhanced. Immediate and substantial improvement in MI skills is necessary to avoid harm and improve patient outcomes.
