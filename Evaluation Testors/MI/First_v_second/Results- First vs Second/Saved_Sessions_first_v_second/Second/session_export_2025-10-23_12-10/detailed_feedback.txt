# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive stance from the outset. The opening statement, “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do to quit,” is authoritarian and dismissive of the patient’s autonomy. The tone throughout is confrontational, with repeated ultimatums (“You have to quit completely,” “This is not optional”) and a lack of warmth or support. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge or validate the patient’s feelings, concerns, or ambivalence. Instead, the patient is repeatedly pressured and challenged, which likely increases defensiveness. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is highly judgmental and directive, lacking respect and support for the patient’s perspective or readiness. This is a critical failure in MI practice.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses stress, ambivalence, and a need to proceed at his own pace, but the psychiatrist ignores these cues and pushes a rigid agenda. There are no reflective statements or attempts to paraphrase or validate the patient’s experience. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly challenges and confronts the patient’s statements, which likely alienates the patient. The lack of empathy is evident in the failure to acknowledge the patient’s stress or the difficulty of quitting smoking while managing diabetes.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is virtually absent. The psychiatrist fails to listen or respond to the patient’s emotional state or perspective.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, expert-driven approach. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands and ultimatums (“You have to quit completely,” “This is not optional,” “No more delays”), showing no collaboration or evocation of the patient’s own motivations. Autonomy is denied outright, with the psychiatrist insisting on immediate commitment without exploring the patient’s readiness or values. There is no partnership or shared decision-making. The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is completely absent.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts a coercive, directive stance that contradicts the fundamental principles of MI.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient expresses some tentative change talk (e.g., willingness to cut back, desire to quit eventually), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these statements. Instead of amplifying or exploring the patient’s expressed willingness, the psychiatrist repeatedly shuts down any incremental steps and demands immediate quitting. This approach likely suppresses change talk rather than cultivating it.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is neither effectively evoked nor reinforced; the psychiatrist’s approach likely inhibits motivational language.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and pressure rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s ambivalence as “excuses” or “stalling” and insists on immediate action. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. The patient’s concerns about stress and readiness are ignored or invalidated, which likely entrenches sustain talk and resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than skillfully softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The interaction is dominated by commands and ultimatums, with no invitation for the patient to share his perspective or co-create a plan. The psychiatrist repeatedly overrides the patient’s expressed feelings and preferences, minimizing the patient’s expertise in his own life. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
There is no evidence of partnership; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance.

---

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must abandon the directive, confrontational style.  
- Develop skills in reflective listening and empathy.  
- Embrace the MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.  
- Learn to recognize and reinforce change talk.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from telling and directing to exploring and partnering.  
   - Invite the patient’s perspective and respect autonomy.

2. **Develop Reflective Listening Skills:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Use reflections to validate ambivalence and build rapport.

3. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses willingness or desire, reflect and explore it further.  
   - Avoid shutting down incremental steps like cutting back.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Normalize ambivalence as part of the change process.

5. **Use Open Questions Effectively:**  
   - Ask open-ended, nonjudgmental questions to invite elaboration and exploration.

6. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize and reinforce any positive intentions or past attempts.

7. **Collaborative Planning:**  
   - Work with the patient to develop realistic, patient-centered goals and timelines.  
   - Avoid imposing deadlines or ultimatums.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Ultimatums:**  
   - Recognize that pressure often increases resistance and decreases motivation.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, empathy, and collaboration, leading to patient resistance and disengagement. To improve, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a patient-centered, autonomy-supportive approach that values reflective listening, evocation of motivation, and strategic handling of ambivalence. Without these changes, the effectiveness of interventions will be severely compromised, potentially harming patient outcomes.
