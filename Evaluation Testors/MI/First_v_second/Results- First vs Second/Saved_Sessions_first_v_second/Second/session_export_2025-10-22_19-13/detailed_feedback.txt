# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental, directive, and confrontational stance throughout the session. From the very first utterance, the psychiatrist states, “I’m going to tell you what you need to do,” which immediately establishes an authoritarian tone and undermines any sense of unconditional positive regard. The patient’s hesitations, ambivalence, and expressions of difficulty are met with criticism and demands rather than empathy or support. For example, phrases like “You really need to stop making excuses,” “You must push through the tiredness,” and “Are you going to take this seriously or keep making excuses?” are accusatory and dismissive. There is no evidence of warmth, respect, or acceptance of the patient’s experience or ambivalence. This approach likely alienates the patient and reduces engagement.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental and directive, lacking any unconditional positive regard or acceptance. This is a fundamental failure in MI spirit and severely limits the potential for effective engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence, fatigue, and fear of failure, but these are met with dismissal rather than empathetic reflection. For instance, when the patient says, “I don’t want to feel like I’m beating myself up,” or “I’m worried that if I try to do too much at once, I’ll just give up,” the psychiatrist responds with commands and challenges rather than acknowledging these feelings. There are no reflective statements that validate the patient’s struggle or explore the emotional content behind the ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s responses are primarily confrontational and lack any attempt to convey understanding.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is virtually absent. The psychiatrist fails to listen reflectively or validate the patient’s emotional experience, which is critical in MI.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is dominated by an authoritarian, expert-driven style. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You have to…,” “Stop making excuses,” “Are you going to commit or not?”) and does not collaborate with the patient or evoke their own motivations. Autonomy is not supported; instead, the patient is pressured and challenged. There is no evidence of partnership or evocation. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s ideas or preferences but insists on immediate compliance with a rigid plan. This approach is antithetical to the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support. It is directive, confrontational, and controlling.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the psychiatrist does elicit some change talk by asking about exercise and diet habits, the approach is coercive rather than supportive. The patient offers some change talk (e.g., “I’ve been thinking about working out more,” “Maybe starting small with push-ups is doable”), but the psychiatrist does not reinforce or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist challenges and pressures the patient, which risks increasing resistance. There is no strategic use of reflections or affirmations to deepen change talk or explore the patient’s own motivations. The patient’s tentative commitments are met with demands rather than encouragement.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not skillfully cultivated or reinforced. The coercive style likely undermines the patient’s motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (e.g., “I don’t know if I’m really being lazy,” “I’m worried I’ll give up if I try too much at once”) is met with direct confrontation and dismissal. The psychiatrist labels these as “excuses” and demands the patient “stop hesitating.” There are no double-sided reflections, no normalization of ambivalence, and no gentle redirection. Instead, sustain talk is met with resistance, which is likely to increase patient defensiveness and reduce openness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften or skillfully manage sustain talk, instead reinforcing resistance through confrontation.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The tone is authoritarian and directive, with repeated commands and challenges. The patient is not invited to share their perspective or co-create a plan. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s expressed concerns and preferences, insisting on immediate and total compliance. This creates a clear power imbalance and undermines the therapeutic alliance.

**Summary:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist dominates the interaction and disregards the patient’s autonomy.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Avoid commands and directives. Invite the patient’s perspective and preferences.  
   - Use inclusive language and co-create goals and plans.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.  
   - Validate the difficulty of change and normalize setbacks.

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions that invite elaboration.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen understanding and evoke motivation.

4. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or ability, reflect and reinforce these statements.  
   - Explore the patient’s own reasons and values for change.

5. **Softly Manage Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses.”

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.  
   - Ask permission before giving advice or information.

7. **Collaborative Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Summarize key points and change talk to reinforce motivation.  
   - Develop realistic, patient-centered action plans.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Judgment:**  
   - Replace “You have to” with “What do you think about…” or “How might you…”  
   - Recognize and affirm small steps and efforts.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, engagement, and motivation. There is a critical need to adopt MI principles of empathy, acceptance, collaboration, and evocation to effectively support the patient’s behavior change. Without substantial improvement, this approach risks alienating patients and reducing the likelihood of successful outcomes.
