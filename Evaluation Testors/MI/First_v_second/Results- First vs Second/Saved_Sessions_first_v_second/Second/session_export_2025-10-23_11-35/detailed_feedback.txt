# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive from the outset. The tone is authoritative and pressuring rather than supportive or respectful. For example, statements like “You have to quit as soon as possible,” “You really should stop now,” and “Do you understand how dangerous smoking is?” convey urgency but lack warmth or positive regard. There is no evidence of affirming the patient’s efforts or acknowledging his success in quitting smoking for two months. The psychiatrist does not validate the patient’s experience or struggles, which undermines acceptance. The patient’s initial positive and proud disclosures are met with skepticism and pressure rather than support.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance is predominantly judgmental and directive, lacking unconditional positive regard or warmth. This risks alienating the patient and reducing engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no evidence of empathic listening or understanding. The psychiatrist does not reflect or paraphrase the patient’s statements, nor do they acknowledge the patient’s feelings or perspective. Instead, the psychiatrist immediately challenges and questions the patient in a confrontational manner (“Why didn’t you succeed?” “You really must understand…”). The patient expresses pride and commitment, but the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead pushes a rigid agenda. This lack of empathy likely inhibits rapport and openness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy or reflective listening, missing critical opportunities to understand and validate the patient’s experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive rather than collaborative or autonomy-supportive. The psychiatrist repeatedly uses commands (“You have to quit immediately,” “You must understand,” “You have to be 100% committed”) and does not invite the patient’s perspective or choices. There is no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values beyond superficial questioning. The psychiatrist does not support autonomy or partnership, instead imposing a rigid agenda and pressuring compliance.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the session is dominated by persuasion and directive commands, undermining collaboration and autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the psychiatrist asks questions about quitting history and commitment, these are framed in a confrontational and judgmental way that risks eliciting sustain talk or resistance rather than genuine change talk. The patient does offer some change talk (e.g., “I did quit smoking,” “I’m proud,” “I want to be healthier”), but the psychiatrist does not reinforce or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist challenges and pressures, which may suppress further change talk.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not skillfully evoked or reinforced; the psychiatrist’s approach may inhibit deeper exploration of motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not acknowledge or validate any sustain talk or ambivalence. Instead, sustain talk or hesitation is met with confrontation and pressure (“You have to be 100% committed,” “No half measures,” “This isn’t optional”). There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. This approach likely increases resistance and defensiveness.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of softening sustain talk; the psychiatrist reinforces sustain talk through confrontation and lack of validation.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritative stance throughout. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or negotiation of goals. The patient’s input is minimized or challenged rather than invited or respected. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands and warnings rather than collaborating or eliciting the patient’s agenda.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and fails to engage the patient as an equal partner.

---

- The psychiatrist’s style is highly directive, judgmental, and confrontational.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directive commands to partnership and shared decision-making.  
   - Use inclusive language and invite the patient’s perspective.

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and experiences.  
   - Affirm the patient’s successes and strengths (e.g., quitting smoking for two months).

3. **Evoke Change Talk Skillfully:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions that explore the patient’s motivations and values.  
   - Reflect and amplify change talk to deepen commitment.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change and validate the patient’s concerns.

5. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s control over choices and pace of change.  
   - Avoid ultimatums or “must” statements.

6. **Use Affirmations and Summaries:**  
   - Recognize and reinforce patient strengths and efforts.  
   - Summarize key points to consolidate motivation and plan next steps.

7. **Avoid Confrontation and Judgment:**  
   - Replace “You have to” and “You must” with collaborative language.  
   - Focus on eliciting rather than imposing reasons for change.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s approach is confrontational, directive, and lacking in empathy, acceptance, and collaboration. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a client-centered, autonomy-supportive style that skillfully evokes and reinforces the patient’s own motivations for change. Without these changes, the session risks alienating the patient and undermining progress toward smoking cessation.
