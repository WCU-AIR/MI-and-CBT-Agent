# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach throughout the session is judgmental, directive, and lacking in warmth or respect. The repeated use of phrases like “You really should just quit completely right now,” “You have to quit now,” and “You need to get serious” convey a disapproving and authoritarian tone. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s ambivalence with accusations of “making excuses,” “laziness,” and “overcomplicating,” which undermines any sense of unconditional positive regard. There is no evidence of normalization, validation, or acceptance of the patient’s struggles or feelings. The patient’s hesitancy and ambivalence are met with pressure and confrontation rather than empathy or support.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance or unconditional positive regard. The stance is judgmental and directive, which is counterproductive in motivational interviewing.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The responses do not demonstrate reflective listening or perspective-taking. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with commands and questions that dismiss the patient’s expressed fears, ambivalence, and emotional struggles. For example, when the patient expresses fear of failure and feeling overwhelmed, the psychiatrist responds with “You’re just making excuses” or “You’re overcomplicating this,” which invalidates the patient’s experience. There are no complex or simple reflections that capture the patient’s feelings or thoughts. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s progress or struggles empathetically.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is largely absent. The psychiatrist fails to listen reflectively or validate the patient’s emotional state, which damages rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is highly authoritarian and directive, with the psychiatrist repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding immediate action. There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s perspective or explore their motivations; instead, the patient is pressured to comply. The repeated commands (“You have to quit now,” “You need to get serious,” “Stop making excuses”) reflect a controlling stance. The patient’s autonomy is ignored, and the psychiatrist does not support or respect the patient’s readiness or ambivalence.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent. The session is dominated by persuasion and directive commands rather than collaboration and evocation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient expresses some change talk (e.g., “I want to be healthier,” “I want to be here for my family”), the psychiatrist does not reflect, amplify, or explore these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on pushing the patient to quit immediately, ignoring the patient’s ambivalence and readiness. There are no strategic reflections or questions designed to deepen the patient’s motivation or explore reasons for change. The psychiatrist’s confrontational style likely suppresses change talk rather than cultivates it.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not effectively evoked or reinforced. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to build motivation and deepen commitment.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly confronts and challenges sustain talk with accusations and demands rather than softening or redirecting it. The patient’s sustain talk about fear, ambivalence, and using smoking as a coping mechanism is met with “You’re making excuses,” “That’s laziness,” and “You’re overcomplicating this.” There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s sustain talk while gently guiding toward change. This approach likely increases resistance and defensiveness.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened or redirected, which is counterproductive.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is one-sided, with the psychiatrist dominating the conversation and issuing commands. The patient’s input is repeatedly dismissed or challenged. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or negotiation of goals. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in their own life or invite them to co-create a plan.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance.

- Reflective listening and empathy need to be consistently demonstrated.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—must replace directive commands.  
- Change talk should be actively evoked and reinforced.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling or blaming language.  
   - Normalize ambivalence and struggles.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion.  
   - Validate the patient’s experience and fears.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than direct.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy and choice.

4. **Elicit and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and explore these statements.  
   - Use open-ended questions to deepen motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Avoid confrontation or challenges that increase resistance.

6. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Ask questions that invite elaboration and exploration.  
   - Affirm the patient’s strengths, efforts, and progress.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Negotiate next steps with the patient.  
   - Summarize key points and reinforce commitment.

8. **Avoid Pressuring Language:**  
   - Replace “You have to” and “You need to” with invitational language.  
   - Respect the patient’s readiness and pace.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental style undermines rapport, empathy, and patient engagement. To provide effective motivational interviewing, the psychiatrist must radically shift to a collaborative, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive approach that skillfully evokes and reinforces the patient’s own motivations for change. Without these changes, the risk of patient resistance, disengagement, and poor outcomes is high.
