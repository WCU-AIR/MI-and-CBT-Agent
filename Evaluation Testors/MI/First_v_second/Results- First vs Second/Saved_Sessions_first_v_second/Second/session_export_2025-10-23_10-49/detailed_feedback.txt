# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. The opening statements such as “you really need to make changes,” “you have to quit immediately,” and “there’s no excuse” communicate disapproval and pressure rather than unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly insists on what the patient “must” do, which undermines acceptance. There is no evidence of warmth, respect for the patient’s autonomy, or validation of the patient’s perspective. The patient’s guarded and defensive responses are met with blunt commands rather than empathy or understanding. This approach likely increases resistance rather than fostering openness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely authoritarian and directive, lacking the fundamental acceptance necessary for effective motivational interviewing.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no evidence of empathic listening or reflective statements. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective, feelings, or ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly asks “why haven’t you quit yet?” in a confrontational manner, ignoring the patient’s expressed ambivalence and resistance. The patient’s statements about controlling use, attending only because of his wife, and acknowledging some relational strain are not explored or validated. The lack of reflective listening or emotional attunement results in a missed opportunity to build rapport or deepen understanding.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy, missing critical chances to connect with the patient’s experience or acknowledge ambivalence.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive approach with repeated commands and ultimatums (“you need to quit,” “there’s no excuse”). There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s perspective or explore his motivations; instead, the patient is positioned as someone who must comply. The repeated “why haven’t you quit?” questions function as confrontational challenges rather than invitations to explore ambivalence. This style is antithetical to the MI spirit and likely to increase resistance.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks the core MI elements of partnership, evocation, and autonomy support, instead adopting a coercive and confrontational stance.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. The patient offers minimal change talk (e.g., “sometimes it causes fights,” “I’m here because my wife said so”), but the psychiatrist ignores these cues and does not reflect or amplify them. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly presses for immediate cessation without exploring the patient’s own reasons or readiness for change. There is no strategic elicitation of desire, ability, reasons, or need for change, nor any attempt to elicit commitment language.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is largely ignored and not cultivated, missing critical opportunities to build motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and pressure rather than softening or strategic redirection. The patient’s sustain talk (“I don’t think it’s a problem,” “I keep it balanced,” “I’m here because my wife said so”) is met with repeated commands and “no excuse” ultimatums. There are no double-sided reflections, no normalization of ambivalence, and no attempts to validate the patient’s perspective while gently guiding toward change. This approach likely entrenches sustain talk and resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced by confrontational responses rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and directives, with no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s autonomy. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of commands rather than an active partner. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or preferences, nor does he acknowledge the patient’s expertise in his own life.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks partnership and is characterized by an expert-driven, authoritarian approach.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- None identified. The psychiatrist’s approach is consistently counterproductive to MI principles.

### Areas for Improvement  
- Replace confrontational, directive language with open-ended questions and reflective listening.  
- Build rapport through empathy and acceptance.  
- Elicit and reinforce change talk rather than ignoring or confronting sustain talk.  
- Support patient autonomy and collaborate on goals and plans.  
- Avoid ultimatums and “no excuse” language that provoke resistance.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental Stance:** Begin sessions by expressing genuine acceptance and respect for the patient’s perspective, even if resistant. Avoid labeling or blaming language.

2. **Use Reflective Listening:** Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and encourage patient elaboration.

3. **Ask Open-Ended Questions:** Replace “why haven’t you quit?” with questions like “What are your thoughts about your drug use?” or “How do you see it affecting your life?”

4. **Elicit Change Talk:** When the patient mentions relational strain or ambivalence, explore these statements to evoke motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:** Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge the patient’s perspective while gently highlighting discrepancies.

6. **Support Autonomy:** Emphasize that change is the patient’s choice and control, avoiding pressure or ultimatums.

7. **Collaborate on Planning:** When readiness emerges, work together to develop achievable steps rather than demanding immediate cessation.

8. **Avoid Confrontation:** Replace “you have to quit now” with curiosity and partnership to reduce defensiveness.

This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental approach undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s motivation, and likely increases resistance. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, empathic, and autonomy-supportive style that skillfully elicits and reinforces change talk while softening sustain talk. Without these changes, the session risks alienating the patient and impeding progress.
