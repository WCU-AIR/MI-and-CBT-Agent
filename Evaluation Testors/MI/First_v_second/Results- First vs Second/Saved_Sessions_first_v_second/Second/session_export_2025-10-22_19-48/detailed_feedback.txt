# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s tone throughout the session is largely judgmental and directive rather than accepting. Statements such as “You really need to quit smoking and start exercising more,” “You have to understand,” and “No excuses” convey a lack of unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s reasons for not changing with a confrontational style (“You have to stop making excuses,” “You must understand,” “No more complaints”), which risks alienating the patient. There is minimal evidence of warmth, empathy, or validation of the patient’s experience. The patient’s expressions of exhaustion and hopelessness are met with demands rather than acceptance. This pattern is consistent across the session, indicating a predominantly disapproving and directive stance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate unconditional positive regard and instead adopts a judgmental, pressuring approach that undermines rapport and patient engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows limited effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient repeatedly expresses exhaustion, hopelessness, and reliance on self-harm and smoking as coping mechanisms, yet the psychiatrist dismisses these with commands and challenges rather than reflective listening. There are no complex or emotional reflections that capture the patient’s distress or ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s responses do not convey understanding of the patient’s emotional state or the difficulty of change. For example, when the patient says, “I’m just really exhausted,” the psychiatrist replies, “You have to push yourself,” which invalidates the patient’s experience rather than empathizing with it.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is minimal; the psychiatrist misses opportunities to validate and explore the patient’s feelings, resulting in a disconnect and lack of emotional attunement.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is highly authoritarian and directive, lacking collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You have to,” “No excuses,” “Just commit”) and questions that imply blame (“Why didn’t you just push through?” “Why didn’t you do that before?”). There is no evidence of partnership or respect for the patient’s autonomy. The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change but instead imposes an agenda. The patient is positioned as passive and responsible for failure, which is contrary to the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The session exemplifies an authoritarian, expert-driven style with no collaboration or evocation, fundamentally opposing the spirit of motivational interviewing.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist attempts to elicit change talk by asking about prior quit attempts and reasons to quit, but these efforts are framed as challenges or demands rather than open invitations. The patient offers some change talk (e.g., “I don’t want serious problems,” “Maybe I could try gum”), but the psychiatrist does not reinforce, reflect, or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist quickly returns to pressuring language, which likely suppresses further change talk. There is no strategic use of reflections or affirmations to deepen or sustain motivation.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and poorly reinforced; the psychiatrist’s confrontational style likely inhibits the patient’s willingness to explore or express motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is frequent in the patient’s speech (e.g., “Cutting is the only thing that helps,” “I’m too tired,” “It feels impossible”), but the psychiatrist does not soften or skillfully respond to it. Instead, sustain talk is met with direct challenges and demands (“Stop making excuses,” “You have to push through tiredness”), which risks increasing resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead reinforces resistance through confrontational responses.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The interaction is dominated by commands and questions that imply blame and failure. The patient is not invited to share expertise about their own experience or to co-create goals or plans. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s autonomy. The psychiatrist’s language (“You have to,” “No excuses”) reinforces a power imbalance.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks partnership; the psychiatrist assumes an authoritarian role rather than working as an equal collaborator.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently addresses the health risks associated with smoking and cutting.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is judgmental, directive, and confrontational.  
- There is minimal empathy or acceptance of the patient’s experience.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is absent.  
- Change talk is poorly cultivated and sustain talk is not softened.  
- Partnership is not established; the patient is positioned as passive and blamed.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid commands and blame. Use language that conveys respect and unconditional positive regard.

2. **Enhance Empathy and Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion. Validate the patient’s exhaustion and struggle.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient, evoke their own motivations, and support autonomy.

4. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Invite exploration of feelings, values, and ambivalence rather than focusing on compliance.

5. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Reflect and reinforce any statements favoring change to deepen motivation.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and validate the difficulty of change to reduce resistance.

7. **Incorporate Affirmations and Summaries:**  
   - Recognize patient strengths and efforts; summarize to reinforce motivation and understanding.

8. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to identify feasible, patient-centered steps rather than issuing demands.

9. **Avoid Persuasion Without Permission:**  
   - Ask permission before giving advice or information.

This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, empathy, and patient engagement. There is a critical need to shift toward a collaborative, accepting, and evocative approach that respects the patient’s autonomy and emotional experience. Without substantial improvement, this approach risks increasing patient resistance and reducing the likelihood of meaningful behavior change.
