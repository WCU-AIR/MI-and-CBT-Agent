# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely directive and judgmental rather than accepting. From the outset, the psychiatrist uses language that implies blame and pressure (“Why haven’t you quit yet?”, “You really need to cut back or stop completely,” “You have to understand…”). There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth toward the patient’s experience. The patient’s expressions of ambivalence and limitations are met with impatience and dismissal rather than validation. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s struggles or feelings, instead focusing on what the patient “should” do. This creates a judgmental atmosphere rather than a supportive one.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance is predominantly critical and directive, lacking genuine acceptance or respect for the patient’s perspective and ambivalence.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient shares feelings of discouragement, physical limitations, and ambivalence about change, but the psychiatrist responds with repeated commands and challenges rather than reflective listening. There are no reflective statements that capture the patient’s emotional experience or validate his struggles. The psychiatrist’s responses do not convey understanding or empathy but instead push the patient toward compliance. The patient’s subtle cues of distress and hesitation are ignored or overridden.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist fails to accurately perceive or convey the patient’s feelings and perspective, missing opportunities for reflective listening.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with the psychiatrist repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding immediate compliance. There is no collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore his motivations or values. Instead, the patient is positioned as someone who must follow orders. The spirit of MI—partnership, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist’s style is coercive and controlling.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks any MI spirit; it is dominated by an authoritarian, prescriptive approach that undermines collaboration and autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient offers some change talk (e.g., “I want to feel better,” “Maybe we can talk about smaller steps”), the psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce this language. Instead, the psychiatrist dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and attempts at expressing readiness at his own pace. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore the patient’s motivations or to amplify his tentative change talk. The focus remains on compliance rather than eliciting the patient’s own reasons and desires for change.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally cultivated; the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce the patient’s own motivations.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly confronts and challenges sustain talk with pressure and admonishment rather than softening or exploring it. The patient’s expressions of difficulty, ambivalence, and barriers are met with demands and accusations (“stop making excuses,” “you have to take control”). There is no use of double-sided reflections or normalization of ambivalence. Sustain talk is reinforced as a problem to be overcome immediately rather than understood and gently redirected.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened or explored, which likely increases patient resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and commands, with no invitation for the patient to participate in decision-making. The patient’s perspective is overridden, and his autonomy is ignored. The psychiatrist repeatedly insists on immediate behavior change without negotiation or shared planning.

**Summary:**  
There is no partnership; the psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritarian stance that marginalizes the patient’s voice.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary  
The psychiatrist’s approach in this session is fundamentally inconsistent with motivational interviewing principles. The session is characterized by judgment, lack of empathy, authoritarianism, and coercion. The patient’s ambivalence and limitations are ignored or dismissed, and there is no collaborative exploration of motivation or barriers. Change talk is not effectively evoked or reinforced, and sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening. The psychiatrist fails to establish a partnership or support autonomy.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian, directive, and confrontational, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.  
- No affirmations, partnership, or autonomy support are present.  
- The psychiatrist uses closed, leading questions and provides unsolicited advice without permission.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and physical limitations are dismissed rather than explored.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from directing and commanding to partnering with the patient. Invite the patient’s perspective and preferences.

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and struggles. Avoid judgmental or confrontational language.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   Ask questions that invite elaboration and exploration of the patient’s motivations, values, and barriers.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and reinforce these statements to build motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalization to acknowledge ambivalence and reduce resistance.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

7. **Avoid Unsolicited Advice:**  
   Ask permission before offering information or suggestions.

8. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   Work with the patient to identify realistic, manageable steps toward change.

9. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   Use summaries to consolidate motivation and clarify next steps.

This session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational approach undermines rapport, empathy, and collaboration. The patient’s ambivalence and limitations are ignored, and there is no effective evocation of motivation or support for autonomy. Immediate and substantial improvement in MI skills is necessary to avoid harm and improve patient outcomes.
