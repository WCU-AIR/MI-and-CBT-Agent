# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. The tone is authoritative and pressuring, with repeated commands such as “You really should just stop,” “You have to take this seriously,” and “Just commit to quitting this week.” There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or unconditional positive regard. The patient’s resistance and dismissive attitude are met with insistence and confrontation rather than acceptance or validation. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s perspective or feelings, instead repeatedly invalidating the patient’s minimization and impatience. This approach risks alienating the patient and increasing resistance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive, lacking respect for the patient’s autonomy and perspective. There is minimal acceptance or supportive communication.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is little to no evidence of empathic understanding or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or convey the patient’s perspective or emotional state. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with admonishments and demands. The patient’s expressions of impatience, minimization, and compliance-oriented attitude are ignored or dismissed rather than explored or reflected. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to validate the patient’s feelings or acknowledge the difficulty of change. The lack of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s disengagement and sustain talk.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy or reflective listening, resulting in a disconnected and confrontational interaction.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly commands the patient to quit, sets ultimatums, and ignores the patient’s expressed ambivalence and resistance. There is no attempt to evoke the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change, nor is the patient invited to participate in decision-making. Autonomy is not supported; instead, the psychiatrist insists on immediate commitment without negotiation or exploration. This approach is antithetical to the spirit of motivational interviewing.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive stance with no collaboration or evocation, undermining the core spirit of MI.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce any change talk. The patient’s minimal change talk (“I just wanna get whatever I need to get done, done”) is ignored or overridden by repeated commands to quit immediately. The psychiatrist fails to explore or amplify any statements that could indicate readiness or reasons for change. Instead, the focus remains on pressuring the patient to commit, which likely suppresses any emerging motivation. There is no strategic use of reflections or questions to deepen change talk.

**Summary:**  
No meaningful evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and insistence rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s minimization and resistance with admonishments (“stop making excuses,” “you have to take this seriously”), which likely entrenches sustain talk. There are no double-sided reflections, affirmations, or normalization of ambivalence. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to acknowledge the patient’s perspective and gently guide toward change.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and escalated by confrontational responses; no skillful softening or redirection is evident.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains an expert-driven, authoritative stance throughout. The patient is not invited to share their views or participate in setting goals. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands and demands, ignoring the patient’s expressed feelings and resistance. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or power-sharing. The interaction is hierarchical and directive, which undermines partnership.

**Summary:**  
No collaborative partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and disregards patient autonomy.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid commands and ultimatums. Respect the patient’s perspective and feelings.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy and Reflective Listening:**  
   - Use reflections to show understanding of the patient’s ambivalence and resistance.  
   - Validate the patient’s experience and emotions.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy and choice.

4. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Invite the patient to elaborate on their thoughts and feelings about marijuana use and quitting.

5. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses any desire or reason for change, reflect and explore it further.

6. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and affirmations to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.

7. **Engage in Collaborative Planning:**  
   - Work with the patient to set realistic goals and next steps based on their readiness.

8. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   - Periodically summarize the patient’s statements to reinforce motivation and clarify goals.

This session reflects a fundamental misunderstanding or misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, engagement, and motivation. Immediate training and supervision in MI techniques are strongly recommended.
