# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a largely judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the tone is authoritative and prescriptive (“you really need to quit smoking immediately,” “you have to take this seriously now”), which undermines unconditional positive regard. The patient’s expressions of frustration and ambivalence are met with repeated insistence and pressure rather than acceptance or validation. There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or normalization of the patient’s struggle. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s perspective and resistance, which likely contributes to the patient’s defensiveness and withdrawal.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly directive and judgmental, lacking the respectful, supportive stance essential for acceptance in MI.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses frustration with repetitive advice and the difficulty of quitting, yet the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead escalates pressure. There are no reflective statements or attempts to paraphrase the patient’s feelings or ambivalence. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s stressors or validate the complexity of her situation. The patient’s subtle change talk (“common sense somewhere in there”) is not reflected or explored empathetically.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist does not demonstrate accurate understanding or convey it back to the patient, missing opportunities to build rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with frequent persuasion and little collaboration. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“you have to quit immediately,” “start the patches tomorrow,” “10 minutes of walking, no questions”) and demands commitment without eliciting the patient’s own motivations or values. Autonomy is not supported; instead, the patient is pressured and confronted. There is no evocation of the patient’s reasons for change or exploration of ambivalence beyond superficial questioning. The psychiatrist does not work in partnership with the patient but rather imposes a rigid agenda.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the session is dominated by control and persuasion rather than collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient offers subtle change talk (“common sense somewhere in there”), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses it, instead focusing on sustain talk and resistance. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify the patient’s desire or reasons for change. The psychiatrist does not explore the patient’s ambivalence or elicit commitment language in a supportive manner. Instead, the focus is on demanding immediate change, which likely suppresses further change talk.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated; the psychiatrist misses critical opportunities to evoke and strengthen the patient’s motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk and resistance are met with confrontation and insistence rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s reasons for not quitting (“stop making excuses,” “you have to push through it”), which likely entrenches sustain talk and defensiveness. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s experience while gently guiding toward change. The patient’s ambivalence is ignored rather than explored or normalized.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced rather than softened; the psychiatrist’s responses escalate resistance instead of reducing it.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands, demands, and a rigid agenda. The patient’s input is minimized, and her autonomy is overridden by the psychiatrist’s insistence on immediate action. There is no negotiation or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient to express her own goals or preferences, nor does she acknowledge the patient’s expertise in her own life.  

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the session is authoritarian and directive, lacking client-centered collaboration.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently maintains focus on smoking cessation and health risks.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks acceptance and empathy.  
- There is no evidence of MI spirit: no collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support.  
- Change talk is ignored and sustain talk is reinforced.  
- The patient’s resistance is met with pressure rather than strategic softening.  
- Partnership is not established; the patient is not engaged as an equal participant.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.3/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks MI spirit.  
- There is no acceptance or empathy; the patient’s perspective is dismissed.  
- Change talk is not evoked or reinforced; sustain talk is reinforced.  
- The psychiatrist fails to establish partnership or support autonomy.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directing and commanding to partnering with the patient.  
   - Invite the patient’s perspective and acknowledge her expertise in her own life.

2. **Increase Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of quitting and the frustration with repetitive advice.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Ask questions that invite elaboration and exploration of motivations and barriers.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to deepen understanding and rapport.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses any desire or reason for change, reflect and explore it further.  
   - Avoid dismissing subtle change talk; instead, build on it.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Avoid confrontational language that increases resistance.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize that the patient has control over her choices and pace of change.  
   - Ask permission before offering advice or information.

7. **Collaborative Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Work with the patient to develop achievable goals and plans.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and clarify next steps.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s approach is directive, confrontational, and dismissive of the patient’s perspective, which undermines rapport and motivation. There is no evidence of MI spirit, empathy, or skillful handling of ambivalence. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, empathetic style that evokes the patient’s own motivations and supports autonomy, using core MI skills such as open questions, reflections, affirmations, and strategic handling of sustain talk. Without these changes, the patient is unlikely to engage meaningfully or progress toward change.
