# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive rather than accepting. The tone is confrontational and pressuring, with statements like “You need to quit now,” “Why didn’t you just stop then?” and “You have to take this seriously.” These phrases imply blame and lack of understanding of the patient’s struggle. There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist dismisses the patient’s stress and ambivalence, labeling it as “excuses” rather than legitimate barriers. This undermines acceptance and creates a potentially adversarial dynamic.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate unconditional positive regard or respect for the patient’s experience. The stance is judgmental and directive, which is counterproductive in MI.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist does not reflect or acknowledge the patient’s feelings, stress, or ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s concerns by calling stress an “excuse” and insisting on immediate cessation without exploring the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses stress and social triggers as real challenges, but the psychiatrist ignores these and pushes a rigid agenda. No reflective listening or perspective-taking is evident.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows little effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, missing critical opportunities to build rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, lacking collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly commands the patient to quit “now” and “no excuses,” ignoring the patient’s expressed ambivalence and motivation. There is no invitation for the patient to share their thoughts or feelings, no elicitation of the patient’s own reasons for change, and no support for autonomy. The psychiatrist’s stance is controlling and coercive, which is antithetical to the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to embody the collaborative, evocative, and autonomy-supportive spirit of MI, instead adopting a confrontational and prescriptive approach.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**  
While the psychiatrist asks about prior quit attempts and medication use, these questions are framed in a judgmental way (“Why didn’t you just stop then?” “Why haven’t you done that yet?”) that risks eliciting defensiveness rather than genuine change talk. The psychiatrist does not strategically reinforce or amplify the patient’s expressed desire to quit or their concerns about health. The patient does express change talk (e.g., “I want to quit,” “I’m serious about stopping”), but the psychiatrist does not capitalize on these statements to deepen motivation.

**Summary:**  
There is some attempt to elicit change talk, but it is undermined by judgmental phrasing and lack of reinforcement or amplification.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften or skillfully respond to sustain talk. Instead, the patient’s expressions of stress and social triggers are dismissed as “excuses” and the patient is told to “be stronger.” This approach reinforces sustain talk by invalidating the patient’s legitimate concerns and increasing resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or empathetic acknowledgments of ambivalence.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead exacerbates resistance through confrontational responses.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritative stance with no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The patient is repeatedly told what to do, with no invitations to collaborate or co-create a plan. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in their own life or negotiate goals or strategies. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
There is no meaningful partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and disregards the patient’s input.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently emphasizes the importance of quitting smoking.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is judgmental, directive, and lacks empathy.  
- There is no collaboration or support for autonomy.  
- Change talk is not effectively cultivated or reinforced.  
- Sustain talk is ignored or invalidated rather than softened.  
- The power differential is unbalanced, with no partnership.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.3/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid blaming or shaming language.  
   - Recognize and validate the patient’s struggles and ambivalence.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy Through Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion.  
   - Acknowledge stress and social triggers as real challenges.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy and choice.

4. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Invite elaboration and exploration of feelings, values, and barriers.

5. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize past attempts and courage to seek help.

6. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Avoid dismissing or confronting sustain talk.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Discuss strategies for managing stress and social triggers alongside medication.  
   - Summarize key points and check for patient agreement.

8. **Avoid Persuasion Without Permission:**  
   - Ask before giving advice or prescribing.  
   - Respect patient readiness and pace.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental style undermines rapport, motivation, and collaboration. To improve, the psychiatrist must fundamentally shift toward a respectful, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive approach that elicits and reinforces the patient’s own motivations and addresses ambivalence with skillful reflections and partnership. Without these changes, the session risks alienating the patient and reducing the likelihood of successful behavior change.
