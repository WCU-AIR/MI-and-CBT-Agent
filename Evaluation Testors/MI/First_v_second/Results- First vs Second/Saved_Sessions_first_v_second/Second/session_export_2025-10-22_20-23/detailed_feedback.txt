# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is judgmental, directive, and lacks warmth or unconditional positive regard. From the outset, the psychiatrist issues commands (“you really should stop smoking immediately,” “You have to understand,” “Just quit now”) and repeatedly pressures the patient without acknowledging her feelings or struggles. There is no evidence of respect for the patient’s autonomy or validation of her experience. The tone is authoritarian and dismissive of the patient’s ambivalence and emotional state. This is the opposite of acceptance, which requires a nonjudgmental, supportive stance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance, instead adopting a harsh, directive tone that risks alienating the patient.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
There is no demonstration of empathy or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or convey the patient’s perspective. Instead, the psychiatrist interrupts with commands and questions that imply blame (“Why didn’t you succeed?” “What exactly is stopping you?”). The patient’s expressions of difficulty, fear, and ambivalence are ignored or minimized. The psychiatrist does not reflect or validate the patient’s emotional experience, nor does he acknowledge the complexity of her situation.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal to no effort to understand or empathize with the patient’s perspective.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely authoritarian and directive, lacking collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly tells the patient what she “has to” do, issues ultimatums, and fails to invite the patient’s input or explore her motivations. There is no partnership or respect for the patient’s autonomy; instead, the psychiatrist assumes an expert, controlling stance. This is antithetical to the spirit of MI.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is coercive and directive, with no evidence of MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Instead, the psychiatrist challenges the patient’s sustain talk with confrontation and demands rather than exploring or amplifying her expressed desire to quit. The patient does express change talk spontaneously, but the psychiatrist misses all opportunities to reflect or reinforce it. The focus is on compliance rather than motivation.

**Summary:**  
No effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk; missed opportunities to build motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften sustain talk but rather confronts and challenges it aggressively (“you’re making excuses,” “stress is no excuse”). There is no use of double-sided reflections or validation of ambivalence. Sustain talk is met with judgment and pressure, which likely increases resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening or strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
There is no partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation, issues commands, and fails to invite the patient’s perspective or co-create a plan. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of orders rather than an active participant.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership; the interaction is expert-driven and authoritarian.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary  
**Strengths:** None observed.  
**Areas for Improvement:** The psychiatrist must radically shift from a directive, judgmental stance to one of acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation.  

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on smoking cessation as the target behavior.  
- The patient is cooperative and provides rich information about her ambivalence and motivations.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and judgmental, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to use reflective listening, affirmations, or open-ended questions that invite exploration.  
- Change talk is ignored rather than cultivated.  
- Sustain talk is confronted rather than softened.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs.  
- The psychiatrist does not support autonomy or evoke the patient’s own motivations.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid commands and ultimatums. Instead, express understanding and respect for the patient’s experience and ambivalence.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Affirmations:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s statements to show understanding and encourage elaboration. Affirm her strengths and efforts to build confidence.

3. **Ask Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Use questions that invite the patient to explore her own motivations, barriers, and values.

4. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and amplify these statements to strengthen motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and validate ambivalence rather than confronting or dismissing sustain talk.

6. **Support Autonomy and Partnership:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient to set goals and develop a plan that fits her readiness and life context.

7. **Avoid Persuasion and Directives Without Permission:**  
   - Offer advice or information only after asking permission and in a collaborative manner.

8. **Summarize and Consolidate:**  
   - Periodically summarize the patient’s statements to reinforce motivation and clarify next steps.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s motivation, and risks increasing resistance. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive approach that skillfully evokes and reinforces the patient’s own reasons for change. Without these changes, the session is unlikely to facilitate meaningful progress toward smoking cessation.
