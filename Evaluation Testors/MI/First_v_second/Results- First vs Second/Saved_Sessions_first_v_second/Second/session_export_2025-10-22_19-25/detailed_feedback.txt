# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance throughout the session is predominantly judgmental and directive rather than accepting. From the outset, the psychiatrist adopts a confrontational tone (“I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” “You really shouldn’t be messing around with alcohol like that”), which undermines unconditional positive regard. The patient’s expressions of ambivalence and social context are repeatedly dismissed as “excuses” or “complications,” rather than being met with empathy or validation. The psychiatrist repeatedly questions the patient’s seriousness about health in a way that implies blame (“Are you just not serious about your health?”), which is likely to increase resistance. There is minimal evidence of warmth or support, and the patient’s concerns about team belonging are not normalized or accepted.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely directive and judgmental, lacking the respectful, supportive stance essential for acceptance in MI. This significantly impairs rapport and patient openness.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows limited effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient repeatedly expresses the difficulty of quitting due to team dynamics and fear of social exclusion, yet the psychiatrist dismisses these as “excuses” rather than exploring or validating the emotional complexity. There are no reflective statements that capture the patient’s ambivalence or emotional struggle. The psychiatrist’s repeated insistence on quitting “no matter what” and pressing for promises without acknowledging the patient’s feelings or barriers demonstrates a lack of empathic listening. The patient’s frustration and hesitancy are met with pressure rather than understanding.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is minimal; the psychiatrist fails to accurately understand or convey the patient’s perspective, missing opportunities to build rapport and deepen engagement.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is almost entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, directive stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding promises. There is no evidence of collaboration or partnership; the psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s ideas or motivations but instead imposes a rigid agenda. Autonomy is undermined by repeated demands and challenges to the patient’s seriousness, which likely increase resistance. The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s own reasons for change or explore ambivalence in a supportive way.  

**Summary:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, prescriptive approach that contradicts the core MI principles of partnership, evocation, and autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient spontaneously offers some change talk (e.g., “I know it’s bad for me,” “I want to quit eventually”), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce this language. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s ambivalence and pushes for immediate cessation without exploring the patient’s motivations or confidence. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify change talk. The repeated demands for promises may provoke sustain talk or resistance rather than deepen commitment.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not effectively cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist’s responses.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk related to social pressures and fear of exclusion is met with confrontation and dismissal. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s explanations as “excuses” and “complications,” repeatedly insisting the patient “stop making excuses.” There is no use of double-sided reflections or strategic reframing to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change. This approach likely entrenches sustain talk and resistance rather than softening it.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk, instead reinforcing resistance through confrontational responses.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The session is dominated by directives, demands, and challenges, with no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s expertise in his own life. The psychiatrist repeatedly presses for promises and questions the patient’s seriousness, which undermines partnership. The patient’s voice is marginalized, and the psychiatrist controls the agenda entirely.  

**Summary:**  
There is a clear power imbalance with an authoritarian stance; partnership is essentially absent.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directive commands to partnership. Invite the patient’s perspective and acknowledge their expertise in their own life.

2. **Increase Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Validate the patient’s feelings and social context. Avoid labeling explanations as “excuses.” Use reflective listening to demonstrate understanding.

3. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - Use open-ended questions and reflections to draw out the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change. Reinforce and amplify change talk when it arises.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and strategic reframing to acknowledge ambivalence and reduce resistance.

5. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change. Avoid pressuring for promises or commitments prematurely.

6. **Use Affirmations and Summaries:**  
   - Recognize the patient’s strengths and efforts. Summarize key points to consolidate motivation and clarify next steps.

7. **Avoid Confrontation and Judgment:**  
   - Replace confrontational language with curiosity and respect. Avoid questioning the patient’s seriousness or blaming.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational approach undermines rapport, fails to evoke motivation, and likely increases resistance. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, empathic style that supports autonomy, explores ambivalence, and skillfully cultivates change talk while softening sustain talk. Without these changes, the effectiveness of interventions and patient outcomes will be severely compromised.
