# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and authoritarian stance throughout the session. The opening statements are directive and lack warmth or respect for the patient’s perspective (e.g., “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” “You really should quit immediately”). There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or acceptance of the patient’s ambivalence or current readiness. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s expressed harm reduction goals and readiness to take small steps, instead insisting on immediate cessation. This approach is likely to alienate the patient and reduce engagement.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is disapproving, directive, and lacks respect for the patient’s autonomy and current stage of change.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no evidence of empathic listening or understanding. The psychiatrist does not reflect or validate the patient’s feelings, fears, or ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient with “Why haven’t you quit already?” and “You must commit now,” which invalidates the patient’s experience and readiness. The patient’s statements about using heroin to escape, concerns about overdose, and readiness for harm reduction steps are ignored or dismissed rather than explored or acknowledged.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy or reflective listening, missing critical opportunities to understand and validate the patient’s perspective.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands and demands commitment without inviting the patient’s input or exploring their motivations. There is no partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist’s repeated insistence on immediate quitting and labeling harm reduction as “band-aids” undermines the patient’s autonomy and readiness.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the session is coercive and directive rather than collaborative and autonomy-supportive.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient expresses readiness to take harm reduction steps and acknowledges risks, the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these statements. Instead of amplifying the patient’s expressed motivation (“I’m very ready to take some steps”), the psychiatrist focuses solely on immediate cessation and questions the patient’s commitment in a confrontational manner. There is no strategic elicitation or reinforcement of the patient’s own reasons or confidence for change.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is neither cultivated nor reinforced; the psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to build on the patient’s expressed motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften or skillfully respond to sustain talk. The patient’s ambivalence and reasons for continued use (escape, relaxation) are met with confrontation and accusations of “making excuses.” The psychiatrist’s responses likely increase resistance rather than reduce sustain talk. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal, exacerbating resistance rather than softening it.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared power. The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritarian stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do without inviting collaboration or input. The patient’s perspective is overridden by demands and ultimatums. The psychiatrist does not negotiate agenda or goals, nor does he acknowledge the patient’s expertise in their own life.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and fails to engage the patient as an equal collaborator.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.  
- Empathic listening and reflective statements are essential to understand and validate the patient’s perspective.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—must replace the current directive approach.  
- Change talk should be actively elicited and reinforced.  
- Sustain talk requires skillful softening rather than confrontation.  
- Partnership and shared decision-making are critical to engage the patient effectively.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The patient demonstrates readiness for harm reduction and some motivation for change.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks MI spirit.  
- There is no acceptance, empathy, or partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.  
- The use of closed, leading questions and absence of reflections undermine engagement.  
- No affirmations or collaborative planning are present.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Respectful Stance:**  
   - Avoid commands and ultimatums.  
   - Express unconditional positive regard for the patient’s experience and ambivalence.

2. **Use Empathic Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Validate the patient’s feelings and struggles.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy and choice.

4. **Elicit and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses readiness or reasons for change, reflect and reinforce these statements.  
   - Ask open-ended questions to deepen motivation.

5. **Softly Respond to Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Avoid confrontation or labeling sustain talk as “excuses.”

6. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Invite the patient to share their perspective and feelings.  
   - Affirm strengths, efforts, and small steps taken.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Explore feasible next steps with the patient.  
   - Summarize key points and reinforce commitment.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, engagement, and motivation. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, empathic, and collaborative approach that actively elicits and reinforces the patient’s own motivations and readiness for change. Without these changes, the session risks increasing resistance and disengagement, ultimately harming patient outcomes.
