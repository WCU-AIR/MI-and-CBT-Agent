# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a largely judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. The tone is frequently confrontational and pressuring, with repeated commands such as “You really need to quit,” “You have to stop now,” and “This isn’t optional.” There is little to no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s expressions of ambivalence and readiness are met with skepticism or dismissal rather than acceptance. For example, when the patient says, “I’m not sure I’m ready,” the psychiatrist responds with “You need to stop hesitating,” which undermines acceptance. The psychiatrist fails to normalize the patient’s struggle or validate their feelings, which is critical in MI.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive, lacking the respectful, supportive stance required for effective MI. This severely limits rapport and patient openness.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal evidence of reflective listening or attempts to understand the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist repeatedly asks “Why haven’t you done it already?” and issues commands without acknowledging the patient’s feelings or ambivalence. The patient’s statements about feeling unready or lacking support are ignored or met with pressure rather than empathetic reflection. For example, when the patient says, “It feels like a big deal, and I’m not sure I’m there yet,” the psychiatrist does not reflect this emotional nuance but instead insists on immediate action. The lack of paraphrasing or validation of the patient’s experience indicates poor empathy.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate accurate understanding or convey empathy, missing opportunities to build trust and explore ambivalence.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is dominated by an authoritarian, directive style with repeated commands and warnings. There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly tells the patient what to do (“You must throw away cigarettes,” “You have to be serious,” “This isn’t optional”) without inviting the patient’s perspective or exploring their motivations. The patient’s autonomy is disregarded, and the psychiatrist does not elicit or reinforce the patient’s own reasons for change. This approach is antithetical to the MI spirit.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive stance, lacking collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. Instead of eliciting the patient’s own motivations or reasons for quitting, the psychiatrist issues commands and questions that challenge or confront the patient’s ambivalence (“Why haven’t you done it already?”). The patient offers tentative statements about readiness and understanding risks, but these are not amplified or explored. There is no strategic use of reflections or open questions to deepen change talk or commitment language.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated; the psychiatrist’s approach suppresses rather than encourages patient motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and pressure rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s ambivalence and reasons for delay with “You have to stop hesitating,” “No more maybe later,” and “This isn’t optional.” There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change. This approach likely increases resistance rather than reducing sustain talk.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced or confronted harshly, lacking skillful softening or ambivalence resolution.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains an expert-driven, authoritative stance throughout. There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The patient’s views and feelings are overridden by commands and demands. The psychiatrist does not invite collaboration or negotiate goals or plans. For example, repeatedly asking “Are you ready to commit today?” after dismissing the patient’s expressed ambivalence is coercive rather than collaborative.  

**Summary:**  
The session lacks partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and minimizes patient input.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist consistently emphasizes the health risks of smoking and the urgency of quitting.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks the core MI elements of acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and autonomy are disregarded, which likely increases resistance.  
- There is no use of reflective listening, affirmations, or open questions to build rapport or deepen motivation.  
- Sustain talk is confronted rather than softened, missing opportunities to resolve ambivalence.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs, limiting patient engagement and commitment.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid commands and pressure. Instead, express genuine curiosity and respect for the patient’s experience and readiness.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s ambivalence and feelings to demonstrate understanding and build rapport.

3. **Support Autonomy and Collaboration:**  
   - Invite the patient’s perspective and preferences. Avoid insisting on immediate quitting; instead, explore readiness and barriers.

4. **Evoke Change Talk Strategically:**  
   - Use open-ended questions and reflections to draw out the patient’s own reasons and motivations for change.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and affirmations to acknowledge the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change.

6. **Incorporate Affirmations:**  
   - Recognize and reinforce patient strengths, efforts, and willingness to engage.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to set achievable goals and strategies, respecting their pace and readiness.

8. **Avoid Persuasion Without Permission:**  
   - Ask permission before giving advice or information to maintain partnership and respect.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive approach undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s motivation, and likely increases resistance. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, empathetic style that respects patient autonomy, uses reflective listening, and strategically evokes change talk. Without these changes, the effectiveness of counseling and patient outcomes will be severely compromised.
