# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive rather than accepting. From the outset, the psychiatrist states, “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” which immediately establishes an authoritarian tone and undermines client autonomy. The repeated insistence that the patient “must” quit drinking completely and the labeling of the patient’s responses as “making excuses” further diminish acceptance. There is no evidence of warmth, positive regard, or validation of the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s strengths or efforts, instead focusing on compliance and discipline.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a largely judgmental and directive stance with minimal acceptance or respect for the patient’s autonomy and perspective.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows little to no empathy throughout the session. The patient expresses ambivalence and a desire for gradual change, but the psychiatrist dismisses these concerns and pushes for immediate, strict abstinence. There are no reflective statements or attempts to understand the patient’s feelings or motivations. The psychiatrist’s repeated accusations of “making excuses” and demands for compliance indicate a lack of perspective-taking or emotional attunement. The patient’s polite but hesitant responses are met with pressure rather than understanding.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy or reflective listening, instead adopting a confrontational and dismissive tone.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do without eliciting the patient’s own motivations or ideas. There is no collaboration or partnership; the psychiatrist ignores the patient’s expressed ambivalence and willingness to consider smaller steps. Autonomy is not supported; rather, the patient is pressured to comply with a rigid plan. This approach is antithetical to MI principles.  

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by directive, prescriptive communication with no evidence of MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient expresses willingness to try cutting back and acknowledges the impact of alcohol on energy, the psychiatrist dismisses these statements and insists on complete abstinence. There is no strategic amplification or exploration of the patient’s own reasons for change. The patient’s tentative commitment to small steps is ignored rather than reinforced.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present in the patient’s speech but is not cultivated or supported by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk—patient’s reluctance to quit drinking completely—is met with confrontation and dismissal. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s concerns as “making excuses” and refuses to acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s ambivalence. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize the difficulty of change. Instead, sustain talk is met with pressure and demands, likely increasing resistance rather than softening it.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead reinforces resistance through confrontational responses.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist repeatedly tells the patient what to do and demands commitment without negotiation or shared decision-making. The patient’s suggestions for smaller, manageable steps are dismissed. The power differential is stark, with the psychiatrist adopting an expert, authoritarian role.  

**Summary:**  
The session lacks any meaningful partnership or client-centered collaboration.

---

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Avoid telling the patient what to do. Instead, invite their perspective and preferences. Use language that supports autonomy.

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings and ambivalence. Validate their experience and struggles without judgment.

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions to explore motivations and barriers. Reflect both content and emotion to deepen understanding.

4. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses willingness or reasons for change, reinforce and explore these statements to build motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalize ambivalence to reduce resistance.

6. **Collaborative Planning:**  
   - Work with the patient to develop achievable, personalized goals rather than imposing strict demands.

7. **Avoid Confrontation and Labels:**  
   - Refrain from accusing the patient of “making excuses” or being undisciplined. This damages rapport and motivation.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational approach undermines acceptance, empathy, and collaboration, leading to missed opportunities to evoke and reinforce change talk. The patient’s autonomy and ambivalence are disregarded, likely increasing resistance and reducing the likelihood of sustained behavior change. Immediate and substantial improvement in MI skills is essential to avoid harm and improve patient outcomes.
