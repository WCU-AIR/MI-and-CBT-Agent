# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental and confrontational stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“you need to quit smoking”—the tone is directive and lacking warmth or empathy. The psychiatrist repeatedly uses language that blames and shames the patient (“stop feeling sorry for yourself,” “stop being weak,” “no excuses,” “you’re just making excuses,” “you have to be stronger than that”). There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or respect for the patient’s ambivalence or struggle. The patient’s expressions of fear, guilt, and difficulty are met with criticism rather than support. This approach is likely to increase patient resistance rather than foster collaboration.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is harsh, directive, and lacking in acceptance. There is no demonstration of respect or positive regard for the patient’s experience.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy or reflective listening. The patient’s emotional expressions—fear, guilt, ambivalence, social conflict—are ignored or dismissed. Instead of reflecting or validating the patient’s feelings, the psychiatrist responds with commands and accusations. For example, when the patient expresses feeling overwhelmed or scared, the psychiatrist replies with “stop being scared,” “no room for feeling overwhelmed,” and “you have to be strong.” There are no attempts to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, nor to explore the emotional complexity of her situation.

**Summary:**  
There is a near-complete absence of empathy. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or reflect the patient’s feelings or perspective.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“you have to quit,” “just do it,” “no more excuses”) and uses shaming language. The patient is positioned as someone who must comply rather than as an active partner in change. There is no elicitation of the patient’s own motivations or values, no support for autonomy, and no partnership. The psychiatrist’s stance is the antithesis of the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent. The session is dominated by control, persuasion, and directive commands.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient does express some change talk (e.g., “I want to be responsible for this baby,” “I want to quit,” “I’m trying”), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce this language. Instead, the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and change talk, focusing instead on commands and criticism. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify the patient’s motivation. The patient’s change talk is present but not cultivated or deepened.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present in the patient but is not effectively evoked or reinforced by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften sustain talk; rather, sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal. The patient’s expressions of difficulty, fear, social pressure, and ambivalence are labeled as “excuses,” “weakness,” or “selfishness.” There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change. This approach likely increases resistance and sustain talk rather than softening it.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted and not softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains an authoritarian, expert-driven stance throughout. The patient is repeatedly told what to do, with no invitation to collaborate or share power. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise or autonomy, nor does she negotiate goals or plans. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of orders rather than an equal partner.

**Summary:**  
There is no partnership or collaboration; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently maintains focus on the goal of smoking cessation.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is harsh, judgmental, and directive, lacking acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- There is no reflective listening or validation of the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.  
- Change talk is not cultivated or reinforced.  
- Sustain talk is confronted rather than softened.  
- The spirit of MI is absent.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid blaming or shaming language.  
   - Express unconditional positive regard for the patient’s experience and struggle.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy Through Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion.  
   - Validate the patient’s fears, guilt, and ambivalence.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and values.  
   - Support autonomy and choice.

4. **Skillfully Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Reflect and reinforce statements favoring change.  
   - Explore reasons, desires, and confidence for quitting.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change.

6. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Invite elaboration and exploration.  
   - Affirm patient strengths and efforts specifically.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Negotiate feasible steps and timelines.  
   - Summarize progress and reinforce commitment.

8. **Avoid Coercion and Commands:**  
   - Replace directives with invitations and suggestions.  
   - Ask permission before giving advice or information.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian, confrontational, and lacking in empathy, acceptance, and collaboration. The patient’s ambivalence and struggle are met with criticism rather than support, likely increasing resistance and undermining motivation. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a client-centered, empathetic, and collaborative style that evokes and reinforces the patient’s own motivations for change. Without these changes, the effectiveness of intervention and patient outcomes will be severely compromised.
