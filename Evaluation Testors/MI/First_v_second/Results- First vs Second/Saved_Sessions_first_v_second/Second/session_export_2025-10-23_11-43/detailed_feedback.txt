# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach throughout the session is largely judgmental and directive rather than accepting. The repeated use of phrases like “stop making excuses,” “you need to be tougher,” and “just do it” convey a lack of unconditional positive regard and come across as critical and dismissive of the patient’s experience. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge or validate the patient’s feelings, struggles, or context, which is essential for acceptance. The patient’s emotional expressions of stress and ambivalence are met with admonishment rather than empathy or support. This pattern is consistent across the session, indicating a failure to establish a respectful, supportive environment.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance is predominantly judgmental and directive, lacking warmth and genuine acceptance. This undermines rapport and client engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient repeatedly expresses the complexity of her life stressors and ambivalence about quitting smoking, yet the psychiatrist responds with blunt commands and challenges rather than reflective listening or validation. There are no complex or simple reflections that capture the patient’s emotional state or underlying concerns. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s fears, exhaustion, or motivation, which results in a disconnect and likely contributes to patient resistance.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist does not demonstrate accurate or consistent understanding of the patient’s perspective or emotional experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with the psychiatrist repeatedly telling the patient what to do and dismissing her concerns. There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore her motivations or values beyond superficial references to her son’s health. Instead, the psychiatrist uses confrontational language (“stop making excuses,” “you have to be stronger”) that undermines partnership and autonomy. This approach is antithetical to the spirit of motivational interviewing.  

**Summary:**  
The session lacks the fundamental MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support; it is instead coercive and directive.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient expresses several statements of change talk (desire to be healthy for her son, recognition of smoking’s dangers), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce this language. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on pushing the patient to quit immediately, ignoring the patient’s ambivalence and readiness. There are no strategic reflections or questions designed to deepen change talk or explore the patient’s motivations. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to amplify the patient’s expressed desire and commitment to change.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not effectively cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly confronts and challenges sustain talk with admonishments and dismissive language. Rather than softening or exploring sustain talk, the psychiatrist labels it as “excuses” and “hesitation,” which likely increases patient resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s concerns about stress and readiness, missing critical opportunities to reduce sustain talk’s impact.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than skillful softening or exploration.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritative stance throughout the session. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or negotiation of goals. The patient’s input is minimized or ignored, and the psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands rather than invitations. This power imbalance is likely to alienate the patient and reduce engagement. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in her own life or collaborate on a plan.  

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by an authoritarian stance with minimal partnership or client-centeredness.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses” or “weakness.”  
   - Express genuine respect and understanding for the patient’s struggles.

2. **Enhance Empathy Through Reflective Listening:**  
   - Use simple and complex reflections to validate and explore the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to deepen understanding.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Support autonomy by inviting the patient to share her thoughts and make choices.

4. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and explore these statements to strengthen motivation.

5. **Softly Address Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change.

6. **Use Affirmations and Summaries:**  
   - Affirm the patient’s strengths, efforts, and intentions.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and clarify next steps.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Invite the patient to co-create a quit plan that fits her life and readiness.  
   - Discuss options for support and resources with permission.

8. **Ask Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Use questions that invite elaboration and exploration rather than yes/no answers.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s motivation, and likely reduces the patient’s readiness to change. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive approach that skillfully evokes and reinforces the patient’s own reasons for change while gently addressing ambivalence. Without these changes, the effectiveness of interventions and patient outcomes will be severely compromised.
