# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is consistently judgmental, directive, and confrontational throughout the session. From the very first utterance, the psychiatrist uses language that implies blame and pressure (“You really should quit as soon as possible,” “You must stop,” “You have to be honest here,” “You must take this seriously,” “You must quit immediately”). There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or respect for the patient’s perspective. The patient’s defensive and minimization responses are met with dismissive and accusatory remarks (“You’re just making excuses,” “Stop making excuses,” “You have to take responsibility here”). This style is likely to increase resistance rather than foster collaboration. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge or validate the patient’s experience or ambivalence, which is critical for acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental, directive stance with no warmth or support. This is far from the acceptance required in MI and risks alienating the patient.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of empathic listening or reflective statements that convey understanding of the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with admonishments and questions that challenge the patient’s honesty or motivation without attempting to understand the patient’s rationale or feelings. The patient expresses frustration and a sense of being misunderstood (“You’re pushing me too hard without really understanding how I see it”), but the psychiatrist does not respond to this emotional content. The lack of reflective listening or validation results in a missed opportunity to build rapport or explore ambivalence.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal to no effort to understand or convey empathy, instead focusing on persuasion and confrontation.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is highly authoritarian and directive, with the psychiatrist repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding commitment (“You must stop,” “Are you going to commit to quitting now or not?”). There is no collaboration, no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values, and no support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore their perspective beyond challenging it. The patient’s autonomy is ignored or overridden, which is antithetical to the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, expert-driven stance with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Instead, the approach is to confront and demand change, which leads the patient to resist and sustain their current behavior. The psychiatrist asks closed, leading questions focused on compliance (“Are you going to quit this week or not?” “Are you going to set a quit date now or keep risking your health?”) rather than open questions that explore the patient’s own reasons or desires for change. There is no amplification or reinforcement of any tentative change talk the patient offers.

**Summary:**  
No effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the approach likely suppresses motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with direct confrontation and accusations (“You’re just making excuses,” “Stop making excuses,” “You have to take responsibility here”). The psychiatrist does not use reflective listening, double-sided reflections, or any strategies to soften sustain talk or explore ambivalence. Instead, sustain talk is challenged aggressively, which likely increases resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced negatively rather than softened or explored.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation, issues commands, and repeatedly demands answers and commitments. The patient’s perspective is dismissed or ignored. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
No partnership or collaboration; the interaction is authoritarian and directive.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.  
- Empathic listening and reflective statements are essential to understand and validate the patient’s perspective.  
- The spirit of MI requires collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support, all absent here.  
- Change talk must be skillfully elicited and reinforced rather than demanded or confronted.  
- Sustain talk should be softened through reflective and strategic responses.  
- Partnership requires sharing power and working as equals, not issuing ultimatums.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist consistently maintains focus on the smoking cessation topic.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly directive, confrontational, and judgmental, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and instead provokes sustain talk and resistance.  
- No partnership or autonomy support is provided.  
- The session lacks collaborative planning or summarizing.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling, blaming, or pressuring language.  
   - Show genuine respect for the patient’s perspective and experience.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.

3. **Shift from Directive to Collaborative:**  
   - Invite the patient’s input and explore their motivations and concerns.  
   - Support autonomy by emphasizing choice and control.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Use open-ended questions to explore reasons, desires, and confidence for change.  
   - Reflect and reinforce any statements favoring change.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Avoid confrontation; gently guide toward exploring change.

6. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Summarize key points and elicit the patient’s ideas for next steps.  
   - Support commitment without coercion.

7. **Avoid Closed, Leading Questions:**  
   - Use open questions that invite elaboration and exploration.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s approach is directive, confrontational, and judgmental, which undermines rapport, increases resistance, and fails to engage the patient’s motivation for change. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, empathic, and autonomy-supportive style that skillfully evokes and reinforces the patient’s own reasons for change. Without these changes, the effectiveness of smoking cessation counseling will be severely limited.
