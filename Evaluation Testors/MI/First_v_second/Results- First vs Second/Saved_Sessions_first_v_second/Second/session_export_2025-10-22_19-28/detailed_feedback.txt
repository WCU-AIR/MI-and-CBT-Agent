# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental, directive, and confrontational from the outset. Statements such as “You really should stop smoking as soon as possible,” “You have to understand,” “No excuses,” and “You have to be firm about it” communicate a lack of unconditional positive regard. The patient’s perspective and autonomy are disregarded, and the tone is coercive rather than supportive. There is no evidence of warmth, respect, or validation of the patient’s feelings or experiences. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and concerns, which is antithetical to acceptance in MI.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate acceptance, instead adopting a harsh, authoritarian stance that risks alienating the patient.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no evidence of empathic understanding or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand the patient’s perspective or feelings about smoking. Instead, the psychiatrist interrupts with directives and challenges without acknowledging the patient’s ambivalence or emotional state. The patient expresses concerns about social belonging and low perceived risk, but these are ignored or dismissed rather than explored or validated. The lack of empathy is evident in the repeated “You have to” and “No excuses” statements, which shut down dialogue rather than foster understanding.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is essentially absent; the psychiatrist does not listen reflectively or convey understanding of the patient’s viewpoint.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is completely missing. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven style, telling the patient what to do without invitation or partnership. There is no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values, no support for autonomy, and no collaborative agenda-setting. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands and ultimatums (“You have to quit now,” “No excuses,” “Are you ready to quit today?”), which contradict MI principles.

**Summary:**  
The session is directive and controlling, lacking any collaborative or autonomy-supportive elements.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or cultivate change talk. Instead of eliciting the patient’s own reasons, desires, or confidence for change, the psychiatrist issues demands and warnings. The patient’s tentative statements about occasional smoking and social pressures are met with challenges rather than reflections or amplifications. There is no strategic reinforcement or deepening of any change talk that might have emerged. The psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses rather than encourages change talk.

**Summary:**  
No skillful evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the psychiatrist’s style likely inhibits patient motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and pressure rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s ambivalence and sustain talk with ultimatums and admonishments (“You really can’t keep saying that,” “Stop making excuses”). There are no double-sided reflections or validations of the patient’s struggle. This approach risks increasing resistance and entrenching sustain talk.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than skillfully softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a partnership with the patient. The interaction is one-sided, with the psychiatrist dominating the conversation and issuing commands. The patient’s input is ignored or dismissed, and there is no negotiation or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist’s language (“You have to,” “No excuses,” “Are you ready to quit now?”) reinforces a power imbalance and undermines collaboration.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership; the psychiatrist assumes an authoritarian role.

---

### **Overall Global Ratings Summary**  
The psychiatrist’s style is antithetical to motivational interviewing principles. The session is directive, confrontational, and lacking in acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and skillful handling of ambivalence. This approach is likely to increase patient resistance and reduce motivation for change.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** 1/5

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks MI spirit.  
- No acceptance, empathy, or partnership is demonstrated.  
- No reflective listening or affirmations are used.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and instead confronts sustain talk harshly.  
- Autonomy is ignored, and the patient’s readiness is not respected.  
- Planning is unilateral and lacks collaboration.  
- The approach risks increasing resistance and disengagement.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from directive commands to partnership. Invite the patient’s perspective and respect autonomy.

2. **Demonstrate Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   Use reflective listening to understand and validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   Ask questions that encourage elaboration and exploration rather than yes/no or leading questions.

4. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   Explore the patient’s own reasons, desires, and confidence for change. Reflect and amplify change talk.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and validate ambivalence rather than confronting or dismissing it.

6. **Affirm Strengths and Efforts:**  
   Recognize any positive intentions or past efforts to build rapport and motivation.

7. **Collaborate on Planning:**  
   Develop a change plan together, ensuring the patient’s buy-in and readiness.

8. **Avoid Ultimatums and Pressure:**  
   Replace “You have to” and “No excuses” with supportive, autonomy-respecting language.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style is likely to alienate the patient and reduce motivation for change. Immediate and substantial improvement is needed in acceptance, empathy, collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support to provide effective motivational interviewing.
