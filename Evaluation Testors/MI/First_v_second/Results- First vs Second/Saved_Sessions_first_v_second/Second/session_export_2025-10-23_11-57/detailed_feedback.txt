# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. The repeated insistence that the patient "needs to quit immediately" and admonishments such as "stop making excuses" and "you have to be strict with yourself" communicate disapproval rather than unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s emotional state or validate his struggles, instead repeatedly blaming the patient for lack of willpower or commitment. There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or acceptance of the patient’s ambivalence or stressors. This approach likely contributed to the patient’s defensive and frustrated responses.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely confrontational and directive, lacking the respectful, supportive tone essential for acceptance in MI. This undermines rapport and client engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. While the patient repeatedly expresses feeling overwhelmed, stressed, and ambivalent, the psychiatrist dismisses these concerns as "excuses" and insists on willpower and commitment without exploring the emotional context. There are no reflective statements that capture the patient’s feelings of overwhelm, frustration, or attachment to his dog. The psychiatrist’s responses are mostly closed and judgmental, missing opportunities to demonstrate understanding or validate the patient’s experience.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited and superficial. The psychiatrist does not engage in reflective listening or perspective-taking, which impairs the therapeutic alliance.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with frequent persuasion and pressure to quit immediately. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s reasons for not quitting, labels his statements as "excuses," and demands commitment without collaboration or evocation. There is no evidence of supporting autonomy or partnership; instead, the psychiatrist imposes an agenda and disregards the patient’s expressed ambivalence and barriers. This approach is antithetical to the MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.  

**Summary:**  
The session lacks the fundamental MI spirit. It is coercive and directive, which likely alienates the patient and reduces motivation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient does express some change talk (e.g., acknowledging smoking harms health, expressing desire to quit), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce this language. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on pushing the patient to commit immediately, ignoring the patient’s tentative and ambivalent statements. There are no strategic reflections or questions designed to deepen change talk or explore the patient’s motivations. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to amplify the patient’s expressed desire and willingness to consider change on his own terms.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but poorly cultivated. The psychiatrist’s confrontational style stifles deeper exploration and reinforcement of motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s sustain talk as "excuses" and "blaming," reinforcing resistance rather than softening it. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s ambivalence or barriers. Instead, sustain talk is met with confrontation and pressure, which likely increases defensiveness. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s stressors or emotional attachments, missing critical opportunities to gently guide the patient toward change.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than skillfully softened, undermining rapport and motivation.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritative stance throughout the session. There is no evidence of collaboration or shared decision-making. The patient is repeatedly told what he "has to" do, with no invitation to explore his own goals or preferences. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in his own life or negotiate the agenda. This power imbalance likely contributes to the patient’s frustration and resistance.  

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent. The session is dominated by a directive, top-down approach.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently addresses the health risks of smoking.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental tone to foster acceptance.  
- Empathy and reflective listening need significant enhancement.  
- The session should embody the MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.  
- Change talk should be actively cultivated and sustain talk skillfully softened.  
- Partnership and shared decision-making must replace the authoritarian approach.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is overly confrontational, directive, and judgmental, which damages rapport and motivation.  
- There is a critical lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The MI spirit is absent; collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support must be prioritized.

- The psychiatrist fails to engage the patient in planning or shared decision-making.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses.”  
   - Validate the patient’s feelings and struggles to build rapport.

2. **Enhance Empathy and Reflective Listening:**  
   - Use complex reflections to capture both content and emotion.  
   - Reflect ambivalence and emotional conflict to demonstrate understanding.

3. **Embody the MI Spirit:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy by emphasizing choice and control.

4. **Cultivate Change Talk and Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - When change talk arises, reflect and amplify it.  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.

5. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions to explore the patient’s perspective.  
   - Affirm patient strengths, efforts, and willingness to consider change.

6. **Avoid Persuasion Without Permission:**  
   - Ask permission before giving advice or information.  
   - Tailor suggestions to patient readiness and preferences.

7. **Engage in Collaborative Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Summarize key points and ambivalence to clarify understanding.  
   - Collaboratively develop feasible, patient-centered next steps.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental approach undermines acceptance, empathy, and collaboration. The patient’s ambivalence and emotional barriers are dismissed rather than explored, and change talk is not cultivated. To improve, the psychiatrist must fundamentally shift to a respectful, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive style that evokes the patient’s own motivations and supports gradual, patient-centered change. Without these changes, the session risks alienating the patient and reducing the likelihood of successful behavior change.
