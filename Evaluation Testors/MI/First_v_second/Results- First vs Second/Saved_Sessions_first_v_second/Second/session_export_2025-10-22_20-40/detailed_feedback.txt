# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s tone throughout the session is largely judgmental and directive rather than accepting. Statements such as “You really should stop now,” “You have to cut down immediately,” and “No more excuses” communicate disapproval and pressure rather than unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s ambivalence with admonishments rather than empathy or understanding. There is little evidence of warmth or support; instead, the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance that risks alienating the patient. The patient’s attempts to express ambivalence or barriers are met with dismissal or demands rather than validation.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive approach, lacking the respectful, supportive stance essential for MI. This undermines rapport and client engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses social pressures and awkwardness around drinking less, but the psychiatrist does not explore or validate these feelings. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly pushes for immediate behavior change without acknowledging the patient’s ambivalence or emotional experience. Reflections are largely absent; the psychiatrist paraphrases facts but does not demonstrate deeper understanding or emotional attunement. The patient’s subtle cues of discomfort or hesitation are ignored or overridden by directive statements.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited to factual summarizing without genuine perspective-taking or emotional validation, reducing the patient’s sense of being heard.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session lacks the core MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, prescriptive stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding commitment. There is no genuine partnership or invitation for the patient to explore their own motivations or reasons for change. Autonomy is undermined by commands (“Just do it,” “No more excuses”) rather than supported. The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s values or goals, nor does he express curiosity or openness to the patient’s perspective.  

**Summary:**  
The session is directive and controlling, antithetical to MI spirit, which severely limits the potential for effective motivational interviewing.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the psychiatrist asks about drinking quantity and consequences, there is little skillful evocation or reinforcement of change talk. The patient offers some tentative change talk (“I guess I can try to keep it to one or two drinks”), but the psychiatrist does not amplify or explore this language. Instead, the psychiatrist quickly shifts back to commands and warnings, missing opportunities to deepen motivation or explore ambivalence. The patient’s tentative commitments are met with pressure rather than encouragement.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and poorly reinforced; the psychiatrist misses chances to build motivation through reflective listening and strategic questioning.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is frequent in the patient’s responses (e.g., social norms, awkwardness, difficulty cutting down), but the psychiatrist does not soften or skillfully respond to it. Instead, sustain talk is met with confrontation and admonishment (“Stop making excuses,” “You have to be strong”). There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s responses likely increase resistance rather than reduce sustain talk.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead reinforces resistance through confrontational language.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and directives, with little invitation for patient input or shared decision-making. The patient’s perspective is overridden by commands and pressure. There is no evidence of co-creating goals or plans. The psychiatrist’s language (“You have to,” “Just do it”) reflects an expert-driven, authoritarian stance.  

**Summary:**  
Partnership is virtually absent; the psychiatrist controls the session without fostering equality or collaboration.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently addresses the target behavior (drinking).  
- Attempts to gather factual information about drinking patterns and consequences.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, judgmental, and confrontational.  
- Lack of empathy, acceptance, and partnership undermines rapport and engagement.  
- Failure to evoke and reinforce change talk or soften sustain talk.  
- No support for autonomy or collaborative exploration of ambivalence.  

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

- Maintains focus on drinking as the issue.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly directive, confrontational, and judgmental, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- Empathy, acceptance, and partnership are lacking, reducing patient engagement and openness.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk, missing key MI opportunities.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid admonishments and commands. Use language that conveys respect and unconditional positive regard.

2. **Enhance Empathy and Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion. Validate the patient’s experience and ambivalence.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient, evoke their own motivations, and support autonomy rather than directing or persuading.

4. **Skillfully Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or ability to change, reflect and explore these statements to deepen motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalize ambivalence to reduce resistance.

6. **Use More Open Questions:**  
   - Ask questions that invite elaboration and exploration rather than yes/no answers.

7. **Incorporate Affirmations and Summaries:**  
   - Recognize patient strengths and efforts. Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and clarify next steps.

8. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Engage the patient in setting realistic goals and strategies, respecting their readiness and autonomy.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s motivation, and likely increases resistance. To improve, the psychiatrist must shift to a collaborative, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive approach that skillfully evokes change talk and softens sustain talk. Without these changes, the session risks alienating patients and reducing the likelihood of meaningful behavior change.
