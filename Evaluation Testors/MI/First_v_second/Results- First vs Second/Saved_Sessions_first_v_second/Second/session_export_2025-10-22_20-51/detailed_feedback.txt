# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive rather than accepting. The repeated use of phrases like “you really need to quit,” “you must understand,” and “you have to stop making excuses” communicates disapproval and pressure rather than unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge or validate the patient’s struggles or ambivalence, nor do they express warmth or empathy. The tone is authoritative and somewhat confrontational, which likely increases patient defensiveness. There is no evidence of normalization or acceptance of the patient’s current state or feelings.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance is predominantly judgmental and directive, lacking the respectful, supportive tone essential for acceptance in MI.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The questions are closed and accusatory (“Why haven’t you quit yet?”), and the statements dismiss the patient’s explanations as “excuses.” There is no reflective listening or paraphrasing of the patient’s expressed ambivalence, stress, or fears. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the complexity of the patient’s situation or the emotional burden described. This lack of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s guarded and defensive responses.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist does not demonstrate accurate understanding or convey validation of the patient’s experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with the psychiatrist repeatedly telling the patient what to do and emphasizing urgency without collaboration or evocation. There is no support for autonomy; instead, the psychiatrist pressures the patient to “just decide” and “stop hesitating.” The patient’s autonomy is undermined by the persistent commands and lack of invitation to explore motivations or barriers. The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is essentially absent.

**Summary:**  
The session reflects an authoritarian, expert-driven approach, lacking collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Instead, the focus is on demanding change and dismissing sustain talk as “excuses.” The patient’s expressions of desire to feel better and ambivalence are ignored or challenged rather than explored. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify the patient’s motivation or reasons for change. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to elicit commitment language or explore confidence and ability.

**Summary:**  
No effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly confronts and challenges the patient’s sustain talk (e.g., stress as a reason for not quitting) without validating or exploring it. The patient’s explanations are labeled as “excuses,” and the psychiatrist insists on pushing through without acknowledging the legitimacy of the patient’s feelings or barriers. There are no double-sided reflections or strategic softening of sustain talk to reduce resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than skillful softening or redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist dominates the conversation with directive statements and questions that imply blame or failure. There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The patient is positioned as someone who must comply rather than collaborate. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s perspective or preferences beyond demanding answers. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
No meaningful partnership; the interaction is expert-driven and directive.

---

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.  
- Empathy and reflective listening are critically lacking.  
- The session lacks the collaborative, autonomy-supportive spirit of MI.  
- Change talk is neither evoked nor reinforced; sustain talk is confronted rather than softened.

### Areas for Improvement

- There is a critical lack of empathy, reflective listening, and acceptance.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is absent.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses.”  
   - Express understanding and validate the patient’s struggles and ambivalence.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings and thoughts to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Paraphrase ambivalence and fears to deepen rapport.

3. **Support Autonomy and Collaboration:**  
   - Invite the patient’s perspective and preferences.  
   - Avoid pressuring or demanding change; instead, explore readiness.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions that elicit reasons, desires, and confidence for change.  
   - Reflect and reinforce any statements favoring change.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Normalize ambivalence and validate the difficulty of quitting.

6. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Replace closed, confrontational questions with open, exploratory ones.  
   - Affirm patient strengths, efforts, and intentions.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Explore readiness and collaboratively set achievable goals.  
   - Summarize motivation and plan to consolidate commitment.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s approach is directive, judgmental, and confrontational, lacking empathy, acceptance, and collaboration. The patient’s ambivalence and motivation are neither evoked nor supported. To improve, the psychiatrist must fundamentally shift to a client-centered, autonomy-supportive style that uses reflective listening, open questions, and strategic evocation of change talk. Without these changes, the session risks alienating the patient and undermining motivation for change.
