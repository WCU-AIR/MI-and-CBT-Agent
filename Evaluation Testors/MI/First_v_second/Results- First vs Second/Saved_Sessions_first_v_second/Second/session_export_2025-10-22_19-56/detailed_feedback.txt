# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. The tone is authoritative and lacks warmth or genuine respect for the patient’s perspective. The patient’s expressions of stress, ambivalence, and coping strategies are met with dismissive language such as “stop making excuses” and “you have to,” which conveys disapproval rather than acceptance. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or normalization of the patient’s struggle. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the complexity of the patient’s life circumstances or validate her feelings, which is critical for acceptance.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive, undermining acceptance. The patient’s ambivalence and stress are not met with empathy or respect, resulting in a low score.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The responses are focused on pushing the patient toward quitting rather than exploring or validating her experience. There are no reflective statements or paraphrases that demonstrate understanding of the patient’s stress, ambivalence, or coping needs. The patient’s nuanced reasons for smoking and difficulties with quitting are ignored or dismissed. The lack of empathic listening likely contributes to the patient’s defensive posture and guarded responses.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited to surface-level acknowledgment without genuine understanding or reflective listening. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to connect with the patient’s emotional experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive approach with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly commands the patient to “just do it” and “stop making excuses,” which is antithetical to the MI spirit. There is no attempt to elicit the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change, nor is there support for her autonomy or choice. The power differential is stark, with the psychiatrist assuming an expert role and the patient positioned as a passive recipient of instructions.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent. The session is dominated by persuasion and directive commands, with no collaboration or evocation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient offers some tentative change talk (e.g., “I’ve thought about quitting,” “I want to feel better”), the psychiatrist fails to reflect, amplify, or explore these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on pushing directives and challenging the patient’s reasons, which likely suppresses further change talk. There is no strategic use of open-ended questions or reflections to deepen motivation or explore ambivalence.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally cultivated and often overshadowed by confrontational and directive statements.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s explanations as “excuses” and insists on immediate quitting without acknowledging the legitimacy of the patient’s concerns or ambivalence. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that validate sustain talk while gently guiding toward change. This approach likely entrenches resistance rather than softening sustain talk.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced or ignored rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritative role with no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The patient is repeatedly told what to do without invitation to collaborate or express her views. The psychiatrist’s language (“you have to,” “just do it”) minimizes the patient’s autonomy and expertise in her own life. There is no negotiation of agenda or explicit acknowledgment of the patient’s perspective as valuable.  

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the session is dominated by an expert-driven, directive approach.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

- The psychiatrist consistently maintains focus on smoking cessation.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must develop acceptance and empathy skills to create a supportive environment.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely missing.  
- Change talk is not cultivated; sustain talk is confronted rather than softened.  
- Partnership is nonexistent; the patient is positioned as passive and disempowered.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.3/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses.”  
   - Validate the patient’s experience and acknowledge the difficulty of quitting under stress.

2. **Develop Empathic Listening Skills:**  
   - Use reflective listening to demonstrate understanding of the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to build rapport.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Shift from directing to collaborating.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy by emphasizing choice and control.

4. **Cultivate Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and amplify these statements.  
   - Use open-ended questions to explore motivations and confidence.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Normalize ambivalence and validate the patient’s struggle.

6. **Enhance Partnership:**  
   - Invite the patient to co-create the agenda and plan.  
   - Avoid authoritative language; use inclusive and collaborative phrasing.

7. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Increase the use of open-ended questions to encourage elaboration.  
   - Affirm patient strengths, efforts, and protective behaviors (e.g., not smoking around her son).

8. **Collaborative Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Develop a realistic quit plan together, considering the patient’s life context.  
   - Summarize key points and reinforce commitment.

To improve, the psychiatrist must fundamentally shift toward a collaborative, empathic, and autonomy-supportive approach consistent with MI principles.
