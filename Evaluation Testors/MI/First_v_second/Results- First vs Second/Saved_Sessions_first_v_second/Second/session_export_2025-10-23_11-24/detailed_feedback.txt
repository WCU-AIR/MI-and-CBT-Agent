# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Smoking Cessation Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance throughout the session is predominantly judgmental and directive rather than accepting. The repeated commands (“You need to quit immediately,” “No, you don’t get to negotiate here”) and accusatory questions (“Are you just avoiding real effort?”) communicate disapproval and lack of empathy. The patient’s hesitations and ambivalence are met with impatience and dismissal rather than understanding. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or normalization of the patient’s struggle. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s perspective or emotional state in a supportive way.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely authoritarian and critical, undermining acceptance. This creates resistance rather than rapport.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s feelings or perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence, anxiety, and a desire for a manageable plan, but the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead pushes a rigid agenda. There are no reflective statements or attempts to paraphrase the patient’s concerns or values. The patient’s motivation tied to family and retirement is acknowledged only superficially and quickly overridden by the psychiatrist’s insistence on immediate quitting. The lack of empathic listening likely contributes to the patient’s defensiveness and reluctance.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited to factual questioning and directive statements, with no genuine understanding or validation of the patient’s experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is almost entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do without invitation or partnership. The patient’s autonomy is denied (“No, you don’t get to negotiate here”), and the psychiatrist uses confrontational language (“Are you going to keep making excuses?”). There is no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values beyond superficial questions that are immediately followed by commands. The session lacks any collaborative exploration or respect for the patient’s readiness or pace.  

**Summary:**  
The session is directive, confrontational, and controlling, violating core MI principles.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the psychiatrist asks about prior quit attempts and readiness, these questions are framed in a judgmental way that risks eliciting sustain talk or defensiveness rather than genuine change talk. The patient does express some change talk (desire to be healthy for retirement, willingness to try), but the psychiatrist does not reinforce or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist immediately challenges or dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and partial readiness. There is no strategic use of reflections or affirmations to deepen change talk or build confidence.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and poorly reinforced; the psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses rather than cultivates motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently confronts and challenges sustain talk with harsh language and accusations (“You’re just making excuses,” “Are you going to keep making excuses?”). There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s ambivalence or difficulty. The patient’s concerns about feeling overwhelmed and needing support are dismissed rather than explored. This approach likely increases resistance and sustain talk rather than softening it.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal, lacking any skillful softening or strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The patient is repeatedly told what to do, with no negotiation or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist’s language is authoritarian and controlling, minimizing the patient’s expertise and autonomy. The patient’s suggestions or concerns are ignored or invalidated. There is no evidence of co-creating a plan or agenda.  

**Summary:**  
The session is expert-driven and directive, with no meaningful partnership or shared power.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently maintains focus on the smoking cessation goal.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks acceptance, empathy, and collaboration.  
- There is no evocation or reinforcement of change talk.  
- Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening.  
- The patient’s autonomy and readiness are disregarded, undermining engagement and motivation.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the smoking cessation goal.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian, confrontational, and directive, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and instead confronts sustain talk harshly.  
- No reflective listening, affirmations, or collaborative planning are evident.  
- The patient’s autonomy and readiness are disregarded, likely increasing resistance and reducing motivation.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directing and commanding to partnering with the patient.  
   - Invite the patient’s perspective and respect their autonomy.

2. **Increase Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of quitting and acknowledge the patient’s efforts.

3. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and explore these statements to deepen motivation.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Avoid accusatory or judgmental language.

5. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions that invite elaboration.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to build rapport and understanding.

6. **Support Autonomy and Confidence:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s control over the change process.  
   - Explore manageable steps and build confidence gradually.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to identify feasible next steps.  
   - Summarize progress and reinforce commitment.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational approach undermines acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation of change talk. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a patient-centered, autonomy-supportive style that validates ambivalence, softens sustain talk, and collaboratively builds motivation and plans for change. Without these changes, the approach risks alienating patients and reducing the likelihood of successful behavior change.
