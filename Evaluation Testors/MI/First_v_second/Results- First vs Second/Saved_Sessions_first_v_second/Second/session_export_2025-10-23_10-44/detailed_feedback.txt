# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. The tone is frequently confrontational and admonishing, with statements such as “You really should stop now,” “You have to understand,” and “You really need to stop making excuses.” These phrases communicate disapproval and pressure rather than unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge or validate the patient’s feelings of overwhelm or ambivalence, nor does he express warmth or support. The patient’s hesitations and concerns are met with impatience and demands rather than empathy or acceptance. This approach risks alienating the patient and undermining rapport.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive, lacking the respectful, supportive stance essential for MI. There is minimal evidence of acceptance or positive regard.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows limited effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient repeatedly expresses feelings of overwhelm, uncertainty, and fear of failure, but the psychiatrist does not acknowledge or explore these emotions. Instead, the psychiatrist responds with challenges and commands, ignoring the patient’s emotional state. There are no reflective statements or paraphrases that demonstrate understanding of the patient’s ambivalence or anxiety. The lack of empathic listening is evident in the repeated “You have to…” and “Why haven’t you…” questions, which feel accusatory rather than supportive.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is minimal; the psychiatrist fails to convey understanding or validate the patient’s experience, missing opportunities for reflective listening.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with frequent persuasion and pressure. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You have to get the patches today,” “Stop hesitating,” “You have to take responsibility”) and challenges the patient’s readiness and motivation. There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore his own motivations or values. Instead, the interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and insistence on immediate action, which is contrary to the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support, reflecting an authoritarian and directive approach inconsistent with MI principles.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient offers some change talk (e.g., “I want to quit,” “I know I should try”), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on pushing the patient to act rather than exploring or amplifying the patient’s own motivations. There are no strategic reflections or questions designed to deepen change talk or elicit commitment language. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore the patient’s values or reasons for change and does not reinforce any expressions of desire, ability, or need.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but weakly evoked and not reinforced or amplified by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften or skillfully respond to sustain talk. The patient expresses sustain talk related to feeling overwhelmed, lack of confidence, and fear of failure, but the psychiatrist responds with confrontation and demands rather than gentle redirection or double-sided reflections. There is no normalization of ambivalence or validation of the difficulty of change. Instead, sustain talk is met with impatience and pressure, which likely increases resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and pressure rather than softening or strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a clear expert-driven, authoritative stance throughout the session. The patient is repeatedly asked “Why haven’t you…?” and told what to do without invitation or collaboration. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or co-creation of goals or plans. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in his own life or invite his perspective in a meaningful way. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction with little collaboration or shared power.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist consistently emphasizes the urgency of quitting smoking and its health implications.  
- Maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly directive, confrontational, and judgmental, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a near-complete absence of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.  
- No reflective listening, affirmations, or collaborative planning are present.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and emotional experience are ignored or dismissed.  
- The power differential is stark and unmitigated, undermining engagement and autonomy.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Supportive Stance:**  
   - Replace confrontational language with acceptance and validation of the patient’s feelings and struggles.  
   - Avoid blaming or shaming; instead, express understanding of ambivalence and difficulty.

2. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s statements to demonstrate understanding and encourage elaboration.  
   - Use complex reflections to capture underlying emotions and ambivalence.

3. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions that invite the patient to explore reasons, desires, and confidence for change.  
   - Reflect and reinforce any change talk to strengthen motivation.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge the patient’s concerns while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Normalize ambivalence and validate the difficulty of quitting smoking alongside diabetes management.

5. **Support Autonomy and Partnership:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient to set goals and develop plans.  
   - Emphasize the patient’s control over decisions and pace of change.

6. **Avoid Persuasion Without Permission:**  
   - Ask permission before offering advice or information.  
   - Use invitational language rather than commands.

7. **Develop Collaborative Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Summarize key points, especially change talk, to reinforce motivation.  
   - Co-create actionable, manageable steps with the patient.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly confrontational, directive, and judgmental, lacking acceptance, empathy, and collaboration. The patient’s ambivalence and emotional experience are ignored, and change talk is neither evoked nor reinforced. Sustain talk is met with pressure rather than softening. To improve, the psychiatrist must fundamentally shift to a patient-centered, empathetic, and collaborative style that supports autonomy and skillfully navigates ambivalence. Without these changes, the risk of disengagement and treatment failure is high.
