# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the very first utterance, the psychiatrist labels the patient’s behavior as problematic and frames the patient as failing or making excuses (“You need to stop making excuses,” “You really should just quit completely,” “You’re just wasting your time”). There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s feelings and experiences are dismissed or minimized repeatedly. The psychiatrist’s tone is authoritarian and critical rather than supportive or respectful. This is evident in repeated commands and ultimatums without validation or empathy.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance or respect. The approach is harsh, judgmental, and directive, which likely alienates the patient and undermines rapport.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective or emotional experience. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts or overrides the patient’s expressions of frustration, discouragement, and ambivalence with demands and accusations. For example, when the patient expresses feeling overwhelmed and tired, the psychiatrist responds with “No, you really have to commit fully,” ignoring the emotional content. The psychiatrist does not use reflective listening or validate the patient’s feelings at any point.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows little effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, resulting in a cold, dismissive interaction.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely lacking in the spirit of motivational interviewing. There is no collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. Instead, the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding compliance. The patient’s autonomy is ignored or undermined, with repeated ultimatums (“Are you ready to do that or not?”). The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change, nor does the psychiatrist support the patient’s sense of choice or control. The interaction is coercive rather than collaborative.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is antithetical to MI spirit, relying on persuasion, pressure, and control rather than partnership and evocation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient expresses desire and reasons for change (e.g., “I want to lose weight,” “I’m serious about wanting to lose weight”), the psychiatrist ignores these statements and instead focuses on compliance with tracking. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify the patient’s motivation. The psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts or redirects the conversation back to demands, missing opportunities to explore the patient’s ambivalence or elicit commitment language.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present in the patient’s speech but is not cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s expressions of ambivalence, fear, and resistance) is met with confrontation and pressure rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s statements about feeling overwhelmed or not ready, labeling them as “excuses” and “half-measures.” There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s responses likely increase resistance rather than reduce it.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than skillfully softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation, issues commands, and demands compliance. The patient’s input is ignored or dismissed. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient to share their perspective or co-create a plan. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, lacking any meaningful partnership.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.  
- Empathy and reflective listening are essential to understand and validate the patient’s experience.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—must replace authoritarian directives.  
- Change talk should be actively elicited and reinforced.  
- Sustain talk requires softening through strategic reflections and normalization.  
- Partnership must be established by sharing power and inviting patient input.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is counterproductive, likely increasing patient resistance and disengagement.  
- The session lacks all core MI elements: acceptance, empathy, collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.  
- The psychiatrist must learn to listen reflectively, validate the patient’s experience, and evoke intrinsic motivation.  
- The current style risks damaging the therapeutic relationship and undermining patient motivation.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling or blaming language.  
   - Show genuine respect for the patient’s efforts and struggles.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion.  
   - Validate the patient’s feelings of frustration and ambivalence.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than dictate.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own reasons and motivations for change.  
   - Support autonomy and choice.

4. **Elicit and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and explore these statements.  
   - Avoid dismissing or ignoring change talk.

5. **Softly Address Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change.

6. **Use Open Questions:**  
   - Invite the patient to explore their feelings, values, and barriers.

7. **Collaboratively Develop Plans:**  
   - Negotiate goals and steps with the patient.  
   - Avoid ultimatums or demands.

8. **Avoid Persuasion and Confrontation:**  
   - Replace directives with invitations and suggestions.  
   - Ask permission before giving advice.

This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s motivation, and likely increases resistance. Immediate and substantial retraining in MI techniques is essential to prevent harm and improve patient outcomes.
