# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist uses language that is controlling and confrontational (“I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” “You really should cut down immediately,” “You have to cut down immediately before it causes serious health problems”). There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s defensiveness and guarded responses reflect the lack of acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s statements in a way that implies blame (“Why haven’t you stopped already?” “Why haven’t you taken this seriously yet?”), which likely increases resistance. There is no normalization or validation of the patient’s perspective or ambivalence. This is consistent across all turns, meeting the lowest rating criteria.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate reflective listening or perspective-taking. The patient’s concerns, feelings, and social context are ignored or dismissed. For example, the patient expresses the importance of friends and the feeling of not wanting to be “the odd one out,” but the psychiatrist does not acknowledge or explore these feelings. Instead, the psychiatrist repeats warnings and directives without attempting to understand the patient’s viewpoint or emotional state. There are no reflections, paraphrases, or empathic statements. The patient’s frustration and defensiveness escalate, indicating a lack of empathic connection.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly tells the patient what to do, uses commands (“You have to cut down immediately,” “Just stop hanging out with those friends who smoke”), and asks confrontational questions (“Why haven’t you stopped already?”). There is no invitation for the patient’s perspective or input, no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values, and no support for autonomy. The psychiatrist’s stance is controlling and coercive, which is antithetical to the MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. The patient offers minimal change talk (e.g., “I guess maybe I should think about cutting back more”), but the psychiatrist does not reflect, amplify, or explore these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on warnings and commands, which likely suppresses change talk. There is no strategic use of open-ended questions or reflections to deepen the patient’s own reasons for change or confidence.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly confronts and challenges sustain talk without attempting to soften or reframe it. The patient’s sustain talk (e.g., “My friends don’t see it as a big deal,” “I’m not ready to quit,” “It’s not every day”) is met with direct contradiction and pressure. There are no double-sided reflections, no validation of ambivalence, and no attempts to reduce resistance. This approach likely increases sustain talk and patient defensiveness.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is one-sided, with the psychiatrist dominating the conversation and issuing directives. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of instructions rather than an active participant. There is no negotiation of agenda, no acknowledgment of the patient’s expertise in his own life, and no shared decision-making. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

---

### **Summary of Global Ratings:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally inconsistent with motivational interviewing principles. The session is characterized by judgment, confrontation, lack of empathy, and authoritarianism. There is no evidence of acceptance, collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. Change talk is neither cultivated nor reinforced, and sustain talk is met with direct opposition rather than softening. This results in a highly resistant patient and a failed MI interaction.

**Overall Global Ratings Score: 1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and judgmental, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, reflective listening, and acceptance.  
- The psychiatrist fails to engage the patient collaboratively or support autonomy.  
- No open-ended questions or reflections are used to evoke change talk or explore ambivalence.  
- Sustain talk is confronted rather than softened, increasing resistance.  
- No affirmations, summaries, or planning are present.  
- The patient’s social context and emotional experience are ignored.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from telling and directing to partnering with the patient. Invite the patient’s perspective and acknowledge their expertise.

2. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Replace closed, confrontational questions with open-ended ones that encourage exploration (e.g., “What do you think about your smoking?” “How does smoking fit into your life?”).

3. **Practice Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and build rapport.

4. **Affirm Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize any positive intentions or past efforts to change, even if small.

5. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize that the patient has control over their choices and pace of change.

6. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses any desire, ability, reason, or need for change, reflect and amplify these statements.

7. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and validate ambivalence rather than confronting or challenging sustain talk directly.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Judgment:**  
   - Refrain from blaming or pressuring the patient; instead, express genuine curiosity and acceptance.

9. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - When readiness emerges, work with the patient to set achievable goals and next steps.

10. **Summarize Regularly:**  
    - Use summaries to consolidate understanding and reinforce motivation.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational, and directive approach undermines rapport, increases patient resistance, and fails to evoke motivation for change. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a collaborative, empathic, and autonomy-supportive style that skillfully elicits and reinforces the patient’s own motivations and strengths. Without these changes, the risk of alienating patients and impeding behavior change is high.
