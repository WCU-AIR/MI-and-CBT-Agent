# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the very first utterance, the tone is commanding and lacks warmth or empathy (“you need to quit smoking,” “You really should just quit now”). The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s expressed barriers and feelings, labeling them as “excuses” and implying a lack of willpower (“stop making excuses,” “you have to toughen up”). There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or respect for the patient’s lived experience. The patient’s attempts to explain his challenges are met with impatience and reprimand rather than understanding. This is far from the respectful, supportive stance required for MI.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate reflective listening or perspective-taking. The patient expresses stress, time constraints, and feelings of being overwhelmed, yet the psychiatrist ignores these emotional cues and instead responds with admonishments and repeated demands. There are no reflections or paraphrases that validate or explore the patient’s experience. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s ambivalence or emotional state, missing critical opportunities to build rapport and understanding. The lack of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s defensive and frustrated responses.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with the psychiatrist repeatedly telling the patient what to do and when to do it (“You have to quit now,” “Set a quit date this week or not”). There is no collaboration, no evocation of the patient’s own motivations, and no support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s perspective or preferences but instead imposes a rigid agenda. This approach is antithetical to the MI spirit, which emphasizes partnership, evocation, and autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient expresses some desire and reasons for quitting, the psychiatrist ignores these and instead focuses on pushing the patient to commit prematurely. There are no strategic reflections or affirmations to amplify the patient’s own motivations. The psychiatrist’s confrontational style likely suppresses change talk rather than cultivates it.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s explanations as “excuses” and insists on immediate quitting without acknowledging the legitimacy of the patient’s ambivalence or barriers. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize sustain talk. Instead, the psychiatrist escalates pressure, which likely increases resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a hierarchical, expert-driven stance throughout. The patient is not invited to collaborate or share in decision-making. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands compliance and sets ultimatums (“Are you going to start using nicotine replacement this week or not?”). There is no evidence of shared power or respect for the patient’s autonomy.

---

### **Global Ratings Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally inconsistent with motivational interviewing principles. The session is characterized by judgment, confrontation, and directive commands, with no evidence of empathy, collaboration, or evocation. This style is likely to alienate the patient, reduce engagement, and undermine motivation for change.

**Overall Global Ratings Score: 1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains a clear focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is confrontational, judgmental, and directive, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, reflective listening, and acceptance.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.  
- No collaborative partnership or autonomy support is evident.  
- The session lacks open questions, affirmations, reflections, and summaries.  
- Planning is coercive rather than negotiated.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses.”  
   - Express genuine respect and positive regard for the patient’s experience.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings and ambivalence to build rapport.  
   - Validate the difficulty of quitting amid stress and life demands.

3. **Shift from Directive to Collaborative:**  
   - Invite the patient’s perspective and preferences.  
   - Support autonomy by emphasizing choice and control.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions to explore reasons for change.  
   - Reflect and affirm statements that favor quitting.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Normalize challenges and validate the patient’s struggles.

6. **Collaboratively Develop a Realistic Plan:**  
   - Explore small, achievable steps rather than demanding immediate quitting.  
   - Negotiate timelines and strategies that fit the patient’s life.

7. **Avoid Ultimatums and Coercion:**  
   - Replace “set a quit date this week or else” with invitations to consider readiness.  
   - Build motivation gradually rather than imposing deadlines.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session falls well below the standards of motivational interviewing. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental approach undermines rapport, suppresses motivation, and risks disengagement. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must fundamentally change their style to one of empathy, collaboration, and evocation, supporting the patient’s autonomy and readiness for change. Without these changes, the likelihood of successful smoking cessation is minimal, and the patient may feel alienated and resistant.
