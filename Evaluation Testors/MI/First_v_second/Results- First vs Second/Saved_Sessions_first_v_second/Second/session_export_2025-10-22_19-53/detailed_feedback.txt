# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive rather than accepting. The opening statement, “You really need to quit smoking as soon as possible,” sets a confrontational tone rather than expressing unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s perspective (“You have to understand that feeling fine now doesn’t mean you’re safe”) and issues commands (“You need to cut down immediately,” “Just try going a day without smoking”). There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or validation of Eric’s feelings or ambivalence. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge Eric’s autonomy or struggles, instead implying blame and urgency without support. This approach risks alienating the patient and shutting down openness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance or respect for Eric’s perspective, instead adopting a judgmental and directive stance.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist does not reflect or validate Eric’s feelings, concerns, or ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist pushes a rigid agenda, ignoring Eric’s guarded and ambivalent stance. The questions are closed and leading, focusing on compliance rather than understanding Eric’s experience. The psychiatrist does not attempt to explore Eric’s rationale for smoking, his stress coping, or his readiness to change beyond superficial questioning. This lack of empathic engagement likely contributes to Eric’s guarded responses.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows little effort to understand or convey Eric’s perspective, missing critical opportunities for reflective listening and emotional attunement.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session lacks the core elements of MI spirit: collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, prescriptive stance, repeatedly telling Eric what he “has to” do and demanding commitment. There is no evidence of partnership or eliciting Eric’s own motivations or values. Autonomy is not supported; instead, the psychiatrist pressures Eric to commit immediately, disregarding his ambivalence and readiness. The session is directive and confrontational, which is antithetical to MI principles.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to embody the MI spirit, instead using a coercive, expert-driven approach that undermines collaboration and client autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While asking about prior quit attempts and cigarette use, the questions are leading and framed to elicit compliance rather than genuine motivation. There is no effort to explore Eric’s own reasons for change, values, or goals. The psychiatrist does not reflect or amplify any tentative change talk Eric offers (“I’m not closing the door on thinking about it more”). Instead, the focus is on commands and warnings, which tend to elicit sustain talk or resistance.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated; the psychiatrist misses opportunities to evoke and reinforce Eric’s own motivations and ambivalence.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften or strategically respond to sustain talk. Eric’s statements about feeling fine, the role of his job, and his guardedness are met with challenges and directives rather than reflections or validations. The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or affirm the legitimacy of Eric’s concerns, which would help reduce resistance. Instead, sustain talk is met with confrontation, likely increasing defensiveness.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is ignored or confronted rather than softened or redirected, missing a key MI skill.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation, issuing commands and expecting compliance. Eric’s input is minimized, and his autonomy is not respected. The psychiatrist does not invite Eric to co-create goals or plans, nor does he acknowledge Eric’s expertise in his own life. The interaction is hierarchical and directive.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to establish a collaborative partnership, instead adopting an authoritarian stance.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid confrontational language and commands.  
   - Express genuine respect for Eric’s perspective and ambivalence.

2. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect Eric’s statements and emotions to demonstrate understanding and encourage elaboration.

3. **Support Autonomy and Collaboration:**  
   - Invite Eric to share his thoughts and preferences.  
   - Avoid pressuring for immediate commitment; respect his readiness.

4. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions that explore Eric’s own reasons for change and values.  
   - Reflect and amplify any change talk to strengthen motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change.

6. **Avoid Persuasion Without Permission:**  
   - Ask permission before giving advice or information.  
   - Share information collaboratively.

7. **Develop Collaborative Plans:**  
   - Work with Eric to set achievable goals and next steps based on his readiness.

8. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   - Use summaries to consolidate motivation and clarify plans.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental style undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s own motivations, and likely increases resistance. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, empathic, and autonomy-supportive approach that skillfully evokes change talk and softens sustain talk. Without these changes, the session risks alienating the patient and reducing the likelihood of meaningful behavior change.
