# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely directive and judgmental rather than accepting. The opening statements such as “We need to talk about your smoking habit because it’s really important you quit as soon as possible” and “You really should stop now to avoid serious health problems” convey urgency but lack warmth or unconditional positive regard. The tone implies pressure and a lack of empathy for the patient’s complex situation. There is no explicit acknowledgment or validation of the patient’s struggles or strengths. The psychiatrist does not normalize the difficulty of quitting or express understanding of the patient’s ambivalence. This creates a somewhat confrontational atmosphere rather than a supportive one.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate unconditional positive regard or warmth. The stance is more authoritative and directive, which undermines acceptance.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. Questions such as “Why didn’t you succeed?” and “Have you thought about using nicotine patches or gum?” are closed and somewhat accusatory rather than exploratory. There are no reflective statements or paraphrases that demonstrate understanding of the patient’s ambivalence or external barriers (e.g., childcare, job stress). The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expressed challenges or emotional state, missing opportunities to build rapport and deepen empathy.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited to factual questioning without reflective listening or validation of the patient’s experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session lacks the core MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, directive stance, repeatedly telling the patient what they “need” to do and emphasizing urgency without eliciting the patient’s own motivations or values. There is no evidence of partnership or support for autonomy; instead, the psychiatrist uses persuasion and pressure. The patient’s autonomy is undermined by the repeated commands and lack of invitation to explore their own reasons or readiness for change.

**Summary:**  
The session is predominantly authoritarian and directive, with no collaboration or evocation of the patient’s own motivations.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. The questions focus on past failures (“Why didn’t you succeed?”) and compliance (“Have you thought about using patches?”) rather than eliciting the patient’s desires, reasons, or confidence for change. There is no strategic reflection or amplification of any change talk expressed by the patient. The psychiatrist misses the opportunity to explore the patient’s expressed confidence (“My confidence sits about at a 10”) or to build on the patient’s openness to ideas.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is neither elicited nor reinforced; the psychiatrist’s approach may inadvertently suppress motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not acknowledge or soften sustain talk. The patient’s mention of stress, childcare, and job search as barriers is ignored or dismissed. The psychiatrist’s approach risks reinforcing sustain talk by focusing on failure and compliance rather than exploring ambivalence or validating the patient’s challenges. There is no use of double-sided reflections or normalization of sustain talk.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is unaddressed and potentially reinforced by the psychiatrist’s confrontational style.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation with directives and closed questions, leaving little room for the patient’s input or autonomy. The patient’s perspective is not invited or explored collaboratively. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in their own life or negotiate goals or plans.

**Summary:**  
The session is expert-driven and directive, lacking collaboration or client-centeredness.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist clearly communicates the health risks of smoking.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a more accepting, empathetic, and collaborative stance.  
- Reflective listening and validation of the patient’s experience are needed.  
- The session should evoke and reinforce change talk rather than focus on compliance and failure.  
- Sustain talk should be acknowledged and softened, not ignored or dismissed.  
- Partnership and autonomy support must replace authoritarian directives.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

- The psychiatrist’s style is overly directive, lacking acceptance, empathy, and collaboration.  
- There is no reflective listening or affirmation of the patient’s strengths or challenges.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Warm, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Express unconditional positive regard and validate the patient’s struggles and strengths.

2. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and build rapport.

3. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions that invite the patient to explore their own motivations, desires, and confidence.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalize ambivalence to reduce resistance.

5. **Support Autonomy and Partnership:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient to set goals and develop plans, respecting their expertise and readiness.

6. **Avoid Directives and Persuasion Without Permission:**  
   - Offer advice only after asking permission and exploring the patient’s perspective.

7. **Use Summaries to Consolidate Motivation:**  
   - Summarize the patient’s change talk and ambivalence to reinforce motivation and clarify next steps.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s approach is directive, judgmental, and lacks empathy, acceptance, and collaboration. The failure to evoke change talk or soften sustain talk severely limits the potential for motivating the patient toward change. To improve, the psychiatrist must fundamentally shift toward a client-centered, autonomy-supportive, and evocative style consistent with motivational interviewing best practices.
