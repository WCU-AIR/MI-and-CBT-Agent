# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental, directive, and confrontational stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“you need to quit smoking”—the tone is authoritarian and lacks warmth or respect for the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s concerns and feelings, labeling her behavior as “making excuses” and “hesitating,” which is likely to provoke resistance rather than collaboration. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or validation of the patient’s experience. The patient’s frustration and guardedness are met with impatience and pressure rather than acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate respect or unconditional positive regard. The approach is harsh, directive, and dismissive, which undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses legitimate concerns about inhaler side effects, time constraints, and ambivalence about quitting smoking, but these are ignored or invalidated. The psychiatrist does not use reflective listening or attempt to explore the patient’s feelings or fears. Instead, the responses are blunt commands and challenges (“stop making excuses,” “enough with the doubts”), which demonstrate a lack of empathy and perspective-taking.

**Summary:**  
There is little to no empathic engagement. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to acknowledge and validate the patient’s concerns, which would be critical to building rapport and facilitating motivation.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, expert-driven approach with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues ultimatums (“you must quit,” “this isn’t optional”) and demands yes/no answers, ignoring the patient’s ambivalence and resistance. There is no attempt to evoke the patient’s own motivations or to support her autonomy. The power differential is stark and unmitigated, with the psychiatrist dominating the conversation and disregarding the patient’s voice.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is entirely absent. The session is directive, controlling, and coercive rather than collaborative and autonomy-supportive.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient offers some implicit change talk (e.g., attending the session despite being busy, acknowledging worry about asthma), the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead focuses on demanding compliance. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify the patient’s expressed concerns or tentative motivations. The psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses change talk rather than cultivates it.

**Summary:**  
No effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk is present. The psychiatrist misses critical opportunities to build on the patient’s own reasons for change.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is frequent and clear in the patient’s statements (e.g., skepticism about inhalers, resistance to quitting, concerns about side effects, time constraints). The psychiatrist does not soften or strategically redirect sustain talk but instead confronts and challenges it aggressively (“stop making excuses,” “you have to commit now”). This approach likely entrenches sustain talk and resistance rather than softening it.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead reinforces resistance through confrontation and pressure.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The conversation is dominated by commands and demands, with no invitation for the patient to share her perspective or co-create a plan. The psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts or overrides the patient’s expressions of concern and frustration. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s expertise in her own life.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance that alienates the patient.

---

### **Overall Global Ratings Summary**  
This session demonstrates a complete failure to embody the core principles of motivational interviewing. The psychiatrist’s approach is directive, confrontational, and dismissive, which undermines rapport, empathy, and collaboration. The patient’s concerns and ambivalence are ignored or invalidated, and there is no skillful evocation of change talk or softening of sustain talk. The session is unlikely to motivate change and may increase resistance.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is harsh, confrontational, and dismissive, which damages rapport and motivation.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.  
- No collaborative partnership or autonomy support is provided.  
- The use of closed questions and commands dominates the session, limiting patient engagement.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from authoritarian commands to partnership. Invite the patient’s perspective and acknowledge her expertise in her own life.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s concerns and feelings, especially about inhaler side effects and life stressors.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Encourage elaboration and exploration of ambivalence rather than demanding yes/no answers.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses worry or tentative motivation, reflect and reinforce these statements to build momentum.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and validate the difficulty of change to reduce resistance.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control her behavior, avoiding ultimatums.

7. **Collaborative Planning:**  
   - Explore readiness and barriers before setting goals or making plans. Summarize key points to reinforce motivation.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Judgment:**  
   - Replace “stop making excuses” and “you have to” with supportive, nonjudgmental language.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive style alienates the patient, suppresses motivation, and fails to build rapport or evoke change talk. Immediate and substantial improvement is needed in empathy, acceptance, collaboration, and MI-consistent communication skills to avoid harm and enhance patient engagement and outcomes.
