# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental and directive from the outset. The opening line, “you need to quit smoking,” is blunt and lacks any warmth or respect for the patient’s autonomy. Throughout the session, the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses Laura’s expressed feelings and ambivalence, labeling her hesitation as “making excuses” or “procrastination.” There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or validation of Laura’s struggle. The tone is coercive rather than supportive, which likely undermines rapport and trust.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance or respect, instead adopting a confrontational and authoritarian stance that is counterproductive in MI.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand Laura’s perspective. Laura expresses ambivalence, fear of failure, and the emotional difficulty of quitting, but the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead pushes a rigid agenda. There are no reflective statements or attempts to explore Laura’s feelings or values. The psychiatrist’s responses are dismissive and do not convey understanding or validation of Laura’s experience.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is virtually absent; the psychiatrist does not demonstrate reflective listening or perspective-taking.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive approach with repeated commands (“you have to quit,” “no more delays,” “no excuses”). There is no collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly pressures Laura to act immediately without exploring her motivations or readiness. This style is antithetical to the MI spirit, which emphasizes partnership, evocation, and autonomy support.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is completely lacking; the psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive stance.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While Laura offers some change talk (e.g., valuing health, wanting to quit, acknowledging difficulty), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or amplify this language. Instead, the psychiatrist ignores or overrides Laura’s expressions of ambivalence and readiness, focusing solely on pushing immediate action. There is no strategic reinforcement or exploration of Laura’s own reasons for change.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present in the patient but is not effectively cultivated or deepened by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (Laura’s hesitations, fears, and ambivalence) is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels Laura’s hesitations as “excuses” and “procrastination,” which likely increases resistance rather than reducing sustain talk. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations of the legitimacy of ambivalence.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and escalated rather than softened or managed skillfully.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The interaction is dominated by commands and ultimatums, with no invitation for Laura’s input or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist repeatedly overrides Laura’s expressed concerns and preferences, minimizing her role in the process. This creates a clear power imbalance and undermines partnership.  

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- Minimal; the psychiatrist consistently pushes for change, which aligns with the goal but is done in a counterproductive manner.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must develop acceptance, empathy, and a collaborative spirit.  
- Reflections and validation of patient ambivalence are essential.  
- Change talk should be evoked and amplified rather than overridden.  
- Sustain talk must be softened, not confronted.  
- Partnership and autonomy support are critical to effective MI.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from directing and commanding to partnering with the patient. Invite Laura’s input and respect her autonomy.

2. **Develop Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   Use reflective listening to understand and validate Laura’s ambivalence and feelings. Avoid judgmental language.

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   Ask open-ended questions to explore Laura’s motivations and barriers. Reflect her statements to demonstrate understanding and deepen the conversation.

4. **Affirm Strengths and Efforts:**  
   Recognize Laura’s attempts to cut down and her values related to health and well-being.

5. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When Laura expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and explore these to strengthen motivation.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and validate ambivalence rather than confronting or dismissing it.

7. **Support Autonomy and Readiness:**  
   Respect Laura’s need to prepare and set her own timeline. Avoid pressuring immediate action.

8. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   Work with Laura to set achievable goals and identify supports, rather than imposing deadlines.

9. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   Periodically summarize Laura’s change talk and ambivalence to reinforce motivation and clarify next steps.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, increases resistance, and fails to engage the patient in meaningful change talk or planning. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and skillful evocation of the patient’s own motivations and strengths. Without these changes, the likelihood of successful behavior change is minimal, and the therapeutic relationship is at risk.
