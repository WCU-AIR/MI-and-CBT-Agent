# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive stance from the outset, repeatedly dismissing the patient’s perspective and expressing impatience with his resistance. Phrases such as “you need to quit smoking immediately,” “stop making excuses,” and “no more delays” convey disapproval rather than acceptance. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s feelings, ambivalence, or autonomy, instead insisting on compliance. There is no evidence of warmth, respect, or unconditional positive regard. The patient’s defensiveness and withdrawal are likely exacerbated by this approach.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian and confrontational, lacking basic respect and acceptance of the patient’s experience and readiness. This severely undermines rapport and engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not demonstrate understanding or reflective listening. The patient expresses frustration, ambivalence, and relational concerns (e.g., fear of losing his wife), but the psychiatrist ignores these emotional cues and instead repeats commands and warnings. There are no reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s feelings or perspective. The patient’s statements about routine, resistance, and relational motivation are met with dismissal rather than empathy.

**Summary:**  
There is minimal to no effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, resulting in a cold, mechanical interaction that likely increases resistance.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is highly directive and confrontational, with the psychiatrist repeatedly telling the patient what to do and insisting on immediate action. There is no collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore his motivations but instead pushes a rigid agenda. This approach contradicts the core MI principles of partnership, evocation, and autonomy support.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the session resembles a paternalistic lecture rather than a motivational interview.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not evoke or reinforce any change talk. When the patient tentatively expresses relational motivation (“I don’t want to lose my wife”) or acknowledges health concerns, the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these cues. Instead of amplifying these statements, the psychiatrist focuses on commands and warnings. There is no strategic elicitation or reinforcement of the patient’s own reasons or desires for change.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is neither elicited nor cultivated; the psychiatrist misses critical opportunities to build motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s resistance as “excuses” and “not ready nonsense,” thereby reinforcing sustain talk and resistance. There are no double-sided reflections, validations, or attempts to normalize ambivalence. The confrontational style likely entrenches sustain talk rather than softening it.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal, exacerbating resistance rather than softening it.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an authoritarian, expert-driven role, issuing commands and demands without inviting collaboration or input. The patient is repeatedly told what to do, with no negotiation or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise or autonomy, nor does he attempt to minimize the power differential.

**Summary:**  
There is no partnership; the interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and control.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must abandon the confrontational, directive style.  
- Develop skills in reflective listening and empathy.  
- Embrace MI spirit by fostering collaboration and autonomy.  
- Actively evoke and reinforce change talk.  
- Skillfully soften sustain talk through validation and double-sided reflections.  
- Build genuine partnership with the patient.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly confrontational, directive, and dismissive, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.  
- No partnership or collaboration is established; autonomy is ignored.  
- The session lacks open questions, affirmations, reflections, and summaries.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and relational motivations are ignored rather than explored.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from telling to asking. Invite the patient’s perspective and acknowledge his autonomy.

2. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and build rapport.

3. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses any motivation or concern, amplify and explore it.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and validate ambivalence rather than labeling it as “excuses.”

5. **Ask Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Encourage elaboration and exploration of feelings, values, and motivations.

6. **Affirm Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize the patient’s attempts and courage to attend despite resistance.

7. **Negotiate and Collaborate on Planning:**  
   - Work with the patient to identify small, manageable steps rather than demanding immediate quitting.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Commands:**  
   - Replace directives with invitations and permission-seeking.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and dismissive style undermines rapport, increases resistance, and fails to engage the patient’s motivation for change. To improve, the psychiatrist must develop core MI skills including empathy, acceptance, reflective listening, evocation of change talk, and partnership. Without these, the likelihood of facilitating meaningful behavior change is minimal.
