# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive, lacking warmth or genuine acceptance. The repeated commands ("You really need to quit smoking now," "You have to stop," "You have to take this seriously") convey pressure rather than unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge or validate the patient’s struggles or ambivalence, nor does he express empathy or understanding of the patient’s context. The patient’s honest disclosures about stress, ambivalence, and competing priorities are met with insistence and challenge rather than acceptance. This creates a confrontational rather than supportive atmosphere, which is counterproductive in MI.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance is largely authoritarian and directive, with minimal demonstration of respect or acceptance of the patient’s perspective. This undermines rapport and client engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is little to no evidence of empathic listening or reflective statements. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or reflect the patient’s feelings, struggles, or ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly asks the same questions and issues commands, ignoring the patient’s expressed concerns about stress, competing priorities, and readiness. The patient’s emotional state and motivations (e.g., desire to be a better father, fear of relapse) are not acknowledged or explored. This lack of empathy likely contributes to patient defensiveness and resistance.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy or reflective listening, missing critical opportunities to connect with the patient’s experience and build trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by a highly directive, confrontational style that contradicts the core MI principles of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly pressures the patient to quit immediately, dismissing the patient’s expressed ambivalence and readiness. There is no evidence of partnership or eliciting the patient’s own motivations or values. Autonomy is not supported; instead, the patient is repeatedly asked to comply with the psychiatrist’s agenda. This approach is likely to increase resistance and reduce motivation.  

**Summary:**  
The session lacks the collaborative, autonomy-supportive, and evocative spirit of MI, instead adopting an authoritarian and coercive stance.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient expresses multiple statements indicating desire, reasons, and commitment to change (e.g., wanting to be a better father, working on drinking and violence), the psychiatrist ignores these and instead repeats commands and questions about quitting smoking immediately. There is no amplification or reinforcement of the patient’s change talk, nor any strategic elicitation of commitment or confidence language. The psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses further change talk.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present in the patient’s speech but is not cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist, representing a missed opportunity to strengthen motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften or skillfully respond to sustain talk. The patient expresses sustain talk related to stress, readiness, and competing priorities, but the psychiatrist responds with confrontation and insistence rather than reflective or double-sided statements that acknowledge ambivalence. The repeated challenges ("You have to stop," "You’re making excuses") likely exacerbate sustain talk and resistance rather than soften it.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than strategic softening or redirection, undermining the MI process.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and directives, with no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s autonomy. The patient is repeatedly pressured to answer “yes” or “no” to quitting immediately, with no exploration of the patient’s perspective or negotiation of goals. ## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from directive commands to a partnership approach that respects the patient’s autonomy and perspective.

2. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   Reflect the patient’s statements and emotions to demonstrate understanding and build rapport.

3. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   Explore and amplify the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change, reinforcing commitment language.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and validate ambivalence to reduce resistance and support movement toward change.

5. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   Encourage the patient to elaborate on their thoughts and feelings rather than asking yes/no questions.

6. **Affirm Strengths and Efforts:**  
   Recognize and affirm the patient’s existing efforts and strengths to build confidence.

7. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Explicitly acknowledge the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

8. **Collaboratively Plan:**  
   Work with the patient to develop achievable goals and next steps.

9. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   Use summaries to consolidate motivation and clarify plans.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive style undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s motivation, and risks increasing resistance. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, empathic, and autonomy-supportive approach that skillfully evokes change talk and softens sustain talk. Without these changes, the effectiveness of the intervention will be severely compromised, potentially harming the therapeutic alliance and patient outcomes.
