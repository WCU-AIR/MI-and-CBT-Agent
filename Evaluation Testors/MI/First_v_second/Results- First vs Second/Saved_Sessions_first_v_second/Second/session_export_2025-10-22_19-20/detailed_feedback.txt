# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive stance from the outset. The opening statements such as “You really should stop smoking as soon as possible,” “You need to cut down immediately,” and “You really must stop making excuses and just do it” communicate disapproval and lack of respect for the patient’s autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s expressed feelings and ambivalence, labeling her coping mechanisms as “excuses” and “crutches.” There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s attempts to share her perspective are met with confrontation rather than acceptance. This is a clear violation of MI principles and scores at the lowest level.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate understanding or reflective listening. There are no reflections or attempts to paraphrase the patient’s concerns or emotional state. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with directives and accusations (“You need to stop rationalizing,” “Why haven’t you just quit cold turkey?”). The patient expresses ambivalence and emotional difficulty, but the psychiatrist ignores these cues and does not validate or explore them. The lack of empathy is stark and contributes to a disengaged, defensive patient response.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive throughout. The psychiatrist repeatedly insists on immediate cessation and cold turkey quitting, refusing to collaborate or evoke the patient’s own motivations. There is no support for autonomy; instead, the psychiatrist demands compliance (“This isn’t optional,” “Can you do that or not?”). The patient’s autonomy is overridden by pressure and confrontation. There is no partnership or evocation, only persuasion and control. This is the antithesis of MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient offers several statements indicating desire and reasons for change (“I want to cut back,” “I’m worried about my health,” “Maybe two drinks a night”), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these. Instead of amplifying or exploring these statements, the psychiatrist focuses on pushing an all-or-nothing quitting approach. There is no strategic elicitation or reinforcement of commitment language or confidence.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and judgment rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s explanations as “excuses” and “rationalizations,” which likely increases resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s approach likely entrenches sustain talk rather than softening it.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritarian stance with no evidence of collaboration or shared decision-making. The patient is repeatedly pressured to comply with the psychiatrist’s agenda. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise or lived experience. There is no negotiation of goals or plans. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

---

### **Global Ratings Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacking in MI spirit. There is no acceptance, empathy, or partnership. Change talk is ignored, and sustain talk is met with judgment. This session exemplifies poor MI practice and risks alienating the patient and undermining motivation.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (alcohol use).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly confrontational, directive, and judgmental, violating core MI principles.  
- There is no acceptance or empathy; the patient’s perspective is dismissed.  
- No reflective listening or affirmations are used.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk.  
- Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than strategic softening.  
- The patient’s autonomy is overridden repeatedly.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs.  
- The overall approach risks increasing resistance and disengagement.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Communicate unconditional positive regard and respect for the patient’s experience and ambivalence.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and build rapport.

3. **Support Autonomy and Collaboration:**  
   - Invite the patient’s perspective and preferences; avoid ultimatums or demands.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and explore these statements to deepen motivation.

5. **Softly Redirect Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance.

6. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Encourage elaboration and exploration rather than yes/no answers.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to set realistic, achievable goals and next steps.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Persuasion:**  
   - Replace directive commands with invitations and support.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental approach undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s motivation, and risks increasing resistance. Immediate and substantial improvement is needed in acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation skills to provide effective MI-based care.
