# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental, directive, and confrontational from the outset. Statements such as “you really need to quit smoking,” “stop making excuses,” and “you don’t get to say you’re ‘not ready’” communicate disapproval and lack of respect for the patient’s autonomy and current state. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s feelings and hesitations, showing no warmth or unconditional positive regard. There is no evidence of acceptance or normalization of ambivalence. The patient’s discomfort and defensiveness are clear responses to this harsh stance.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate acceptance, instead adopting a critical and authoritarian tone that likely alienates the patient.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no evidence of empathic listening or understanding. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s expressions of fear, ambivalence, and difficulty, instead pushing a rigid agenda. The patient’s statements about feeling overwhelmed, scared of failure, and needing more time are met with dismissal rather than reflection or validation. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand the patient’s perspective or emotional experience, nor do they use reflective listening to convey understanding.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a lack of empathy, failing to acknowledge or explore the patient’s feelings and concerns.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with no collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly commands the patient to “just decide and stick to it,” “stop making excuses,” and “set a quit date this week—no ifs, no buts.” This approach contradicts the core MI principles of partnership, evocation, and autonomy support. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of orders rather than an active agent in their change process.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s style is antithetical to the spirit of MI, lacking collaboration, evocation, and respect for autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient expresses desire and reasons for change (e.g., wanting to feel better, acknowledging health risks), the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead pushes with commands and criticisms. There is no strategic use of reflections or open questions to deepen or amplify the patient’s motivation. The patient’s tentative change talk is met with pressure rather than encouragement.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present in the patient but is not cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften sustain talk; instead, they confront and challenge it aggressively. The patient’s sustain talk—hesitation, fear, ambivalence—is met with accusations of “making excuses” and demands to “just do it.” There are no double-sided reflections, validations, or gentle redirections. This approach likely increases resistance rather than reducing sustain talk.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening or strategic handling.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritarian role, issuing commands and ignoring the patient’s input or perspective. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or collaborative agenda-setting. The patient is repeatedly pressured to comply rather than engaged as an equal partner.  

**Summary:**  
There is no partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and disregards the patient’s autonomy.

---

### Overall Global Ratings Summary  
**Overall Score: 1/5**  

The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally inconsistent with motivational interviewing principles. The session is directive, judgmental, and confrontational, lacking acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and skillful evocation of change talk. This style is likely to increase patient resistance and reduce motivation for change.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, judgmental, and confrontational, violating core MI principles.  
- There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to use open questions, reflections, affirmations, or summaries.  
- Change talk is ignored and sustain talk is confronted rather than softened.  
- The patient’s autonomy and readiness are disregarded, likely increasing resistance.  
- No collaborative planning or evocation of motivation occurs.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling or criticizing the patient. Express unconditional positive regard and normalize ambivalence.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy Through Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to show understanding of the patient’s fears and struggles.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient, evoke their own motivations, and support autonomy rather than directing or commanding.

4. **Use Open-Ended Questions and Complex Reflections:**  
   - Encourage the patient to explore their thoughts and feelings about smoking and quitting.

5. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize any attempts or desires to change, even if tentative.

6. **Softly Handle Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and validate ambivalence to reduce resistance.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Explore readiness and preferences before suggesting quit dates or next steps.

8. **Avoid Persuasion Without Permission:**  
   - Ask permission before offering advice or information.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session exemplifies a poor application of motivational interviewing. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s motivation, and likely increases resistance. Immediate and substantial improvement is needed in acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation skills to provide effective MI and support meaningful behavior change.
