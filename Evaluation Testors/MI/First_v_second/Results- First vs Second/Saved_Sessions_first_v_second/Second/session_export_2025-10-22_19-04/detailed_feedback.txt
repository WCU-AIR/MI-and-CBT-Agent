# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Smoking Cessation Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly adopts a judgmental, confrontational, and directive tone throughout the session. Phrases such as “you really need to quit smoking,” “stop making excuses,” and “you have to set a quit date now” communicate disapproval and pressure rather than unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge or validate the patient’s feelings, ambivalence, or autonomy. There is no evidence of warmth, respect, or support; instead, the stance is authoritarian and coercive. This approach is likely to provoke resistance rather than engagement.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate acceptance. The communication is judgmental and directive, undermining rapport and client-centeredness.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence, stress coping, and readiness concerns, but the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead pushes a rigid agenda. There are no reflective statements or attempts to paraphrase the patient’s feelings or reasons for hesitation. The psychiatrist’s responses do not convey understanding or validation of the patient’s experience, resulting in a lack of empathy.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is essentially absent. The psychiatrist does not listen reflectively or acknowledge the patient’s perspective.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is dominated by an authoritarian, directive style with repeated commands and pressure to “just pick a date” and “stop making excuses.” There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and readiness, insisting on immediate commitment. This approach contradicts the core MI principles of partnership, evocation, and autonomy support.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is not present. The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive stance that undermines client engagement and motivation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Instead of exploring the patient’s tentative statements about wanting to feel better or eventual quitting, the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these and focuses on pressing the patient to commit. There are no strategic reflections or questions designed to deepen or amplify the patient’s own motivations for change.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to evoke and reinforce the patient’s expressed desires or reasons for quitting.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly confronts and challenges sustain talk with admonishments and demands (“stop making excuses,” “you have to quit now”). There is no use of double-sided reflections, normalization, or gentle redirection. This confrontational approach likely strengthens sustain talk and resistance rather than softening it.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced rather than softened. The psychiatrist’s responses escalate resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The conversation is dominated by commands and pressure, with no invitations for the patient to share their views or co-create a plan. The psychiatrist repeatedly overrides the patient’s expressed ambivalence and readiness, demonstrating a clear power imbalance.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent. The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritarian stance.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must abandon the confrontational, directive style.  
- Develop skills in reflective listening and empathy.  
- Embrace MI spirit by supporting autonomy and collaboration.  
- Learn to evoke and reinforce change talk while softening sustain talk.  
- Build partnership by inviting patient input and respecting ambivalence.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is confrontational, directive, and judgmental, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, reflective listening, and acceptance.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and instead escalates sustain talk and resistance.  
- No partnership or autonomy support is demonstrated.  
- The session lacks collaborative planning and summarizing.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Supportive Stance:**  
   - Avoid commands, accusations, or pressure. Use language that conveys respect and acceptance.

2. **Develop Reflective Listening Skills:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and validate ambivalence.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions to Evoke Motivation:**  
   - Ask questions that invite the patient to explore their own reasons for change and concerns.

4. **Support Autonomy and Collaboration:**  
   - Emphasize that the decision to quit is the patient’s choice and invite them to co-create plans.

5. **Amplify Change Talk and Softly Redirect Sustain Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and reinforce these statements.  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.

6. **Avoid Persuasion Without Permission:**  
   - Ask permission before giving advice or information.

7. **Summarize to Consolidate Motivation and Plan:**  
   - Use summaries to highlight change talk and confirm next steps agreed upon collaboratively.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental approach is likely to increase resistance and disengagement. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a collaborative, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive style that skillfully evokes and reinforces the patient’s own motivations for change.
