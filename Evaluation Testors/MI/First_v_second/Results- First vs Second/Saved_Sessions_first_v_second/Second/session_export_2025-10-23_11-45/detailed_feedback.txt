# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Smoking Cessation Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a largely judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. The repeated commands such as “you need to quit smoking now,” “no more delays,” and “you have to get serious” convey pressure rather than acceptance. There is little to no evidence of warmth, empathy, or unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge or validate the patient’s fears, ambivalence, or struggles. Instead, the patient’s concerns are dismissed or minimized, which likely contributes to the patient’s defensive and hesitant responses. This pattern is inconsistent with acceptance and instead borders on disapproval and confrontation.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly directive and judgmental, lacking the respectful, supportive tone necessary for effective MI. This undermines rapport and client engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient repeatedly expresses fear, ambivalence, and difficulty managing asthma alongside smoking cessation, but the psychiatrist does not reflect or explore these emotions. Instead, the psychiatrist responds with repeated commands and questions that do not demonstrate perspective-taking or emotional attunement. There are no reflective statements or attempts to paraphrase the patient’s concerns, which would have conveyed understanding. The patient’s anxiety and ambivalence are met with pressure rather than empathy.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist fails to demonstrate accurate understanding or convey that understanding to the patient.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is highly authoritarian and directive, with the psychiatrist repeatedly telling the patient what she “has to” do and pressing for immediate commitment. There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore her motivations or values. Instead, the patient is positioned as someone who must comply with instructions. This approach is antithetical to the MI spirit, which emphasizes partnership, evocation, and autonomy support.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks any meaningful MI spirit; it is dominated by persuasion and directive commands.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. The patient offers several statements indicating ambivalence and tentative desire to change (“I want to, I really do,” “I’m scared,” “It’s really difficult”), but the psychiatrist does not reflect or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient with “why haven’t you quit yet?” and “have you set a quit date?” without exploring or reinforcing the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change. There is no strategic elicitation or reinforcement of commitment language.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is largely ignored or suppressed by the psychiatrist’s confrontational style.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The patient expresses sustain talk frequently, including fears about side effects, stress, and difficulty managing asthma. The psychiatrist does not soften or reframe this sustain talk but instead challenges and dismisses it (“stop worrying,” “no more delays,” “you have to”). This approach likely increases resistance rather than reducing sustain talk. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than skillful softening or redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The interaction is dominated by commands and pressure, with no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands answers and compliance without inviting the patient’s perspective or preferences. The patient is positioned as passive and pressured rather than an active partner in her care.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently maintains focus on the health risks of smoking and asthma triggers.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacking in empathy and acceptance.  
- There is no evidence of MI spirit, partnership, or evocation of change talk.  
- Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and fears are ignored or dismissed, undermining engagement and motivation.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist consistently emphasizes the importance of smoking cessation and asthma management.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks empathy.  
- There is no use of MI techniques such as reflective listening, affirmations, or evocation.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and sustain talk are met with pressure rather than understanding.  
- The psychiatrist fails to build partnership or support autonomy, which likely reduces patient engagement and motivation.  
- The session lacks collaborative planning or summarizing.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directive commands to partnership. Invite the patient’s perspective and preferences.  
   - Use language that supports autonomy (“What do you think about trying…?” “How do you feel about…?”).

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s fears and ambivalence to show understanding.  
   - Validate the difficulty of managing asthma and quitting smoking.

3. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   - Paraphrase and reflect both change talk and sustain talk to deepen understanding and reduce resistance.

4. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions that explore the patient’s own reasons and motivations for change.  
   - Amplify any statements indicating desire, ability, reasons, or need for change.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change.

6. **Avoid Confrontation and Pressure:**  
   - Replace “you have to” statements with collaborative invitations.  
   - Avoid pressing for immediate commitment; instead, explore readiness and confidence.

7. **Collaborative Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Work with the patient to set achievable goals and next steps.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and clarify plans.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational approach undermines acceptance, empathy, partnership, and evocation of change talk. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, empathetic style that respects the patient’s autonomy and ambivalence, skillfully elicits and reinforces motivation, and supports the patient in developing a manageable plan for change. Without these changes, the patient is unlikely to engage meaningfully or make sustained progress.
