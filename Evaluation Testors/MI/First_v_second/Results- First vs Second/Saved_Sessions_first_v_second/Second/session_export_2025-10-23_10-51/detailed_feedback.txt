# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental, directive, and confrontational from the outset. The opening statement, “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do to quit,” immediately establishes an authoritarian stance rather than a respectful, client-centered one. The repeated use of commands (“You really should stop,” “You need to take this seriously,” “This isn’t optional”) and accusations (“Why didn’t you stick to it?”, “Stop making excuses”) communicates disapproval and a lack of unconditional positive regard. The patient’s defensive posture and resistance clearly reflect the absence of acceptance. There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or validation of the patient’s perspective or ambivalence.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance, instead adopting a harsh, judgmental tone that alienates the patient and undermines rapport.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is virtually no demonstration of empathy or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, feelings, or ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with accusations and directives, ignoring the patient’s expressed concerns and rationalizations. The patient’s statements about social context, enjoyment, and readiness are dismissed or challenged rather than explored or reflected. The lack of any paraphrasing, summarizing, or emotional validation results in a failure to build understanding or trust.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal to no effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective, leading to a breakdown in empathic connection.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, prescriptive stance, repeatedly demanding compliance and issuing ultimatums. There is no collaboration or partnership; the patient is positioned as a problem to be fixed rather than an autonomous agent. The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change, nor does he support the patient’s autonomy or readiness. Instead, the approach is coercive and directive, which is antithetical to MI principles.  

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by a controlling, directive style that negates the core MI spirit of partnership, evocation, and autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient occasionally expresses tentative change talk (e.g., “Maybe if I got sick…”), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these statements rather than amplifying or exploring them. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on sustain talk and resistance, responding with confrontation rather than strategic reflections or open questions to deepen motivation. There is no attempt to elicit commitment language or confidence statements.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but neglected and not cultivated; the psychiatrist’s responses undermine rather than support emerging motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly confronts and challenges sustain talk with accusations and demands, which tends to reinforce resistance rather than soften it. There are no double-sided reflections, no normalization of ambivalence, and no strategic redirection away from sustain talk. The patient’s rationalizations and social context are dismissed as “excuses,” which likely increases defensiveness.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and pressure rather than skillful softening or strategic handling.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The session is characterized by a power imbalance, with the psychiatrist assuming an expert, authoritarian role and the patient positioned as a passive recipient of orders. There is no negotiation of agenda, no invitation for the patient’s input or perspective, and no shared decision-making. The repeated ultimatums and demands (“This isn’t optional,” “Are you going to do it or not?”) reinforce a hierarchical dynamic.  

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership or shared power; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and excludes the patient’s voice.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must abandon the authoritarian, confrontational style.  
- Develop skills in acceptance, empathy, and reflective listening.  
- Embrace the MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.  
- Learn to skillfully evoke and reinforce change talk while softening sustain talk.  
- Build a genuine partnership with the patient, respecting their autonomy and readiness.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (drinking).  

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly directive, confrontational, and judgmental, which alienates the patient and undermines motivation.  
- There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and reflective listening.  
- The MI spirit is absent; the psychiatrist does not collaborate, evoke, or support autonomy.  
- Change talk is ignored and sustain talk is confronted rather than softened.  
- No affirmations, open questions, or summaries are used.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and social context are dismissed rather than explored.  
- The psychiatrist issues ultimatums and demands immediate commitment, which is counterproductive.  

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from telling and directing to partnering with the patient.  
   - Use inclusive language and invite the patient’s perspective.

2. **Develop Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Practice reflective listening to understand and validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.  
   - Avoid judgmental or confrontational language.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Encourage the patient to elaborate on their experiences, values, and motivations.  
   - Avoid yes/no or leading questions.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses any desire or reasons for change, reflect and explore these statements.  
   - Reinforce confidence and commitment language.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change.

6. **Respect Autonomy:**  
   - Avoid ultimatums or demands.  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

7. **Collaborative Planning:**  
   - When the patient is ready, co-create achievable goals and next steps.  
   - Summarize progress and reinforce motivation.

8. **Training and Supervision:**  
   - Engage in formal MI training and supervision to develop skills in MI-consistent behaviors.  
   - Review recorded sessions to identify and correct directive or confrontational tendencies.

Immediate and substantial improvement is required in acceptance, empathy, collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support to meet even basic standards of motivational interviewing practice.
