# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance throughout the session is largely judgmental and directive rather than accepting. The opening statements and subsequent responses repeatedly convey disapproval and pressure (“You really should quit completely,” “You have to stop now,” “You really need to stop making excuses”). There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or unconditional positive regard. The patient’s expressions of difficulty and ambivalence are met with admonishments rather than understanding. This creates a coercive atmosphere rather than a supportive one. The psychiatrist fails to normalize the patient’s struggle or acknowledge the complexity of quitting smoking, which would be expected even at a minimal level of acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive, lacking the fundamental acceptance necessary for effective MI.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence, peer influence, fear, and difficulty, but the psychiatrist responds with challenges and demands rather than reflective listening or validation. There are no reflective statements that capture the patient’s feelings or struggles. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the emotional complexity or the social context influencing the patient’s behavior. This lack of empathy likely contributes to patient resistance and disengagement.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to reflect and validate the patient’s experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with the psychiatrist repeatedly telling the patient what to do and emphasizing urgency and compliance. There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s perspective or explore motivations but instead insists on immediate action and compliance. This approach is antithetical to the MI spirit, which prioritizes partnership and client autonomy.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks any MI spirit; it is coercive and directive rather than collaborative and autonomy-supportive.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist attempts to elicit commitment by repeatedly asking if the patient is “ready” or “going to start” quitting, but these are framed as demands rather than invitations. The patient offers some change talk (“I want to quit,” “It’s bad”), but the psychiatrist does not reinforce, reflect, or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist challenges sustain talk and ambivalence harshly, which likely suppresses further change talk. There is no strategic evocation or reinforcement of the patient’s own motivations.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and not skillfully cultivated; the approach likely inhibits deeper exploration of motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly confronts and challenges sustain talk and ambivalence with admonishments and demands (“stop making excuses,” “you have to be stronger,” “no middle ground”). There is no use of reflective listening, double-sided reflections, or normalization to soften sustain talk. This confrontational style likely increases resistance and defensiveness rather than helping the patient resolve ambivalence.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening or strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritative stance throughout. The patient is repeatedly told what to do, with no invitation to collaborate or share power. There is no evidence of co-creating goals or plans, nor of acknowledging the patient’s expertise or autonomy. The language is directive and controlling, which undermines partnership.

**Summary:**  
No meaningful partnership is established; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently emphasizes the importance of quitting smoking.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is coercive, judgmental, and directive.  
- There is a near-complete absence of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is missing.  
- Change talk is not cultivated or reinforced; sustain talk is confronted harshly.  
- Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist controls the agenda and decisions.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is coercive, judgmental, and directive, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a critical lack of acceptance, empathy, and reflective listening.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is absent.  
- Change talk is not cultivated or reinforced; sustain talk is confronted harshly.  
- No affirmations, summaries, or collaborative planning are present.  
- The psychiatrist fails to engage the patient effectively, likely increasing resistance.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling or blaming language.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of quitting and acknowledge the patient’s struggle.

2. **Increase Empathy and Reflective Listening:**  
   - Use reflections to validate feelings and ambivalence.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to deepen understanding.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy by emphasizing choice and control.

4. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons to quit, reflect and explore these statements.  
   - Avoid confronting sustain talk; instead, use double-sided reflections and softening strategies.

5. **Use Affirmations and Summaries:**  
   - Recognize patient strengths, efforts, and intentions.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and clarify next steps.

6. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Explore feasible steps with the patient rather than imposing directives.  
   - Check for readiness and confidence before moving forward.

7. **Avoid Confrontation and Coercion:**  
   - Replace “you have to” and “you must” with open-ended questions and invitations.  
   - Respect the patient’s pace and ambivalence as part of the change process.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s approach is coercive, judgmental, and directive, lacking acceptance, empathy, and collaboration. The patient’s ambivalence and sustain talk are met with confrontation rather than skillful softening or evocation. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a client-centered, autonomy-supportive stance, deepen reflective listening, and skillfully cultivate change talk. Without these changes, the session risks alienating the patient and undermining motivation for change.
