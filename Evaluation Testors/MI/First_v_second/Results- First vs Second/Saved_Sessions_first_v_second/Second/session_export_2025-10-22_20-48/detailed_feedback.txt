# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session with Jason Miller

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental and confrontational stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“We need to talk about your smoking habit because it’s really important you quit as soon as possible”—the tone is directive and accusatory rather than respectful or supportive. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s perspective, labels his behavior as “excuses,” and demands compliance (“You have to stop,” “You must cut this out now”). There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s feelings, values, or ambivalence, instead repeatedly pushing a rigid agenda. This approach likely alienates the patient and undermines rapport.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian and lacks respect or empathy, resulting in a judgmental and unsupportive atmosphere.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or validate the patient’s perspective, feelings, or social context. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with directives and challenges the patient’s explanations without exploring underlying motivations or concerns. The patient’s ambivalence and social pressures are ignored rather than explored. The psychiatrist’s responses do not reflect or paraphrase the patient’s statements, nor do they acknowledge the patient’s struggle or perspective. This lack of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s defensiveness and resistance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate understanding or convey empathy, missing critical opportunities to connect with the patient’s experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly commands the patient to “stop making excuses,” “take control,” and “avoid those risky situations,” without inviting the patient’s input or exploring his own motivations. There is no partnership or shared decision-making; the psychiatrist imposes an agenda and expects compliance. The patient’s autonomy is neither acknowledged nor supported. The spirit of MI—collaborative, evocative, and autonomy-supportive—is entirely absent.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive stance that contradicts the fundamental principles of motivational interviewing.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. When the patient tentatively expresses any ambivalence or mild change talk (e.g., “Maybe I should be more careful,” “I guess I could think about it”), the psychiatrist ignores or overrides these cues with repeated commands and accusations. There are no strategic reflections or questions designed to elicit the patient’s own reasons, desires, or confidence for change. The psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses rather than cultivates change talk.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is neither elicited nor reinforced; the psychiatrist’s style likely inhibits the patient’s motivation to change.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently confronts and challenges sustain talk rather than softening or exploring it. The patient’s rationalizations, social pressures, and ambivalence are met with accusations of “making excuses” and demands to “stop overcomplicating” or “take responsibility.” There are no double-sided reflections, no normalization of ambivalence, and no attempts to gently guide the patient toward change. This confrontational approach likely entrenches sustain talk and resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than skillful softening or strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation with directives and rhetorical questions that imply blame (“Why didn’t you just skip the party?”). The patient’s input is dismissed or ignored. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient to share his perspective or co-create a plan. The power differential is stark and unmitigated, with the psychiatrist assuming an expert, authoritarian role.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks any meaningful partnership; the psychiatrist exerts control rather than working as an equal with the patient.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance to build rapport.  
- Empathy and reflective listening are essential to understand and validate the patient’s perspective.  
- The spirit of MI requires collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support, all absent here.  
- Change talk must be actively elicited and reinforced rather than overridden.  
- Sustain talk should be skillfully softened, not confronted.  
- Partnership and shared decision-making must replace authoritarian directives.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the presenting issue.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian, confrontational, and directive, which is counterproductive in motivational interviewing.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and instead confronts sustain talk aggressively.  
- The patient’s autonomy and perspective are ignored, damaging rapport and engagement.  
- The session lacks collaboration, affirmations, planning, and summarizing.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Respectful Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling or blaming language.  
   - Express genuine interest in the patient’s perspective.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy by emphasizing choice and control.

4. **Elicit and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions that invite the patient to explore reasons for change.  
   - Reflect and reinforce any statements favoring change.

5. **Softly Handle Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change without confrontation.

6. **Build Partnership:**  
   - Invite the patient to co-create goals and plans.  
   - Share power and avoid an expert-driven approach.

7. **Avoid Repetitive Commands and Closed Questions:**  
   - Use open questions to explore the patient’s experience.  
   - Avoid yes/no questions that imply blame.

8. **Incorporate Affirmations and Summaries:**  
   - Recognize patient strengths and efforts.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and clarify next steps.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient, and likely diminishes motivation for change. To improve, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a collaborative, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive approach that skillfully evokes and reinforces the patient’s own motivations while gently navigating ambivalence. Without these changes, the effectiveness of future sessions will be severely compromised.
