# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the tone is confrontational and lacks warmth or empathy (“you need to quit smoking,” “you just have to stop making excuses”). The patient’s expressions of ambivalence and hesitation are met with impatience and pressure rather than understanding or validation. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or normalization of the patient’s struggle. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s concerns and feelings, which likely undermines rapport and trust.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely disapproving and directive, with minimal acceptance of the patient’s perspective or emotional state. This severely limits the patient’s comfort and openness.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows limited effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses stress, uncertainty, and a need for support and information, but the psychiatrist responds with blunt commands and dismissive remarks (“you’re overcomplicating this,” “stress isn’t an excuse”). There are no reflective statements or attempts to explore the patient’s feelings or barriers in a nuanced way. The patient’s subtle cues of frustration and hesitation are ignored rather than acknowledged.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is minimal, with a focus on persuasion rather than understanding. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to validate the patient’s experience or explore ambivalence.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, lacking collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly insists on immediate quitting and commitment without inviting the patient’s input or exploring her motivations. The patient is told what to do, when to do it, and how to do it, with no room for negotiation or partnership. The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s own reasons for change or support her autonomy; instead, the patient is pressured and controlled.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent. The interaction is dominated by control and persuasion, which is antithetical to motivational interviewing principles.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient does express some tentative change talk (e.g., willingness to start patches if supported), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or amplify this language. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on commands and urgency, which may provoke resistance rather than deepen motivation. There is no strategic reinforcement or exploration of the patient’s own reasons or confidence for change. The psychiatrist misses chances to elicit commitment language in a supportive way.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but weakly cultivated. The psychiatrist’s approach likely stifles rather than encourages the patient’s motivation.

**Summary:**

### 6. Partnership

The psychiatrist does not negotiate goals, explore patient preferences, or co-create a plan. The patient’s requests for information and support are met with commands rather than partnership.  

**Summary:**  
Partnership is essentially absent. The interaction is hierarchical and directive, undermining patient engagement.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directive commands to partnership. Invite the patient’s input and preferences.  
   - Use inclusive language and avoid ultimatums.

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings and ambivalence. Validate the difficulty of quitting and the patient’s concerns.  
   - Normalize ambivalence and struggle.

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions to explore motivations, barriers, and feelings.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen understanding and demonstrate empathy.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses willingness or reasons for change, reflect and reinforce these statements.  
   - Explore confidence and readiness gently.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Avoid labeling patient concerns as “excuses.”

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize that the patient has control over the timing and manner of quitting.  
   - Avoid pressuring the patient to commit before readiness.

7. **Enhance Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Collaboratively develop a quit plan that incorporates patient preferences and readiness.  
   - Summarize key points and change talk to reinforce motivation.

8. **Provide Information with Permission:**  
   - Ask permission before giving advice or prescribing aids.  
   - Tailor information to the patient’s expressed needs and questions.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamentally flawed approach to motivational interviewing. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and autonomy support. Change talk is not effectively evoked or reinforced, and sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening. The lack of reflective listening, affirmations, and open questions severely limits patient engagement and motivation. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt the core MI spirit of partnership, empathy, and evocation, and develop skills in reflective listening and strategic handling of ambivalence. Without these changes, the risk of patient resistance and disengagement is high, potentially compromising treatment outcomes.
