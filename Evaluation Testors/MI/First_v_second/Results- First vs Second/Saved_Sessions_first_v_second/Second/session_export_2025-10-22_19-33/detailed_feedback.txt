# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist uses language that is critical and blaming, such as “You really should stop,” “You need to quit now,” and “No excuses.” The patient’s expressions of struggle and discouragement are met with dismissive and confrontational responses (“stop making excuses,” “you have to be tougher”), which clearly communicate disapproval rather than unconditional positive regard. There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or validation of the patient’s experience. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s feelings and circumstances, which is antithetical to acceptance in MI.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance, instead adopting a harsh, judgmental tone that likely alienates the patient and undermines rapport.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient repeatedly expresses feelings of frustration, discouragement, and ambivalence, yet the psychiatrist ignores these emotional cues and instead focuses on lecturing and pushing for immediate commitment. There are no reflective statements or attempts to paraphrase the patient’s concerns or emotional state. The psychiatrist’s responses lack any acknowledgment of the patient’s struggle or the complexity of his situation, demonstrating a failure to convey empathy.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is essentially absent; the psychiatrist does not demonstrate understanding or validation of the patient’s feelings or perspective.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with the psychiatrist repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding immediate commitment. There is no collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist uses confrontational language (“stop making excuses,” “you have to be tougher”) and issues ultimatums (“Are you going to quit today or not?”), which are contrary to the MI spirit. The patient’s autonomy is ignored, and the psychiatrist does not invite the patient to explore his own motivations or values. The interaction is coercive rather than collaborative.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is completely absent; the session is dominated by control, persuasion, and lack of partnership.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient offers some change talk (e.g., “I want to stop,” “I wish something would go right”), the psychiatrist ignores these statements and instead focuses on pushing for commitment without exploring or amplifying the patient’s motivations. There are no reflective or open-ended questions designed to deepen change talk or explore ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses change talk rather than cultivates it.

**Summary:**  
No effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk; missed opportunities to build motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly confronts and challenges sustain talk with judgmental and critical language (“stop making excuses,” “you have to be tougher”), which likely increases resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s ambivalence or struggles. Sustain talk is met with direct opposition rather than gentle redirection or normalization, which is counterproductive in MI.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened or skillfully managed.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The interaction is dominated by commands, ultimatums, and a lack of respect for the patient’s autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or share power in decision-making. The patient is positioned as someone who must comply rather than as an active partner in change.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership; the session is authoritarian and directive.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is counterproductive, characterized by judgment, confrontation, and lack of empathy.  
- The session lacks core MI elements: acceptance, empathy, collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.  
- The psychiatrist fails to engage the patient in meaningful dialogue or to explore ambivalence constructively.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental Stance:**  
   Replace confrontational language with respectful, supportive communication that validates the patient’s experience.

2. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and build rapport.

3. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   Use open-ended questions and reflections to draw out the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Employ double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance and ambivalence.

5. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

6. **Collaborate and Partner:**  
   Invite the patient to co-create goals and plans rather than issuing ultimatums.

7. **Avoid Directives and Ultimatums:**  
   Replace “you have to” and “no excuses” with invitations and explorations.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, judgmental, and confrontational style is likely to alienate the patient, increase resistance, and undermine motivation for change. Immediate and substantial improvement is required to align with MI best practices, including adopting empathy, acceptance, collaboration, and skillful evocation of change talk. Without these changes, the psychiatrist risks causing harm and failing to engage patients effectively.
