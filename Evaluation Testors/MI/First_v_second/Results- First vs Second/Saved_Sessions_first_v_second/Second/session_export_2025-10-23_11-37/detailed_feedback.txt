# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the very first utterance, the psychiatrist states, “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” which is authoritarian and dismissive of the patient’s autonomy. The repeated commands such as “You have to stop making excuses,” “You really need to stop hesitating,” and “Just get it done” communicate disapproval and lack of respect for the patient’s perspective. There is no evidence of warmth, positive regard, or validation of the patient’s feelings or concerns. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s expressed motivations and barriers, instead repeatedly pushing a rigid agenda. This is the opposite of unconditional positive regard and acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is highly judgmental and directive, lacking any genuine acceptance or respect for the patient’s autonomy or perspective.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy or reflective listening. The patient clearly expresses her motivations, barriers, and confidence, yet the psychiatrist ignores these and responds with confrontational and dismissive language. There are no reflections, paraphrases, or attempts to understand the patient’s experience. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s concerns about contracts, sales pressure, or the need for a flexible gym environment. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates these concerns by labeling them as “excuses” and pushing for immediate action without exploration. This shows minimal effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist does not demonstrate empathy or understanding, instead dismissing the patient’s concerns and motivations.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding commitment. There is no collaboration or partnership; the psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s own reasons for change but rather imposes a rigid agenda. Autonomy is undermined by commands and pressure, with statements like “You have to stop making excuses” and “Just get it done.” The psychiatrist does not support or respect the patient’s right to choose or pace the change process.

**Summary:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive approach that contradicts the core MI principles of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the psychiatrist repeatedly asks if the patient will sign up this week, this is done in a confrontational manner rather than skillfully evoking change talk. The patient spontaneously offers multiple statements of change talk (e.g., “I want to feel stronger and more energetic,” “I’m confident I can do this,” “I’m committed to visiting the YMCA this week”), but the psychiatrist does not reinforce, reflect, or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses the patient’s elaborations and focuses on pushing for immediate action. The repeated closed questions (“Are you going to do it or not?”) pressure the patient but do not deepen motivation or explore ambivalence.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present from the patient but is not effectively cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s legitimate concerns about contracts, sales pressure, and readiness as “excuses” and “hesitation,” thereby reinforcing sustain talk rather than softening it. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s ambivalence. The psychiatrist does not normalize the difficulty of change or acknowledge the patient’s thoughtful approach. Instead, the confrontational style likely increases resistance and sustain talk.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead reinforces resistance through judgmental and confrontational language.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist repeatedly tells the patient what to do, uses directive and commanding language, and does not invite the patient’s input or preferences. The patient’s expertise about her own needs and barriers is ignored. The psychiatrist’s stance is expert-driven and authoritarian, creating a clear power imbalance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership and instead dominates the interaction with directive commands.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from an authoritarian to a partnership approach. Invite the patient’s input and respect her expertise about her own needs.

2. **Demonstrate Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and concerns. Avoid labeling concerns as “excuses.”

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   Encourage elaboration and exploration of motivations and barriers rather than yes/no answers.

4. **Affirm Strengths and Motivations:**  
   Recognize and reinforce the patient’s expressed confidence and commitment.

5. **Evoke Change Talk Skillfully:**  
   Reflect and amplify the patient’s change talk to deepen motivation.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalize ambivalence to reduce resistance.

7. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

8. **Collaborate on Planning:**  
   Co-create a realistic, flexible plan that fits the patient’s preferences and lifestyle.

9. **Avoid Confrontation and Commands:**  
   Replace directive language with invitational and supportive statements.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, engagement, and motivation. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically change approach to one grounded in acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and skillful evocation of the patient’s own motivations and strengths. Without these changes, the risk of patient resistance and disengagement is high, potentially causing harm rather than facilitating change.
