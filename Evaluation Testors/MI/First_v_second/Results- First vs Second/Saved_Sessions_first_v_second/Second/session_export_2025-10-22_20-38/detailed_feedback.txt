# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive rather than accepting. The repeated commands such as “you really need to quit smoking now,” “don’t you agree you should just stop now?” and “stop making excuses” convey a lack of unconditional positive regard. The tone is confrontational and lacks warmth or empathy. There is no evidence of normalization or validation of the patient’s struggle. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s feelings or the complexity of quitting smoking, instead focusing on compliance and urgency. This approach likely contributes to patient defensiveness and resistance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates inconsistent respect and a largely judgmental stance, which undermines rapport and client engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no evidence of empathic listening or understanding. The psychiatrist does not reflect or validate the patient’s emotional experience or perspective. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with directives and challenges the patient’s statements, e.g., “You have to stop making excuses about stress,” “You have to be honest—do you want to quit or not?” This approach dismisses the patient’s expressed frustration and stress, which are central to his ambivalence. The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate perspective-taking or to convey understanding of the patient’s lived experience.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows little effort to understand or convey empathy, resulting in a dismissive and confrontational interaction.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly issuing commands and ultimatums (“You have to stop,” “Just start the patches”). There is no evidence of evoking the patient’s own motivations or exploring ambivalence. Autonomy is undermined by the persistent pressure and lack of invitation for the patient’s input beyond yes/no answers. The psychiatrist’s style is directive and coercive rather than collaborative and supportive.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks the fundamental MI spirit, instead reflecting a controlling and confrontational approach.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. The patient expresses some desire and reasons for quitting (“I want to,” “I know smoking’s bad”), but the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead challenges or dismisses them. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify the patient’s motivation. The psychiatrist’s repeated demands and accusations (“stop making excuses,” “why haven’t you started?”) likely suppress change talk rather than cultivate it.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated; the psychiatrist’s approach likely inhibits the patient’s motivation to change.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s reasons for continuing smoking, stress, frustration) is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s explanations as “excuses” and insists on “pushing through” stress without acknowledging its validity. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. This approach likely escalates resistance and sustain talk.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and escalated rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and directives, with no genuine invitation for the patient’s perspective or shared decision-making. The patient is repeatedly pressured to comply rather than engaged as an equal partner. The psychiatrist’s language is controlling and lacks inclusivity.

**Summary:**  
There is no evidence of partnership; the psychiatrist maintains an authoritarian stance.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks empathy.  
- There is no evidence of MI spirit, acceptance, or partnership.  
- Change talk is neither evoked nor reinforced; sustain talk is met with resistance.  
- The patient’s emotional experience and ambivalence are ignored or dismissed.  

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly confrontational, directive, and lacks empathy.  
- There is no evidence of MI spirit: no collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support.  
- The psychiatrist fails to use open questions, reflections, or affirmations.  
- Change talk is not cultivated; sustain talk is escalated.  
- The patient’s emotional experience and ambivalence are ignored or dismissed.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs.  
- The interaction likely increases patient resistance and disengagement.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt an Accepting, Nonjudgmental Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses.”  
   - Validate the difficulty of quitting and the patient’s emotional experience.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy Through Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to show understanding.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen rapport.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy by emphasizing choice and control.

4. **Use Open Questions to Explore Ambivalence:**  
   - Replace closed, leading questions with open-ended ones that invite elaboration.

5. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Reflect and affirm statements favoring change.  
   - Strategically evoke commitment and confidence language.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Normalize sustain talk and gently guide toward change.

7. **Engage in Collaborative Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Co-create a realistic plan with the patient.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and next steps.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Pressure:**  
   - Replace commands and ultimatums with invitations and support.  
   - Recognize that pressure often increases resistance.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive style undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s motivation, and likely increases resistance. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, empathic, and autonomy-supportive approach that skillfully evokes and reinforces the patient’s own reasons for change. Without these changes, the session risks alienating the patient and reducing the likelihood of successful behavior change.
