# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental, directive, and authoritarian stance throughout the session. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s concerns and emotional experience are dismissed or minimized repeatedly (e.g., “You really need to get serious,” “You must be disciplined,” “Stop making excuses”). The psychiatrist labels the patient’s behavior as “laziness” and “slacking off,” which is stigmatizing and likely to alienate the patient. There is no attempt to normalize the patient’s ambivalence or struggles, and the tone is harsh and confrontational. This approach is antithetical to acceptance in motivational interviewing.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate respect or support, instead adopting a critical and blaming tone that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no evidence of empathic understanding or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge or validate the patient’s fears, ambivalence, or social concerns. The patient expresses vulnerability about fear of stigma and difficulty managing diabetes, but the psychiatrist ignores these emotional cues and instead responds with commands and accusations. The psychiatrist does not attempt to paraphrase or reflect the patient’s perspective, nor do they explore the emotional meaning behind the patient’s statements. This lack of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s guarded and resistant responses.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows little effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, missing critical opportunities to build rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely directive, authoritarian, and persuasive without collaboration or evocation. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You have to,” “No more excuses,” “Just do it”) and pressures the patient to commit immediately. There is no support for autonomy or partnership; the patient is positioned as someone who must comply rather than as an active agent in their care. The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s own motivations or values, nor do they explore ambivalence or readiness to change. This approach is the opposite of the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support, instead adopting a controlling and confrontational style.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient offers tentative change talk (e.g., “I want to try taking my meds more regularly,” “I want to get better at it”), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these statements and instead focuses on compliance and discipline. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify the patient’s expressed desire or willingness to improve. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to explore the patient’s motivations or to build confidence and commitment.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present in the patient’s speech but is not cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften sustain talk or ambivalence. Instead, sustain talk (e.g., fear of stigma, difficulty managing diabetes, reluctance to disclose to friends) is met with confrontation and blame. The psychiatrist labels forgetting medication as “laziness” and dismisses the patient’s emotional concerns as “excuses.” There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s experience while gently guiding toward change. This approach likely entrenches resistance rather than softening sustain talk.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with harsh judgment rather than skillful redirection or validation.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is one-sided, with the psychiatrist controlling the agenda and issuing directives. The patient is not invited to share their perspective or co-create a plan. There is no negotiation or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist’s language (“You have to,” “No more trying”) reinforces a power imbalance and undermines the patient’s autonomy.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks partnership and client-centeredness, instead reflecting an authoritarian, expert-driven approach.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the clinical issue of diabetes management.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, judgmental, and confrontational, which is counterproductive in motivational interviewing.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.  
- The session lacks collaboration, partnership, and support for autonomy.  
- The psychiatrist misses critical opportunities to explore the patient’s fears and ambivalence, which are central to effective MI.  
- The use of stigmatizing language (“laziness,” “no excuses”) is harmful and undermines therapeutic alliance.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Supportive Stance:**  
   Replace blaming and confrontational language with acceptance and positive regard. Avoid labeling or shaming the patient.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   Reflect the patient’s feelings and concerns to demonstrate understanding and build trust. Validate ambivalence and fears.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   Collaborate with the patient, evoke their own motivations, and support their autonomy. Avoid directing or pressuring.

4. **Cultivate Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses desire or willingness to improve, reflect and amplify these statements to strengthen motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and validate the patient’s struggles while gently guiding toward change.

6. **Engage in Collaborative Planning:**  
   Work with the patient to set realistic, achievable goals and develop a plan that respects their readiness and context.

7. **Avoid Stigmatizing Language:**  
   Use neutral, respectful language that encourages openness rather than defensiveness.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, judgmental, and directive approach is likely to increase patient resistance and reduce engagement. To improve, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a collaborative, empathic, and autonomy-supportive style that skillfully evokes and reinforces the patient’s own motivations for change. Without these changes, the effectiveness of the intervention will be severely compromised, potentially harming the therapeutic relationship and patient outcomes.
