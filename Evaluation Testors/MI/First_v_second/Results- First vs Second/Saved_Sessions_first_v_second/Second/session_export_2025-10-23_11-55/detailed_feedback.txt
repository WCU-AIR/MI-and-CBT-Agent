# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive rather than accepting. From the outset, the psychiatrist uses language that is confrontational and accusatory (“You really need to quit as soon as possible,” “You really need to stop making excuses,” “No ifs, ands, or buts”). This tone conveys disapproval and pressure rather than unconditional positive regard. The patient’s responses show defensiveness and frustration, indicating the psychiatrist’s stance is not experienced as supportive or respectful. There is no evidence of normalization or validation of the patient’s ambivalence or current struggles. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s perspective or readiness, which is critical for acceptance.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance is largely directive and judgmental, lacking warmth or genuine acceptance. This undermines rapport and patient engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The responses are repetitive commands and questions that dismiss the patient’s expressed concerns and ambivalence. There are no reflective statements or paraphrases that convey understanding of the patient’s feelings about readiness, energy levels, or the desire to take manageable steps. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s emotional state or validate their cautious approach. The patient’s frustration and defensiveness suggest the psychiatrist’s lack of empathy is palpable.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is essentially absent. The psychiatrist fails to listen reflectively or convey understanding, which damages the therapeutic alliance.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is not present. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and insisting on immediate quitting without exploring the patient’s motivations or values. There is no evocation of the patient’s own reasons for change or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s expressed readiness and priorities, instead pushing a rigid agenda. This approach is antithetical to MI principles.  

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by directive persuasion and lacks collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient expresses clear change talk (desire to improve health, readiness to start with physical activity), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these statements. Instead of amplifying the patient’s motivation or exploring their reasons, the psychiatrist repeatedly insists on immediate quitting and use of nicotine replacement without eliciting the patient’s own ideas or commitment. There is no strategic reinforcement or deepening of change talk.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present in the patient but is not cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften sustain talk; rather, they confront and challenge it aggressively. The patient’s sustain talk—hesitancy, concerns about readiness, prioritizing physical activity—is met with accusations of “making excuses” and “stalling.” There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s approach likely increases resistance rather than reducing sustain talk.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal, exacerbating resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands and directives, with no invitation for the patient to share their perspective or co-create a plan. The psychiatrist repeatedly overrides the patient’s expressed priorities and readiness, demonstrating a clear power imbalance. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s expertise in their own life.  

**Summary:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, lacking partnership or shared power.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None apparent in the psychiatrist’s approach.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.  
- Reflective listening and empathy are essential to understand and validate the patient’s perspective.  
- Collaboration and autonomy support must replace directive commands.  
- Change talk should be evoked and reinforced rather than ignored or dismissed.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Use language that conveys respect and unconditional positive regard.  
   - Avoid labeling or accusing the patient of making excuses.

2. **Enhance Empathy Through Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings, concerns, and ambivalence to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen emotional connection.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and values for change.  
   - Support autonomy by acknowledging the patient’s right to choose timing and steps.

4. **Skillfully Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and explore these statements.  
   - Avoid dismissing or ignoring change talk.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Validate the difficulty of change and normalize hesitation.

6. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions to invite elaboration.  
   - Affirm the patient’s strengths, efforts, and intentions.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to identify manageable next steps.  
   - Summarize key points and confirm commitment.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Persuasion:**  
   - Replace commands with invitations and permission-seeking.  
   - Recognize that pushing too hard can increase resistance.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive style alienates the patient, undermines engagement, and fails to evoke or support motivation for change. To improve, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a collaborative, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive approach that respects the patient’s readiness and priorities. Without these changes, the effectiveness of motivational interviewing will be severely compromised, potentially harming patient outcomes.
