# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental, directive, and lacking in warmth or positive regard. From the outset, the psychiatrist states, “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” and repeatedly uses commanding language such as “You really should stop,” “You have to,” and “Just do it.” This communicates a lack of unconditional positive regard and fails to create a safe, supportive environment. The patient’s minimal and guarded responses reflect discomfort and disengagement, likely due to this harsh stance. There is no evidence of normalization, validation, or acceptance of the patient’s perspective or ambivalence.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance is authoritarian and directive, undermining acceptance and rapport. This is a critical failure in MI practice.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not demonstrate reflective listening or attempt to understand the patient’s perspective. Instead, the psychiatrist interrupts with rapid-fire questions and statements that dismiss the patient’s experience. There are no reflections, paraphrases, or empathic statements that acknowledge the patient’s feelings, concerns, or ambivalence. The patient’s repeated polite but dismissive responses indicate that their perspective is not being heard or understood.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is essentially absent. The psychiatrist fails to convey understanding or validate the patient’s experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely directive and persuasive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You must have the willpower,” “No ifs or buts,” “Just do it”) and ignores the patient’s expressed ambivalence and resistance. The patient is not invited to share their motivations or concerns, nor is their autonomy respected. This authoritarian style is the antithesis of MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The MI spirit is completely absent. The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, expert-driven approach.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not evoke or reinforce any change talk. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on telling the patient what to do and pressing for compliance. The patient’s minimal change talk is not acknowledged or amplified. There are no open-ended questions designed to explore the patient’s own reasons for change or confidence. The approach likely suppresses any emerging motivation.

**Summary:**  
No effort to cultivate change talk; the approach likely stifles motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly confronts and challenges the patient’s sustain talk with admonitions and commands. There is no use of reflective listening, double-sided reflections, or normalization to soften sustain talk. Instead, the psychiatrist’s responses escalate resistance and defensiveness.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than skillful softening or redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The patient is treated as a passive recipient of instructions rather than an active participant. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation, issues ultimatums, and ignores the patient’s perspective. There is no shared decision-making or negotiation.

**Summary:**  
No partnership; the interaction is authoritarian and directive.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- Develop a respectful, nonjudgmental stance that conveys acceptance.  
- Practice reflective listening to demonstrate empathy and understanding.  
- Adopt the MI spirit by collaborating, evoking patient motivations, and supporting autonomy.  
- Use open-ended questions to elicit and reinforce change talk.  
- Skillfully soften sustain talk rather than confront it.  
- Build a genuine partnership with the patient, sharing power and decision-making.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the smoking cessation topic.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks MI principles.  
- There is no acceptance, empathy, or partnership.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and sustain talk are met with judgment and commands, increasing resistance.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk.  
- The session lacks open questions, reflections, affirmations, and autonomy support.  
- The imposed plan is unlikely to be effective given the lack of collaboration.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental Stance:**  
   Begin sessions with genuine acceptance and positive regard. Avoid commands and judgments.

2. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   Reflect patient statements to demonstrate understanding and encourage elaboration.

3. **Ask Open-Ended Questions:**  
   Invite the patient to explore their own motivations, concerns, and ambivalence.

4. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Emphasize that change is the patient’s choice and collaborate on goal-setting.

5. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   Use strategic questions and reflections to draw out the patient’s own reasons and desire for change.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance.

7. **Collaborate on Planning:**  
   Develop plans jointly, ensuring patient buy-in and readiness.

8. **Avoid Persuasive or Authoritarian Language:**  
   Replace ultimatums with invitations and partnership language.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, directive style alienates the patient, suppresses motivation, and fails to build rapport or partnership. Without immediate and substantial changes to approach, this style risks disengagement and poor clinical outcomes. The psychiatrist must undergo focused MI training and supervision to develop core skills in acceptance, empathy, evocation, and collaboration.
