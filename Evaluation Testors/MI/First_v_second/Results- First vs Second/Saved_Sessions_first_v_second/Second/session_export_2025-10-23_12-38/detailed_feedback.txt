# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do to quit”—the tone is authoritarian and lacks any warmth or respect for the patient’s autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s feelings and concerns, labeling them as “excuses” or “overcomplicating,” which is clearly disapproving and invalidating. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or acceptance of the patient’s ambivalence or perspective. The patient’s attempts to express hesitation and ambivalence are met with blunt commands and pressure rather than empathy or support.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance, instead adopting a harsh, judgmental tone that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist does not reflect or validate the patient’s feelings, concerns, or ambivalence. The patient repeatedly shares the emotional significance of drinking as a social and coping mechanism, yet the psychiatrist ignores these cues and responds with commands and accusations. For example, when the patient expresses fear of losing social connection or stress relief, the psychiatrist dismisses these as “excuses” rather than exploring or acknowledging their importance. The lack of reflective listening or perspective-taking is stark.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows little effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, missing critical opportunities to build empathy and connection.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is dominated by an authoritarian, directive approach with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly insists on “quitting cold turkey” and demands a yes/no commitment, ignoring the patient’s readiness and ambivalence. There is no invitation for the patient to explore their own motivations or values, nor any support for autonomy. The psychiatrist’s language is coercive (“You have to,” “No ifs or buts,” “Are you going to take responsibility or keep making excuses?”), which is antithetical to the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to embody the MI spirit, instead adopting a controlling, confrontational style that likely alienates the patient.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist attempts to elicit commitment language by repeatedly asking if the patient will quit or cut back, but these efforts are coercive rather than skillful evocation. The patient does express some tentative change talk (“I could try cutting back,” “I want to take this seriously”), but the psychiatrist does not reinforce or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist’s harsh tone likely inhibits deeper exploration of change talk. The final acceptance of cutting back to two drinks is the only moment where the psychiatrist somewhat aligns with the patient’s readiness, but it is framed rigidly and without collaborative planning.

**Summary:**  
Some attempts to evoke change talk are present but are undermined by confrontational style and lack of reinforcement.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with direct confrontation and dismissal. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s explanations as “excuses” and “avoiding the truth,” rather than acknowledging ambivalence or exploring sustain talk in a nonjudgmental way. There are no double-sided reflections or efforts to normalize the patient’s struggle. This approach likely increases resistance rather than softening sustain talk.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk, instead escalating resistance through confrontation.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The session is characterized by commands, demands, and a power imbalance. The patient is repeatedly pressured to make a firm decision on quitting immediately, with no negotiation or shared agenda-setting. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in their own life or invite co-creation of goals or plans. The only moment of partial collaboration is when the psychiatrist reluctantly accepts cutting back, but even then it is framed as a strict, non-negotiable rule.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to minimize power differentials or work as an equal partner with the patient.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacking in empathy or acceptance.  
- No evidence of MI spirit: collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support are absent.  
- Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening.  
- Partnership is nonexistent; the patient is pressured rather than engaged.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (alcohol use).  
- Some elicitation of change talk and tentative patient commitment to cutting back.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly confrontational, directive, and lacking in empathy or acceptance.  
- No use of reflective listening, affirmations, or supportive language.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and sustain talk are dismissed rather than explored or softened.  
- The psychiatrist fails to embody the MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.  
- Planning is rigid and imposed rather than co-created.  
- Engagement and rapport are poor, likely reducing patient motivation and openness.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Avoid commands and demands. Invite the patient to share their perspective and co-create goals.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses.”

3. **Evoke Change Talk Skillfully:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or ability to change, reflect and amplify these statements.  
   - Explore the patient’s values and motivations rather than imposing a timeline.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge the patient’s reasons for maintaining current behavior while gently guiding toward change.

5. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.  
   - Avoid pressuring for immediate decisions.

6. **Use Affirmations and Summaries:**  
   - Recognize patient strengths and efforts.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and clarify next steps.

7. **Develop Collaborative, Realistic Plans:**  
   - Work with the patient to create achievable goals that fit their readiness and context.  
   - Avoid rigid ultimatums.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session reflects a fundamental misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental approach undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s intrinsic motivation, and likely increases resistance. To improve, the psychiatrist must embrace the MI spirit of empathy, collaboration, and autonomy support, and develop skills in reflective listening, evoking change talk, and softening sustain talk. Without these changes, the effectiveness of interventions and patient outcomes will be severely compromised.
