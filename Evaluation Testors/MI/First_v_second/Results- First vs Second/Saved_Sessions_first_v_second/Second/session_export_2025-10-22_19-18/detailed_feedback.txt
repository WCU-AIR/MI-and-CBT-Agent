# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental, directive, and confrontational style throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist issues commands and ultimatums (“you need to quit drinking so much,” “you have to stop making excuses,” “no ifs, ands, or buts”) without any warmth or positive regard. The patient’s ambivalence and concerns are dismissed or met with impatience and criticism rather than understanding. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or respect for the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s feelings and struggles, which likely increases defensiveness and disengagement.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian and lacks any acceptance or supportive stance. This is a fundamental failure in MI principles and severely undermines rapport and engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal to no effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence, fear, and practical barriers, but the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead pushes a rigid agenda. There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s experience. The psychiatrist’s responses are accusatory and dismissive, which likely alienates the patient and shuts down open communication.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is essentially absent. The psychiatrist fails to listen or convey understanding, which is critical for effective MI.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive stance with no collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands compliance, issues ultimatums, and invalidates the patient’s autonomy (“You don’t get to say ‘not ready’,” “You have to commit fully starting today”). There is no partnership or invitation for the patient to explore their own motivations or values. The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy—is completely absent.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s style is the antithesis of MI spirit, undermining the foundational principles of motivational interviewing.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Instead, the approach is to demand change and criticize sustain talk. The patient offers tentative change talk (e.g., “I want to get better,” “I’m willing to think about it”), but the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these statements rather than amplifying or exploring them. There are no strategic reflections or questions to deepen the patient’s motivation or commitment.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated; the psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses it.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s ambivalence as “making excuses” and “dragging feet,” which likely increases resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or normalization of ambivalence. The patient’s legitimate concerns about social connection and readiness are ignored or invalidated.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened, which is counterproductive.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist exerts full control over the session, issuing commands and ultimatums without inviting the patient’s input or collaboration. The patient is repeatedly pressured to “promise” or “commit” without negotiation or respect for their autonomy. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or co-creation of goals.

**Summary:**  
The session is authoritarian and lacks any sense of partnership or client-centeredness.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- None identified.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist must abandon the confrontational, authoritarian style.  
- Develop skills in reflective listening and empathy to build rapport.  
- Embrace MI spirit by collaborating with the patient and supporting autonomy.  
- Use open questions and reflections to evoke and amplify change talk.  
- Soften sustain talk with double-sided reflections and normalization.  
- Avoid ultimatums and demands; instead, negotiate goals and plans collaboratively.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Supportive Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling or blaming language.  
   - Express genuine interest in the patient’s perspective.

2. **Develop Reflective Listening Skills:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen exploration.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Invite the patient to elaborate on their thoughts and feelings.  
   - Avoid yes/no or leading questions.

4. **Support Autonomy and Collaboration:**  
   - Invite the patient to set their own goals and pace.  
   - Avoid pressuring or demanding compliance.

5. **Skillfully Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or willingness, reflect and explore it further.  
   - Reinforce statements that favor change.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Summarize motivation and barriers.  
   - Co-create achievable, realistic next steps.

8. **Avoid Ultimatums and Demands:**  
   - Recognize that readiness to change varies and cannot be forced.  
   - Build motivation through empathy and partnership.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a profound misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style alienates the patient, suppresses change talk, and fails to build rapport or support autonomy. Without immediate and substantial improvement in MI skills—particularly acceptance, empathy, and evocation—this approach risks harming the therapeutic relationship and undermining patient motivation and outcomes.
