# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diabetes Medication Adherence Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive rather than accepting. From the outset, the psychiatrist uses language that implies blame and lack of understanding, such as “You really need to take your medication exactly as prescribed,” “Why haven’t you stuck to those simple tools yet?” and “Missing doses is unacceptable.” These statements communicate disapproval and a lack of unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly questions the patient’s commitment in a confrontational manner (“Are you ready to commit to this now?”), which likely increases defensiveness. There is no evidence of warmth, validation, or normalization of the patient’s struggles. The patient’s ambivalence and barriers are dismissed or minimized rather than accepted.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate unconditional positive regard and instead adopts a critical, authoritarian tone that undermines acceptance. This is a significant barrier to effective MI.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The responses are directive and do not demonstrate reflective listening or perspective-taking. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expressed difficulties (e.g., feeling overwhelmed, busy life, forgetting despite trying) beyond repeating the need for discipline. There are no complex or simple reflections that validate the patient’s feelings or struggles. The patient’s statements about being overwhelmed or needing a manageable plan are ignored or overridden by repeated commands.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist does not convey understanding or validation of the patient’s experience, which likely contributes to patient defensiveness and disengagement.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is highly authoritarian and directive, lacking collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You must start immediately,” “There’s no excuse,” “You have to commit now”) and frames the conversation as compliance rather than partnership. There is no evidence of evoking the patient’s own motivations or exploring ambivalence. Autonomy is not supported; instead, the patient is pressured to commit immediately without exploration of readiness or barriers. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or co-create a plan.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent. The approach is coercive and controlling, which is counterproductive to motivational interviewing principles.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist attempts to elicit commitment repeatedly by asking “Are you ready to commit now?” but does so in a confrontational manner that does not foster genuine change talk. The patient offers some change talk (“I want to be healthier,” “I’m willing to try”), but the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke, reinforce, or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on compliance and discipline, missing opportunities to explore the patient’s motivations or reasons for change in depth. There is no strategic use of reflections or open questions to deepen change talk.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally cultivated and often stifled by the confrontational style.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and admonishment rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels missed doses as “unacceptable” and dismisses the patient’s explanations as “excuses.” There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s ambivalence or legitimate barriers. The patient’s sustain talk about being overwhelmed or forgetting is met with demands rather than empathy or normalization.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced rather than softened, which likely increases resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and directives. The patient is repeatedly pressured to commit immediately without negotiation or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s perspective or preferences in a meaningful way. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.  

**Summary:**  
There is no evidence of partnership; the approach is authoritarian and directive.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient behavior as “unacceptable” or “excuses.”  
   - Express unconditional positive regard and validate the patient’s struggles.

2. **Enhance Empathy Through Reflective Listening:**  
   - Use simple and complex reflections to demonstrate understanding of the patient’s feelings and barriers.  
   - Reflect ambivalence and emotional content to deepen rapport.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Shift from directing and commanding to collaborating and evoking.  
   - Support patient autonomy and choice explicitly.

4. **Use Open-Ended Questions to Explore Motivation and Barriers:**  
   - Ask questions like “What makes it hard to take your medication every day?” or “What would help you remember better?”

5. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or willingness, reflect and explore these statements to strengthen motivation.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge the patient’s difficulties while gently guiding toward change.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Realistic Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to identify manageable steps and supports that fit their life.

8. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   - Periodically summarize the patient’s motivations, barriers, and agreed-upon next steps to reinforce commitment.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian, confrontational, and directive, which undermines acceptance, empathy, and collaboration. The patient’s ambivalence and barriers are dismissed rather than explored, and change talk is not skillfully evoked or reinforced. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a nonjudgmental, empathic stance; use reflective listening; support autonomy; and engage the patient in a collaborative process that respects their readiness and capacity for change. Without these changes, the effectiveness of motivational interviewing is severely compromised, risking patient disengagement and poor health outcomes.
