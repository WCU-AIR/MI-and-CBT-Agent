# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is judgmental, directive, and lacks warmth or positive regard. The repeated commands ("you really need to quit," "you have to stop now," "it’s not optional") communicate disapproval and pressure rather than acceptance. There is no evidence of respect for the patient’s autonomy or acknowledgment of her perspective. The psychiatrist dismisses the patient’s expressed concerns and values, failing to affirm or validate her experience. This is a clear example of a judgmental and disapproving stance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate unconditional positive regard, instead adopting a confrontational and pressuring tone that undermines acceptance.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s statements about her values, ambivalence, and readiness, instead repeating the same directive messages. No reflective listening or paraphrasing is present. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s emotional experience or the complexity of her situation, showing a lack of perspective-taking.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates little to no empathy, failing to listen or respond to the patient’s expressed feelings and thoughts.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with the psychiatrist repeatedly telling the patient what she must do. There is no collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore her motivations. Instead, the approach is coercive, undermining the core MI principles of partnership and autonomy support.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive stance that contradicts MI principles.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not evoke or reinforce change talk. Instead, the psychiatrist issues commands and questions that challenge the patient but do not elicit her own motivations or reasons for change. The patient offers change talk voluntarily, but the psychiatrist does not respond to or amplify it. There is no strategic evocation or reinforcement of the patient’s statements about family or health.

**Summary:**  
No skillful evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the psychiatrist misses opportunities to deepen motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly confronts and challenges the patient’s sustain talk with admonishments and pressure. There is no use of reflective listening, double-sided reflections, or normalization to soften sustain talk. The approach likely increases resistance rather than reducing it.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and pressure, exacerbating resistance rather than softening it.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The language is directive and controlling, with no invitation for shared decision-making or client input. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s expressed concerns and values, failing to minimize the power differential.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and disregards the patient’s autonomy.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- Must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.  
- Develop reflective listening skills to demonstrate empathy.  
- Embrace MI spirit by collaborating and supporting autonomy.  
- Actively evoke and reinforce change talk.  
- Use strategies to soften sustain talk rather than confront it.  
- Build partnership and share power with the patient.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks MI spirit.  
- There is no acceptance or empathy demonstrated.  
- The psychiatrist fails to engage the patient in a collaborative process.  
- No use of reflective listening, affirmations, or evocation of change talk.  
- Sustain talk is met with confrontation, likely increasing resistance.  
- The patient’s values and readiness are ignored, undermining motivation.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid commands and judgments. Affirm the patient’s experience and values.

2. **Develop Reflective Listening Skills:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient, support autonomy, and evoke motivation.

4. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Invite the patient to explore her own reasons and readiness for change.

5. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Values:**  
   - Recognize the patient’s commitment to family and desire to quit.

6. **Softly Address Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance.

7. **Collaboratively Plan Next Steps:**  
   - Develop a quit plan together, respecting the patient’s pace and readiness.

8. **Avoid Pressuring Language:**  
   - Replace “you have to” with “what do you think about…” or “how might you approach…”

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s approach is directive, confrontational, and dismissive of the patient’s perspective, values, and readiness. To improve, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a collaborative, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive style that skillfully evokes and reinforces the patient’s own motivations for change. Without these changes, the interaction risks increasing resistance and undermining the patient’s willingness to engage in behavior change.
