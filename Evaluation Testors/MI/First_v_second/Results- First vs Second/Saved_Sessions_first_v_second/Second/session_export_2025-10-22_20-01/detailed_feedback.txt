# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is judgmental, directive, and lacks warmth or respect. The opening statements (“I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” “You really should quit now”) communicate a controlling, authoritarian stance rather than unconditional positive regard. The patient’s flat, resistant responses reflect discomfort and disengagement, likely exacerbated by the psychiatrist’s lack of acceptance. There is no evidence of validation, normalization, or empathy for the patient’s struggle. The tone is coercive rather than supportive, which is counterproductive in MI.  
**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance or respect, instead adopting a confrontational and directive style that alienates the patient.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective or feelings. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly lectures and pushes the patient without acknowledging his frustration, resignation, or ambivalence. The patient’s expressions of difficulty and disengagement (“It just didn’t change anything,” “It’s always the same fight”) are ignored or dismissed. No reflective listening or emotional attunement is evident.  
**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows little effort to understand or convey the patient’s experience, missing critical opportunities to build rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, lacking collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly tells the patient what to do, demands commitment, and uses coercive language (“You have to be serious,” “No ifs, ands, or buts”). There is no invitation for the patient’s input or exploration of his own motivations. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of orders rather than an active agent in change. This approach is antithetical to the MI spirit.  
**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to embody the MI spirit, instead adopting a controlling, prescriptive stance that undermines client autonomy and engagement.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient offers some tentative statements about willingness (“I guess I could try,” “Yeah, I could try cutting back”), these are met with commands rather than reflections or amplifications. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore the patient’s ambivalence or reasons for change and does not reinforce any expressions of desire, ability, or commitment. Instead, the psychiatrist’s repeated directives likely suppress further change talk.  
**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and not reinforced; the psychiatrist’s style likely inhibits the patient’s motivation to explore change.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is frequent and clear in the patient’s responses (“It’s always the same fight,” “Doesn’t feel like it matters much,” “Feels pointless”), but the psychiatrist does not soften or skillfully respond to it. Instead, sustain talk is met with confrontation and admonishment (“Stop making excuses,” “You’re just overcomplicating things”). There are no double-sided reflections, validations, or attempts to normalize ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s approach likely entrenches sustain talk rather than softening it.  
**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than strategic softening, missing critical MI techniques to reduce resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is one-sided, with the psychiatrist issuing commands and the patient responding passively or with resistance. There is no evidence of shared decision-making, agenda-setting, or respect for the patient’s expertise in his own experience. The psychiatrist’s language (“You have to,” “No more messing around”) reinforces a power imbalance and undermines partnership.  
**Summary:**  
The session lacks partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and fails to engage the patient as an equal collaborator.

- Embrace MI spirit by supporting autonomy and collaboration.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Respectful Stance:**  
   - Avoid commands and directives; instead, express genuine interest and acceptance of the patient’s experience.  

2. **Develop Reflective Listening Skills:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and build rapport.  

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient, evoke their own motivations, and support autonomy.  

4. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Invite elaboration and exploration rather than yes/no answers.  

5. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize any attempts or willingness to change, no matter how small.  

6. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or ability, reflect and explore these statements to deepen motivation.  

7. **Softly Respond to Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance.  

8. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Involve the patient in setting goals and strategies, respecting their readiness and preferences.  

9. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   - Use summaries to consolidate motivation and reinforce commitment.  

10. **Avoid Authoritarian Language:**  
    - Replace “You have to” with “What do you think about…” or “How would you like to approach…”
