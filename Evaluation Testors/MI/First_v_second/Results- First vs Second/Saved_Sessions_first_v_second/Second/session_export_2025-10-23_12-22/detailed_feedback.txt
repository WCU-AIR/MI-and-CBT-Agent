# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly adopts a judgmental and confrontational tone, exemplified by statements such as “You have to stop feeling sorry for yourself,” “You have to stop doubting yourself,” and “You have to stop feeling helpless and just do it.” These phrases communicate disapproval and blame rather than unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge or validate the patient’s expressed feelings of helplessness and isolation, instead dismissing them as “excuses” or “feeling sorry for yourself.” There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or support; rather, the stance is authoritarian and directive. This approach likely exacerbates the patient’s sense of shame and hopelessness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is judgmental and directive, lacking respect and unconditional positive regard, which is detrimental to building rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient repeatedly expresses feelings of helplessness, isolation, and ambivalence, yet the psychiatrist ignores these emotional cues and instead responds with commands and accusations. There are no reflective statements or attempts to paraphrase or validate the patient’s experience. For example, when the patient says, “I just feel helpless about this whole deal,” the psychiatrist responds with “Feeling helpless is just an excuse,” which invalidates the patient’s feelings. This lack of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s withdrawal and minimal engagement.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy or reflective listening, missing critical opportunities to connect with and understand the patient’s emotional state.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style that contradicts the core MI principles of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You have to quit,” “You must pick a date,” “You have to stop feeling sorry for yourself”) and uses confrontational language that undermines partnership. There is no evidence of evoking the patient’s own motivations or supporting autonomy; instead, the psychiatrist imposes a rigid agenda and insists on immediate action without exploring the patient’s readiness or barriers. This approach is likely to increase resistance rather than foster motivation.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian and directive, lacking collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support fundamental to MI.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient offers some change talk (e.g., “I really shouldn’t have done it,” “I want to commit”), the psychiatrist ignores these statements and instead focuses on pushing the patient to act immediately. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify the patient’s expressions of desire or regret. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore the patient’s ambivalence or to elicit commitment language in a supportive manner.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not cultivated; the psychiatrist fails to reinforce or deepen the patient’s motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften sustain talk but rather dismisses and challenges it harshly. The patient’s sustain talk—feelings of helplessness, isolation, and doubt—is met with accusations of “making excuses” and “feeling sorry for yourself.” There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s responses likely intensify sustain talk and resistance rather than reduce it.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than skillful softening or redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands and directives, with no invitation for the patient’s input or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands immediate commitment and action without exploring the patient’s perspective or readiness. The power differential is stark and unmitigated, undermining the therapeutic alliance.

**Summary:**  
There is no evidence of partnership; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance that marginalizes the patient’s voice.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary  
**Overall Score: 1/5**  

The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally inconsistent with motivational interviewing principles. The session is marked by judgment, lack of empathy, authoritarianism, and failure to evoke or support the patient’s motivation. This style is likely to alienate the patient, increase resistance, and reduce the likelihood of behavior change.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Maintains focus on smoking cessation topic.  

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly judgmental, confrontational, and directive, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, reflective listening, and validation of the patient’s feelings.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.  
- No partnership or autonomy support is evident; the patient is repeatedly commanded rather than engaged collaboratively.  
- The session lacks affirmations, summaries, and collaborative planning.  
- The psychiatrist does not use open questions effectively to explore ambivalence or motivation.  
- The overall style is likely to increase patient resistance and disengagement.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Supportive Stance:**  
   Replace confrontational language with acceptance and unconditional positive regard. Avoid blaming or shaming the patient.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy Through Reflective Listening:**  
   Use reflections to validate the patient’s feelings of helplessness and isolation. Reflect both content and emotion to build rapport.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   Foster collaboration, evoke the patient’s own motivations, and support autonomy. Avoid commands and directives; instead, invite the patient to explore their ambivalence.

4. **Skillfully Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses regret or desire to change, reflect and explore these statements to deepen motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalization to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.

6. **Use Open Questions to Explore Ambivalence:**  
   Ask open-ended questions that invite elaboration rather than yes/no answers or compliance.

7. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Efforts:**  
   Recognize any attempts or desires to change, no matter how small, to build confidence.

8. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   Work with the patient to set realistic, achievable goals and support gradual steps toward quitting.

9. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   Periodically summarize the patient’s statements to reinforce motivation and clarify next steps.
