# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive rather than accepting. The tone is authoritative and pressing, with repeated insistence that the patient “must quit” and “stop making excuses.” There is no evidence of warmth, positive regard, or validation of the patient’s perspective. The patient’s ambivalence and readiness are dismissed or challenged rather than accepted. For example, statements like “You really have to stop making excuses” and “You can’t just say it’s overwhelming and keep smoking” are confrontational and undermine acceptance. The psychiatrist fails to create a safe, nonjudgmental space for the patient to explore ambivalence.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a largely judgmental and directive stance, lacking unconditional positive regard or supportive acceptance.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses that smoking is occasional, not perceived as urgent, and that quitting feels overwhelming. The psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead pushes a rigid agenda. There are no reflective statements or attempts to paraphrase or validate the patient’s feelings or ambivalence. For example, the psychiatrist repeatedly asks “Why haven’t you cut down already?” or “Why didn’t you stick to it?” without acknowledging the patient’s stated reasons or emotional experience. This lack of empathy likely contributes to patient disengagement.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist does not demonstrate accurate understanding or convey the patient’s perspective.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly commands the patient to quit immediately, dismisses the patient’s ambivalence, and uses coercive language (“You must commit fully,” “No ifs or buts”). There is no invitation for the patient to share their own motivations or reasons for change, nor any support for autonomy. The psychiatrist’s stance is controlling and persuasive rather than collaborative.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the approach is authoritarian and directive, lacking collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. The patient offers tentative statements about occasional smoking and some awareness of risks, but the psychiatrist does not build on these or explore the patient’s own motivations. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on pushing the agenda and challenging the patient’s reasons for not quitting. There are no strategic reflections or questions designed to elicit desire, ability, reasons, or need for change. The patient’s minimal change talk is neither amplified nor deepened.  

**Summary:**  
No meaningful evocation or cultivation of change talk; missed opportunities to explore and reinforce patient motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften or skillfully respond to sustain talk. The patient expresses sustain talk by minimizing the urgency and expressing reluctance to quit immediately. The psychiatrist responds with confrontation and insistence rather than reflective or double-sided statements that acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change. For example, the psychiatrist says, “You really have to stop making excuses” and “You can’t just say it’s overwhelming and keep smoking,” which reinforce sustain talk defensively rather than softening it.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening or strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and directives, with no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for patient autonomy. The patient is repeatedly told what to do without invitation to discuss or negotiate. The psychiatrist’s language (“You must commit,” “Just throw away your cigarettes”) is controlling and does not minimize power differentials.  

**Summary:**  
No partnership or collaboration; the psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritarian stance.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently communicates the health risks of smoking.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks acceptance, empathy, and collaboration.  
- There is no evocation or reinforcement of change talk.  
- Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening.  
- The patient’s autonomy and ambivalence are ignored or dismissed.  

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist consistently communicates the health risks of smoking and maintains focus on the topic.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks core MI skills such as acceptance, empathy, and collaboration.  
- There is no reflective listening or affirmations.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.  
- The patient’s autonomy and ambivalence are ignored, which likely reduces engagement and motivation.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid confrontational language and judgmental statements.  
   - Validate the patient’s feelings and perspective, even if ambivalent.

2. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen exploration.

3. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions that invite the patient to explore their own reasons for change.  
   - Amplify and reinforce any change talk that emerges.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Normalize ambivalence as part of the change process.

5. **Support Autonomy and Partnership:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.  
   - Collaborate on setting goals and plans rather than issuing directives.

6. **Avoid Persuasion and Directives Without Permission:**  
   - Ask permission before giving advice or information.  
   - Use invitational language rather than commands.

7. **Develop Collaborative Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Summarize key points, especially change talk, to reinforce motivation.  
   - Co-create a realistic, patient-centered plan for change.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamentally flawed approach to motivational interviewing. The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian, confrontational, and directive, lacking acceptance, empathy, and collaboration. Core MI skills such as reflective listening, evocation of change talk, and softening of sustain talk are absent. The patient’s autonomy and ambivalence are ignored, which undermines engagement and motivation. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a client-centered, collaborative stance and develop core MI competencies to effectively support behavior change.
