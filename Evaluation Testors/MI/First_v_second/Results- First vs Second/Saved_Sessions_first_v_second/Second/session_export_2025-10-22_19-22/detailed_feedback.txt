# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the tone is confrontational and lacks warmth or genuine acceptance (e.g., “you really need to quit smoking,” “you have to have more willpower,” “stop making excuses”). The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s ambivalence with blunt statements rather than expressing understanding or validating the patient’s struggle. There is no evidence of normalization or unconditional positive regard. The patient’s expressions of difficulty and ambivalence are met with criticism rather than empathy. This approach risks alienating the patient and undermining rapport.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive, with minimal demonstration of acceptance or positive regard. This significantly limits the patient’s comfort and openness.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows limited effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses complex ambivalence—acknowledging the harms of smoking but also describing social and emotional barriers to quitting. However, the psychiatrist responds with repeated challenges and demands rather than reflective listening or perspective-taking. There are no reflective statements or attempts to explore the patient’s feelings of loneliness, boredom, or fear of failure. The psychiatrist’s questions and statements are mostly accusatory or prescriptive, which fails to convey empathy or build trust.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is minimal; the psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to reflect and validate the patient’s experience, instead opting for confrontation.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session lacks the core MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding immediate commitment. There is no evidence of partnership or invitation to explore the patient’s own motivations or values. Autonomy is undermined by commands and ultimatums (“you have to pick a quit date today,” “no more delays”). The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s reasons for change beyond superficial questioning and does not support the patient’s sense of control or choice.

**Summary:**  
The session is directive and controlling, with no collaborative or autonomy-supportive elements characteristic of MI.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the psychiatrist asks about quitting history and reasons for failure, these questions are framed in a confrontational manner that risks eliciting defensiveness rather than genuine change talk. The patient does express some change talk (desire to quit, concerns about health and future), but the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke, reinforce, or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on pushing the patient to commit prematurely, which may suppress authentic change talk. There is no strategic use of reflections or affirmations to deepen motivation.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not effectively cultivated or reinforced; the psychiatrist’s approach may inhibit deeper exploration of motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s ambivalence, social barriers, fear of failure) is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels patient statements as “excuses” and demands immediate action, which likely intensifies sustain talk or resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change. The lack of skillful handling of sustain talk undermines the therapeutic alliance and the patient’s readiness.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is ignored or confronted harshly, with no evidence of softening or skillful management.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands, ultimatums, and a prescriptive agenda. The patient’s input is minimized, and their autonomy is overridden by demands (“you have to pick a quit date today,” “no more waiting around”). There is no negotiation or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s expertise in their own life or to co-create a plan that fits the patient’s readiness and context.

**Summary:**  
The session is authoritarian and expert-driven, lacking partnership or shared power.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).  
- The patient remains engaged and expresses ambivalence and some change talk despite the challenging style.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is overly confrontational, judgmental, and directive.  
- There is a critical lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support is absent.  
- Change talk is not skillfully cultivated or reinforced.  
- Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened.  
- Partnership is nonexistent; the patient is not empowered or involved in decision-making.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses.”  
   - Express unconditional positive regard and normalize ambivalence.

2. **Increase Empathic Listening and Reflective Responses:**  
   - Use reflections to validate the patient’s feelings and struggles.  
   - Reflect both content and emotional undertones to deepen understanding.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing or commanding.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy by offering choices and respecting readiness.

4. **Skillfully Cultivate Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and amplify these statements.  
   - Avoid pushing for premature commitment; instead, explore ambivalence.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Validate the difficulty of change and the patient’s concerns.

6. **Build Partnership and Shared Decision-Making:**  
   - Invite the patient to co-create a quit plan that fits their readiness and context.  
   - Negotiate goals and timelines collaboratively.

7. **Use Affirmations and Summaries:**  
   - Affirm patient strengths, efforts, and intentions.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and clarify next steps.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, judgmental, and directive style undermines rapport, empathy, and patient autonomy. The session lacks the collaborative, evocative, and autonomy-supportive spirit essential to MI. Change talk is not effectively cultivated, and sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a fundamentally different approach grounded in acceptance, empathy, and partnership to effectively support the patient’s motivation and behavior change.
