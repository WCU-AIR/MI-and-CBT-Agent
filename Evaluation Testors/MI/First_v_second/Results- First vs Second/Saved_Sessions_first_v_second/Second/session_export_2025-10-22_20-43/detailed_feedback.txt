# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s tone throughout the session is predominantly judgmental and directive rather than accepting. The repeated commands ("You have to quit," "No excuses," "You must set a quit date") and admonishments ("stop making excuses," "you really can’t keep putting this off") communicate disapproval and pressure rather than unconditional positive regard. There is no evidence of warmth, validation, or normalization of the patient’s ambivalence. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s perspective or struggles in a supportive way, which likely contributes to the patient’s defensiveness and resistance.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely authoritarian and directive, lacking the respectful, supportive stance necessary for effective MI. This undermines rapport and client engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. There are no reflective statements or paraphrases that demonstrate active listening or emotional attunement. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with questions and commands without acknowledging the patient’s expressed feelings or ambivalence. The patient’s concerns about social belonging and readiness are ignored or dismissed rather than explored empathetically. This lack of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s defensive and minimizing responses.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited to a superficial acknowledgment of the problem without genuine understanding or validation of the patient’s experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with frequent persuasion and little collaboration. The psychiatrist repeatedly insists on immediate quitting and setting a quit date, ignoring the patient’s expressed ambivalence and readiness. There is no evidence of evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values, nor support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient to explore options or express their own reasons for change. Instead, the interaction is dominated by commands and pressure, which is antithetical to the MI spirit.  

**Summary:**  
The session lacks collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support, reflecting a coercive rather than motivational interviewing approach.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient occasionally offers change talk (e.g., acknowledging coughing, tiredness of symptoms, considering cutting back), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly returns to commands and warnings, missing opportunities to reflect or amplify the patient’s own reasons for change. There is no strategic elicitation of commitment language or confidence statements. The patient’s tentative change talk is overshadowed by the psychiatrist’s confrontational style, which likely inhibits further elaboration.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but poorly cultivated; the psychiatrist misses multiple chances to deepen and reinforce motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently confronts and challenges sustain talk rather than softening or exploring it. The patient’s minimizations, social concerns, and ambivalence are met with admonishments and demands rather than reflective or double-sided reflections. There is no attempt to normalize ambivalence or validate the difficulty of quitting. The confrontational approach likely entrenches sustain talk and resistance rather than reducing it.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and pressure, lacking the skillful softening or strategic redirection essential in MI.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritative stance throughout the session. The patient is repeatedly told what to do, with no invitation to collaborate or share power. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in their own life or negotiate goals or plans. The interaction is one-sided, with the psychiatrist dominating the agenda and decision-making. This undermines the development of a trusting, equal partnership.  

**Summary:**  
The session is highly directive and lacks genuine collaboration or partnership.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directive commands to partnership. Invite the patient’s perspective and acknowledge their expertise in their own life.

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence. Normalize the difficulty of quitting.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Ask questions that invite elaboration and exploration rather than yes/no or leading questions.

4. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize any attempts to cut back or consider alternatives to build motivation.

5. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Reflect and reinforce statements that indicate desire, ability, reasons, or need for change.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and validate ambivalence to reduce resistance.

7. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Explicitly acknowledge the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

8. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to set realistic goals and next steps, ensuring buy-in.

9. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   - Use summaries to reinforce motivation and clarify the patient’s own reasons for change.

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational approach undermines rapport, engagement, and motivation. Without these changes, the risk of alienating the patient and impeding behavior change is high.
