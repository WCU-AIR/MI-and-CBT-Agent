# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a largely judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the tone is authoritative and admonishing (“You need to quit now,” “You really should just stop,” “You have to be tougher”), which undermines unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s ambivalence with blunt commands and dismisses her explanations as “excuses.” There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or genuine acceptance of the patient’s lived experience or struggles. The patient’s expressions of stress and coping are met with criticism rather than understanding. This approach risks alienating the patient and reducing engagement.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive, lacking the respectful, supportive stance essential for MI. Acceptance is minimal and inconsistent.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows limited effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient clearly expresses her ambivalence, stressors, and coping needs, but the psychiatrist responds with repeated “why didn’t you just…” questions and admonishments rather than reflective listening. There are no complex or simple reflections that validate the patient’s feelings or acknowledge the difficulty of her situation. The patient’s emotional experience is largely ignored, and the psychiatrist fails to convey accurate understanding or empathy. The patient’s frustration and vulnerability are met with pressure rather than support.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is minimal and mostly absent. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to reflect and validate the patient’s perspective, resulting in a disconnect.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is highly authoritarian and directive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly commands the patient to “just quit,” “stop making excuses,” and “be tougher,” which contradicts the MI spirit. There is no invitation for the patient to explore her own motivations or values, nor any support for her autonomy. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation with a prescriptive agenda, ignoring the patient’s ambivalence and readiness. This approach is antithetical to MI principles.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is essentially absent. The session is coercive and controlling rather than collaborative and autonomy-supportive.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient offers some change talk (e.g., “I want to do better for my son,” “I’ve tried quitting before”), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce it. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on pushing the patient to quit immediately, missing opportunities to explore the patient’s own reasons, desires, or confidence. The repeated “why didn’t you just…” questions serve more to elicit sustain talk or defensiveness than to cultivate genuine change talk. There is no strategic amplification or reinforcement of the patient’s expressed motivations.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but poorly cultivated. The psychiatrist’s approach tends to shut down rather than evoke or deepen motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften or skillfully respond to sustain talk. Instead, sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal (“stop making excuses,” “you have to be tougher”). There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. The patient’s rationalizations and coping needs are invalidated rather than gently explored or reframed. This approach likely increases resistance and sustain talk rather than reducing it.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and confronted rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritarian role throughout the session. The patient is not invited to collaborate or share power in the conversation. The psychiatrist issues commands and demands compliance without negotiation or respect for the patient’s autonomy. There is no evidence of partnership or co-creation of goals or plans. The patient’s voice is marginalized, and the power differential is stark.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is essentially absent. The session is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda with no client-centered collaboration.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently emphasizes the seriousness of smoking risks.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, judgmental, and confrontational.  
- There is a lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, autonomy—is missing.  
- Change talk is not cultivated; sustain talk is confronted rather than softened.  
- Partnership is absent; the patient is not engaged as an equal partner.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling or criticizing the patient’s behavior or reasons.  
   - Express genuine respect and understanding for the patient’s struggles.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing or commanding.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and support autonomy.

4. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and explore these statements.  
   - Avoid confrontational “why didn’t you…” questions that provoke defensiveness.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change and validate coping strategies.

6. **Enhance Partnership:**  
   - Invite the patient to co-create goals and plans.  
   - Share power and respect the patient’s expertise in her own life.

7. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions that invite elaboration.  
   - Affirm the patient’s strengths, efforts, and intentions.

8. **Avoid Persuasion Without Permission:**  
   - Ask permission before giving advice or information.

9. **Incorporate Summaries and Planning:**  
   - Summarize key points, especially change talk, to reinforce motivation.  
   - Collaboratively develop a concrete, realistic quit plan.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamentally flawed approach to motivational interviewing. The psychiatrist’s directive, confrontational style undermines rapport, empathy, and collaboration. The patient’s ambivalence and coping needs are dismissed rather than explored, and there is no effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt the core MI principles of acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation, and move away from authoritarian, judgmental tactics that risk disengaging the patient and reducing the likelihood of successful behavior change.
