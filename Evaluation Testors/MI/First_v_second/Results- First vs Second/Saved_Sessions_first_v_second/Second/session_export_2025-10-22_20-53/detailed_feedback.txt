# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is judgmental, directive, and disapproving throughout the session. From the outset, the tone is confrontational (“you really need to quit smoking,” “you have to take this seriously”), and the patient is repeatedly challenged and accused of making excuses or not being committed enough. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The psychiatrist dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and struggles, instead insisting on immediate action without empathy or understanding. This is the opposite of acceptance and creates a hostile environment that likely inhibits open communication.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate acceptance. The interaction is marked by judgment, pressure, and lack of respect for the patient’s experience.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no evidence of empathy. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or reflect the patient’s feelings, fears, or ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s expressions of difficulty and hesitation (“stop making excuses,” “you need to be strong and do it”). The patient’s statements about feeling overwhelmed, scared, or stuck are ignored or met with demands rather than reflective listening. The psychiatrist misses every opportunity to acknowledge the patient’s emotional state or perspective.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is essentially absent. The psychiatrist does not demonstrate any reflective listening or perspective-taking.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is completely lacking. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, directive stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding immediate commitment. There is no collaboration or partnership; the patient’s autonomy is ignored and undermined by pressure and confrontation. The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change but instead imposes a rigid agenda. This approach is antithetical to MI principles.

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by a controlling, prescriptive style with no evidence of MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient occasionally expresses desire or ambivalence about quitting, the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these statements rather than exploring or amplifying them. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands immediate quitting without eliciting the patient’s own reasons, confidence, or plans. There is no strategic use of reflections or questions to deepen change talk.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated; the psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses it.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s resistance, ambivalence, or reasons for maintaining smoking) is met with confrontation and pressure rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s statements as “excuses” and “hesitation,” and repeatedly insists on quitting cold turkey immediately. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. Sustain talk is reinforced by the adversarial tone.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation, issues commands, and repeatedly challenges the patient’s commitment. The patient is not invited to share their perspective or co-create a plan. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, lacking any sense of partnership.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- Demonstrate unconditional positive regard and acceptance.  
- Practice reflective listening and empathy.  
- Adopt a collaborative, autonomy-supportive stance.  
- Skillfully evoke and reinforce change talk.  
- Soften sustain talk with strategic reflections.  
- Build partnership and share power with the patient.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Maintains focus on smoking cessation.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian, confrontational, and directive, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and sustain talk are met with pressure and judgment rather than understanding and strategic softening.  
- No effort is made to evoke or amplify change talk.  
- The psychiatrist fails to support autonomy or build partnership.  
- The session lacks collaborative planning or summarizing.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling or confronting the patient. Instead, express genuine respect and positive regard.

2. **Practice Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and validate the patient’s experience.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Invite the patient to elaborate on their thoughts, feelings, and motivations.

4. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - Explore the patient’s own reasons, desires, and confidence for quitting.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalize ambivalence to reduce resistance.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize that the patient controls the pace and nature of change.

7. **Build Partnership:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient to set goals and develop a feasible plan.

8. **Avoid Pressuring or Demanding:**  
   - Replace commands with invitations and collaborative problem-solving.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, empathy, and collaboration, likely reducing the patient’s motivation and engagement. To improve, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a respectful, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive approach that skillfully evokes and reinforces the patient’s own motivations for change.
