# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. The tone is authoritarian and lacks warmth or empathy. The patient’s expressions of fear, ambivalence, and distress are met with blunt commands and accusations rather than understanding or support. For example, phrases like “you really need to quit smoking,” “you have to understand,” and “stop making excuses” communicate disapproval and disregard for the patient’s perspective. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or validation of the patient’s feelings or struggles. The psychiatrist repeatedly questions “Why haven’t you just stopped already?” in a confrontational manner, which likely increases patient defensiveness.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance or respect, instead adopting a harsh, judgmental tone that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist does not reflect or acknowledge the patient’s fears, anxieties, or ambivalence. The patient repeatedly expresses concerns about ongoing pain, fear of cancer, and feeling overwhelmed, yet the psychiatrist ignores these emotional cues and instead insists on immediate cessation of smoking. The psychiatrist’s responses do not paraphrase or validate the patient’s experience but rather dismiss or minimize it. For example, the psychiatrist says, “You have to stop making excuses,” which invalidates the patient’s genuine concerns. There is no evidence of reflective listening or perspective-taking.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows little effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, missing critical opportunities to build empathy and connection.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is highly directive and authoritarian, lacking collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly commands the patient to quit smoking immediately, ignoring the patient’s expressed ambivalence and readiness. The patient is not invited to share her motivations or concerns in a way that is heard or explored. There is no evidence of partnership or eliciting the patient’s own reasons for change. Instead, the psychiatrist uses confrontational language (“you have to,” “why haven’t you just stopped already?”) that undermines autonomy and likely increases resistance.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to embody the MI spirit; the approach is coercive rather than collaborative or evocative.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient expresses some desire and tentative reasons for quitting (“I want to quit,” “I’ve tried cutting down”), the psychiatrist does not reflect or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with demands and questions that focus on compliance rather than motivation. There is no strategic use of reflections or open questions to deepen the patient’s own motivations or confidence. The psychiatrist’s style likely suppresses change talk rather than cultivates it.  

**Summary:**  
No skillful evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the psychiatrist’s approach likely stifles motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften or skillfully respond to sustain talk. The patient expresses sustain talk related to fear, uncertainty, and reliance on smoking for coping, but the psychiatrist dismisses these concerns as “excuses” and insists on immediate cessation. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s ambivalence or validate the difficulty of change. The psychiatrist’s confrontational style likely intensifies sustain talk and resistance.  

**Summary:**  
No evidence of softening sustain talk; the psychiatrist’s responses are confrontational and invalidating.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritative stance with no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The patient is repeatedly told what to do without invitation to collaborate or negotiate goals. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in her own experience or invite her input on readiness or next steps. The repeated demands and rhetorical questions (“Why haven’t you just stopped already?”) reinforce a power imbalance and likely damage the therapeutic alliance.  

**Summary:**  
No partnership or collaboration; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and disregards patient autonomy.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Use language that invites the patient’s perspective and respects her autonomy.  
   - Avoid commands and accusations; instead, express genuine curiosity and concern.

2. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and validate the patient’s experience.  
   - For example, “It sounds like you’re really scared about what might be causing your pain.”

3. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions to explore the patient’s own reasons for quitting and confidence.  
   - Amplify any statements favoring change.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence (“On one hand, smoking helps you cope; on the other, you’re worried about your health.”)  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change.

5. **Affirm Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize the patient’s attempts to cut down and her willingness to discuss quitting.  
   - Affirm her courage in facing health concerns.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize that the decision to quit is ultimately hers and that you are there to support her when she is ready.

7. **Collaborative Planning:**  
   - Explore readiness and collaboratively develop a plan that fits the patient’s current stage of change.

8. **Avoid Confrontation:**  
   - Replace confrontational questions with empathetic, open-ended inquiries.

**Summary:**  
This session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. To improve, the psychiatrist must embrace the MI spirit of collaboration, empathy, and autonomy support, using reflective listening and strategic evocation to foster change talk and soften sustain talk. Without these fundamental changes, the patient’s ambivalence and fears will remain unaddressed, and meaningful progress toward smoking cessation is unlikely.
