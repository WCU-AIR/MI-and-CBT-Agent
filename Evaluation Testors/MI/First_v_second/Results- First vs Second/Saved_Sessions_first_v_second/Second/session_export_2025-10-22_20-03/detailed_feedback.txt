# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental, directive, and authoritarian stance throughout the session. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s feelings and concerns, labeling her reasons for smoking as “excuses” and demanding immediate cessation without empathy or validation. For example, statements like “You don’t get to pick and choose here,” and “You have to toughen up and quit now” are harsh and dismissive. This approach likely alienates the patient and undermines rapport. There is no normalization of ambivalence or struggle, which is critical in MI.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance or respect, instead adopting a confrontational and coercive tone that is counterproductive in MI.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses stress, ambivalence, and the social role of smoking, but the psychiatrist ignores these cues and responds with admonishments rather than reflective listening. There are no complex or simple reflections that capture the patient’s emotional experience or ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with directives and challenges, e.g., “You have to quit now—no ifs, ands, or buts,” which shuts down exploration of the patient’s feelings.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is essentially absent; the psychiatrist does not demonstrate understanding or validation of the patient’s experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is completely lacking. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do without inviting her perspective or evoking her motivations. Autonomy is denied explicitly (“You don’t get to pick and choose here,” “This isn’t optional”). There is no partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist’s approach is coercive and directive, which is antithetical to MI principles.

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by a controlling, directive approach with no evidence of MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient offers tentative change talk (e.g., “Maybe I could try to cut back a bit,” “I’m not saying I won’t stop someday”), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these statements rather than amplifying or exploring them. There are no strategic reflections or questions designed to deepen the patient’s motivation or commitment. Instead, the psychiatrist challenges and confronts, which likely suppresses change talk.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present in the patient but is not cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften sustain talk; rather, sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal. The patient’s reasons for smoking (stress relief, social belonging) are labeled as “excuses” and “crutches,” with no attempt to acknowledge or validate these concerns. There are no double-sided reflections or strategic redirections to gently shift sustain talk toward change. The confrontational style likely entrenches sustain talk rather than softening it.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with harsh confrontation rather than skillful softening or redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist repeatedly asserts authority and control, ignoring the patient’s input and denying her autonomy. The patient’s attempts to express ambivalence or negotiate are dismissed. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s expertise or preferences, nor does she co-create a plan. The interaction is one-sided and directive.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks any meaningful partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from authoritarian to partnership-based communication. Invite the patient’s perspective and respect her autonomy.

2. **Develop Reflective Listening Skills:**  
   Use simple and complex reflections to demonstrate understanding and validate the patient’s experience.

3. **Express Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   Acknowledge the patient’s ambivalence and struggles without judgment.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and explore these statements to deepen motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and validate the patient’s reasons for smoking while gently guiding toward change.

6. **Use Open Questions Effectively:**  
   Ask open-ended questions that invite elaboration rather than yes/no answers or leading questions.

7. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Explicitly affirm the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

8. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   Work with the patient to create a realistic, stepwise plan for change, including managing stress and social pressures.

9. **Avoid Confrontation and Ultimatums:**  
   Replace demands and threats with supportive, motivational dialogue.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive style undermines rapport, suppresses change talk, and likely increases resistance. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically change approach to one grounded in empathy, collaboration, and evocation of the patient’s own motivations and strengths. Without these changes, the risk of patient disengagement and treatment failure is high.
