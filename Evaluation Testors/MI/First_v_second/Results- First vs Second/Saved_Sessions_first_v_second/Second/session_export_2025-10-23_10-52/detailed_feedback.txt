# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Smoking Cessation Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive rather than accepting. From the outset, the tone is authoritarian and pressuring (“you have to quit smoking,” “you really should just stop immediately”), which communicates disapproval rather than unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and reasons for smoking, labeling them as “excuses” and showing little empathy for the patient’s lived experience or emotional state. There is no evidence of normalization or validation of the patient’s struggle. The patient’s resistance and guardedness are met with confrontation rather than acceptance.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to establish a respectful, supportive environment. The stance is largely judgmental and directive, undermining rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal reflective listening or perspective-taking. The patient expresses ambivalence, stress as a coping mechanism, and a desire for autonomy, but these are not acknowledged or explored empathetically. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with directives and challenges, showing limited effort to understand the patient’s viewpoint. There are no complex or nuanced reflections capturing the patient’s emotional experience or ambivalence. The patient’s statements about stress and control are met with dismissal rather than validation.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist does not convey accurate understanding or emotional attunement to the patient’s perspective.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is largely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding immediate compliance. There is no genuine partnership or invitation for the patient to explore their own motivations or values. Autonomy is explicitly undermined by commands and ultimatums (“you don’t get to delay or negotiate,” “no more excuses”). The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s own reasons for change but instead imposes a rigid agenda.  

**Summary:**  
The session is directive and controlling, lacking the collaborative, autonomy-supportive spirit essential to MI.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient does express some tentative change talk (“I can quit,” “maybe I can pick a day”), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on pushing the patient to commit immediately, which appears to provoke resistance rather than deepen motivation. There are no strategic reflections or amplifications of change talk; the psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore the patient’s own reasons or confidence. The approach tends to shut down ambivalence rather than work with it.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not effectively cultivated or amplified; the psychiatrist’s approach may inhibit further exploration of motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s reasons for continuing smoking, stress coping, desire for control) is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels patient statements as “excuses” and insists on immediate action, which likely increases resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s ambivalence. The psychiatrist fails to normalize the difficulty of quitting or validate the patient’s experience, missing critical opportunities to reduce sustain talk’s impact.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced or ignored rather than softened; the psychiatrist’s responses exacerbate resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands, ultimatums, and a rigid agenda. The patient’s input is minimized, and their autonomy is overridden by the psychiatrist’s insistence on immediate quitting and use of nicotine replacement. There is no negotiation or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s concerns and preferences, undermining any sense of equality or mutual respect.  

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance that marginalizes the patient’s voice.

---

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently emphasizes the health risks of smoking, which is factually accurate.  
- The psychiatrist attempts to secure a quit date, which is a relevant clinical goal.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is overly directive, confrontational, and lacks empathy.  
- There is no evidence of MI spirit—no collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and sustain talk are not acknowledged or softened, leading to resistance.  
- Change talk is not effectively cultivated or reinforced.  
- Partnership and acceptance are minimal, undermining rapport and engagement.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from authoritarian commands to partnership. Invite the patient’s perspective and preferences. Use inclusive language and avoid ultimatums.

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   Validate the patient’s feelings, struggles, and ambivalence. Normalize the difficulty of quitting and affirm the patient’s efforts and strengths.

3. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   Employ simple and complex reflections to demonstrate understanding and encourage deeper exploration of ambivalence and motivation.

4. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   Ask open-ended questions that invite the patient to articulate their own reasons, desires, and confidence for change. Amplify and reinforce change talk when it arises.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and affirmations to acknowledge sustain talk without reinforcing it. Help the patient resolve ambivalence gently.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Respect the patient’s right to choose the timing and manner of quitting. Avoid pressuring or coercing immediate action.

7. **Incorporate Affirmations and Summaries:**  
   Recognize patient strengths and progress. Summarize key points to consolidate motivation and plan next steps collaboratively.

8. **Use Open Questions More Effectively:**  
   Replace leading or closed questions with open-ended ones that encourage elaboration and self-reflection.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s approach is directive, confrontational, and lacking in empathy, acceptance, and collaboration. The patient’s ambivalence and sustain talk are met with resistance rather than skillful softening, and change talk is not effectively cultivated. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt the MI spirit, deepen reflective listening, and support patient autonomy to foster genuine motivation and engagement in smoking cessation.
