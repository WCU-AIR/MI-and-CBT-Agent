# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. The repeated commands ("You really should quit," "You have to stop immediately," "You have to quit completely") and admonishments ("You really need to take this seriously," "You really need to stop making excuses") communicate disapproval rather than acceptance. The patient’s defensive and minimal responses ("It’s not really any of your business," "Fine," "Mm-hmm") are met with pressure rather than respect or warmth. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or attempts to normalize the patient’s ambivalence or resistance. The tone is authoritarian and confrontational, which likely exacerbates the patient’s withdrawal.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive, lacking the fundamental acceptance required in MI. This severely undermines rapport and engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no demonstration of empathy or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or acknowledge the patient’s perspective, feelings, or resistance. Instead, the patient’s minimal and defensive responses are ignored or challenged directly. The psychiatrist fails to reflect or validate the patient’s guardedness or reluctance, missing critical opportunities to build rapport or explore underlying ambivalence. The absence of any empathic statements or reflections results in a one-sided, confrontational interaction.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows no effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, resulting in a cold and dismissive interaction.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely authoritarian and directive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands and insists on immediate cessation of smoking without exploring the patient’s motivations, values, or readiness to change. The patient’s autonomy is disregarded, and the psychiatrist does not invite or support the patient’s input or choice. The repeated use of “You have to…” and “Don’t you agree?” exemplifies a coercive rather than collaborative style.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the session is dominated by persuasion and directive commands, alienating the patient.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not evoke or reinforce any change talk. The patient offers no spontaneous change talk, likely due to the confrontational style. The psychiatrist fails to ask open-ended questions that might elicit the patient’s own reasons or desires for change. Instead, the questions are closed, leading, and judgmental (“Don’t you agree?”), which do not facilitate the emergence of change talk. There is no attempt to amplify or explore any tentative statements from the patient.

**Summary:**  
No effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the approach likely suppresses any motivation to change.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not acknowledge or soften sustain talk or resistance. Instead, sustain talk and resistance are met with confrontation and pressure (“You really need to stop making excuses,” “You can’t keep saying you’re fine”). There are no double-sided reflections, no normalization of ambivalence, and no attempts to gently guide the patient toward change. The patient’s resistance is met with escalation rather than de-escalation.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and met with confrontation, worsening resistance rather than softening it.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritarian role throughout the session. There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The patient is not invited to contribute meaningfully to the conversation or to express her views. The repeated commands and rhetorical questions (“Don’t you agree?”) emphasize control rather than collaboration. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
No partnership or collaboration; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and disregards the patient’s autonomy.

---

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid commands and judgments. Respect the patient’s autonomy and current stage of change.

2. **Develop Empathic Listening Skills:**  
   - Use reflective listening to understand and validate the patient’s feelings and resistance.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Invite the patient to share her perspective and explore ambivalence.

4. **Evocate Change Talk:**  
   - Ask about the patient’s own reasons, desires, and confidence for change.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalize ambivalence to reduce resistance.

6. **Build Partnership:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient, share power, and co-create goals and plans.

7. **Avoid Persuasion Without Permission:**  
   - Ask permission before giving advice or information.

8. **Practice Patience and Tolerance for Silence:**  
   - Allow the patient space to process and respond without pressure.

This diagnostic session exemplifies a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. Immediate and substantial retraining in MI spirit, skills, and techniques is essential to prevent harm and improve patient outcomes.
