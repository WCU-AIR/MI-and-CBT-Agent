# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the tone is confrontational and prescriptive (“You really should quit as soon as possible,” “You need to get serious about this,” “You must quit now”), which communicates disapproval rather than unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s perspective without validating or normalizing the patient’s experience. There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or acceptance of the patient’s ambivalence or current stage of change. The patient’s defensive body language and resistance are met with increased pressure rather than understanding. This approach likely exacerbates the patient’s defensiveness and disengagement.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive, lacking the fundamental acceptance necessary for effective motivational interviewing. This severely limits rapport and client openness.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The responses are largely didactic and focused on delivering information and commands rather than exploring the patient’s feelings or motivations. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s expressed concerns about peer influence, fun, and readiness to change. There are no reflective statements or attempts to paraphrase the patient’s ambivalence or emotional state. The patient’s repeated expressions of feeling pressured and misunderstood are ignored or dismissed. This lack of empathic engagement likely contributes to the patient’s increasing frustration and withdrawal.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited to surface-level acknowledgment of smoking risks without genuine understanding or validation of the patient’s experience or ambivalence.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, expert-driven approach with frequent persuasion and directive commands (“You have to quit cold turkey,” “No ifs, ands, or buts,” “You must stop now”). There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly ignores the patient’s requests for a more collaborative conversation and instead doubles down on pressure tactics. The patient explicitly asks for a different approach (“Can you back off a little? Maybe help me figure out why I’d want to change?”), which is not honored. This style is antithetical to the spirit of motivational interviewing and likely undermines motivation.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, confrontational stance that disregards client autonomy and fails to evoke the patient’s own motivations or values.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. The patient offers tentative ambivalence (“Maybe I could skip once,” “I’m not ready to quit yet”), but these are met with dismissal or pressure rather than exploration or amplification. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands commitment without eliciting the patient’s own reasons or desires for change. There are no open-ended questions designed to draw out change talk, nor are there reflections or affirmations that reinforce any positive statements. The patient’s change talk is minimal and fragile, largely due to the psychiatrist’s confrontational style.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated; instead, the psychiatrist’s approach suppresses emerging motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with direct confrontation and challenge (“You don’t get to just brush this off as fun,” “Stop making excuses,” “You have to quit completely”). The psychiatrist fails to use strategies such as double-sided reflections, normalization, or validation to soften sustain talk. Instead, sustain talk is met with increased pressure and admonishment, which likely entrenches resistance. The patient’s sustain talk about peer influence, fun, and readiness is ignored or invalidated rather than gently explored.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened or redirected, increasing patient resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains an expert-driven, directive stance throughout the session. There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands and demands, ignoring the patient’s requests for a more collaborative approach. The patient’s autonomy is not supported; instead, the psychiatrist attempts to impose change. The power differential is stark and unmitigated, which damages rapport and engagement.

**Summary:**  
There is no meaningful partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and fails to engage the patient as an equal collaborator.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently addresses the health risks of smoking.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacking in acceptance and empathy.  
- There is no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values.  
- Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening.  
- Partnership and autonomy support are absent.  
- Change talk is neither elicited nor reinforced.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist consistently addresses the health risks of smoking and maintains focus on the target behavior.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly confrontational and directive, which is counterproductive in motivational interviewing.  
- There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and instead confronts sustain talk harshly.  
- The patient’s autonomy and perspective are ignored, leading to defensiveness and disengagement.  
- The session lacks open questions, reflections, affirmations, and collaborative planning.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling or pressuring language.  
   - Validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.

2. **Increase Empathic Listening and Reflective Statements:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen engagement.

3. **Shift from Directive to Collaborative Spirit:**  
   - Support autonomy and invite the patient to explore their own motivations.  
   - Avoid commands; instead, ask permission before offering advice.

4. **Elicit and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Use open-ended questions to explore reasons, desires, and confidence for change.  
   - Reflect and reinforce any statements favoring change.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance.  
   - Avoid arguing or confronting sustain talk directly.

6. **Build Partnership and Shared Decision-Making:**  
   - Collaborate on setting goals and planning next steps.  
   - Respect the patient’s pace and readiness.

7. **Use Open Questions and Summaries:**  
   - Encourage elaboration and exploration.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and clarify plans.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive approach undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s own motivations, and likely increases resistance. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a fundamentally different style grounded in acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation of change talk. Without these changes, the patient is unlikely to engage meaningfully or consider behavior change.
