# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive rather than accepting. The tone is confrontational, with repeated accusations of “making excuses” and “not being serious,” which undermines unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and struggles, labeling coping mechanisms as “crutches” and “half-measures.” There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or validation of the patient’s experience. The patient’s resistance and guardedness reflect this lack of acceptance.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate respect or unconditional positive regard. The stance is largely disapproving and directive, which likely increases patient resistance.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s expressions of stress, ambivalence, and readiness, instead pushing a rigid agenda. No reflective listening or paraphrasing is evident. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the emotional difficulty of quitting or the patient’s reasons for smoking (stress relief). The patient’s frustration and resistance are met with repeated challenges rather than empathy.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates little to no empathy, failing to connect with or validate the patient’s feelings or perspective.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with no collaboration or evocation of the patient’s own motivations. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands commitment to quitting “today” and dismisses any ambivalence or partial efforts. Autonomy is not supported; instead, the patient is pressured and challenged. The psychiatrist’s language (“You have to be tougher,” “stop making excuses”) is coercive and controlling, contrary to MI spirit.  

**Summary:**  
The session lacks collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support, reflecting an authoritarian, directive approach.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not evoke or reinforce any change talk. When the patient tentatively suggests cutting down or using patches, the psychiatrist dismisses these as “half-measures” and does not explore or amplify these statements. There is no strategic elicitation of desire, ability, reasons, or need for change. The patient’s change talk is minimal and unsupported, leading to its quick dismissal.  

**Summary:**  
No effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk; patient’s tentative statements are ignored or invalidated.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and challenge rather than softening or reflective strategies. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s reasons for smoking as “excuses” and “crutches,” which likely increases resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. The patient’s sustain talk is met with pressure and demands, which is counterproductive.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and escalated rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritarian role, giving directives and demanding compliance. There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The patient is repeatedly asked to commit without negotiation or exploration of readiness. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or acknowledge their expertise in their own experience.  

**Summary:**  
No partnership or collaboration; the session is expert-driven and directive.

### Areas for Improvement

- There is no empathy, acceptance, or partnership.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling or confronting the patient.  
   - Express genuine respect and positive regard for the patient’s experience.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Validate the difficulty of quitting and the patient’s ambivalence.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy and choice.

4. **Elicit and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient mentions cutting down or using patches, explore and reinforce these statements.  
   - Ask open-ended questions to deepen motivation.

5. **Softly Handle Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Normalize the struggle and validate the patient’s feelings.

6. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Explore feasible next steps with the patient.  
   - Summarize motivation and commitment to reinforce progress.

7. **Avoid Authoritarian Language:**  
   - Replace “You have to” and “Are you going to commit today?” with invitational, autonomy-supportive language.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamentally flawed approach to motivational interviewing. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive style undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s motivations, and likely increases resistance. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt MI principles of acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation, and skillfully handle ambivalence to support meaningful behavior change.
