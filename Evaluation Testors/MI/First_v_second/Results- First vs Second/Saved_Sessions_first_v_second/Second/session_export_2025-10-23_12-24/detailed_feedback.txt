# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach throughout the session is judgmental, directive, and confrontational rather than accepting. Statements such as “You really should quit smoking,” “You need to take this seriously,” and “You’re just making excuses” communicate disapproval and lack of unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s expressed ambivalence and concerns about stress, labeling them as “excuses” or “overthinking.” There is no evidence of warmth, respect, or validation of Luka’s experience. The patient’s cautious and reflective tone is met with pressure and criticism rather than empathy or support.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance, instead adopting a harsh, judgmental stance that likely undermines rapport and patient openness.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect Luka’s perspective. The patient repeatedly expresses that stress is a significant barrier and that he is working on managing it, yet the psychiatrist dismisses this repeatedly (“Stress is no excuse,” “You have to stop making excuses”). There are no reflective statements or attempts to paraphrase or validate the patient’s feelings or struggles. The psychiatrist’s responses are primarily confrontational questions and commands, showing little to no perspective-taking or emotional attunement.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is essentially absent; the psychiatrist does not demonstrate understanding or convey that Luka’s experience is heard or valued.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with the psychiatrist repeatedly telling Luka what he “must” do and pressing for immediate commitment. There is no collaboration or evocation of Luka’s own motivations or values. Autonomy is not supported; instead, the psychiatrist attempts to coerce compliance (“You must commit now,” “No more half-measures”). The patient’s autonomy is ignored, and the psychiatrist does not invite or explore Luka’s readiness or ambivalence in a respectful manner.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is completely lacking; the session is dominated by control, persuasion, and disregard for patient autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While Luka expresses some desire and reasons for change (“I want to get healthier,” “I’m working on cutting back”), the psychiatrist ignores these and instead focuses on pressuring for immediate quitting and quit dates. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify Luka’s change talk. The psychiatrist’s confrontational style likely suppresses further change talk rather than cultivating it.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is neither effectively evoked nor reinforced; the psychiatrist misses opportunities to build motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk related to stress and readiness is met with confrontation and dismissal (“Stress is just an excuse,” “You’re making excuses”). The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or validate the difficulty of change. Instead, sustain talk is challenged aggressively, which likely increases resistance rather than softening it.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than skillfully softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands, accusations, and pressure. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for Luka’s expertise in his own life. The psychiatrist repeatedly asks “Why haven’t you done that?” in a challenging tone rather than inviting dialogue. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.  
- Empathy and reflective listening are essential to understand and validate patient experience.  
- Collaboration and autonomy support must replace coercion and pressure.  
- Change talk should be evoked and reinforced, not ignored or suppressed.  
- Sustain talk requires skillful softening, not confrontation.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly confrontational, judgmental, and directive, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and concerns about stress are dismissed rather than explored.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.  
- Autonomy and partnership are ignored, replaced by pressure and demands.  
- The session lacks affirmations, open questions, and collaborative planning.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses” or “overthinking.”  
   - Express genuine respect and positive regard for the patient’s experience.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Validate the difficulty of managing stress and ambivalence.

3. **Support Autonomy and Collaboration:**  
   - Invite the patient to share their perspective and preferences.  
   - Avoid commands; instead, offer options and ask permission before advice.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and explore these further.  
   - Ask open-ended questions that invite elaboration on motivations.

5. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Normalize the struggle and validate the patient’s feelings.

6. **Develop Collaborative Planning:**  
   - Work with the patient to set realistic, patient-driven goals.  
   - Summarize progress and next steps collaboratively.

7. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Replace confrontational questions with open-ended ones that invite dialogue.  
   - Affirm patient strengths, efforts, and intentions sincerely.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, judgmental, and directive approach is likely to increase patient resistance and reduce motivation. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and skillful evocation of change talk. Without these changes, the risk of harming the therapeutic relationship and undermining patient progress is high.
