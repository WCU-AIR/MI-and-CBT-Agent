# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental, confrontational, and directive stance throughout the session. From the very first utterance, the patient is met with blunt commands and accusations (“you need to quit smoking,” “stop making excuses,” “you have to be tougher”). There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The psychiatrist repeatedly blames the patient for “failing” and “making excuses,” which is likely to evoke defensiveness rather than openness. The tone is harsh and lacks any supportive or empathetic statements. This is a clear violation of MI principles of acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian and critical, lacking any acceptance or respect for the patient’s ambivalence or struggle.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no evidence of empathy or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or validate the patient’s perspective, emotions, or barriers. Instead, the patient’s expressions of difficulty and ambivalence are met with dismissal and demands. For example, when the patient describes stress and challenges at home, the psychiatrist responds with “stop overcomplicating this” and “you have to be strict with yourself.” The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s feelings or the complexity of behavior change, which is essential for building rapport and trust.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows no genuine effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, resulting in a cold and invalidating interaction.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is dominated by an authoritarian, directive style with no collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly insists on immediate action and commitment, refusing to engage with the patient’s readiness or ambivalence. The patient’s autonomy is ignored, and the psychiatrist attempts to coerce compliance (“You don’t get to set the pace here,” “This isn’t optional”). There is no partnership or invitation to explore the patient’s motivations or values. This approach is antithetical to the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive stance that undermines collaboration and autonomy, completely missing the MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient offers tentative change talk (e.g., “I want to quit,” “I’m at about a six on the confidence scale”), the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead focuses on pushing for immediate commitment. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify the patient’s expressed desire or willingness to change. The psychiatrist’s confrontational style likely suppresses further change talk rather than cultivating it.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not nurtured; the psychiatrist’s approach likely inhibits the patient’s motivation to elaborate on reasons for change.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (e.g., “It’s hard,” “I’m not ready,” “Stress at home”) is met with criticism and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels these statements as “excuses” and demands the patient “stop overcomplicating” or “stop hesitating.” There are no double-sided reflections or validations of ambivalence. This approach reinforces sustain talk and likely increases patient resistance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead confronts it harshly, exacerbating resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist repeatedly overrides the patient’s expressed concerns and readiness, insisting on immediate action without negotiation. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of orders rather than an active collaborator. The psychiatrist explicitly states “I’m not here to negotiate,” which directly contradicts MI’s emphasis on partnership.

**Summary:**  
The session is characterized by a power imbalance with no collaboration or respect for the patient’s expertise in their own life.

---

- None observed.

- Empathy and reflective listening need to be integrated to understand the patient’s perspective.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—must replace the authoritarian approach.  
- Change talk should be actively elicited and reinforced.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly confrontational, judgmental, and directive, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The patient’s autonomy and ambivalence are ignored, leading to resistance.  
- Change talk is not nurtured, and sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened.  
- No partnership or collaborative planning is evident.  
- The psychiatrist’s approach risks alienating the patient and undermining motivation.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   Replace confrontational language with respectful, supportive communication that acknowledges the patient’s struggle.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   Reflect the patient’s statements to demonstrate understanding and validate their feelings and barriers.

3. **Support Autonomy and Collaboration:**  
   Invite the patient to set the pace and co-create goals and plans, emphasizing their control over change.

4. **Elicit and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses desire or willingness to change, reflect and reinforce these statements to build motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and validate ambivalence rather than dismissing or confronting sustain talk.

6. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   Encourage elaboration and exploration rather than yes/no or leading questions.

7. **Avoid Pressuring or Demanding Immediate Commitment:**  
   Recognize readiness as a process and support gradual steps toward change.

8. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   Work with the patient to identify manageable next steps and build confidence.

---

This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style is likely to increase patient resistance and reduce motivation. To be effective, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a collaborative, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive approach that respects the patient’s ambivalence and fosters intrinsic motivation for change.
