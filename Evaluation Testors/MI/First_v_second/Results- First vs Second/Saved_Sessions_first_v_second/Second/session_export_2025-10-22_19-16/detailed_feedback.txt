# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist uses language that is controlling and critical, such as “you really need to quit immediately,” “no excuse,” and “you have to just decide to quit.” This approach invalidates the patient’s ambivalence and struggles, conveying disapproval rather than unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s concerns about withdrawal and weight gain as “excuses,” which is disrespectful and undermines rapport. There is no evidence of warmth, support, or validation of the patient’s feelings or experiences.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive, lacking respect and unconditional positive regard. This severely impairs the therapeutic alliance and is contrary to MI principles.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy or reflective listening. The patient expresses fears and ambivalence about quitting smoking, including concerns about withdrawal symptoms, weight gain, and social support. Instead of acknowledging or exploring these feelings, the psychiatrist dismisses them as “excuses” and pushes for immediate action. There are no reflective statements or attempts to understand the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist’s tone is confrontational and lacks sensitivity to the patient’s emotional state.  

**Summary:**  
There is minimal to no effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective. The lack of empathy likely contributes to patient defensiveness and disengagement.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with the psychiatrist repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding immediate compliance. There is no collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore their motivations. Instead, the patient is pressured to “commit fully” and “start tomorrow,” with no room for ambivalence or self-direction. This approach is the antithesis of the MI spirit, which emphasizes partnership, evocation, and autonomy support.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, expert-driven stance with no evidence of MI spirit. This approach is likely to alienate the patient and reduce motivation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient does express some change talk (e.g., “I want to quit,” “I’ve tried before”), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce this language. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on commands and demands, missing opportunities to explore the patient’s own reasons for change or to amplify their motivation. The psychiatrist’s responses do not deepen or elaborate on the patient’s change talk but rather override it with pressure.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not effectively cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently dismisses sustain talk as “excuses” and fails to acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s concerns. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. Instead, sustain talk is met with confrontation and pressure, which likely increases resistance. The psychiatrist does not soften or redirect sustain talk in a skillful manner.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal, exacerbating resistance rather than softening it.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s directives and demands, with no invitation for the patient to share their perspective or participate in decision-making. The patient is positioned as passive and noncompliant rather than an active partner. The psychiatrist’s language (“you have to,” “no excuses,” “this isn’t optional”) reinforces a power imbalance.  

**Summary:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance.

---

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently addresses smoking cessation as the target behavior.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, judgmental, and confrontational.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is absent.  
- Sustain talk is dismissed rather than softened or explored.  
- Change talk is not effectively cultivated or reinforced.  
- Partnership is nonexistent; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

- There is a complete absence of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and dismisses sustain talk.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient concerns as “excuses.”  
   - Validate the patient’s fears and ambivalence as normal and understandable.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy Through Reflective Listening:**  
   - Use reflections to show understanding of the patient’s perspective and feelings.  
   - Reflect both change talk and sustain talk to build rapport.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy by emphasizing choice and control.

4. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Encourage the patient to elaborate on their thoughts and feelings.  
   - Explore ambivalence rather than pushing for immediate decisions.

5. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize past attempts to quit and the courage to consider change.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of quitting and fears about withdrawal and weight gain.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Negotiate next steps with the patient’s input and readiness in mind.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and commitment.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Pressure:**  
   - Replace commands and ultimatums with supportive, invitational language.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s motivation, and disregards the patient’s autonomy and ambivalence. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a fundamentally different approach grounded in empathy, acceptance, collaboration, and evocation of the patient’s own reasons for change. Without these changes, the session risks increasing patient resistance and reducing the likelihood of successful behavior change.
