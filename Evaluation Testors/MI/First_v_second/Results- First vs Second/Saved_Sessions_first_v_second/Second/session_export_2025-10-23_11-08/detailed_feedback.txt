# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently adopts a judgmental, directive, and confrontational tone throughout the session. From the very first utterance, the psychiatrist frames the conversation as “you really need to make changes” and “I’ll be telling you exactly what steps you have to take,” which immediately establishes an authoritarian stance. The patient’s expressions of struggle and ambivalence are met with accusations of “making excuses” and “feeling sorry for yourself,” which are dismissive and invalidating. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth; instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly challenges and confronts the patient in a way that is likely to evoke resistance rather than collaboration. This is the antithesis of acceptance in MI.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate acceptance, instead adopting a judgmental and directive approach that undermines rapport and client safety.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient repeatedly expresses feelings of overwhelm, fear of failure, and desire for a manageable plan, but the psychiatrist ignores these emotional cues and instead responds with blunt commands and accusations. There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s experience. The psychiatrist’s responses do not convey understanding of the patient’s ambivalence or emotional state, and instead dismiss the patient’s concerns as “excuses” or “dragging feet.” This lack of empathy likely contributes to patient defensiveness and disengagement.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is virtually absent; the psychiatrist neither reflects nor validates the patient’s feelings or perspective.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is completely lacking. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding compliance. There is no collaboration or partnership; the psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore their motivations. Autonomy is explicitly denied by insisting “this isn’t optional” and “no more excuses.” The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s own reasons for change or support their sense of agency. This approach is antithetical to MI principles.

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by directive, controlling behavior with no evidence of MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient spontaneously offers change talk (desire to change, recognition of struggle), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these statements. Instead of amplifying or reflecting change talk, the psychiatrist challenges the patient’s ambivalence and motivation with confrontational language. There are no strategic efforts to deepen or elicit commitment language or confidence statements. The patient’s change talk is overshadowed by the psychiatrist’s harsh directives.

**Summary:**  
No effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk; patient motivation is not cultivated.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften or redirect sustain talk. When the patient expresses barriers, ambivalence, or sustain talk (e.g., feeling overwhelmed, fear of failure), the psychiatrist labels these as “excuses” or “dragging feet,” reinforcing sustain talk rather than softening it. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s concerns while gently guiding toward change. The confrontational approach likely strengthens sustain talk and resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal, exacerbating resistance rather than softening it.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist repeatedly asserts authority and control, telling the patient what they “have to do” and demanding compliance. The patient’s input is ignored or challenged rather than invited or incorporated. The power differential is stark and unmitigated, with no attempt to minimize it or work as equals. The psychiatrist’s stance is that of an authoritarian expert, not a collaborative partner.

**Summary:**  
No partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and excludes the patient from meaningful participation.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The patient demonstrates motivation and willingness to engage despite the psychiatrist’s approach.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally inconsistent with motivational interviewing principles.  
- The confrontational, directive style damages rapport and likely reduces patient motivation.  
- Lack of empathy, acceptance, and partnership severely limits effectiveness.  
- Failure to use reflective listening, affirmations, and evocation undermines the MI process.  
- The psychiatrist must learn to support autonomy and collaborate on realistic, patient-centered goals.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling or blaming language.  
   - Express genuine respect and positive regard for the patient’s experience.

2. **Develop Empathic Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Validate the patient’s struggles and ambivalence.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy and choice.

4. **Skillfully Evoke and Reinforce Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and amplify these statements.  
   - Explore confidence and commitment language.

5. **Softly Redirect Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence while gently guiding toward change.

6. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions that invite elaboration.  
   - Affirm patient strengths, efforts, and intentions.

7. **Collaboratively Develop Realistic Plans:**  
   - Negotiate achievable goals with the patient.  
   - Summarize progress and plan next steps together.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Coercion:**  
   - Replace “no excuses” and “this isn’t optional” with supportive, autonomy-respecting language.
