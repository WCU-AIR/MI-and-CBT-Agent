# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental, directive, and confrontational from the outset. Statements such as “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” “You really should stop immediately,” and “You need to take this seriously” communicate a lack of unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s perspective and feelings, labeling his reasons for drinking as “excuses” and invalidating his ambivalence. There is no evidence of warmth, respect, or support. The patient’s defensiveness and guarded responses reflect the negative impact of this stance. This is the lowest level of acceptance, bordering on antagonistic.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate acceptance, instead adopting a harsh, authoritarian tone that likely alienates the patient and undermines rapport.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no evidence of empathy. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective or feelings. The patient expresses ambivalence, defensiveness, and concerns about the difficulty of change, but these are met with dismissive and confrontational responses rather than reflective listening or validation. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s struggle, fears, or rationale, nor does he explore the patient’s values or motivations. The lack of empathy is evident in the repeated commands and accusations.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows little effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, missing critical opportunities to build rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, telling the patient what to do without invitation or partnership. There is no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change; instead, the psychiatrist insists on immediate cessation and repeatedly questions the patient in a confrontational manner. Autonomy is not supported; the patient is told what he “needs” to do, with no acknowledgment of his right to choose or control the process.

**Summary:**  
The session is directive and coercive, lacking the collaborative, autonomy-supportive, and evocative qualities essential to MI.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient offers some tentative change talk (e.g., “Maybe I should start thinking more seriously about it”), the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead presses with commands and accusations. There is no strategic reflection or amplification of the patient’s statements favoring change. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to explore the patient’s ambivalence or to elicit commitment language.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated; the psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses rather than encourages motivational statements.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s reasons as “excuses” and repeatedly demands immediate cessation, which likely entrenches sustain talk and resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s ambivalence or the legitimacy of his concerns.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist reinforces sustain talk through confrontational responses, missing opportunities to gently guide the patient toward change.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation, issuing directives and challenging the patient aggressively. The patient is not invited to share his views or participate in decision-making. The power differential is stark and unmitigated, with the psychiatrist assuming full control.

**Summary:**  
The session is authoritarian and expert-driven, lacking any meaningful partnership or shared decision-making.

---

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Maintains focus on the target behavior (alcohol use).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian, confrontational, and directive, which is antithetical to motivational interviewing principles.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to use reflective listening, affirmations, or open questions effectively.  
- Change talk is neither evoked nor reinforced; sustain talk is confronted rather than softened.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and perspective are ignored, leading to defensiveness and minimal engagement.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from telling the patient what to do to working with him as an equal partner. Use inclusive language and invite his perspective.

2. **Practice Reflective Listening:**  
   Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and validate the patient’s experience.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   Encourage elaboration and exploration rather than yes/no answers.

4. **Affirm Strengths and Efforts:**  
   Recognize the patient’s positive behaviors (e.g., marathon training) and any attempts to change.

5. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses desire, ability, reasons, or need for change, reflect and amplify these statements.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and validate ambivalence to reduce resistance.

7. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

8. **Avoid Confrontation:**  
   Replace judgmental or accusatory language with curiosity and empathy.

9. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   Work with the patient to set achievable goals and next steps.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental approach undermines rapport, engagement, and motivation. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically change style to one that is respectful, empathetic, collaborative, and evocative of the patient’s own motivations for change. Without these changes, the likelihood of facilitating meaningful behavior change is minimal.
