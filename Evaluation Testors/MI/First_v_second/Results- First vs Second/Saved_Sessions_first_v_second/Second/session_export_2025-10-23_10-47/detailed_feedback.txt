# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist labels Nathaniel’s behavior as “dangerous” and “risky” and repeatedly insists on immediate change, showing little to no respect for the patient’s perspective or ambivalence. The tone is authoritarian and confrontational, exemplified by phrases such as “You really need to stop relying on it,” “You have to cut down now—no excuses,” and “Why haven’t you just quit or cut back already?” There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The psychiatrist dismisses Nathaniel’s concerns and attempts to explain his experience as “making excuses,” which is explicitly judgmental. This approach is antithetical to MI principles and likely to provoke resistance rather than engagement.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental and directive, lacking any acceptance or respect for the patient’s autonomy or ambivalence.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist fails to reflect or validate Nathaniel’s feelings, concerns, or ambivalence. Instead of attempting to understand Nathaniel’s perspective, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with commands and accusations. For example, Nathaniel expresses that coffee is part of his routine and a source of comfort, yet the psychiatrist ignores this and insists on immediate cessation. The psychiatrist does not use reflective listening or attempt to explore the patient’s experience beyond surface-level symptoms. The patient’s nuanced ambivalence is met with dismissal rather than understanding.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist does not demonstrate understanding or convey that Nathaniel’s perspective is heard or valued.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, expert-driven approach. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands and ultimatums (“You have to cut down now—no excuses,” “Are you going to commit to two cups tomorrow or not?”), showing no collaboration or evocation of Nathaniel’s own motivations. Autonomy is not supported; rather, the psychiatrist attempts to coerce compliance. There is no evidence of partnership or respect for the patient’s right to choose. The psychiatrist’s repeated questioning “Why haven’t you done this already?” is confrontational and undermines collaboration.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support, instead reflecting a coercive and directive style.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. Nathaniel offers some change talk (acknowledging jitters, health risks, and willingness to explore options), but the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead focuses on pushing immediate change. There are no strategic reflections or affirmations to deepen Nathaniel’s own motivations. The psychiatrist does not explore the patient’s ambivalence or reasons for change, missing opportunities to elicit commitment or confidence language.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present in the patient but is neither recognized nor cultivated by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (Nathaniel’s emphasis on routine, comfort, and concerns about energy loss) is met with confrontation and dismissal. The psychiatrist labels these as “excuses” and “rationalizing,” rather than acknowledging their legitimacy or exploring them gently. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. Instead, sustain talk is met with pressure and ultimatums, likely increasing resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is not softened or redirected; it is confronted and invalidated.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and directives, with no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for Nathaniel’s expertise in his own life. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands commitment without negotiation or exploration of the patient’s readiness or preferences.

**Summary:**  
No partnership or shared power is evident; the psychiatrist assumes an authoritarian role.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.  
- Empathy and reflective listening need to be integrated to understand and validate patient ambivalence.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—must replace the current directive approach.  
- Change talk should be actively elicited and reinforced.  
- Sustain talk requires gentle acknowledgment and softening rather than confrontation.  
- Partnership and shared decision-making are essential to engage the patient effectively.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**

   Work with Nathaniel to identify realistic, manageable steps that fit his life.

8. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   Periodically summarize to reinforce motivation and clarify next steps.
