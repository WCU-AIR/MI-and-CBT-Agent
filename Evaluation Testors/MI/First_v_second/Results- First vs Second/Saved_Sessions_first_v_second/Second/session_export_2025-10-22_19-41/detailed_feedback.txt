# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive from the outset. The opening statement, “you need to quit smoking,” is authoritative and lacks warmth or empathy. The tone throughout is confrontational, with phrases like “you’re just digging your own grave” and “no more excuses,” which communicate disapproval rather than unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s ambivalence or struggles in a supportive way. There is no normalization of difficulty or validation of the patient’s current coping mechanisms. The patient’s attempts to express her priorities and ambivalence are met with dismissal and pressure rather than acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance, lacking the respectful, supportive tone essential for MI. This risks alienating the patient and undermining rapport.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient clearly expresses that smoking is a lower priority compared to gambling and debt, and that smoking serves as stress relief. Instead of reflecting or exploring these feelings, the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses them as “excuses.” There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s emotional experience or ambivalence. The patient’s humor, nervousness, and conflicted feelings are ignored rather than acknowledged. The psychiatrist’s responses are mostly confrontational and directive, which do not convey empathy or understanding.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist does not demonstrate accurate understanding or convey the patient’s perspective, missing opportunities to build rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, expert-driven style. The psychiatrist repeatedly tells the patient what she “needs” to do, uses threats (“digging your own grave”), and pressures for immediate commitment without collaboration. There is no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values, no support for autonomy, and no partnership. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s expressed ambivalence and instead pushes a rigid agenda. This approach is antithetical to the MI spirit, which emphasizes collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s style is directive and coercive, lacking the fundamental MI spirit of partnership and evocation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist attempts to elicit commitment to quit smoking but does so through pressure and confrontation rather than skillful evocation. There are no open-ended questions designed to explore the patient’s own reasons for change or to amplify existing change talk. The patient offers tentative statements about readiness (“maybe it’s worth a try,” “I’m ready to stop the excuses”), but the psychiatrist does not reflect or reinforce these. Instead, the psychiatrist pushes for immediate action without exploring the patient’s ambivalence or motivations in depth.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and not skillfully cultivated; the approach risks eliciting resistance rather than motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal. The patient’s explanations for delaying quitting (stress relief, gambling problems, fear of failure) are labeled as “excuses” and met with threats and pressure. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s sustain talk or ambivalence, which is critical to softening resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced rather than softened, increasing the likelihood of patient resistance and disengagement.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritative role throughout the session. There is no evidence of collaboration or shared decision-making. The patient’s input is largely ignored or dismissed. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands immediate commitment and compliance, undermining the patient’s autonomy. The language used (“you need to,” “no more excuses,” “you have to”) is directive and controlling.

**Summary:**  
There is no meaningful partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and fails to engage the patient as an equal collaborator.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the health risks of smoking and attempts to move toward action.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The approach is highly confrontational and directive, lacking acceptance, empathy, and MI spirit.  
- The psychiatrist fails to explore or validate the patient’s ambivalence and sustain talk.  
- Change talk is not skillfully evoked or reinforced.  
- Partnership and autonomy support are absent, replaced by coercion and pressure.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directive commands to partnership language. Invite the patient’s perspective and co-create goals.

2. **Enhance Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence. Avoid judgmental language.

3. **Skillfully Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - Use open-ended questions and reflections to draw out the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.

5. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

6. **Use Affirmations:**  
   - Recognize and affirm the patient’s strengths, efforts, and insights to build confidence.

7. **Collaborative Planning:**  
   - Develop a concrete, realistic plan together, checking for readiness and commitment.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Pressure:**  
   - Replace threats and ultimatums with supportive, motivational dialogue.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a poor application of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive style undermines rapport, acceptance, empathy, and collaboration. The patient’s ambivalence and sustain talk are dismissed rather than explored, and change talk is not effectively evoked or reinforced. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a fundamentally different approach grounded in MI spirit, reflective listening, and patient-centered collaboration. Without these changes, the risk of patient resistance and disengagement is high, limiting the potential for meaningful behavior change.
