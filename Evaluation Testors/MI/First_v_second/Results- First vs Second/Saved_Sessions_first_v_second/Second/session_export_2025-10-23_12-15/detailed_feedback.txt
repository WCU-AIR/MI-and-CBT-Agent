# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental and directive from the outset. Statements such as “You really need to quit smoking and start exercising,” “You have to do these things,” and “No more excuses” communicate a lack of unconditional positive regard. The tone is authoritarian and dismissive of the patient’s ambivalence or hesitation. There is no evidence of warmth, respect, or support for the patient’s current state or feelings. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s concerns and fears, which likely increases resistance rather than acceptance. This is consistent throughout the session with no instances of genuine acceptance or normalization.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate acceptance, instead adopting a confrontational and judgmental stance that undermines rapport and patient engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no evidence of empathic understanding or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge or validate the patient’s expressed fears, ambivalence, or struggles. For example, when the patient expresses feeling overwhelmed or scared about quitting smoking, the psychiatrist dismisses these feelings as “excuses” or “overthinking.” The patient’s hesitations and emotional cues are ignored or met with pressure rather than empathy. The psychiatrist’s responses are primarily commands or challenges, showing little effort to understand the patient’s perspective or emotional experience.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is virtually absent; the psychiatrist does not demonstrate understanding or validation of the patient’s feelings or ambivalence.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is completely lacking. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, directive stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding commitment without exploring the patient’s motivations or concerns. There is no evocation of the patient’s own reasons for change, no support for autonomy, and no partnership. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues ultimatums (“Are you going to quit this week or not?”) and invalidates the patient’s ambivalence, which is antithetical to MI principles.

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by a controlling, prescriptive approach with no evidence of MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient occasionally expresses tentative change talk (e.g., “Maybe if I tried to cut back first”), the psychiatrist dismisses these statements as “excuses” and demands immediate, total commitment. There is no strategic reflection or amplification of the patient’s own motivations or desires to change. Instead, the psychiatrist’s confrontational style likely suppresses further change talk.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated; the psychiatrist’s approach likely inhibits the patient’s willingness to express or explore motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s ambivalence, fear, and hesitation) is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s fears as “excuses” and repeatedly pressures for immediate action, which likely entrenches sustain talk rather than resolving ambivalence. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s concerns.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and intensified by the psychiatrist’s confrontational responses.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation, issues commands, and repeatedly demands yes/no answers without inviting the patient’s input or perspective. The patient is positioned as passive and subordinate, with no shared decision-making or negotiation. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s expertise in his own life or to co-create a plan.

**Summary:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, lacking any meaningful partnership.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must abandon the confrontational, directive style.  
- Develop skills in acceptance, empathy, and reflective listening.  
- Embrace the MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.  
- Learn to skillfully evoke and reinforce change talk while softening sustain talk.  
- Build genuine partnership with the patient.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on relevant health behaviors (smoking, exercise).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and judgmental, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and instead intensifies sustain talk.  
- The session lacks open questions, reflections, affirmations, and collaborative planning.  
- The patient is positioned as passive and pressured, likely increasing resistance and reducing motivation.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from telling and directing to partnering and exploring.  
   - Use inclusive language and invite the patient’s perspective.

2. **Increase Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.  
   - Use reflective listening to demonstrate understanding.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Encourage the patient to elaborate on thoughts and feelings.  
   - Avoid yes/no questions that pressure for immediate commitment.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Reflect and reinforce any statements favoring change.  
   - Explore the patient’s own reasons, desires, and confidence.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.  
   - Avoid ultimatums and demands.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to set realistic, achievable goals.  
   - Summarize progress and reinforce commitment.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental style undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s motivation, and likely increases resistance. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically change approach to embrace acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and skillful evocation of change talk. Without these changes, the session risks alienating patients and impeding meaningful behavior change.
