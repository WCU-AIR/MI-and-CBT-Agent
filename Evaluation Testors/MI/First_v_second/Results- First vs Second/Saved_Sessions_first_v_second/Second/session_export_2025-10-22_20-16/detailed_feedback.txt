# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently adopts a judgmental, confrontational, and directive stance throughout the session. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s expressions of ambivalence and attempts to explain his perspective, labeling them as “excuses” and “overcomplicating.” For example, statements like “You really need to stop making excuses,” “No, that doesn’t make sense,” and “You don’t get to make excuses about social life” are harsh and invalidating. This approach likely alienates the patient and undermines rapport. There is no normalization or acceptance of the patient’s struggle or ambivalence.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate acceptance, instead adopting a critical and authoritarian tone that is counterproductive to MI principles.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses complex ambivalence about quitting drinking, concerns about social isolation, and readiness to change, but the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these nuances. There are no reflective statements or attempts to paraphrase the patient’s feelings or struggles. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with directives and challenges the patient’s statements without validation. For example, when the patient says, “Maybe I need to figure out a way to balance things,” the psychiatrist responds with “No, you really can’t work on that middle ground,” shutting down exploration. This lack of empathy likely increases patient resistance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist does not demonstrate reflective listening or perspective-taking, missing critical opportunities to build understanding and rapport.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is dominated by an authoritarian, expert-driven approach. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You have to quit completely,” “You need to throw it out immediately,” “You don’t get to make excuses”) and pressures the patient to commit to an all-or-nothing quit plan. There is no collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s ideas or preferences, nor does he acknowledge the patient’s right to make choices. The patient’s ambivalence is met with confrontation rather than exploration. This approach is antithetical to the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to embody the collaborative, autonomy-supportive, and evocative spirit of MI, instead adopting a coercive and directive style.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the psychiatrist repeatedly asks about prior quit attempts and readiness to quit, these questions are framed in a confrontational manner that risks eliciting sustain talk or resistance rather than genuine change talk. The psychiatrist does not strategically reinforce or amplify the patient’s change talk. For example, when the patient expresses desire to get better or acknowledges difficulty, the psychiatrist dismisses these as excuses rather than building on them. There is no effort to explore the patient’s motivations or values in depth. The patient does produce some change talk spontaneously, but the psychiatrist does little to cultivate it.

**Summary:**  
Some attempts to evoke change talk are present but are undermined by confrontational delivery and lack of reinforcement.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently confronts and challenges sustain talk rather than softening or rolling with it. The patient’s justifications for drinking, concerns about social isolation, and ambivalence are met with statements like “You don’t get to make excuses,” “No, small steps won’t work here,” and “You have to stop overcomplicating this.” There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change. This approach likely increases resistance and reduces the patient’s openness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead reinforces resistance through confrontation.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands and demands, with no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s expertise in his own life. The psychiatrist repeatedly overrides the patient’s attempts to express his perspective or negotiate a plan. For example, when the patient suggests exploring reasons for drinking or taking small steps, the psychiatrist dismisses these ideas outright. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance, failing to build a genuine partnership or share power with the patient.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

- The psychiatrist consistently maintains focus on the target behavior (drinking).  
- Attempts to elicit commitment language, albeit in a coercive manner.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly confrontational, lacking acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- There is no evidence of reflective listening or evocation of the patient’s own motivations.  
- Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening or strategic redirection.  
- The spirit of MI is absent, replaced by an authoritarian, directive style.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses.”  
   - Express unconditional positive regard and normalize ambivalence.

2. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen exploration.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Support autonomy by acknowledging the patient’s right to choose.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and values.

4. **Softening Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Avoid confrontation; gently guide toward change.

5. **Use Open Questions to Explore Ambivalence:**  
   - Ask questions that invite elaboration and exploration of pros and cons.

6. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize and reinforce any positive steps or intentions.

7. **Collaborative Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Work with the patient to develop realistic, incremental goals.  
   - Summarize change talk and plan to consolidate motivation.

8. **Avoid Coercive Language:**  
   - Replace commands with invitations and permission-seeking.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental approach is likely to increase patient resistance and reduce motivation for change. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation of the patient’s own motivations. Without these changes, the intervention risks alienating patients and failing to facilitate meaningful behavior change.
