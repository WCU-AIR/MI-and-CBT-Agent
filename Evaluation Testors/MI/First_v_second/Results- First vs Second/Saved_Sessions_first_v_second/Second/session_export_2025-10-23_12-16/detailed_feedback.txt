# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive stance from the outset, repeatedly pressuring the patient to quit smoking immediately and dismissing her expressed concerns and ambivalence. The tone is authoritative and lacks warmth or empathy. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s reasons for not quitting as “excuses” and invalidates her lived experience managing stress and responsibilities. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or respect for the patient’s autonomy. The patient’s attempts to explain her situation are met with resistance and admonishment rather than acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance or respect, instead adopting a confrontational and directive approach that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist does not engage in reflective listening or validate the patient’s feelings about stress, busy life, or readiness to change. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with directives and challenges the patient’s statements without exploring underlying emotions or motivations. The patient’s ambivalence and expressed fears are ignored or dismissed rather than acknowledged.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows little to no empathy, failing to convey understanding or accurately grasp the patient’s perspective.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, expert-driven style with frequent persuasion and directive commands. The psychiatrist repeatedly insists on immediate cessation and use of nicotine patches, disregarding the patient’s autonomy and readiness. There is no collaboration, evocation of the patient’s own motivations, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist’s approach is coercive rather than invitational, which is antithetical to the spirit of motivational interviewing.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian and directive, lacking collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient expresses some change talk (willingness to cut back, concern for health and her son), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these statements and instead pushes for immediate quitting and patch use. There is no amplification or strategic reflection of the patient’s own motivations or tentative plans. The psychiatrist’s responses tend to shut down rather than cultivate change talk.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present in the patient but is not nurtured or reinforced by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s sustain talk as “excuses” and “hesitation,” and confronts it aggressively rather than softening or exploring it. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s responses escalate resistance rather than reduce sustain talk or help the patient resolve ambivalence.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal, lacking any skillful softening or redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritative role, issuing commands and demands without inviting collaboration or input. The patient’s perspective is overridden repeatedly, and there is no evidence of shared decision-making or co-creation of goals. The psychiatrist’s language (“You have to,” “No ifs or buts,” “Are you going to or not?”) reinforces a power imbalance and undermines partnership.

**Summary:**  
There is no genuine partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and fails to engage the patient as an equal collaborator.

---

### **Overall Global Ratings Summary**  
This session is a clear example of poor motivational interviewing practice. The psychiatrist’s approach is directive, confrontational, and dismissive of the patient’s perspective and autonomy. There is no acceptance, empathy, or collaboration. Change talk is ignored, sustain talk is confronted, and the patient’s ambivalence is not explored or respected. This style is likely to increase resistance and reduce motivation rather than enhance it.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

### Strengths

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and dismissive.  
- There is no acceptance, empathy, or respect for the patient’s autonomy.  
- No reflective listening or affirmations are used.  
- Change talk is ignored and sustain talk is confronted aggressively.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs.  
- The psychiatrist’s style is likely to increase resistance and reduce motivation.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directive commands to partnership. Use inclusive language and invite the patient’s input.

2. **Demonstrate Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Validate the patient’s feelings and challenges. Avoid labeling reasons as “excuses.”  
   - Use reflective listening to show understanding.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Encourage the patient to explore her own motivations and barriers.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Reflect and reinforce statements about desire and willingness to change, even if small steps.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Respect the patient’s readiness and pace. Avoid pressuring immediate commitment.

7. **Collaborative Planning:**  
   - Work with the patient to develop manageable, realistic steps toward change.

8. **Avoid Confrontation:**  
   - Replace “You have to” and “No ifs or buts” with invitational and supportive language.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and dismissive approach is counterproductive and risks alienating the patient. To improve, the psychiatrist must embrace the MI spirit of collaboration, empathy, and autonomy support, and develop skills in reflective listening, evoking change talk, and managing ambivalence. Without these changes, the session is unlikely to facilitate meaningful behavior change.
