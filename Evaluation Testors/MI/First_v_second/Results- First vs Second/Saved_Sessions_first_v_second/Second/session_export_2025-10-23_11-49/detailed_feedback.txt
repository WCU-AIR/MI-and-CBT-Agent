# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach throughout the session is judgmental, directive, and confrontational rather than accepting. The repeated use of phrases like “you really need to quit as soon as possible,” “you have to stop now,” and “stop making excuses” communicates disapproval and pressure rather than unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge Linda’s feelings, ambivalence, or autonomy. There is no warmth or supportive tone; instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly challenges and confronts Linda, which likely increases resistance. This is evident in multiple instances, such as “You can’t keep using that as an excuse” and “You have to take responsibility here.” These statements are coercive and undermine acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate acceptance, instead adopting a judgmental and directive stance that likely alienates the patient.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no evidence of empathy or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or reflect Linda’s perspective, emotions, or ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with directives and accusations. For example, when Linda expresses her struggle and ambivalence (“I’m not sure I can do it,” “I enjoy smoking,” “I’m worried about being a bad role model”), the psychiatrist ignores these cues and presses for immediate commitment. There are no paraphrases, reflections, or validation of Linda’s experience. The psychiatrist’s responses lack any attempt to convey understanding or emotional attunement.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows little effort to understand or convey empathy, missing critical opportunities to build rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands that Linda quit immediately and repeatedly asks for commitment without exploring her motivations or readiness. The language is coercive (“You have to stop now,” “You really need to quit,” “You have to take responsibility”), which is antithetical to the MI spirit. There is no elicitation of Linda’s own reasons for change beyond superficial questioning, and no support for her autonomy or self-efficacy. The psychiatrist does not invite Linda to explore her ambivalence or values, nor does she co-create any plan.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian and directive, lacking the collaborative, evocative, and autonomy-supportive spirit essential to MI.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly demands commitment and focuses on the negative consequences of smoking without exploring Linda’s own motivations or reasons for change. When Linda expresses change talk (e.g., concern about being a bad role model), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses it, failing to reflect or amplify these statements. The repeated push for immediate quitting likely suppresses change talk rather than cultivates it.

**Summary:**  
There is no effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the psychiatrist’s approach likely inhibits client motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften sustain talk but rather confronts and challenges it aggressively. Linda’s sustain talk (enjoyment of smoking, difficulty quitting, ambivalence) is met with accusations and demands rather than reflective or double-sided reflections. The psychiatrist’s responses such as “stop making excuses” and “you can’t keep putting this off” reinforce sustain talk defensively rather than reduce its impact.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead escalates resistance through confrontational responses.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation with directives and demands, leaving no room for Linda’s input or autonomy. The repeated yes/no questions about commitment are coercive rather than collaborative. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge Linda’s expertise in her own life or invite her to co-create goals or plans.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance, failing to establish a collaborative partnership.

---

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must abandon the confrontational, directive style.  
- Develop skills in acceptance, empathy, and reflective listening.  
- Embrace the MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.  
- Learn to skillfully evoke and reinforce change talk while softening sustain talk.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and judgmental, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and reflective listening.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.  
- The session lacks collaboration, partnership, and autonomy support.  
- The psychiatrist’s questions are closed and coercive, limiting client engagement and exploration.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid confrontational language and judgmental statements.  
   - Express unconditional positive regard for the client’s experience and ambivalence.

2. **Develop Empathy and Reflective Listening Skills:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Validate the client’s feelings and struggles.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the client rather than direct.  
   - Evoke the client’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support client autonomy and choice.

4. **Use Open Questions and Complex Reflections:**  
   - Ask questions that invite elaboration and exploration.  
   - Use reflections to deepen understanding and highlight ambivalence.

5. **Skillfully Evoke and Reinforce Change Talk:**  
   - When the client expresses concern or desire to change, reflect and amplify these statements.  
   - Avoid pushing for premature commitment.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Normalize ambivalence and difficulty.

7. **Build Partnership and Plan Collaboratively:**  
   - Invite the client to co-create goals and plans.  
   - Summarize key points and check for understanding and readiness.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental style is likely to increase client resistance and reduce motivation for change. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation of client motivation. Without these changes, the session risks alienating clients and undermining therapeutic progress.
