# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance throughout the session is largely judgmental and directive rather than accepting. The opening statements such as “you really need to quit smoking” and “I’m here to tell you exactly what you should do” communicate a lack of unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient with “Why didn’t you just stop already?” and “You need to stop making excuses,” which conveys disapproval and blame. There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or normalization of the patient’s struggle. The patient’s guarded and hesitant responses suggest discomfort with the psychiatrist’s confrontational tone. This pattern is consistent across multiple turns, indicating a persistent lack of acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive, lacking the respectful, supportive stance essential for motivational interviewing. This undermines rapport and client openness.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist makes minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence and difficulty with quitting due to stress, yet the psychiatrist dismisses these concerns and pushes for immediate action without validating the patient’s feelings. There are no reflective statements or paraphrases that demonstrate understanding of the patient’s emotional experience or ambivalence. The patient’s guarded and brief responses indicate that the psychiatrist’s lack of empathy may be inhibiting deeper disclosure. The psychiatrist’s repeated demands and accusations (“You need to stop making excuses”) further reduce empathic connection.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited and mostly absent. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge or explore the patient’s ambivalence and emotional experience, resulting in a one-sided, confrontational interaction.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly tells the patient what to do (“You have to pick a date and stick to it,” “You need to throw those cigarettes away today”) and challenges the patient’s readiness and honesty. There is no invitation for the patient’s input or exploration of their motivations. The patient is positioned as passive and resistant rather than as an active agent in change. The psychiatrist’s approach contradicts the core MI principles of partnership and autonomy.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent. The session is dominated by persuasion and directive commands, undermining client autonomy and collaboration.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient occasionally expresses tentative change talk (e.g., “Maybe quitting this week sounds good,” “It’d be good to quit”), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke, reinforce, or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on pushing for immediate commitment and action, which appears to shut down the patient’s willingness to elaborate. There are no reflective or open-ended questions designed to deepen the patient’s own motivations or explore ambivalence. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to explore the patient’s desire for a better future or reasons for change.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and not reinforced. The psychiatrist’s confrontational style limits the patient’s expression of motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften or skillfully respond to sustain talk. Instead, sustain talk is met with confrontation and challenges (“You need to stop making excuses,” “You can’t keep putting this off”). There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change. The patient’s expressions of ambivalence and hesitation are dismissed rather than explored or normalized.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced rather than softened. The psychiatrist’s approach likely increases resistance and disengagement.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritative role, telling the patient what to do and demanding compliance. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or negotiation of goals. The patient is not invited to express preferences or collaborate on a plan. The repeated commands and challenges create a power imbalance and reduce the patient’s sense of agency.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent. The session is dominated by an expert-driven, directive approach that undermines client-centered collaboration.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Maintains focus on smoking cessation as the target behavior.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly directive, confrontational, and judgmental, which is counterproductive in motivational interviewing.  
- There is a critical lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership, which damages rapport and client engagement.  
- The psychiatrist fails to use reflective listening, affirmations, or open questions to explore ambivalence or evoke change talk.  
- Sustain talk is met with challenges rather than softening, increasing resistance.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs, missing opportunities to support commitment.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid blaming or confrontational language. Use warm, supportive statements that communicate unconditional positive regard.

2. **Increase Empathy and Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings and ambivalence to demonstrate understanding and build rapport.  
   - Use complex reflections to explore underlying emotions and motivations.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Shift from directing and persuading to collaborating and evoking the patient’s own reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy by emphasizing choice and control.

4. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions that invite elaboration and exploration.  
   - Affirm the patient’s strengths, efforts, and intentions to build confidence.

5. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalization to acknowledge ambivalence without reinforcing resistance.

6. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to identify feasible next steps and consolidate commitment.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and clarify goals.

7. **Avoid Confrontation and Commands:**  
   - Replace directives with invitations and permission-seeking before offering advice or suggestions.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental approach undermines rapport, empathy, and client engagement. There is no evidence of acceptance, partnership, or effective evocation of change talk. To improve, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a collaborative, autonomy-supportive style that respects the patient’s ambivalence and fosters intrinsic motivation for change. Without these changes, the session risks increasing resistance and reducing the likelihood of successful behavior change.
