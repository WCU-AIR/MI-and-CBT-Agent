# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly adopts a judgmental and directive tone, emphasizing the necessity of quitting smoking with phrases like “You really need to quit,” “You have to stop,” and “You have to take this seriously.” This approach conveys pressure and a lack of unconditional positive regard. The patient’s resistance and defensiveness are met with insistence rather than empathy or acceptance. There is no evidence of normalization of struggle or validation of the patient’s feelings or experiences. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s frustration or ambivalence in a supportive way, which is critical given the patient’s defensive stance. The tone is more authoritarian than accepting, which likely exacerbates resistance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance is predominantly directive and pressuring, lacking warmth, respect, and genuine acceptance of the patient’s perspective and ambivalence.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient clearly expresses frustration with repeated advice and side effects from medication, yet the psychiatrist responds with repeated questions about why previous methods “didn’t work” and insists on quitting “now.” There are no reflective statements or attempts to paraphrase or validate the patient’s feelings of frustration, fatigue, or ambivalence. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to acknowledge the patient’s experience, such as the side effects from medication or the emotional difficulty of quitting. The lack of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s withdrawal and defensiveness.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist fails to demonstrate understanding or convey that the patient’s feelings and experiences are heard and valued.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive approach with repeated commands and pressure to quit smoking immediately. There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly asks “Are you ready to commit to quitting now?” without exploring the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change. The patient is not invited to share their perspective beyond brief defensive statements, and the psychiatrist does not support the patient’s autonomy or choice. The interaction is confrontational rather than collaborative, which is antithetical to the spirit of MI.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks the core MI elements of partnership, evocation, and autonomy support, instead relying on persuasion and directive commands.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce any change talk. Although the patient expresses some ambivalence and acknowledges the harms of smoking, the psychiatrist does not reflect or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly asks closed questions about prior quit attempts and readiness to commit, which the patient resists. There is no strategic effort to elicit the patient’s own reasons, desires, or confidence for change. The repeated pressure to “commit now” likely suppresses any emerging change talk.

**Summary:**  
No meaningful evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the psychiatrist’s approach likely inhibits the patient’s motivation to express change-related language.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften or skillfully respond to sustain talk. The patient’s resistance, frustration, and sustain talk (e.g., “I just want to get my teeth fixed,” “I’m tired of being lectured,” “I’m not ready to commit”) are met with repeated insistence and pressure. There are no double-sided reflections, no normalization of ambivalence, and no attempts to validate the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change. The psychiatrist’s responses reinforce sustain talk by focusing on what the patient “has to” do rather than exploring the patient’s perspective.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and pressure rather than softening or strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and directives, with no evidence of shared decision-making or negotiation of goals. The patient is repeatedly asked to commit to quitting immediately, with no invitation to explore their own priorities or readiness. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in their own experience or invite the patient to co-create the plan. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
The session is authoritarian and expert-driven, lacking genuine partnership or client-centered collaboration.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently maintains focus on the health risks of smoking and the importance of quitting.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is directive, pressuring, and lacks empathy and acceptance.  
- There is no evidence of MI spirit: no collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support.  
- Change talk is not cultivated; sustain talk is reinforced rather than softened.  
- The patient’s resistance and ambivalence are not acknowledged or validated.  
- Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist dominates the agenda and decision-making.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from directive commands to partnership. Invite the patient’s perspective and acknowledge their expertise in their own experience.

2. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.

3. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Efforts:**  
   Recognize the patient’s past attempts and willingness to engage, even if ambivalent.

4. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   Ask open-ended questions that explore the patient’s own reasons, desires, and confidence for change. Avoid yes/no questions about readiness.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalization to acknowledge the patient’s resistance while gently guiding toward change.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Explicitly affirm the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

7. **Avoid Unsolicited Advice and Persuasion:**  
   Ask permission before offering information or suggestions.

8. **Develop Collaborative Plans:**  
   Work with the patient to identify small, achievable steps rather than pushing for immediate commitment.

9. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   Use summaries to consolidate motivation and reinforce change talk.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s approach is directive, pressuring, and lacking in empathy, acceptance, and collaboration. The patient’s resistance and ambivalence are met with confrontation rather than skillful MI strategies. To improve, the psychiatrist must embrace the MI spirit, deepen reflective listening, and skillfully evoke and reinforce the patient’s own motivations for change. Without these changes, the session risks alienating the patient and undermining any potential for behavior change.
