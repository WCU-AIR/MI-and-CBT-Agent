# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. The repeated admonishments such as “you really need to stop making excuses” and “you have to push through the discomfort” communicate a lack of unconditional positive regard. The patient’s expressions of difficulty and ambivalence are met with dismissive and critical responses rather than empathy or validation. This creates a coercive atmosphere rather than a supportive one. There is minimal evidence of warmth or acceptance; instead, the psychiatrist frequently blames the patient for lack of willpower or commitment.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive, undermining acceptance and rapport. This severely limits the patient’s comfort and openness.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows limited effort to understand the patient’s perspective beyond surface-level acknowledgment. While the patient clearly expresses stress as a major barrier and emotional difficulty, the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses this as “excuses” and fails to reflect or explore the emotional experience. There are no complex or nuanced reflections that capture the patient’s ambivalence or fear of failure. The patient’s statements about feeling overwhelmed and needing manageable steps are ignored or rebuffed. This lack of empathic listening likely contributes to the patient’s withdrawal and defensiveness.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is minimal and inconsistent, with a predominant focus on pushing the patient rather than understanding her lived experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with frequent commands (“Just do it,” “You have to quit now,” “No excuses”). There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly overrides the patient’s expressed concerns and readiness, insisting on immediate action without negotiation or partnership. The patient’s autonomy is ignored, and the psychiatrist’s stance is coercive rather than supportive. This is antithetical to the spirit of motivational interviewing, which prioritizes client-centered collaboration and evoking intrinsic motivation.  

**Summary:**  
The session lacks any meaningful MI spirit; it is authoritarian and directive, undermining client autonomy and collaboration.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient does express some change talk (e.g., “I want to quit eventually,” “I know smoking is bad”), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on pushing the patient to commit prematurely, which appears to shut down the patient’s willingness to elaborate on change talk. There are no strategic reflections or affirmations that amplify the patient’s motivation. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to explore the patient’s desire and ambivalence in a supportive way.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but poorly cultivated; the psychiatrist’s confrontational style inhibits deeper exploration and reinforcement.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk related to stress, fear of failure, and ambivalence is met with direct confrontation and dismissal (“stop making excuses,” “no room for hesitation”). The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or validate the patient’s struggle. Instead, sustain talk is met with pressure and blame, which likely increases resistance. There is no attempt to normalize ambivalence or gently guide the patient toward change.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened or redirected, increasing patient resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist dominates the conversation with commands and ultimatums, leaving no room for shared decision-making or client input. The patient repeatedly expresses a need for manageable steps and collaboration, which is ignored. The psychiatrist’s language (“You have to,” “Just do it”) reinforces a power imbalance and expert-driven approach. There is no evidence of co-creation of goals or respect for the patient’s expertise in her own experience.  

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance that undermines collaboration and client empowerment.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directive commands to partnership language that respects the patient’s autonomy and expertise.  
   - Use inclusive phrases like “What do you think would help?” or “How do you feel about trying some small steps?”

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings and ambivalence without judgment.  
   - Validate the difficulty of quitting and normalize ambivalence.

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions to explore the patient’s motivations and barriers.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen understanding and convey empathy.

4. **Cultivate Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and amplify these statements.  
   - Explore confidence and commitment gently rather than demanding immediate decisions.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses.”

6. **Support Autonomy and Readiness:**  
   - Respect the patient’s stage of change and readiness to act.  
   - Collaborate on a plan that fits the patient’s current capacity and preferences.

7. **Avoid Confrontation and Ultimatums:**  
   - Replace confrontational language with supportive, nonjudgmental dialogue.  
   - Recognize that pressure often increases resistance rather than motivation.

8. **Incorporate Summarizing and Planning:**  
   - Summarize key points of change talk and ambivalence to reinforce motivation.  
   - Collaboratively develop a realistic, stepwise plan for quitting.

This diagnostic session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines acceptance, empathy, and collaboration. The patient’s ambivalence and sustain talk are met with dismissal and pressure rather than exploration and support. Change talk is not cultivated, and no collaborative planning occurs. To improve, the psychiatrist must fundamentally shift toward a client-centered, empathic, and autonomy-supportive approach consistent with the spirit and techniques of motivational interviewing.
