# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental, directive, and confrontational stance throughout the session. The tone is authoritarian and lacks warmth or respect for the patient’s perspective. Phrases such as “You really need to quit smoking, no ifs or buts,” “You have to understand,” and “Don’t you want to end up with serious health problems?” communicate pressure and disapproval rather than acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s feelings and reasons, labeling them as “excuses” and “dragging your feet.” There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or validation of the patient’s experience or ambivalence. The patient responds defensively and with resistance, which is unsurprising given the lack of acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is highly judgmental and directive, undermining rapport and trust. There is a complete absence of acceptance or supportive communication.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy or reflective listening. The patient’s statements about peer influence, ambivalence, and feelings of being pressured are ignored or challenged rather than understood. The psychiatrist does not attempt to paraphrase, reflect, or validate the patient’s perspective. Instead, the patient’s concerns are met with repeated commands and rhetorical questions that convey disbelief and impatience. For example, when the patient says, “Fitting in’s not just an excuse—it’s real,” the psychiatrist dismisses this without acknowledgment. The lack of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s defensive and resistant responses.

**Summary:**  
There is no evidence of empathic understanding or reflective listening. The psychiatrist’s responses are confrontational and dismissive.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely authoritarian and directive, with no collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly tells the patient what to do, uses ultimatums (“no ifs or buts”), and ignores the patient’s expressed feelings and motivations. There is no attempt to evoke the patient’s own reasons for change or to support their autonomy. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of orders rather than an active participant. This approach is the antithesis of the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks any MI spirit; it is coercive, non-collaborative, and controlling.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient occasionally expresses mild change talk (e.g., “I don’t want health problems”), the psychiatrist does not reflect or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on pushing the patient to quit immediately, which appears to shut down any emerging motivation. There are no strategic questions or reflections designed to deepen the patient’s own reasons or desires for change.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimal and not cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s sustain talk as “excuses” and “dragging your feet,” which reinforces resistance rather than softening it. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to acknowledge the patient’s ambivalence. The psychiatrist fails to normalize the difficulty of change or validate the patient’s perspective, instead confronting and challenging sustain talk aggressively.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal, exacerbating resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a highly authoritative stance, issuing commands and ultimatums without inviting the patient’s input or collaboration. The patient is not treated as an equal partner; rather, the psychiatrist assumes an expert role and pressures the patient to comply. There is no negotiation of agenda or shared decision-making. The patient’s autonomy is disregarded.

**Summary:**  
No partnership or collaboration is evident; the interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.  
- Empathy and reflective listening are essential to understand and engage the patient.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—must replace authoritarianism.  
- Change talk should be actively elicited and reinforced.  
- Sustain talk requires skillful softening, not confrontation.  
- Partnership and shared decision-making must be prioritized.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly confrontational and directive, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and instead escalates resistance.  
- The session lacks reflective listening, affirmations, and autonomy support.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses” or “dragging feet.”  
   - Express genuine respect for the patient’s perspective and struggles.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Validate ambivalence and the difficulty of change.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy and choice.

4. **Elicit and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or concern, reflect and explore it further.  
   - Avoid pushing for immediate commitment; instead, build motivation gradually.

5. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Normalize ambivalence as part of the change process.

6. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions to invite elaboration.  
   - Affirm patient strengths, efforts, and values.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Invite the patient to set goals and timelines.  
   - Summarize key points and check for understanding and agreement.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental style undermines engagement, evokes resistance, and fails to support the patient’s motivation for change. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation of the patient’s own motivations and autonomy. Without these changes, the likelihood of meaningful behavior change is minimal, and the therapeutic relationship is compromised.
