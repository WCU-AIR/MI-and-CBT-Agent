# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates limited acceptance throughout the session. The tone is frequently directive, judgmental, and pressuring rather than respectful or supportive. For example, statements like “You really need to quit smoking,” “You have to be strict about it,” and “You have to take responsibility now” convey a lack of unconditional positive regard and come across as confrontational. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge or validate the patient’s perspective or autonomy. The patient’s attempts to express her own views are met with insistence rather than understanding. There is no normalization of the patient’s experience or struggles, and no explicit warmth or support is conveyed.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely authoritarian and directive, undermining acceptance and rapport. This significantly limits the patient’s comfort and openness.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The responses are mostly statements or questions that push the agenda rather than reflections or empathic statements. For example, the psychiatrist repeatedly insists on the patient “taking responsibility” and “setting strict limits” without acknowledging the patient’s expressed feelings or ambivalence. The patient’s statements about feeling in control and not seeing a problem are not reflected or explored empathetically. There are no complex or simple reflections that demonstrate understanding of the patient’s experience or emotions.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist misses opportunities to validate and explore the patient’s perspective, which would foster trust and openness.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is largely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, directive stance, repeatedly telling the patient what she “has to” do and demanding commitment to strict rules. There is no genuine collaboration or evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values. Autonomy is not supported; instead, the patient is pressured and confronted. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient to explore her own reasons for change or to set her own goals. This approach is antithetical to MI spirit.  

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by a controlling, prescriptive style that undermines MI principles.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Instead, the psychiatrist issues commands and warnings without eliciting the patient’s own reasons, desires, or confidence for change. The patient offers some tentative change talk (e.g., “I do get that drinking too much can cause problems later”), but the psychiatrist fails to reflect or amplify these statements. There are no open-ended questions or reflections designed to deepen change talk or explore ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s approach risks eliciting resistance rather than change talk.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated; the psychiatrist misses critical opportunities to evoke and reinforce motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften sustain talk but rather confronts and challenges it aggressively. The patient’s sustain talk (e.g., “I don’t see why I need to start strict limits,” “I’m okay with being careful but not strict rules”) is met with insistence and pressure rather than reflective listening or double-sided reflections. There is no attempt to acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s perspective or to gently guide toward change. This confrontational style likely increases resistance and sustain talk.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced rather than softened; the psychiatrist’s responses are confrontational and directive.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands, directives, and pressure. The patient’s autonomy and expertise in her own life are disregarded. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient to co-create goals or plans, nor does she acknowledge the patient’s right to make her own choices. The power differential is emphasized rather than minimized.  

**Summary:**  
The session lacks partnership and client-centeredness; it is authoritarian and directive.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary  

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist maintains consistent focus on health behavior change.  

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks acceptance, empathy, and collaboration.  
- There is no evocation or reinforcement of change talk.  
- Sustain talk is met with pressure rather than softening.  
- The patient’s autonomy and perspective are disregarded.  

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on health behavior change topics.  
- The patient remains polite and cooperative despite the confrontational style.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks core MI skills such as acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- There is no use of reflective listening, affirmations, or open questions that invite exploration.  
- Change talk is not evoked or reinforced; sustain talk is met with pressure rather than softening.  
- The patient’s autonomy and perspective are disregarded, undermining engagement and motivation.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from directive commands to a partnership approach that respects the patient’s autonomy and expertise in her own life.

2. **Increase Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and perspectives. Avoid judgmental or confrontational language.

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   Ask open-ended questions to explore the patient’s thoughts and feelings. Reflect both content and emotion to deepen understanding.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses any desire, ability, or reasons for change, reflect and reinforce these statements to build motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and affirm the legitimacy of ambivalence to reduce resistance and guide gently toward change.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Explicitly acknowledge the patient’s right to make her own choices and control the pace of change.

7. **Collaboratively Develop Plans:**  
   Work with the patient to set realistic, personalized goals and next steps rather than imposing strict rules.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Pressure:**  
   Replace “you have to” statements with invitations and explorations of readiness and willingness.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation of change talk. The patient’s autonomy is disregarded, and sustain talk is met with pressure rather than skillful softening. To improve, the psychiatrist must fundamentally shift to a client-centered, empathic, and collaborative approach that respects the patient’s perspective and fosters intrinsic motivation for change.
