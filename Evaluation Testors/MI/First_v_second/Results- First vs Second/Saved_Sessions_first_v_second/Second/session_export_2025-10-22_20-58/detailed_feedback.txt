# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is judgmental, directive, and lacking in warmth or respect. The tone is authoritarian and accusatory, exemplified by statements such as “You have to quit smoking,” “You just need to stop making excuses,” and “You’re not serious about quitting.” There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or validation of the patient’s experience. The psychiatrist repeatedly blames the patient for failure and frames the patient’s ambivalence as weakness or lack of willpower. This approach is likely to increase resistance rather than foster engagement. There are no affirmations or supportive statements.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate acceptance, instead adopting a confrontational and judgmental stance that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no evidence of empathic understanding or reflective listening. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s expressions of overwhelm, fear, and ambivalence, instead pushing a rigid agenda. The patient’s statements about feeling overwhelmed and the complexity of quitting are dismissed or labeled as “making excuses.” The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective or emotional state. The lack of empathy is stark and likely to alienate the patient.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates little effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, missing critical opportunities to build rapport and explore ambivalence.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly commands the patient to quit immediately, dismisses the patient’s concerns, and uses coercive language (“You have to,” “No ifs, ands, or buts,” “This isn’t optional”). There is no invitation for the patient to share their own motivations or ideas. Autonomy is explicitly denied, and the patient is positioned as noncompliant rather than a partner in change.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is entirely absent; the psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive stance that contradicts MI principles.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Instead, the focus is on pushing the patient to commit without exploring their own reasons, desires, or confidence. The patient’s minimal change talk (acknowledging smoking and health risks) is ignored or overridden by the psychiatrist’s commands. There are no open-ended questions designed to elicit the patient’s motivations or ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s questions are closed, leading, or confrontational, which suppresses change talk rather than cultivating it.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is neither effectively evoked nor reinforced; the psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore and amplify the patient’s own motivations.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s expressions of overwhelm and difficulty as “excuses” and “not serious,” which likely entrenches sustain talk and resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change. The approach is punitive rather than supportive.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and escalated rather than softened or redirected, undermining the potential for resolving ambivalence.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is one-sided, with the psychiatrist issuing commands and the patient responding defensively. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s expertise in their own life. The psychiatrist’s language (“You have to,” “Do you understand?” “Good. Now, get started.”) is authoritarian and dismissive of the patient’s autonomy.  

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and fails to engage the patient as an equal collaborator.

---

- Empathic listening and reflective statements are essential to understand the patient’s perspective.  
- Collaboration and autonomy support should replace directive commands.  
- Change talk must be evoked and reinforced through open-ended questions and affirmations.  
- Sustain talk should be met with strategic softening rather than confrontation.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly confrontational, judgmental, and directive, which is counterproductive in motivational interviewing.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and instead escalates sustain talk and resistance.  
- The session lacks open questions, reflections, affirmations, and collaborative planning.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and overwhelm are ignored rather than explored and validated.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   Replace confrontational language with respectful, supportive communication that conveys unconditional positive regard.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   Reflect the patient’s feelings and perspectives to build rapport and understanding.

3. **Employ Open-Ended Questions:**  
   Invite the patient to elaborate on their experience, motivations, and ambivalence.

4. **Evoke and Reinforce Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses any desire or reason for change, reflect and amplify these statements.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and validate the patient’s struggle to reduce resistance.

6. **Support Autonomy and Collaboration:**  
   Engage the patient as an equal partner, allowing them to set the pace and make decisions about change.

7. **Develop Collaborative Plans:**  
   Co-create achievable goals and steps rather than imposing unilateral directives.

8. **Avoid Persuasion and Confrontation:**  
   Focus on guiding rather than directing, and avoid language that shames or blames the patient.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style is likely to increase patient resistance and disengagement. To improve, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a collaborative, empathic, and autonomy-supportive approach that respects the patient’s experience and gently evokes their own motivations for change.
