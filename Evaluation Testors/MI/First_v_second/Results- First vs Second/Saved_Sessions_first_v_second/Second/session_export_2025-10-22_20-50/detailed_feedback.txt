# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Smoking Cessation Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. The tone is frequently confrontational and pressuring, with repeated admonishments such as “You really should stop now,” “You have to just do it,” and “No more delays.” These statements communicate disapproval and a lack of empathy for the patient’s ambivalence and struggle. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge or validate the patient’s concerns about muscle weakness, stress, or readiness, instead dismissing them as “excuses.” There is minimal evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s resistance and hesitancy are met with impatience rather than acceptance.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely directive and judgmental, undermining acceptance. There is a critical need to adopt a more respectful, nonjudgmental, and validating stance to foster trust and openness.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows limited effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient repeatedly expresses concerns about muscle weakness, readiness, and feeling overwhelmed, but the psychiatrist responds with commands and challenges rather than reflective listening or empathetic statements. For example, the psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s emotional experience or validate the difficulty of quitting under current circumstances. Instead, the psychiatrist insists on immediate action without exploring the patient’s feelings or barriers. There are no complex or nuanced reflections that capture the patient’s ambivalence or emotional state.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is minimal and largely absent. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to demonstrate understanding and to build rapport through reflective listening.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with frequent persuasion and pressure. The psychiatrist repeatedly insists on immediate commitment, uses ultimatums (“This isn’t optional”), and dismisses the patient’s autonomy and readiness. There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s perspective or explore motivations; instead, the patient is repeatedly told what to do. This approach is antithetical to the spirit of MI, which emphasizes partnership, evocation, and autonomy support.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is essentially absent. The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, expert-driven stance that undermines motivation and engagement.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. The patient offers minimal change talk, mostly reluctant acceptance and acknowledgment of health risks, but the psychiatrist fails to amplify or explore these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on pressing the patient to commit immediately, which appears to increase resistance rather than elicit motivation. There are no strategic reflections or questions designed to deepen the patient’s own reasons for change or confidence.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated. The psychiatrist’s approach suppresses rather than encourages the patient’s own motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is abundant in the patient’s speech (hesitation, concerns about muscle weakness, readiness, and stress), but the psychiatrist consistently confronts and challenges it directly rather than softening or exploring it. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s concerns as “excuses” and demands immediate action, which likely reinforces sustain talk and resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s ambivalence.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than skillful softening or redirection, exacerbating resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands, ultimatums, and a lack of invitation for the patient’s input or agenda-setting. The patient is repeatedly pressured to commit “today” without negotiation or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in their own experience or work to minimize the power differential.  

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance that undermines client-centeredness.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently maintains focus on the health risks of smoking and the importance of quitting.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacking in empathy and acceptance.  
- There is no evidence of MI spirit, partnership, or evocation.  
- Change talk is not cultivated; sustain talk is confronted rather than softened.  
- The patient’s autonomy and readiness are disregarded, likely increasing resistance.  

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Maintains focus on the health risks of smoking and the importance of quitting.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks empathy and acceptance.  
- There is no evidence of MI spirit: no collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support.  
- The psychiatrist fails to use open questions, reflections, affirmations, or summaries.  
- Change talk is not cultivated; sustain talk is confronted rather than softened.  
- The patient’s autonomy and readiness are disregarded, likely increasing resistance and disengagement.  
- No collaborative planning or negotiation occurs.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient concerns as “excuses.”  
   - Validate the difficulty of quitting and the patient’s ambivalence.

2. **Increase Empathy and Reflective Listening:**  
   - Use complex reflections to capture the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to build rapport.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Shift from directing and persuading to collaborating and evoking.  
   - Support patient autonomy and choice explicitly.

4. **Use Open-Ended Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Ask questions that invite elaboration on motivations, barriers, and values.  
   - Affirm patient strengths, efforts, and intentions.

5. **Skillfully Handle Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Normalize the struggle and explore sustain talk without judgment.

6. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Invite the patient to set their own goals and timelines.  
   - Summarize key points and reinforce change talk to consolidate motivation.

7. **Avoid Ultimatums and Pressure:**  
   - Recognize that readiness varies and forcing commitment often backfires.  
   - Create a safe space for the patient to explore change at their own pace.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental approach undermines acceptance, empathy, and collaboration. The failure to evoke change talk or soften sustain talk results in increased patient resistance and disengagement. To improve, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a patient-centered, autonomy-supportive style that respects the patient’s ambivalence and readiness, uses reflective listening, and collaboratively fosters motivation for change.
