## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**

Instead, the patient is repeatedly reprimanded and pressured with statements like “You really should quit immediately,” “You have to be serious,” and “You don’t get to say you’re scared or overwhelmed.” The psychiatrist dismisses the patient’s expressions of fatigue, fear, and ambivalence, labeling them as “excuses” and “avoidance.” This approach is likely to alienate the patient rather than foster trust or openness. There are no affirmations or supportive comments that acknowledge the patient’s struggle or strengths.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance is harsh, directive, and lacking in respect or empathy, which severely undermines acceptance.

### 2. Empathy

This lack of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s defensive and withdrawn responses.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of empathic listening or perspective-taking; the psychiatrist’s responses are dismissive and invalidating.

- The psychiatrist fails to use core MI skills such as reflective listening, affirmations, open questions, and evocation.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Supportive Stance:**  
   - Begin by expressing empathy and acceptance of the patient’s fatigue and ambivalence.  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses” or “avoidance.”

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Open Questions:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings and concerns to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Ask open-ended questions to explore the patient’s perspective and motivations.

3. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for quitting, reflect and reinforce these statements.  
   - Explore the patient’s values and goals related to smoking cessation.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of quitting and validate the patient’s feelings.

5. **Support Autonomy and Partnership:**  
   - Invite the patient to share her thoughts and preferences.  
   - Collaboratively set goals and develop a plan that respects the patient’s readiness.

6. **Avoid Coercion and Direct Commands:**  
   - Replace “You have to” and “You don’t get to say” with collaborative language.  
   - Ask permission before offering advice or information.

7. **Build Engagement and Trust:**  
   - Show genuine interest in the patient’s experience.  
   - Validate the patient’s efforts and struggles.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and dismissive style is likely to increase patient resistance and reduce motivation to change. To improve, the psychiatrist must embrace the core MI spirit of acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation, and develop skills in reflective listening, affirmations, and strategic handling of ambivalence. Without these changes, the effectiveness of smoking cessation interventions will be severely compromised.
