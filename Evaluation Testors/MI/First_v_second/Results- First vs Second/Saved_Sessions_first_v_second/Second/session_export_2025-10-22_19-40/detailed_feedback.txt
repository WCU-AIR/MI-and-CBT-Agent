# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a largely judgmental and directive stance from the outset. The opening statement, “you need to quit smoking,” is authoritative and prescriptive rather than accepting or exploratory. When the patient clarifies that smoking is not an issue, the psychiatrist quickly shifts focus but continues with a confrontational tone: “You need to stop letting anxiety control you,” and “You have to get a grip on this.” These statements communicate disapproval and a lack of unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist fails to normalize the patient’s struggle or validate her experience, instead implying blame and weakness. There is no evidence of warmth or genuine acceptance, and the patient’s hesitancy and vulnerability are met with pressure rather than support.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely directive and judgmental, lacking the respectful, supportive stance essential for acceptance in MI.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses difficulty with anxiety and avoidance despite efforts to plan and push through, but the psychiatrist responds with commands and questions that imply fault (“Why didn’t you push through it before?” “You have to be tougher on yourself”). There are no reflective statements that capture the patient’s emotional experience or ambivalence. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s fear, frustration, or vulnerability, missing opportunities to build rapport and trust. The patient’s hesitations and expressions of difficulty are met with impatience rather than empathy.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist fails to accurately understand or convey the patient’s perspective and emotional state.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with frequent persuasion and little collaboration. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You have to stop making excuses,” “Just start now,” “You have to be strict”) and frames the patient’s difficulties as personal failings. There is no evidence of evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values, nor support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore her reasons for change beyond superficial questioning. The power differential is stark, with the psychiatrist positioning themselves as the expert who dictates what must be done.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the session is dominated by directive, persuasive, and authoritarian behaviors.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient tentatively expresses desire and ambivalence (“I want to do it,” “Maybe I could try”), the psychiatrist does not reflect or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist pushes for compliance and commitment without exploring the patient’s own motivations or confidence. There is no strategic use of open-ended questions or reflections to deepen change talk or explore ambivalence. The patient’s tentative commitment is met with pressure rather than encouragement.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and not cultivated; the psychiatrist misses opportunities to strengthen motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk, such as the patient’s expressions of fear, avoidance, and difficulty, is met with confrontation and commands rather than softening or reframing. The psychiatrist repeatedly tells the patient to “stop making excuses” and “be tougher,” which reinforces sustain talk by invalidating the patient’s experience and increasing resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change. The approach likely exacerbates sustain talk rather than softening it.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced rather than softened; the psychiatrist’s responses increase resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands and directives, with no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands compliance and frames the patient’s failure to act as personal fault. There is no invitation to co-create goals or plans, and the patient’s input is minimized or dismissed. The power differential is pronounced and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance with no collaboration.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the patient’s presenting problem.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance to foster acceptance.  
- Empathy needs to be demonstrated through reflective listening and validation of the patient’s experience.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is missing and must be integrated.  
- Change talk should be actively evoked and reinforced rather than ignored or overridden.  
- Sustain talk requires skillful softening through reflective and affirming responses.  
- Partnership must be developed by minimizing power differentials and inviting patient input.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid commands and blame. Use language that conveys respect and unconditional positive regard.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy Through Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion. Validate the patient’s struggle and ambivalence.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient, evoke their own motivations, and support autonomy.

4. **Elicit and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Use open-ended questions and reflections to deepen the patient’s own reasons and confidence for change.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and affirmations to acknowledge difficulty while gently guiding toward change.

6. **Build Partnership:**  
   - Invite the patient to co-create goals and plans. Minimize power differentials.

7. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations More Effectively:**  
   - Encourage elaboration and recognize patient strengths and efforts.

8. **Avoid Persuasion Without Permission:**  
   - Ask permission before offering advice or information.

9. **Develop Collaborative Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Summarize key points and reinforce commitment to next steps.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational approach undermines acceptance, empathy, and collaboration. Change talk is not cultivated, and sustain talk is reinforced, likely increasing patient resistance. To improve, the psychiatrist must fundamentally shift to a respectful, empathetic, and collaborative style that evokes the patient’s own motivations and supports autonomy. Without these changes, the session risks alienating the patient and reducing the likelihood of meaningful behavior change.
