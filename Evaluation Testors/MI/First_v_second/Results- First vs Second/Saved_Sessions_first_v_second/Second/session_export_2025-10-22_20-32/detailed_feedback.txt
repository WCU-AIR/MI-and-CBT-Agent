# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive stance from the outset, explicitly stating “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do” and repeatedly insisting on immediate cessation without regard for the patient’s perspective or readiness. The tone is authoritarian and dismissive of the patient’s ambivalence and autonomy. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s responses as “making excuses” and questions motivation in a confrontational manner (“Are you just not motivated enough?”). There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s attempts to express ambivalence and negotiate manageable steps are consistently shut down. This approach is antithetical to acceptance in MI.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is highly judgmental and directive, lacking respect for the patient’s autonomy and perspective. This severely undermines rapport and acceptance.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate understanding or reflective listening. There are no reflections or paraphrases that capture the patient’s feelings, values, or ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with commands and accusations, ignoring the patient’s expressed concerns about social aspects of drinking and readiness for change. The patient’s emotional experience—hesitancy, enjoyment of social drinking, and fear of abrupt change—is not acknowledged or validated. The lack of empathy likely contributes to patient resistance.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is virtually absent. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, leading to a confrontational dynamic.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is completely missing. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and refusing to entertain any negotiation or patient input. Autonomy is denied (“You don’t get to negotiate this”), and evocation is replaced by commands and pressure. The psychiatrist does not elicit the patient’s own motivations or values but instead imposes a rigid agenda. This approach is the antithesis of MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of MI spirit. The session is dominated by directive, coercive communication that undermines collaboration and autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. When the patient tentatively expresses willingness to cut back or acknowledges some risks, the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these statements, instead demanding immediate cessation. There is no strategic amplification or exploration of the patient’s own reasons for change. The patient’s tentative change talk is met with confrontation rather than encouragement, which likely suppresses further change talk.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated; instead, the psychiatrist’s approach likely inhibits the patient’s motivation to explore change.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels patient ambivalence as “making excuses” and “not taking it seriously,” escalating resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or normalization of ambivalence. The patient’s sustain talk about social drinking and readiness is met with ultimatums rather than gentle guidance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened, increasing patient resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands, ultimatums, and refusal to negotiate. The patient is repeatedly told what to do without invitation to share their perspective or co-create a plan. The psychiatrist’s language (“You don’t get to negotiate this,” “It’s not optional”) reinforces a power imbalance and undermines partnership.

**Summary:**  
No partnership is evident; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance that alienates the patient.

---

### Overall Global Ratings Summary  
The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally incompatible with motivational interviewing principles. The session is characterized by judgment, lack of empathy, coercion, and disregard for patient autonomy. This style is likely to increase resistance, reduce engagement, and diminish motivation for change.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly confrontational, directive, and judgmental, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, reflective listening, and acceptance.  
- The psychiatrist fails to support autonomy or collaborate with the patient.  
- Change talk is not evoked or reinforced; sustain talk is harshly confronted.  
- No affirmations, summaries, or collaborative planning are present.  
- The style is likely to increase resistance and reduce motivation.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directive commands to partnership. Invite the patient’s perspective and respect their autonomy.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect patient statements to demonstrate understanding and validate ambivalence.

3. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - Explore and amplify the patient’s own reasons and desires for change rather than imposing reasons.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance.

5. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize and affirm any positive intentions or steps the patient is willing to consider.

6. **Use Open Questions:**  
   - Encourage elaboration and exploration rather than yes/no answers.

7. **Emphasize Autonomy:**  
   - Explicitly support the patient’s right to choose and pace change.

8. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to set realistic, manageable goals and timelines.

9. **Avoid Confrontation and Ultimatums:**  
   - Replace “must” and “have to” language with invitational and supportive language.
