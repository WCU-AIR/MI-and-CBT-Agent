# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental, directive, and authoritarian stance throughout the session. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or respect for the patient’s perspective. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s feelings of overwhelm and resistance, labeling them as “excuses” and “hesitation.” The tone is harsh and confrontational, with commands such as “You have to quit,” “No ifs, ands, or buts,” and “This isn’t optional.” This approach clearly violates the MI principle of acceptance and creates a hostile environment that likely increases patient resistance rather than reducing it.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance or warmth, instead adopting a punitive and coercive tone that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**

For example, when the patient says, “It feels like a huge amount of stuff to do,” the psychiatrist responds with “No, you can’t just say it’s too much,” rather than exploring or reflecting the patient’s experience. This lack of empathy likely exacerbates the patient’s resistance and disengagement.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s emotional state and perspective, failing to build understanding or connection.

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**

The psychiatrist repeatedly issues ultimatums and demands compliance (“You have to,” “No excuses,” “Are you going to do it or not?”). ### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**

The psychiatrist maintains a highly authoritative, expert-driven stance throughout the session. There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist repeatedly overrides the patient’s expressions of difficulty and resistance with demands and ultimatums. **Summary:**

Change talk is neither evoked nor reinforced, and sustain talk is met with resistance rather than softening. ## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and judgmental, violating core MI principles.  
- There is no acceptance, empathy, or partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.  
- No use of open questions, reflections, affirmations, or summaries.  
- The patient’s emotional experience and ambivalence are ignored and dismissed.  
- The session likely increases patient resistance and disengagement.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from authoritarian commands to partnership. Invite the patient’s perspective and acknowledge their expertise in their own life.

2. **Demonstrate Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect and validate the patient’s feelings of overwhelm and ambivalence. Avoid labeling these as “excuses.”

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions to explore motivations and barriers. Reflect both content and emotion to deepen understanding.

4. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses any desire or reason for change, reflect and amplify these statements to build motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance and help the patient resolve ambivalence.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

7. **Collaborative Planning:**  
   - Work with the patient to develop achievable goals and plans, rather than issuing ultimatums.

8. **Avoid Confrontation:**  
   - Replace confrontational language with supportive, nonjudgmental dialogue.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session reflects a fundamental misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive style is counterproductive and likely to increase patient resistance. Immediate and substantial improvement is required to align with MI best practices and effectively support patient behavior change.
