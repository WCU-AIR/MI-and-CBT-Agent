# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental and confrontational stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do”—the tone is authoritarian and directive rather than supportive or respectful. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and attempts to explain his perspective, labeling them as “making excuses” or “being weak.” There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s hesitations and concerns are met with criticism rather than empathy or acceptance. This approach is likely to alienate the patient and reduce engagement.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance or respect. The interaction is dominated by judgment and directive commands, which is antithetical to MI principles.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence, social pressures, and fears of isolation, but the psychiatrist ignores these emotional nuances and instead pushes a rigid agenda. There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s feelings or struggles. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the complexity of the patient’s social context or the difficulty of change. The patient’s expressions of conflict and desire to change “in a way that makes sense” are dismissed outright.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is virtually absent. The psychiatrist does not demonstrate understanding or convey that the patient’s perspective is heard or valued.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, lacking collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands immediate commitment and compliance, using ultimatums (“You have to decide now,” “No excuses,” “Stop being weak”) rather than inviting the patient to explore his own motivations or values. The patient’s autonomy is ignored; the psychiatrist does not support self-direction or partnership but instead imposes a rigid “right way” to change. This approach contradicts the core MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent. The interaction is coercive and controlling rather than collaborative and autonomy-supportive.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the psychiatrist repeatedly demands commitment to change, there is little skillful evocation of change talk. The patient does express some change talk (e.g., “I want to avoid more trouble,” “I’m serious about that”), but the psychiatrist does not strategically reflect or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist pushes for immediate decisions and compliance, which may provoke resistance rather than deepen motivation. The patient’s tentative change talk is met with pressure rather than encouragement.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not effectively cultivated or reinforced. The psychiatrist’s approach risks shutting down motivation rather than fostering it.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (e.g., social pressures, fear of isolation, ambivalence about cutting off friends) is met with confrontation and dismissal. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s explanations as “excuses” and “weakness,” and demands immediate, absolute change. There are no double-sided reflections, no normalization of ambivalence, and no attempts to gently guide the patient toward resolving sustain talk. This harsh approach likely increases resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is not softened but rather confronted aggressively, undermining rapport and motivation.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritarian role, issuing commands and ultimatums without inviting collaboration or input. The patient’s views and concerns are repeatedly overridden. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or co-creation of goals. The psychiatrist’s language (“You have to,” “No excuses,” “Are you ready to commit right now?”) reflects a power imbalance and lack of partnership.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the interaction is dominated by control and directive demands.

---

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- Complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- Authoritarian, confrontational style undermines MI principles.  
- Failure to evoke or reinforce change talk skillfully.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from authoritarian commands to partnership and shared decision-making.  
   - Use inclusive language and invite the patient’s perspective.

2. **Demonstrate Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.  
   - Validate the difficulty of change and the patient’s social context.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Encourage elaboration and exploration of motivations and barriers.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen understanding.

4. **Affirm Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize the patient’s attempts to control drinking and desire to avoid trouble.  
   - Build confidence through genuine affirmations.

5. **Skillfully Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Reflect and reinforce statements favoring change.  
   - Explore values and goals that support change.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance.  
   - Gently guide the patient toward resolving ambivalence.

7. **Support Autonomy and Gradual Change:**  
   - Respect the patient’s readiness and pace.  
   - Collaboratively develop achievable steps and plans.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Ultimatums:**  
   - Replace “you have to” with “what do you think about…” or “how might you approach…”  
   - Encourage self-motivated change rather than coercion.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational approach undermines rapport, engagement, and motivation. There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, partnership, and skillful evocation of change talk. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a collaborative, autonomy-supportive, and empathetic style that respects the patient’s ambivalence and supports self-directed change. Without these changes, the risk of patient resistance and treatment failure is high.
