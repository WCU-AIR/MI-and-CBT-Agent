# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental and directive from the outset. The opening statement, “Today we’re going to get straight to the point about your health habits, and I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do to improve,” immediately establishes an authoritarian, non-collaborative stance. This sets a tone of control rather than acceptance. Throughout the session, the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s feelings and concerns, labeling her expressions of ambivalence and fear as “making excuses” or “stalling.” There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s vulnerability and struggle are met with criticism rather than support.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate respect or acceptance. The stance is judgmental and confrontational, which is contraindicated in MI and likely to alienate the patient.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist ignores or invalidates the patient’s emotional experience, such as her fear of failure, frustration with past quit attempts, and ambivalence about quitting. Instead of reflective listening or validating statements, the psychiatrist responds with blunt commands and accusations (“You’re just making excuses,” “No more delays”). The patient’s attempts to express her feelings and needs are repeatedly shut down. This lack of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s guarded and defensive responses.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows little effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective. Empathy is absent, replaced by a harsh, directive tone.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely authoritarian and directive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly insists on immediate commitment to a quit date, refusing to explore the patient’s ambivalence or readiness. The patient’s autonomy is undermined by demands and ultimatums rather than supported. There is no attempt to evoke the patient’s own motivations or values; instead, the psychiatrist imposes a rigid agenda. This approach is antithetical to the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent. The session is dominated by control and persuasion rather than partnership and evocation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the psychiatrist repeatedly pushes for a quit date, this is done through coercion rather than skillful evocation. The patient does express some change talk (e.g., “I want to quit,” “I’m scared about my health”), but the psychiatrist does not reinforce or amplify these statements. Instead, the focus is on demanding action rather than exploring or strengthening motivation. The patient’s change talk is overshadowed by the psychiatrist’s confrontational style, which risks eliciting resistance rather than genuine commitment.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not effectively cultivated or reinforced. The approach risks suppressing authentic motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently dismisses sustain talk as “excuses” or “stalling,” failing to acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s ambivalence or fears. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize sustain talk. Instead, sustain talk is met with confrontation and pressure, which likely increases resistance. The patient’s expressions of difficulty and hesitation are not softened or redirected in a supportive manner.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than skillfully softened or explored.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritarian role throughout the session. The patient is not invited to share her perspective or collaborate on goals. The psychiatrist repeatedly overrides the patient’s expressed needs and preferences, insisting on immediate compliance. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s autonomy. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent. The session is dominated by a top-down, directive approach.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Maintains focus on smoking cessation as the target behavior.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally incompatible with motivational interviewing principles.  
- The session is dominated by confrontation, lack of empathy, and disregard for patient autonomy.  
- The psychiatrist must develop skills in acceptance, reflective listening, evocation, and partnership.  
- Change talk and sustain talk must be handled with nuance and skill.  
- Planning should be collaborative and supportive, not coercive.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Begin sessions with open-ended questions and invitations to share the patient’s story.  
   - Avoid directives and commands; instead, invite the patient to explore their own motivations.

2. **Develop Reflective Listening Skills:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and build rapport.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen exploration of ambivalence.

3. **Validate Ambivalence and Sustain Talk:**  
   - Normalize the patient’s fears and struggles as part of the change process.  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge both sides of ambivalence.

4. **Skillfully Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, amplify and explore these statements.  
   - Avoid pushing for premature commitment; instead, build readiness.

5. **Support Autonomy and Empowerment:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.  
   - Collaborate on goal-setting and planning.

6. **Avoid Confrontation and Judgment:**  
   - Replace “you have to” statements with “what do you think about…” or “how would you like to approach…”  
   - Recognize that pressure often increases resistance.

7. **Use Summaries to Reinforce Motivation:**  
   - Periodically summarize the patient’s statements to consolidate motivation and clarify next steps.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a profound misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, empathy, and collaboration, which are essential for effective MI. The patient’s ambivalence and fears are dismissed rather than explored, and the insistence on immediate commitment without support is likely to increase resistance and reduce the likelihood of successful behavior change. Immediate and comprehensive retraining in MI fundamentals is strongly recommended to prevent harm and improve clinical effectiveness.
