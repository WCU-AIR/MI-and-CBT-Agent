# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive from the outset. The opening statement, “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do to get better,” sets an authoritarian tone that undermines acceptance. Throughout the session, the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s explanations and concerns, labeling behaviors as “problems” and “must stop” without acknowledging the patient’s perspective or circumstances. There is no evidence of warmth, positive regard, or normalization of the patient’s challenges. The patient’s attempts to explain time constraints and difficulties are met with admonishment rather than understanding. This pattern reflects inconsistent respect and frequent judgment, far from the unconditional positive regard expected in MI.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance is predominantly judgmental and directive, lacking the respectful, supportive tone necessary for effective MI.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses legitimate barriers such as a demanding IB schedule and band commitments, yet the psychiatrist dismisses these as “excuses” and insists on immediate behavior change. There are no reflective statements that validate the patient’s experience or feelings. The patient’s emotional cues (e.g., sighing, crossed arms) are ignored rather than explored. The psychiatrist’s responses focus on delivering commands rather than engaging in perspective-taking or reflective listening, resulting in a limited and superficial understanding of the patient’s situation.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is minimal, with little reflective listening or validation of the patient’s experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The session lacks any semblance of MI spirit. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do without collaboration or evocation. Autonomy is not supported; instead, the patient is pressured and confronted (“You don’t get to say ‘a little’,” “This isn’t optional”). There is no invitation for the patient to share their motivations or ideas, nor any attempt to evoke change talk or explore ambivalence. The interaction is directive and controlling, which is antithetical to the MI principles of partnership, evocation, and autonomy support.

**Summary:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with no evidence of MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient offers tentative commitments (“I’ll try to go for walks,” “I’ll cut down on fast food”), the psychiatrist dismisses “trying” and demands full commitment without exploring the patient’s ambivalence or reasons for change. There is no strategic amplification or reinforcement of the patient’s own motivations. Instead, the psychiatrist’s confrontational style likely suppresses further change talk. The patient’s language remains tentative and defensive, indicating that change talk is not being effectively cultivated.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and not reinforced; the psychiatrist’s style likely inhibits further motivational language.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The patient expresses barriers and ambivalence (“It’s tough to fit in exercise,” “Stopping fast food completely sounds unrealistic”), but the psychiatrist labels these as “excuses” and insists on immediate compliance. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change. This confrontational approach likely increases resistance rather than resolving ambivalence.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is confronted harshly rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands and ultimatums, with no invitation for the patient to participate in decision-making or agenda-setting. The patient’s expertise in their own life is ignored, and power is firmly held by the psychiatrist. The patient’s tentative responses are met with pressure rather than partnership. This dynamic undermines trust and engagement.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership; the session is expert-driven and directive.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Avoid directives and ultimatums. Invite the patient’s perspective and preferences.  
   - Use inclusive language to share power and build partnership.

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s statements and emotions to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Normalize challenges and validate the patient’s experience.

3. **Use Open Questions to Evoke Motivation:**  
   - Ask questions that invite exploration of reasons, desires, and confidence for change.  
   - Avoid yes/no or leading questions.

4. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Reflect and reinforce any statements favoring change.  
   - Explore ambivalence gently rather than confronting sustain talk.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge barriers while highlighting motivation.  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses.”

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.  
   - Ask permission before offering advice or information.

7. **Develop Collaborative Plans:**  
   - Co-create achievable goals with the patient.  
   - Summarize progress and commitments to reinforce motivation.

8. **Avoid Confrontation:**  
   - Replace commands with invitations and suggestions.  
   - Recognize that resistance often signals a need for a different approach.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamentally flawed approach to motivational interviewing. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines acceptance, empathy, and collaboration, resulting in minimal patient engagement and motivation. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt the core MI principles of partnership, evocation, and autonomy support, and develop skills in reflective listening, softening sustain talk, and cultivating change talk. Without these changes, the session risks alienating patients and failing to promote meaningful behavior change.
