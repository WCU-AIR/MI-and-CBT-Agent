# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach throughout the session is judgmental, confrontational, and directive rather than accepting. From the very first statement—“We need to get straight to the point—your drinking is causing problems, and you really have to cut back”—the tone is accusatory and lacks warmth or positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels Brandon’s behavior as excuses and rationalizations (“you’re just making excuses,” “stop rationalizing,” “weak excuse”), which is dismissive and likely to provoke defensiveness. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or normalization of ambivalence. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge Brandon’s perspective or struggles in a respectful way, instead repeatedly invalidating his feelings and minimizing his autonomy.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance is predominantly judgmental and directive, severely lacking in acceptance and positive regard. This undermines rapport and client engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal empathy or reflective listening. There are no reflections or attempts to understand Brandon’s perspective beyond challenging it. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge Brandon’s ambivalence or emotional experience, nor does he validate the difficulty of change. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with confrontational questions and statements, ignoring Brandon’s expressed feelings about stress relief and coping. The lack of empathy is evident in the repeated demands and dismissive language, which likely increases Brandon’s defensiveness.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is virtually absent. The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate understanding or convey that Brandon’s perspective is heard and valued.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is completely missing. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling Brandon what he “has to” do and demanding yes/no answers. There is no evocation of Brandon’s own motivations or values, no support for autonomy, and no partnership. The psychiatrist ignores Brandon’s ambivalence and repeatedly pushes for compliance rather than exploring his readiness or reasons for change. This approach is antithetical to MI principles.

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by a directive, confrontational style with no collaboration or autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although Brandon offers some tentative change talk (e.g., “I’ll try,” “I want to do better”), the psychiatrist dismisses these as “excuses” or “wishy-washy” rather than reflecting or amplifying them. There are no open-ended questions or reflections designed to deepen Brandon’s own motivations or explore his ambivalence constructively. The psychiatrist’s repeated demands for commitment without eliciting Brandon’s own reasons or confidence undermine the cultivation of change talk.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but ignored or invalidated rather than cultivated or reinforced.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. Brandon’s explanations about stress relief and social context are labeled as “weak excuses” and “rationalizations.” The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or validate the legitimacy of sustain talk. Instead, sustain talk is met with repeated challenges and ultimatums, which likely increase resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened or explored.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands and demands yes/no answers, ignoring Brandon’s expressed concerns and ambivalence. The psychiatrist does not invite Brandon to co-create goals or plans, nor does he acknowledge Brandon’s expertise in his own life. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
The session is authoritarian and expert-driven, lacking any meaningful partnership.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (drinking).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is highly confrontational and directive, violating core MI principles.  
- There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to use reflective listening, open questions, or affirmations.  
- Change talk is ignored or invalidated rather than cultivated.  
- Sustain talk is confronted harshly rather than softened.  
- The rigid, ultimatum-driven approach is likely to increase resistance and reduce client engagement.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling or blaming language.  
   - Express genuine respect and positive regard for the client’s experience.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Validate ambivalence and the difficulty of change.

3. **Shift from Directive to Collaborative:**  
   - Invite the client’s perspective and preferences.  
   - Co-create goals and plans rather than imposing ultimatums.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Use open-ended questions and reflections to draw out client motivations.  
   - Reinforce any statements favoring change.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and validate the client’s reasons for maintaining current behavior.  
   - Gently guide toward exploring ambivalence rather than confronting.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the client’s right and ability to choose.  
   - Avoid pressuring for immediate commitments.

7. **Avoid Yes/No Ultimatums:**  
   - Use open questions and reflective statements to explore readiness.  
   - Allow the client space to express ambivalence and uncertainty.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental style undermines rapport, engagement, and motivation. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically change approach—embracing acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and skillful evocation of change talk. Without these changes, the risk of alienating clients and impeding change is high.
