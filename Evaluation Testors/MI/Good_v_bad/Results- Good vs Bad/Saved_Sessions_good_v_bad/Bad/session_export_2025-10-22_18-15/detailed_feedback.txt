# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive from the outset. Statements such as “I’m here to help you fix this drinking problem” and “You really need to cut down if it is” communicate a lack of unconditional positive regard. The tone is confrontational rather than supportive, and the psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s reasons without validating his experience or feelings. There is no evidence of warmth or acceptance; instead, the patient is positioned as someone who must comply. This is inconsistent with MI principles and risks alienating the patient.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates limited respect and acceptance, often adopting a judgmental and prescriptive stance that undermines rapport and client openness.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The questions and statements are primarily focused on compliance and behavior change without exploring the patient’s ambivalence or emotional experience. For example, “Why haven’t you done that already?” is accusatory rather than curious. There is no reflective listening or validation of the patient’s struggles with peer pressure or social connection. The patient’s expressions of difficulty and social context are largely ignored or dismissed.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist fails to demonstrate accurate understanding or convey genuine concern for the patient’s lived experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, lacking collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You have to cut down,” “You have to take this seriously”) and challenges the patient’s reasons without inviting exploration or partnership. There is no evidence of eliciting the patient’s own motivations or supporting his autonomy. The stance is coercive rather than collaborative, which is antithetical to the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The MI spirit is absent; the psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive approach that undermines client engagement and motivation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist attempts to elicit change talk by asking about drinking quantity and reasons for not cutting down. However, these efforts are superficial and framed in a confrontational manner (“Why haven’t you done that already?”), which likely triggers defensiveness rather than genuine change talk. The psychiatrist does not reinforce or amplify any change talk expressed by the patient, nor does he explore the patient’s own reasons or desires for change in depth.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and not skillfully cultivated; the confrontational style likely inhibits deeper exploration of motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften or strategically respond to sustain talk. Instead, sustain talk is met with confrontation and challenge (“You have to take this seriously before it gets worse”). There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s ambivalence or social concerns. This approach risks reinforcing sustain talk and resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with direct confrontation rather than softening or strategic redirection, which is counterproductive.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The session is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and directives, with no invitation for the patient to share his perspective or co-create goals. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of advice rather than an active participant. This power imbalance is stark and undermines the therapeutic alliance.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritarian role without fostering collaboration or shared decision-making.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist asks direct questions about drinking quantity, which is relevant diagnostically.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance to build rapport.  
- Empathy and reflective listening need to be integrated to understand the patient’s perspective.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—must replace the authoritarian approach.  
- Change talk should be elicited and amplified through open-ended questions and affirmations.  
- Sustain talk requires strategic softening rather than confrontation.  
- Partnership and shared agenda-setting are essential for effective MI.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Supportive Stance:**  
   Use language that conveys respect and unconditional positive regard. Avoid labeling or blaming.

2. **Enhance Empathy and Reflective Listening:**  
   Reflect the patient’s statements to demonstrate understanding and validate feelings, especially ambivalence.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   Collaborate with the patient, evoke their own motivations, and support autonomy rather than issuing ultimatums.

4. **Use Open-Ended Questions Effectively:**  
   Ask questions that invite elaboration and exploration of the patient’s values, concerns, and goals.

5. **Skillfully Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and reinforce these statements to deepen motivation.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and validate the patient’s struggles to reduce resistance.

7. **Incorporate Affirmations:**  
   Recognize patient strengths, efforts, and intentions to build confidence.

8. **Collaboratively Plan and Summarize:**  
   Work with the patient to develop achievable goals and summarize key points to consolidate motivation.

9. **Avoid Confrontation and Directives:**  
   Replace commands and ultimatums with invitations and collaborative problem-solving.

Without these changes, the session risks increasing patient resistance and failing to support meaningful behavior change.
