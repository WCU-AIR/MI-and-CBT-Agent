# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive, lacking warmth or genuine acceptance. From the outset, the tone is authoritarian and dismissive of the patient’s perspective (“I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” “No excuses,” “You have to commit now”). There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or validation of the patient’s feelings or ambivalence. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s concerns and struggles, labeling them as “excuses” and showing impatience. This creates a hostile environment that likely inhibits open disclosure and trust. The psychiatrist fails to normalize the patient’s ambivalence or acknowledge the difficulty of change, which is essential for acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance is largely judgmental and directive, with minimal respect or support for the patient’s experience. This severely undermines the spirit of motivational interviewing.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is little to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective or emotional state. Instead, the responses are confrontational and dismissive. For example, when the patient expresses stress and ambivalence about drinking and life challenges, the psychiatrist ignores these cues and insists on immediate compliance. The patient’s humor, hesitation, and expressions of difficulty are met with commands rather than reflective listening or validation. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to explore the patient’s feelings or struggles, which would have fostered rapport and deeper understanding.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy, resulting in a cold, confrontational interaction that likely alienates the patient.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session lacks the fundamental MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do without eliciting the patient’s own motivations or ideas. Autonomy is explicitly denied (“This isn’t optional,” “You don’t get to pick and choose”). The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s reasons for change or explore ambivalence but instead pushes for immediate commitment through pressure and commands. This approach is antithetical to MI principles and likely to provoke resistance rather than engagement.

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by a directive, controlling approach with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or respect for autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the psychiatrist repeatedly demands commitment to change, there is no skillful evocation or reinforcement of change talk. The patient offers tentative change talk (“I’m willing to try,” “I want to commit”), but the psychiatrist does not amplify or explore these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist challenges the patient’s ambivalence harshly, which risks suppressing further change talk. There is no strategic use of reflections or open questions to deepen the patient’s motivation or confidence. The approach is more about compliance than cultivating genuine change talk.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and not skillfully reinforced; the confrontational style likely inhibits further exploration of motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently confronts and dismisses sustain talk rather than softening or exploring it. When the patient expresses ambivalence, stress-related drinking, or difficulty, the psychiatrist labels these as “excuses” and demands immediate change. There are no double-sided reflections, no normalization of ambivalence, and no attempts to reduce resistance. This harsh approach likely increases sustain talk covertly and damages rapport.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than skillful softening or exploration.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands and ultimatums, with no invitation for the patient to share their perspective or co-create a plan. The psychiatrist repeatedly denies the patient’s autonomy and expertise in their own life. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of orders rather than an active participant. This power imbalance is antithetical to MI’s partnership principle.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven role.

---

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacking in empathy or acceptance.  
- There is no evidence of MI spirit: no collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support.  
- Change talk is not skillfully cultivated or reinforced.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian, confrontational, and lacks empathy.  
- No use of MI techniques such as reflective listening, affirmations, or open questions.  
- The patient’s autonomy and ambivalence are ignored or dismissed.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from authoritarian commands to a partnership approach that respects the patient’s autonomy and expertise.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings, ambivalence, and struggles. Normalize the difficulty of change.

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   Increase the use of open-ended questions to explore the patient’s perspective and complex reflections to deepen understanding.

4. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Efforts:**  
   Recognize and affirm the patient’s willingness to consider change and their existing coping strategies.

5. **Evoke Change Talk Skillfully:**  
   When the patient expresses desire or willingness to change, reflect and amplify these statements to build motivation.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and validation to reduce resistance and explore ambivalence without confrontation.

7. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Explicitly acknowledge the patient’s right to choose and control the pace and nature of change.

8. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   Work with the patient to create realistic, achievable steps toward reducing drinking, rather than demanding immediate compliance.

9. **Summarize Progress and Motivation:**  
   Use summaries to consolidate change talk and reinforce commitment.

10. **Avoid Confrontation and Ultimatums:**  
    Replace harsh commands with supportive, motivational dialogue to foster engagement and reduce resistance.
