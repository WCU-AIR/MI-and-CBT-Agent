# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental, directive, and authoritarian stance from the outset. The opening statement, “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” immediately establishes a controlling tone that dismisses the patient’s autonomy and experience. Throughout the session, the psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s feelings and struggles, using language such as “You have to,” “No excuses,” and “You can’t keep making excuses.” There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s expressions of exhaustion and hopelessness are met with impatience and reprimand rather than empathy or acceptance. This approach is likely to increase resistance and disengagement rather than foster collaboration.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is highly judgmental and directive, lacking any genuine acceptance or respect for the patient’s experience.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist fails to reflect or validate the patient’s emotional state, such as exhaustion, hopelessness, or reliance on self-harm as a coping mechanism. Instead, the psychiatrist dismisses these expressions and pushes for immediate behavior change without understanding or exploring the patient’s perspective. The patient’s statements like “Cutting myself is the only thing that helps” are met with commands to stop immediately, without acknowledgment of the underlying distress. The tone is harsh and lacks any attempt to convey understanding or compassion.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist does not demonstrate reflective listening or emotional attunement, missing critical opportunities to connect with the patient’s experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely authoritarian and directive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You have to,” “No more excuses,” “Just do it”) and frames the interaction as compliance rather than partnership. There is no invitation for the patient’s input or exploration of their motivations or values. Autonomy is explicitly denied, and the patient is pressured to commit to changes without readiness or engagement. This approach is antithetical to the spirit of motivational interviewing.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive stance that undermines collaboration and autonomy, failing to embody the MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient occasionally expresses tentative willingness (“Maybe I could try throwing out the drinks”), the psychiatrist ignores these openings and instead focuses on demanding immediate compliance. There are no reflections or amplifications of the patient’s change talk, nor are there strategic questions to deepen motivation or explore ambivalence. The patient’s expressions of desire to stop self-harm are dismissed rather than nurtured.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is neither effectively elicited nor reinforced; the psychiatrist misses critical opportunities to build motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The patient’s statements about exhaustion, reliance on self-harm, and feelings of being stuck are met with “No excuses,” “You have to stop,” and “You can’t keep making excuses.” There are no double-sided reflections or validations of the patient’s ambivalence. This confrontational approach likely entrenches sustain talk and resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than skillfully softened or explored.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands and directives, with no invitation for the patient’s perspective or shared decision-making. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of orders rather than an active participant. The psychiatrist repeatedly overrides the patient’s expressions of readiness and autonomy, undermining trust and rapport.

**Summary:**  
There is no evidence of partnership; the psychiatrist assumes an authoritarian expert role.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- None identified.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally incompatible with motivational interviewing principles.  
- The session is characterized by authoritarianism, lack of empathy, and failure to engage the patient’s perspective.  
- The psychiatrist must develop skills in reflective listening, acceptance, evocation, and partnership.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and sustain talk must be met with validation and strategic softening rather than confrontation.  
- The psychiatrist should learn to support autonomy and collaboratively explore readiness and planning.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Replace commands with open-ended questions and invitations to explore the patient’s experience and motivations.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings and struggles without judgment. Validate the difficulty of change.

3. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses any desire or willingness, reflect and amplify these statements to build motivation.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and affirm ambivalence to reduce resistance.

5. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

6. **Use Affirmations:**  
   - Recognize and reinforce the patient’s strengths, efforts, and courage in facing difficult issues.

7. **Collaborative Planning:**  
   - Develop achievable, patient-driven goals rather than imposing directives.

8. **Seek Training in MI Principles:**  
   - The psychiatrist should undertake formal training and supervision in motivational interviewing to develop these core skills.

---

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session reflects a profound misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style is likely to increase patient resistance and disengagement, undermining any potential for behavior change. Immediate and substantial improvement in MI skills is essential to provide effective, patient-centered care.
