# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental, directive, and authoritarian from the outset. The opening statement, “Today we’re going to focus on getting you to make some real changes for your health. I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” immediately establishes a controlling and non-collaborative tone. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or respect for the patient’s autonomy or perspective. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s concerns and feelings, using language that is coercive (“No, no, you don’t get it,” “You have to,” “This isn’t optional”), which is antithetical to acceptance. The patient’s hesitancy and ambivalence are met with impatience and reprimand rather than understanding or support.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate acceptance, instead adopting a confrontational and authoritarian stance that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is a near-total absence of empathy throughout the session. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or reflect the patient’s feelings, concerns, or ambivalence. The patient expresses discomfort, stress, and the difficulty of making changes, yet the psychiatrist ignores these cues and responds with commands and demands. For example, when the patient says, “I’m already juggling a lot,” or “I want to do better, but I need to do it at a pace I can manage,” the psychiatrist dismisses these statements and insists on immediate compliance. No reflective listening or validation of the patient’s experience is present.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal to no effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, severely limiting the therapeutic alliance.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, directive style, repeatedly issuing ultimatums and demands without inviting the patient’s input or exploring their motivations. The patient is not treated as an equal partner; instead, the psychiatrist assumes an expert role, dictating what must be done. There is no evocation of the patient’s own reasons for change, no support for autonomy, and no collaborative agenda-setting. The repeated insistence on “no excuses,” “no negotiation,” and “this isn’t optional” directly contradicts MI principles.

**Summary:**  
The session is characterized by a coercive, expert-driven approach that negates the core MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient occasionally expresses tentative willingness (“I’m willing to try,” “I want to do better”), these statements are ignored or overridden by the psychiatrist’s demands. There is no strategic reflection or amplification of change talk to strengthen motivation. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts or challenges the patient’s ambivalence, which likely suppresses further change talk. The patient’s expressions of readiness to start small or need for manageable steps are dismissed rather than explored.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not cultivated; the psychiatrist’s style likely inhibits its development.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk—patient’s reasons for maintaining current behavior or ambivalence—is met with confrontation rather than softening. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s concerns and reasons for hesitation, using language that pressures and shames (“No, you don’t get to pick and choose,” “No room for ‘ease into it’ here”). There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s ambivalence. Instead, sustain talk is met with ultimatums and demands, which likely increase resistance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk, instead reinforcing resistance through confrontational responses.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist repeatedly asserts control, refuses to negotiate, and ignores the patient’s attempts to express their perspective or negotiate a manageable plan. The patient explicitly asks for a collaborative approach (“Can’t we talk about what might work for me?” “Can you help me figure out a way to make this work?”), but the psychiatrist refuses. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
The session is authoritarian, with no meaningful partnership or shared decision-making.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must radically shift from an authoritarian, directive style to one that embodies acceptance, empathy, and collaboration.  
- Reflective listening and validation of patient ambivalence are essential.  
- The spirit of MI—evoking motivation and supporting autonomy—must replace coercion and ultimatums.  
- Change talk should be actively cultivated and sustain talk softened through strategic reflections.  
- Partnership and shared agenda-setting are critical to engage the patient effectively.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- None identified. The psychiatrist’s approach is counterproductive to motivational interviewing principles.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist must abandon the authoritarian, directive style.  
- Develop skills in reflective listening, empathy, and acceptance.  
- Embrace the MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.  
- Learn to recognize and cultivate change talk while softening sustain talk.  
- Use open-ended questions and affirmations to engage and empower the patient.  
- Collaboratively develop realistic, patient-centered plans.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from telling and directing to partnering with the patient. Use language that invites dialogue and respects the patient’s expertise in their own life.

2. **Practice Reflective Listening:**  
   Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and validate the patient’s experience.

3. **Evoke Patient Motivation:**  
   Ask open-ended questions that explore the patient’s reasons, desires, and confidence for change.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and affirmations to acknowledge ambivalence without reinforcing resistance.

5. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Explicitly affirm the patient’s right to choose and control the pace and nature of change.

6. **Avoid Ultimatums and Demands:**  
   Replace coercive language with invitations and collaborative goal-setting.

7. **Develop Planning Skills:**  
   Work with the patient to create manageable, realistic steps that fit their life context.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, stifles motivation, and likely increases resistance. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically change approach—embracing empathy, acceptance, collaboration, and evocation—to support meaningful, sustainable behavior change.
