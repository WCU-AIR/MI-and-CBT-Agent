# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental, directive, and authoritarian stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“I’ll tell you exactly what you should do”—the tone is controlling rather than supportive. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s perspective, invalidates his feelings, and labels his behavior as irresponsible without empathy or understanding. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s resistance is met with confrontation and pressure rather than acceptance. This approach is antithetical to MI principles and likely to increase defensiveness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate respect or acceptance. The interaction is dominated by judgment and directive commands, which undermine rapport and client engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence, social context, and concerns about autonomy, but the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead pushes a rigid agenda. There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s feelings or experiences. The psychiatrist’s responses are blunt and dismissive, lacking any nuanced understanding of the patient’s ambivalence or social pressures. This absence of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s resistance.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is essentially absent. The psychiatrist does not demonstrate reflective listening or perspective-taking, missing critical opportunities to build rapport and explore ambivalence.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues ultimatums (“This is non-negotiable,” “No more excuses”) and demands compliance. There is no invitation for the patient to share his own motivations or values, nor any support for autonomy. The psychiatrist’s approach is coercive rather than collaborative, which is the opposite of the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive stance that undermines client autonomy and engagement.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist attempts to elicit commitment to change by repeatedly asking for a “yes or no” answer about limiting drinks. However, these efforts are coercive and lack skillful evocation. The psychiatrist does not explore or amplify the patient’s own reasons or desires for change, nor does he reinforce any spontaneous change talk. The patient’s eventual tentative commitment (“I’ll give it a shot”) emerges despite the psychiatrist’s style, not because of it. There is no strategic reinforcement or exploration of the patient’s ambivalence or motivations.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and not skillfully cultivated. The coercive approach likely limits genuine motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently confronts and challenges sustain talk rather than softening or exploring it. The patient’s resistance, ambivalence, and rationalizations are met with demands and ultimatums rather than reflective or double-sided statements. There is no attempt to normalize ambivalence or validate the patient’s perspective, which would be essential to reduce sustain talk’s impact. Instead, the psychiatrist escalates pressure, likely increasing resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and escalated rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a hierarchical, expert-driven stance throughout the session. There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s input and insists on compliance with a rigid plan. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of orders rather than an active collaborator. This power imbalance is counterproductive to MI principles.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent. The psychiatrist dominates the interaction and fails to engage the patient as an equal partner.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian, confrontational, and directive, which is counterproductive in MI.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directing and commanding to partnering with the patient. Use inclusive language and invite the patient’s input.

2. **Increase Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and experiences. Avoid judgmental or confrontational language.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Encourage the patient to explore and articulate their own motivations, concerns, and ambivalence.

4. **Softening Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance and explore ambivalence.

5. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right and ability to make their own choices. Avoid ultimatums and coercion.

6. **Skillfully Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - When change talk arises, reflect and amplify it to strengthen motivation.

7. **Collaborative Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Work with the patient to develop realistic, mutually agreed-upon goals and summarize progress to reinforce motivation.

8. **Avoid Confrontation:**  
   - Replace confrontational statements with curiosity and empathy to reduce defensiveness.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, increases resistance, and fails to evoke genuine motivation for change. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a collaborative, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive approach that skillfully explores ambivalence and cultivates change talk. Without these changes, the risk of harm and disengagement is high.
