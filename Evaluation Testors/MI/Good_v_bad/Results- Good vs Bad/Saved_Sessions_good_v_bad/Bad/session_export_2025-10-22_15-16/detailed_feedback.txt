# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive from the outset. The opening statement, “I’m here to tell you exactly what you need to do to get better,” sets a controlling and authoritarian tone rather than one of acceptance or partnership. Throughout the session, the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and concerns, labeling her reasons as “excuses” and showing impatience with her perspective. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth; instead, the psychiatrist uses language that could be perceived as shaming (“You really need to stop wasting money,” “No excuses,” “You’re just wasting money and risking your future”). This undermines rapport and acceptance.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate respect or unconditional positive regard. The stance is largely judgmental and directive, which is counterproductive in MI.  

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence, social context, and emotional conflict, but the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with commands and challenges rather than reflective listening. For example, when the patient shares the social importance of pokies and her fear of missing out, the psychiatrist responds with “No, no, you’re overcomplicating it,” dismissing her feelings. There are no complex or simple reflections that validate or explore the patient’s emotional experience or ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s responses lack warmth and do not convey understanding of the patient’s internal conflict.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist does not accurately or consistently convey understanding of the patient’s perspective or emotional state.  

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is highly authoritarian and directive, with the psychiatrist repeatedly telling the patient what she “has to” do and demanding commitment without collaboration. There is no evidence of evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values beyond superficial acknowledgment. Autonomy is not supported; instead, the psychiatrist pressures the patient to “commit” and “be firm” without exploring her readiness or ambivalence. The patient’s attempts to negotiate a more gradual or realistic plan are dismissed outright. This is the antithesis of MI spirit, which emphasizes partnership, evocation, and autonomy support.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive stance with no collaboration or evocation, violating core MI principles.  

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient spontaneously offers some change talk (e.g., “I want to stop,” “I want to prove to myself I can do it,” “I want that future”), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or amplify this language. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on commands and demands, missing opportunities to explore the patient’s motivations or reinforce her statements. For example, when the patient links quitting to her dream of traveling to Egypt, the psychiatrist dismisses this as “overcomplicating it” rather than exploring it further. The psychiatrist does ask for a commitment but does so in a coercive manner rather than evoking genuine motivation.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not effectively cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist’s responses.  

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s ambivalence and explanations as “excuses” and “overcomplicating,” which reinforces sustain talk defensively rather than softening it. There are no double-sided reflections or strategic responses that acknowledge the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change. The patient’s sustain talk about social pressures and enjoyment is met with confrontation rather than empathy or reframing. This approach likely increases resistance rather than reducing sustain talk.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead confronts and challenges it harshly, increasing resistance.  

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The session is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and commands, with no negotiation or shared decision-making. The patient’s suggestions for a more gradual approach or alternative social activities are dismissed or ignored. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands promises and commitments without inviting the patient’s input or acknowledging her expertise in her own life. This creates a clear power imbalance and undermines partnership.  

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership or shared power; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Begin sessions with open-ended questions and invitations to share the patient’s story.  
   - Avoid telling the patient what to do; instead, explore their perspective and readiness.  

2. **Increase Empathy and Reflective Listening:**  
   - Use simple and complex reflections to validate feelings and ambivalence.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to deepen understanding.  

3. **Support Autonomy and Evoke Motivation:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s control over choices and respect their pace.  
   - Evoke change talk by exploring values, goals, and reasons for change.  

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change and validate the patient’s struggle.  

5. **Use Affirmations and Positive Reinforcement:**  
   - Recognize the patient’s strengths, efforts, and insights.  
   - Affirm any movement toward change, no matter how small.  

6. **Collaboratively Develop Plans:**  
   - Invite the patient to co-create realistic, achievable steps.  
   - Summarize and reinforce commitment in a supportive manner.  

7. **Avoid Confrontation and Judgment:**  
   - Replace “you have to” and “no excuses” with curiosity and partnership language.  
   - Focus on eliciting the patient’s own reasons and plans rather than imposing directives.  

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational, and directive style undermines rapport, empathy, and collaboration. The patient’s ambivalence and change talk are dismissed rather than evoked or reinforced. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a client-centered, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive approach that fosters partnership and gently guides the patient toward change. Without these changes, the session risks increasing resistance and disengagement, ultimately harming patient outcomes.
