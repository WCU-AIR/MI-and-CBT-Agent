# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a largely judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist imposes a rigid agenda ("we’re going to focus on what you need to change today") and demands yes/no answers, which limits patient autonomy and expression. The repeated use of phrases like "you have to," "no exceptions," and "no ifs, ands, or buts" communicates a lack of unconditional positive regard and respect for the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and struggles, labeling them as "excuses" rather than valid concerns. There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or acceptance of the patient’s lived experience. The patient’s attempts to explain barriers and ambivalence are met with reprimands rather than understanding.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly authoritarian and confrontational, undermining acceptance. There is minimal evidence of respect or support for the patient’s autonomy or struggles.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows limited effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence, stress, and realistic barriers to change, but the psychiatrist responds with repeated commands and challenges rather than reflective listening or validation. For example, when the patient explains that quitting cold turkey is overwhelming, the psychiatrist dismisses this as "making excuses." There are no complex or simple reflections that capture the patient’s feelings or struggles. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the emotional difficulty or the patient’s expressed fears and instead focuses solely on the medical facts. This results in a lack of emotional attunement and missed opportunities to build rapport and trust.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is minimal, with no reflective listening or validation of the patient’s experience. The psychiatrist prioritizes persuasion over understanding.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is almost entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, directive stance, repeatedly issuing ultimatums and demands. There is no evidence of partnership or evoking the patient’s own motivations or values. Autonomy is explicitly denied, with the psychiatrist insisting on compliance ("you don’t get to negotiate," "no halfway here"). The patient’s attempts to negotiate or express ambivalence are met with resistance and dismissal. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient to explore their own reasons for change or to set their own goals.  

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by a controlling, prescriptive approach that contradicts the core MI principles of collaboration and autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient offers some change talk (e.g., willingness to cut down, desire to quit eventually), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce this language. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s ambivalence and partial progress without amplifying any statements of desire, ability, or reasons for change. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore the patient’s motivations or to ask open-ended questions that might deepen commitment. The focus remains on compliance rather than eliciting the patient’s own arguments for change.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not cultivated or amplified. The psychiatrist’s approach tends to suppress rather than evoke motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk—patient language favoring the status quo—is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s explanations as "excuses" and repeatedly demands immediate, total change. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the patient’s ambivalence or legitimate concerns. The psychiatrist’s responses likely increase resistance rather than reduce sustain talk.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened or explored, undermining the patient’s engagement.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands, ultimatums, and a lack of shared decision-making. The patient is repeatedly told what to do without input or negotiation. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s expertise about their own life and circumstances. The power differential is emphasized rather than minimized.  

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership or shared power; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian role.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist consistently emphasizes the seriousness of the patient’s health condition.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must develop acceptance, empathy, and a collaborative spirit.  
- Reflections and affirmations are absent; these are critical to building rapport and evoking motivation.  
- The approach is overly directive and confrontational, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- Change talk is not cultivated; sustain talk is harshly confronted.  
- Partnership and autonomy support are lacking.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist consistently emphasizes the medical seriousness of the patient’s condition.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian, confrontational, and directive, which is antithetical to motivational interviewing principles.  
- There is a critical lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to use reflective listening, affirmations, or open questions to engage the patient.  
- Change talk is not cultivated; sustain talk is harshly confronted, increasing resistance.  
- The patient’s autonomy and expertise are ignored, undermining motivation and engagement.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from authoritarian commands to partnership and shared decision-making.  
   - Invite the patient’s input and respect their expertise about their own life.

2. **Increase Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and struggles.  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as "excuses."  
   - Affirm patient strengths and efforts, even if partial.

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Replace closed yes/no questions with open-ended questions to explore ambivalence and motivation.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to deepen understanding.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and amplify these statements.  
   - Explore confidence and commitment language.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Explicitly affirm the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.  
   - Avoid ultimatums and demands.

7. **Collaborative Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Work with the patient to develop realistic, achievable goals.  
   - Summarize progress and motivation to reinforce commitment.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines acceptance, empathy, partnership, and evocation of change talk. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, autonomy-supportive approach, use reflective listening and affirmations, and skillfully handle ambivalence. Without these changes, the patient is unlikely to engage meaningfully or make sustained behavior change.
