# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive from the outset. The opening statement, “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do to quit,” immediately establishes an authoritarian tone, which undermines acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s expressed ambivalence and struggles, labeling them as “excuses” and “dragging your feet.” There is no evidence of warmth, respect, or unconditional positive regard. The patient’s attempts to explain his situation and feelings are met with criticism rather than understanding. This creates a confrontational rather than supportive environment, which is counterproductive in motivational interviewing.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance and instead adopts a judgmental, directive stance that likely alienates the patient.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses significant ambivalence and competing priorities (e.g., focusing on alcohol reduction and family), but the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these concerns. There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s feelings or struggles. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly pushes a rigid agenda without acknowledging the patient’s emotional state or rationale. The lack of empathy is evident in the repeated “you have to” and “no excuses” statements, which do not convey understanding.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist does not demonstrate accurate or compassionate understanding of the patient’s perspective.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is highly authoritarian and directive, lacking collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly commands the patient to “just do it,” “no ifs or buts,” and “no more delays,” which contradicts the MI spirit. There is no partnership or invitation for the patient to explore his own motivations or concerns. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s expressed ambivalence and need for pacing, instead insisting on immediate, uncompromising action. This approach is antithetical to MI principles.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive stance that undermines client autonomy and collaboration.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient does express some change talk (e.g., willingness to try patches, desire to get kids back), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or amplify this language. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on commands and demands, missing opportunities to explore the patient’s motivations or reinforce his statements. The patient’s tentative commitments are met with pressure rather than encouragement or reflection. The psychiatrist fails to strategically respond to change talk to deepen motivation or confidence.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but poorly cultivated; the psychiatrist misses chances to reinforce or evoke further motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (e.g., patient’s ambivalence about quitting smoking now, fear of relapse) is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s concerns as “excuses” and “dragging feet,” which likely increases resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or normalization of ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s approach likely entrenches sustain talk rather than softening it.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and escalated by confrontational responses; no evidence of skillful softening.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The session is dominated by commands and ultimatums, with no invitation for the patient to participate in decision-making or express preferences. The psychiatrist repeatedly overrides the patient’s concerns and autonomy, demanding immediate compliance. There is no evidence of shared power or mutual respect.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is overly confrontational and directive, undermining MI principles.  
- There is a critical lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses” or “dragging feet.”  
   - Use warm, supportive language to build rapport.

2. **Use Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s ambivalence and struggles to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Validate the difficulty of change and the patient’s efforts so far.

3. **Collaborate and Support Autonomy:**  
   - Invite the patient to share his thoughts and preferences.  
   - Avoid ultimatums; instead, explore readiness and willingness.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses motivation, reflect and reinforce it.  
   - Ask open-ended questions to deepen exploration of reasons and confidence.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Normalize the patient’s concerns and fears.

6. **Develop Collaborative Plans:**  
   - Work with the patient to create achievable, stepwise goals.  
   - Summarize progress and plans to consolidate motivation.

7. **Avoid Authoritarian Language:**  
   - Replace commands with invitations and suggestions.  
   - Use permission-seeking before offering advice or information.
