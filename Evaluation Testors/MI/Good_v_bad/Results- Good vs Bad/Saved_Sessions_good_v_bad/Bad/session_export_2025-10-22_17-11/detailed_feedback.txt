# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance is predominantly judgmental and directive rather than accepting. From the outset, the tone is authoritarian (“I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” “You really need to cut those out immediately”), which undermines unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient with “Why haven’t you done this sooner?” and “You have to commit,” which conveys disapproval and blame rather than support. There is minimal evidence of warmth or empathy, and the patient’s hesitations are met with pressure rather than understanding. This approach risks alienating the patient and reducing openness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate unconditional positive regard, instead adopting a confrontational and directive style that diminishes acceptance.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows limited effort to understand the patient’s perspective or emotional experience. While the patient expresses ambivalence and difficulty changing long-standing habits, the psychiatrist dismisses these concerns and pushes for immediate compliance. There are no reflective statements that capture the patient’s feelings of overwhelm, fear of deprivation, or desire to stay healthy for retirement. The psychiatrist does not validate the patient’s struggle or acknowledge the complexity of behavior change, resulting in a lack of emotional attunement.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is minimal; the psychiatrist misses opportunities to reflect and validate the patient’s ambivalence and emotional state.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive approach with frequent commands and ultimatums (“You have to stop,” “No excuses,” “This isn’t optional”). There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore his motivations beyond superficial acknowledgment. Instead, the patient is positioned as someone who must comply or face consequences. This style is antithetical to the spirit of MI, which emphasizes partnership, evocation, and autonomy support.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive stance, lacking any collaborative or autonomy-supportive elements essential to MI.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient offers some change talk (e.g., willingness to try cutting back, desire to stay healthy for retirement), the psychiatrist does little to evoke, reinforce, or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on commands and reprimands, which may suppress further change talk. There are no strategic reflections or questions designed to deepen the patient’s own motivations or explore ambivalence constructively. The patient’s tentative commitments are met with pressure rather than encouragement.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not effectively cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist’s approach.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften or skillfully respond to sustain talk. Instead, sustain talk (patient’s hesitations, concerns about difficulty, and ambivalence) is met with confrontation and demands for immediate change. There are no double-sided reflections or normalization of ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s responses risk increasing resistance rather than reducing sustain talk.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and pressure, lacking any softening or strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and directives, with no invitation for the patient to share his perspective or co-create a plan. The patient’s autonomy is undermined by ultimatums and demands. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership or shared decision-making; the psychiatrist assumes an authoritarian role.

---

### **Overall Global Ratings Summary**  
The psychiatrist’s approach in this diagnostic session is largely inconsistent with motivational interviewing principles. The session is directive, confrontational, and lacks empathy, acceptance, and collaboration. Change talk is minimally evoked and sustain talk is met with pressure rather than softening. The patient’s ambivalence and emotional experience are not explored or validated. This style risks disengagement and resistance, undermining the goal of supporting behavior change.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Maintains focus on relevant health behaviors (diet, exercise).  
- Patient expresses some change talk and motivation tied to personal values.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style is inconsistent with MI principles.  
- Lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership undermines rapport and engagement.  
- No use of reflective listening or affirmations to deepen understanding and support.  
- Change talk is not effectively evoked or reinforced; sustain talk is not softened.  
- No collaborative planning or autonomy support; advice is given without permission.  
- Questions are mostly closed and directive, limiting patient expression.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directing and commanding to partnering with the patient. Use inclusive language and invite patient input.

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Reflect patient ambivalence and emotions. Validate the difficulty of change and normalize struggle.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Encourage elaboration and exploration of motivations, values, and concerns.

4. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize and reinforce any positive steps or intentions the patient expresses.

5. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and explore these to deepen motivation.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and avoid confrontation to reduce resistance.

7. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

8. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to set realistic, achievable goals and check for readiness.

9. **Avoid Ultimatums and Threats:**  
   - Replace pressure with encouragement and support to foster engagement.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s approach is directive, confrontational, and lacks empathy, acceptance, and collaboration. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a client-centered, autonomy-supportive style that elicits and reinforces the patient’s own motivations for change while skillfully managing ambivalence. Without these changes, the risk of patient disengagement and poor adherence remains high.
