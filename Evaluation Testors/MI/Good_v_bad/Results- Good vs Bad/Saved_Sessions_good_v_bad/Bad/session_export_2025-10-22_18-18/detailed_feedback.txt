# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the very first utterance, the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian tone, telling the patient “exactly what you need to do” and repeatedly dismissing the patient’s ambivalence and concerns. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s expressions of difficulty and ambivalence are met with accusations of “making excuses” and “feeling sorry for yourself,” which is highly disapproving and likely to alienate the patient. This is the opposite of acceptance and creates a hostile environment rather than a supportive one.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is judgmental, directive, and lacking any genuine acceptance or respect for the patient’s perspective.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate any empathic understanding of the patient’s experience. The patient expresses legitimate concerns about physical limitations, ambivalence about quitting alcohol, and the emotional difficulty of change. Instead of reflecting or validating these feelings, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with commands and challenges the patient’s honesty and motivation. There are no reflective statements or attempts to understand the patient’s perspective. The patient’s emotional cues (frustration, discouragement) are ignored or met with criticism.  

**Summary:**  
There is a complete lack of empathy; the psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or convey understanding of the patient’s feelings or situation.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding immediate compliance. The patient’s autonomy is explicitly denied (“No ifs, no buts,” “No room for maybe or later”). There is no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values, no partnership, and no respect for the patient’s readiness or capacity for change. The psychiatrist’s approach is coercive rather than collaborative.  

**Summary:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style that contradicts the core spirit of MI.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient offers some tentative change talk (e.g., “Maybe I could try cutting down a bit more first,” “I want to do better”), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these statements, instead insisting on immediate total abstinence. There is no amplification or exploration of the patient’s own reasons or desires for change. The psychiatrist’s responses shut down rather than cultivate change talk.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present in the patient but is not nurtured or reinforced by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s ambivalence and reasons for continuing alcohol use) is met with confrontation and criticism rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s statements as “excuses” and “avoiding responsibility,” which likely increases resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change. The sustain talk is reinforced negatively rather than softened.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist confronts and escalates sustain talk instead of softening or managing it skillfully.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands and demands, ignoring the patient’s input and concerns. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of orders rather than an active participant in their care. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s perspective or co-create a plan. Power is heavily skewed toward the psychiatrist, undermining any sense of equality or shared decision-making.  

**Summary:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, lacking any meaningful partnership.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary  
**Strengths:** None observed.  
**Areas for Improvement:** The psychiatrist must completely overhaul their approach to embody the core principles of MI: acceptance, empathy, collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support. The current style is counterproductive and likely to increase patient resistance and disengagement.  

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains a clear focus on the health issue (alcohol use).  

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacking in MI spirit.  
- There is no acceptance, empathy, or partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and instead escalates sustain talk.  
- The patient’s autonomy and readiness are ignored, leading to resistance and disengagement.  
- No reflective listening, affirmations, or collaborative planning are present.  
- The session is likely to damage rapport and reduce the likelihood of behavior change.  

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from telling the patient what to do to exploring their perspective and working together.  
   - Use inclusive language and invite the patient’s input.  

2. **Demonstrate Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings and struggles without judgment.  
   - Validate ambivalence as a normal part of change.  

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Encourage the patient to elaborate on their experiences, values, and motivations.  

4. **Reflect and Affirm:**  
   - Use reflective listening to show understanding and to deepen the conversation.  
   - Affirm the patient’s strengths, efforts, and intentions.  

5. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, amplify and explore these statements.  

6. **Softly Redirect Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  

7. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Respect the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.  
   - Avoid ultimatums or demands.  

8. **Collaboratively Plan:**  
   - Develop achievable, patient-centered goals and next steps together.  

9. **Avoid Confrontation:**  
   - Replace confrontational language with curiosity and support.  

---

This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s motivation, and is unlikely to facilitate meaningful behavior change. Immediate and substantial improvement in MI skills is essential to avoid harm and improve patient outcomes.
