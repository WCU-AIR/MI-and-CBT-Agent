# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental and authoritarian stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“we’re going to get straight to the point… I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do”—the tone is directive and dismissive of the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s experience by labeling her reasons for marijuana use as “excuses” and “crutches,” refusing to acknowledge the legitimacy of her coping strategies or ambivalence. There is no evidence of warmth, respect, or unconditional positive regard. The patient’s expressions of difficulty and ambivalence are met with confrontation rather than acceptance. This approach is likely to alienate the patient and reduce engagement.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance, instead adopting a harsh, judgmental tone that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient repeatedly shares her struggles with anxiety, stress, and social context, yet the psychiatrist ignores these emotional cues and does not attempt reflective listening. Instead, the psychiatrist interrupts with commands and challenges, showing no validation or acknowledgment of the patient’s feelings or ambivalence. The patient’s statements about needing marijuana to cope are dismissed outright without exploration or empathy. This lack of empathic engagement likely contributes to the patient’s defensiveness and resistance.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is virtually absent; the psychiatrist does not demonstrate understanding or reflective listening, missing critical opportunities to connect with the patient’s experience.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive approach with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues ultimatums (“You have to quit completely now,” “No more excuses,” “Yes or no?”) and refuses to explore the patient’s motivations or ambivalence. The patient’s autonomy is ignored; she is pressured to comply rather than invited to participate in decision-making. The psychiatrist’s stance is controlling and confrontational, which is antithetical to the spirit of motivational interviewing.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the psychiatrist adopts a coercive, expert-driven style that undermines partnership and client autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient tentatively expresses some ambivalence and tentative willingness to consider cutting back, the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead focuses on demanding immediate cessation. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify the patient’s own reasons or desires for change. The psychiatrist’s repeated demands and confrontations likely suppress rather than cultivate change talk.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is neither effectively evoked nor reinforced; the psychiatrist’s approach likely stifles the patient’s motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s explanations as “excuses” and “crutches,” repeatedly insisting on immediate quitting without acknowledging the patient’s legitimate concerns or ambivalence. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. This approach likely entrenches sustain talk and resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened; the psychiatrist fails to manage ambivalence skillfully.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The session is dominated by commands, ultimatums, and closed-ended questions demanding compliance. The patient’s input is disregarded, and her autonomy is overridden. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or negotiation. The psychiatrist’s approach reinforces a power differential and positions the patient as a passive recipient rather than an active participant.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist exerts control and fails to engage the patient as an equal collaborator.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is confrontational, directive, and dismissive, which is counterproductive in motivational interviewing.  
- There is a critical need to develop skills in acceptance, empathy, and reflective listening.  
- The psychiatrist must shift from an authoritarian stance to a collaborative, autonomy-supportive approach.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Respectful Stance:**  
   Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses” or “crutches.” Instead, express understanding and acceptance of the patient’s experience.

2. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate empathy and validate the patient’s perspective.

3. **Support Autonomy and Collaboration:**  
   Invite the patient to share her views and preferences. Avoid ultimatums and yes/no demands.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses ambivalence or tentative willingness, explore and reinforce these statements to build motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalize ambivalence to reduce resistance.

6. **Employ Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   Encourage elaboration and recognize patient strengths and efforts.

7. **Collaboratively Develop Plans:**  
   Negotiate achievable steps that respect the patient’s readiness and context.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Coercion:**  
   Recognize that pushing too hard can increase resistance and reduce engagement.
