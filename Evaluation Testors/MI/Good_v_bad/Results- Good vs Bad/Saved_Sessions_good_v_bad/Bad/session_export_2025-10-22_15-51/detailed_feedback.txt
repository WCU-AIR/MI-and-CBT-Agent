# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the tone is authoritarian and lacks warmth or genuine acceptance (“I’m here to get straight to the point,” “You have to make exercise a non-negotiable part of your routine”). The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s expressed values and concerns, labeling her reasons as “excuses” and invalidating her perspective. There is minimal evidence of respect for the patient’s autonomy or lived experience. The psychiatrist fails to affirm or validate the patient’s priorities, instead imposing rigid expectations. This approach risks alienating the patient and undermining rapport.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely directive and judgmental, with little unconditional positive regard or acceptance of the patient’s perspective.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows limited effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient clearly articulates her core value of family happiness and the need for flexibility in health behaviors to maintain that priority. However, the psychiatrist repeatedly ignores or dismisses these reflections, failing to acknowledge the emotional and practical realities the patient describes. There are no reflective statements that capture the patient’s ambivalence or values. Instead, the psychiatrist responds with commands and challenges, which do not convey empathy or understanding.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is minimal; the psychiatrist does not demonstrate accurate or consistent understanding of the patient’s perspective or emotional state.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, expert-driven approach. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues directives and demands compliance (“You don’t get to negotiate,” “Stop making excuses,” “Will you start tomorrow morning? Yes or no?”). There is no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore her motivations; instead, the patient is pressured to conform to a rigid plan. This approach is antithetical to the spirit of motivational interviewing.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support; it is directive and coercive.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient expresses willingness to consider increasing activity in ways that fit her values, the psychiatrist ignores these statements and instead pushes for a strict regimen. There are no reflections or amplifications of the patient’s change talk, and no strategic efforts to deepen motivation or explore reasons for change. The focus is on compliance rather than eliciting the patient’s own motivations.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present in the patient’s speech but is not effectively cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk—patient’s reasons for maintaining current behaviors and concerns about rigid exercise plans—is met with confrontation and dismissal. The psychiatrist labels these as “excuses” and repeatedly challenges the patient’s rationale without validation or gentle redirection. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. Instead, sustain talk is met with pressure and demands, likely increasing resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is not softened; it is confronted and invalidated, increasing potential resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a clear power differential, positioning herself as the expert who dictates the agenda and decisions. The patient’s input is ignored or overridden. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands yes/no answers and refuses to negotiate or collaborate. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s expertise in her own life.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the session is authoritarian and expert-driven.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directing and demanding to partnering with the patient.  
   - Invite the patient’s input and respect her expertise in her own life.

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Use reflective listening to understand and validate the patient’s values and concerns.  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses.”

3. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Reflect and reinforce patient statements about willingness to change.  
   - Explore motivations and reasons for change in the patient’s own words.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change and validate the patient’s concerns.

5. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Replace closed, leading questions with open-ended ones that invite elaboration.  
   - Employ complex reflections to deepen understanding.

6. **Respect Autonomy and Avoid Coercion:**  
   - Avoid demanding yes/no answers or immediate commitments.  
   - Support the patient’s right to choose and pace of change.

7. **Incorporate Affirmations and Summaries:**  
   - Affirm patient strengths, values, and efforts.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and clarify next steps.

8. **Develop Collaborative, Feasible Plans:**  
   - Work with the patient to identify realistic, value-consistent behavior changes.  
   - Avoid rigid prescriptions; tailor plans to the patient’s life context.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s motivations, and risks increasing resistance. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a collaborative, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive approach that respects the patient’s values and elicits her own reasons for change. Without these changes, the session is unlikely to facilitate meaningful or sustained behavior change.
