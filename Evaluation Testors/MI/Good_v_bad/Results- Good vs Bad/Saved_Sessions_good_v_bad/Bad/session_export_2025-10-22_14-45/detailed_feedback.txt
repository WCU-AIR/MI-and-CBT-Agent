# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive rather than accepting. From the outset, the tone is confrontational (“I need to talk to you about your smoking habit and why you really must quit immediately”), which sets a critical and pressuring atmosphere. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s behavior as “excuses,” “weak,” and “dragging feet,” which undermines unconditional positive regard. There is little to no warmth or supportive language, and the patient’s hesitations and ambivalence are met with impatience and criticism rather than understanding. This approach risks alienating the patient and reducing openness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate unconditional positive regard, instead adopting a critical and directive stance that diminishes acceptance.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence, hesitation, and emotional difficulty, but the psychiatrist does not acknowledge or validate these feelings. Instead, the psychiatrist pushes for immediate action and repeatedly challenges the patient’s readiness without exploring underlying concerns or emotions. There are no reflective statements or attempts to paraphrase the patient’s experience, which would demonstrate empathy. The patient’s defensive and hesitant responses suggest a lack of felt understanding.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist does not engage in reflective listening or validate the patient’s ambivalence and emotional state.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, lacking collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly commands the patient to “stop making excuses,” “commit fully,” and “throw away cigarettes right now,” which is contrary to MI’s spirit. There is no invitation for the patient’s input or exploration of their motivations. The psychiatrist does not support autonomy or partnership but instead imposes a rigid agenda. This approach is likely to provoke resistance rather than facilitate change.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks the core MI spirit; it is coercive, directive, and dismissive of patient autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient offers some change talk (e.g., “I’ve thought about quitting,” “Maybe I could try throwing cigarettes away”), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce this language. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on pushing for immediate action and challenges sustain talk harshly, missing opportunities to explore the patient’s own reasons for change or to amplify tentative commitments. The patient’s change talk is minimal and not deepened or reflected back to encourage further elaboration.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but poorly cultivated; the psychiatrist misses chances to evoke or reinforce motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften sustain talk but rather confronts and challenges it aggressively (“You have to stop being so weak,” “No more excuses”). There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. The patient’s sustain talk and ambivalence are met with criticism, which likely increases resistance and defensiveness. This approach is counterproductive to resolving ambivalence.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening or strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritarian role, issuing commands and ultimatums without inviting collaboration or input. The patient is not engaged as an equal partner; instead, the psychiatrist dictates the agenda and expectations. There is no negotiation or shared decision-making, and the patient’s autonomy is disregarded. This power imbalance undermines trust and engagement.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and excludes the patient from collaborative planning.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   Replace confrontational language with supportive, respectful communication that conveys unconditional positive regard.

2. **Increase Empathy and Reflective Listening:**  
   Use reflections to acknowledge the patient’s ambivalence, fears, and barriers. Validate emotions and experiences to build rapport.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   Foster collaboration, evoke the patient’s own motivations, and support autonomy rather than imposing directives.

4. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and explore these statements to deepen motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance and help the patient resolve ambivalence.

6. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   Ask open-ended questions to invite elaboration and affirm the patient’s strengths and efforts to encourage engagement.

7. **Collaboratively Develop Plans:**  
   Work with the patient to set realistic goals and timelines, respecting readiness and capacity.

8. **Avoid Persuasion Without Permission:**  
   Seek permission before offering advice or information to maintain respect and partnership.

Immediate and substantial improvement is required to align with MI best practices and to avoid harming patient engagement and motivation.
