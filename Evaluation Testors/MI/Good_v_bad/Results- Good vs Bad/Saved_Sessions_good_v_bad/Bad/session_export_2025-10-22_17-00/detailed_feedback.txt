# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a largely judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian tone (“I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do”), which immediately undermines acceptance. The patient’s hesitancy and ambivalence are met with repeated dismissals and challenges rather than validation or understanding. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s responses as “excuses” and “sabotaging,” which is disempowering and judgmental. There is minimal evidence of warmth, respect, or unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s struggles or efforts in a supportive way.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly directive and critical, lacking the fundamental acceptance necessary for effective motivational interviewing. This risks alienating the patient and reducing engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows limited effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence, social pressures, and the difficulty of change, but the psychiatrist repeatedly ignores these cues and pushes a rigid agenda. There are no reflective statements or attempts to paraphrase the patient’s feelings or struggles. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the emotional complexity behind the patient’s resistance, such as the importance of social belonging or the challenge of balancing multiple roles. The patient’s statements about feeling overwhelmed and needing space to figure things out are met with dismissal rather than empathy.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is minimal and superficial. The psychiatrist does not demonstrate accurate or consistent understanding of the patient’s perspective, which undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is almost entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do without invitation or collaboration. There is no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values; instead, the psychiatrist demands compliance. Autonomy is explicitly denied (“No, we’re not negotiating here,” “You don’t get to negotiate this”). This approach is antithetical to MI principles and likely to provoke resistance rather than change.  

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by a directive, confrontational style that negates the core MI spirit. This is a critical failure in MI practice.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient offers some change talk (e.g., “Maybe I can work on cutting back,” “I want to do better”), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly challenges and dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and tentative commitments. There is no strategic amplification or exploration of the patient’s own reasons for change. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to reflect and build on the patient’s expressed desire to improve and concerns about health.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but poorly cultivated. The psychiatrist’s confrontational style suppresses rather than encourages the patient’s motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (e.g., social pressure, difficulty quitting, fear of losing connection) is met with harsh judgment and direct confrontation. The psychiatrist labels these as “excuses” and “sabotage” rather than normalizing ambivalence or exploring underlying concerns. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to gently redirect sustain talk toward change. Instead, sustain talk is met with ultimatums and threats, which likely increase resistance.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead reinforces it through confrontation and judgment.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist explicitly denies partnership and collaboration, repeatedly stating “we’re not working together here” and “I’m telling you what you have to do.” The patient’s input is dismissed, and the psychiatrist assumes full control of the agenda and decisions. This creates a power imbalance and undermines the patient’s sense of agency. The patient’s attempts to express ambivalence and negotiate are shut down.  

**Summary:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance that is counterproductive to MI.

---

- The psychiatrist’s approach is overly directive, confrontational, and judgmental.  
- There is a profound lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- The spirit of MI is absent, with no evocation or support for autonomy.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian and confrontational, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a critical lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directive commands to partnership. Invite the patient’s perspective and co-create goals.

2. **Increase Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Validate the patient’s struggles and ambivalence. Use reflective listening to demonstrate understanding.

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions to explore motivations and barriers. Reflect both content and emotion.

4. **Affirm Strengths and Efforts:**  
   - Recognize the patient’s existing efforts (e.g., athletic involvement, awareness of health) to build confidence.

5. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, amplify and explore these statements.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance and ambivalence.

7. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right and ability to choose their path and pace of change.

8. **Collaborative Planning:**  
   - Develop achievable, patient-centered goals and plans rather than issuing ultimatums.

9. **Summarize Regularly:**  
   - Use summaries to consolidate motivation and clarify next steps.

Immediate and substantial improvement in MI skills is necessary to avoid harm and improve patient outcomes.
