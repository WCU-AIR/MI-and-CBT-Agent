# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive from the outset. The opening statement, “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do to get better,” sets a controlling tone that undermines acceptance. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and concerns, showing little respect for the patient’s perspective or autonomy. There is no evidence of warmth, empathy, or unconditional positive regard. The patient’s defensive posture and frustration likely stem from this lack of acceptance.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to establish a respectful, supportive environment. The stance is authoritarian and dismissive, which is counterproductive in MI.  

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The responses are directive and confrontational rather than reflective or validating. For example, when the patient expresses difficulty balancing social life and drinking, the psychiatrist responds with “No, I don’t have time for ‘trying’ or ‘figuring out,’” which invalidates the patient’s experience. There is no reflective listening or nuanced understanding of the patient’s ambivalence or emotional state.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to reflect feelings or explore the patient’s internal conflict.  

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is dominated by an authoritarian, directive style with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands and ultimatums (“You have to cut that down immediately,” “No exceptions,” “End of discussion”), which contradicts the MI spirit. The patient is positioned as a passive recipient of orders rather than an active agent in change. There is no attempt to evoke the patient’s own motivations or values or to support autonomy.  

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent. The session is coercive and controlling rather than collaborative and evocative.  

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient offers some tentative change talk (e.g., willingness to try cutting back, acknowledgment of health risks), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce this language. Instead, the psychiatrist dismisses the patient’s ambivalence and focuses on compliance. There are no strategic reflections or questions to deepen the patient’s own reasons for change or confidence. The patient’s change talk is minimal and fragile, and the psychiatrist’s approach likely inhibits further elaboration.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but weakly evoked and not reinforced or amplified.  

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften or skillfully respond to sustain talk. Instead, sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal (“No, I don’t have time for ‘trying’ or ‘figuring out’”). The patient’s justifications and ambivalence are ignored or invalidated rather than acknowledged or explored. There is no use of double-sided reflections or normalization of ambivalence.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced or ignored rather than softened or redirected.  

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritarian role throughout the session. There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The patient is repeatedly told what to do with no invitation to collaborate or negotiate. The psychiatrist’s language (“End of discussion,” “You have to…”) reinforces a power imbalance and undermines client-centeredness.  

**Summary:**  
No partnership is established; the session is dominated by a directive, controlling stance.  

---

- The psychiatrist’s approach is overly directive and confrontational.  
- There is a lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, autonomy support—is absent.  
- Change talk is not effectively cultivated or reinforced.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Maintains focus on the target behavior (alcohol use).  

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian, confrontational, and directive, which is antithetical to MI principles.  
- There is a critical lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is absent.  
- Change talk is not cultivated or reinforced; sustain talk is ignored or confronted.  
- No partnership or shared decision-making is established.  
- The psychiatrist fails to affirm or support the patient’s strengths or efforts.  
- Planning and summarizing are absent or imposed without collaboration.  

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Replace commands and ultimatums with open-ended questions and reflective listening.  
   - Invite the patient’s perspective and acknowledge ambivalence.  

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Use reflective statements to validate the patient’s feelings and struggles.  
   - Avoid judgmental or dismissive language.  

3. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control change.  
   - Avoid pressuring or demanding compliance.  

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Ask evocative questions about the patient’s values, goals, and reasons for change.  
   - Reflect and reinforce any statements favoring change.  

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Normalize ambivalence as part of the change process.  

6. **Use Affirmations:**  
   - Recognize and affirm the patient’s strengths, efforts, and intentions.  

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Negotiate goals and next steps with the patient rather than imposing them.  
   - Summarize key points and reinforce commitment.  

8. **Avoid Confrontation:**  
   - Replace confrontational language with supportive, nonjudgmental dialogue.  

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, engagement, and motivation. There is a critical need to shift toward a collaborative, empathetic, and autonomy-supportive approach that skillfully evokes and reinforces the patient’s own motivations for change. Without these changes, the session risks alienating the patient and reducing the likelihood of meaningful behavior change.
