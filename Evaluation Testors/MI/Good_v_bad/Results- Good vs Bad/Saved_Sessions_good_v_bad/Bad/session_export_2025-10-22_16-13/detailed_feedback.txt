# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental and authoritarian stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do to get better”—the tone is directive and dismissive of the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates Ethan’s feelings and concerns, labeling his responses as “making excuses” and “not good enough.” There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or respect for Ethan’s autonomy. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s ambivalence or struggles, instead adopting a confrontational and punitive approach. This is the antithesis of acceptance in motivational interviewing.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is harsh, directive, and lacking in respect or warmth, which undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand Ethan’s perspective or emotional experience. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses Ethan’s explanations and feelings as “excuses” and “avoiding responsibility.” The patient expresses legitimate concerns about peer pressure, fear of social exclusion, and difficulty adhering to strict limits, but these are ignored or invalidated. The psychiatrist fails to use reflective listening or validate the patient’s ambivalence, which would be critical to building empathy.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows no genuine effort to understand or reflect Ethan’s perspective, resulting in a cold and confrontational interaction.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling Ethan what he “has to” do and refusing to negotiate or explore the patient’s own motivations or ideas. There is no evocation of Ethan’s reasons for change beyond superficial acknowledgment, and no support for autonomy. The psychiatrist’s repeated demands and ultimatums (“This is not negotiable”) directly contradict MI principles.

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by a directive, coercive style that undermines collaboration and autonomy.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While Ethan does express some change talk (e.g., wanting to avoid sickness and trouble, willingness to try cutting back), the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce this language. Instead, the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses Ethan’s tentative change talk and focuses on pushing a rigid plan. There is no amplification or exploration of Ethan’s own motivations or ideas for change. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to reflect and build on Ethan’s statements about wanting to reduce harm.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but poorly cultivated; the psychiatrist fails to deepen or reinforce it.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk—Ethan’s reasons for continuing drinking, social pressures, and ambivalence—is met with confrontation and invalidation rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels Ethan’s responses as “excuses” and refuses to acknowledge the legitimacy of his concerns. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. This approach likely increases resistance rather than reducing sustain talk.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than skillfully softened or explored.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist repeatedly imposes a plan and demands compliance without negotiation or invitation for collaboration. Ethan’s suggestions and preferences are dismissed outright. The power differential is stark and unmitigated, with the psychiatrist acting as an authoritarian figure rather than a collaborative partner.

**Summary:**  
The session is characterized by a rigid, expert-driven approach with no partnership or client-centeredness.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style is counterproductive and violates core MI principles.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to use reflective listening or affirmations.  
- Change talk is not cultivated; sustain talk is harshly confronted.  
- Autonomy is denied, and advice is imposed without permission.  
- Planning is not collaborative or realistic from the patient’s perspective.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Replace directives with open-ended questions and invitations to explore.  
   - Respect the patient’s autonomy and perspective.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate ambivalence and feelings.  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses.”

3. **Cultivate Change Talk:**  
   - Reflect and amplify patient statements about desire, ability, reasons, and need for change.  
   - Explore the patient’s values and goals to evoke motivation.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Normalize ambivalence as part of the change process.

5. **Seek Permission Before Advising:**  
   - Ask for permission before offering advice or information.  
   - Collaboratively develop plans that feel achievable to the patient.

6. **Build Partnership and Rapport:**  
   - Engage the patient as an equal partner in the conversation.  
   - Avoid confrontational or authoritarian language.

7. **Use Affirmations and Summaries:**  
   - Affirm patient strengths and efforts.  
   - Summarize key points to reinforce motivation and clarify next steps.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session reflects a fundamental misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive approach undermines rapport, fails to engage the patient’s own motivations, and likely increases resistance. To improve, the psychiatrist must embrace the spirit of MI—collaboration, empathy, and autonomy support—and develop skills in reflective listening, evoking change talk, and softening sustain talk. Without these changes, the effectiveness of interventions with patients like Ethan will be severely compromised.
