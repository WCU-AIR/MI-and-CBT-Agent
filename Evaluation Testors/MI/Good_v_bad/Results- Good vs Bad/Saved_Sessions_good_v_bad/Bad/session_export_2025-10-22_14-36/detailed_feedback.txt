# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental and authoritarian stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do to get better”—the tone is directive and dismissive of the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s experience and concerns, using language such as “You don’t get to hesitate here,” “stop making excuses,” and “no ifs, ands, or buts.” There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s attempts to express ambivalence or caution are met with reprimands rather than understanding. This approach is likely to alienate the patient and reduce engagement.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate respect or acceptance, instead adopting a confrontational and coercive style that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist does not reflect or validate the patient’s feelings, concerns, or perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence, fear of failure, and a desire to make a sustainable plan, but the psychiatrist ignores these cues and responds with demands and accusations. For example, when the patient says, “I’m not ready to say yes or no like that,” the psychiatrist replies with “No, you don’t get to ‘think it through’ or hesitate here,” dismissing the patient’s legitimate need for reflection. The psychiatrist’s responses lack reflective listening, emotional attunement, or perspective-taking.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows no effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, resulting in a cold, dismissive interaction.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly issuing ultimatums and demanding immediate compliance. There is no collaboration or partnership; the patient is positioned as someone who must obey orders rather than an active participant in their own change process. Autonomy is explicitly denied (“You don’t get to hesitate here,” “You have to quit alcohol completely—no exceptions”). The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s own motivations or values but instead imposes a rigid agenda.

**Summary:**  
The session is characterized by coercion and directive commands, completely lacking the collaborative, autonomy-supportive spirit essential to MI.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the psychiatrist repeatedly asks the patient if they will quit alcohol and increase training, these questions are framed as demands rather than invitations to explore motivation. The patient does produce some change talk (e.g., “I want to quit drinking,” “I want to push myself more”), but the psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce this language. Instead, the psychiatrist’s confrontational style likely inhibits deeper exploration of the patient’s reasons, desires, or confidence. There is no strategic amplification or reflection of change talk to strengthen motivation.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not effectively cultivated; the psychiatrist’s style likely suppresses rather than encourages elaboration.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with direct confrontation and dismissal. The patient’s ambivalence and sustain talk (e.g., “I’m not ready to say yes or no,” “I’ve only drank a couple times”) are labeled as “excuses” and met with ultimatums. The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections, normalization, or gentle redirection. Instead, sustain talk is challenged aggressively, which is counterproductive and likely to increase resistance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead reinforces resistance through confrontational responses.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands yes/no answers and insists on immediate commitment without negotiation or exploration. The patient is not invited to share expertise about their own experience or to co-create a plan. The power differential is stark and unmitigated.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance, excluding the patient from meaningful participation in the process.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from authoritarian commands to partnership. Invite the patient’s perspective and acknowledge their expertise about their own life.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence. Avoid judgmental or dismissive language.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   Encourage the patient to elaborate on their thoughts, feelings, and motivations rather than demanding yes/no answers.

4. **Affirm Strengths and Efforts:**  
   Recognize and reinforce the patient’s existing efforts and values, such as commitment to soccer and scholarships.

5. **Evoke Change Talk Skillfully:**  
   Reflect and amplify statements favoring change. Explore the patient’s reasons, desires, and confidence in their own words.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance and ambivalence.

7. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

8. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   Work with the patient to create a realistic, achievable plan that respects their readiness and capacity.

9. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   Use summaries to consolidate motivation and reinforce commitment.
