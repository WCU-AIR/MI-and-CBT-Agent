# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a predominantly judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian tone (“I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do”), which immediately undermines acceptance. The patient’s attempts to explain their perspective are met with repeated challenges and dismissals rather than validation or understanding. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s reasoning as “making excuses” and accuses them of “dodging” questions, which is confrontational and likely to evoke resistance. There is little to no evidence of warmth, empathy, or unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist fails to normalize the patient’s ambivalence or acknowledge the difficulty of quitting, which are key components of acceptance in MI.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive, lacking the respectful, supportive stance necessary for effective MI. This significantly impairs rapport and client engagement.

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient clearly expresses ambivalence, stress-related triggers, and a tentative plan to quit by basketball season, but the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead repeatedly presses for immediate cessation. There are no reflective statements or attempts to paraphrase the patient’s feelings or motivations. The psychiatrist’s responses are mostly confrontational questions and commands, which do not convey understanding or validation. The patient’s frustration and defensiveness escalate, indicating a failure to attune empathically.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist does not demonstrate accurate or consistent understanding of the patient’s perspective, missing opportunities to build rapport and explore ambivalence.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive approach with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly insists on immediate quitting, dismisses the patient’s expressed readiness and plan, and refuses to negotiate or explore the patient’s motivations. The patient is repeatedly told what to do without invitation or partnership. This style is antithetical to the MI spirit, which emphasizes client autonomy, partnership, and evoking intrinsic motivation. The psychiatrist’s repeated demands and accusations (“stop dodging,” “no excuses,” “you don’t get to set the terms”) undermine any sense of collaboration.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks the fundamental MI spirit; it is coercive and directive rather than collaborative and autonomy-supportive.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directive commands to partnership language. Invite the patient’s perspective and preferences.  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses” or accusing them of “dodging.”

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s ambivalence and feelings.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of quitting and acknowledge the patient’s efforts and intentions.

3. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Explicitly affirm the patient’s right to set the pace and terms of change.  
   - Avoid ultimatums; instead, explore readiness and confidence.

4. **Cultivate and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and reinforce these statements.  
   - Use open-ended questions that invite elaboration on motivations and goals.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Avoid confrontational challenges that increase resistance.

6. **Collaborative Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Work with the patient to develop a realistic, personalized quit plan.  
   - Summarize key points, change talk, and agreed-upon next steps to consolidate motivation.

7. **Use Permission-Based Advice:**  
   - Ask permission before offering information or suggestions (e.g., “Would it be okay if I shared some ideas about quitting aids?”).
