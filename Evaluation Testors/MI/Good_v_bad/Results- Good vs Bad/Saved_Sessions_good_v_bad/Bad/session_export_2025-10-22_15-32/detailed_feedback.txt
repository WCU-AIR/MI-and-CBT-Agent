## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**

The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s behavior as excuses, rationalizations, and stubbornness (“you’re just making excuses,” “stop being so stubborn”), which is dismissive and invalidating. **Summary:**

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and judgmental, which is counterproductive in motivational interviewing.  
- There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to use reflective listening, affirmations, or open questions.  
- Change talk is not evoked or reinforced; sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs.  
- The patient’s autonomy and readiness are disregarded, likely increasing resistance and reducing engagement.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as excuses or stubbornness.  
   - Express genuine respect and positive regard for the patient’s experience and ambivalence.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy Through Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to show understanding.  
   - Validate the difficulty of quitting and the patient’s coping strategies.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than dictate.  
   - Support patient autonomy and evoke their own motivations for change.

4. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Invite the patient to elaborate on their reasons, values, and concerns.  
   - Affirm any positive intentions or efforts the patient expresses.

5. **Skillfully Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses willingness or reasons for change, reflect and explore these statements.  
   - Avoid confronting sustain talk; instead, use double-sided reflections and gentle redirection.

6. **Collaboratively Develop a Feasible Plan:**  
   - Negotiate next steps that the patient feels ready to take.  
   - Summarize key points and reinforce commitment.

7. **Avoid Confrontation and Ultimatums:**  
   - Replace “you have to” with “what do you think about…” or “how might you approach…”  
   - Recognize that readiness to change varies and respect the patient’s pace.
