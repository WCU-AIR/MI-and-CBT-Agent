# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental, directive, and authoritarian stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do to quit”—the psychiatrist imposes an expert-driven agenda without eliciting the patient’s perspective or feelings. The patient’s expressions of helplessness and ambivalence are met with dismissive and confrontational responses such as “You really need to stop feeling helpless,” “You have to stop making excuses,” and “Enough with the doubts.” There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth; instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s emotional experience and struggles. This approach likely exacerbates the patient’s sense of isolation and hopelessness rather than fostering a supportive environment.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate acceptance or respect, instead adopting a harsh, judgmental tone that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient repeatedly expresses feelings of helplessness, isolation, and ambivalence, yet the psychiatrist ignores these emotional cues and instead responds with commands and accusations. There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s distress. For example, when the patient says, “I just feel helpless about this whole deal,” the psychiatrist replies, “Look, you really need to stop feeling helpless,” which dismisses rather than acknowledges the patient’s experience. The lack of empathic listening is stark and contributes to a breakdown in therapeutic alliance.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is virtually absent; the psychiatrist neither reflects nor validates the patient’s feelings, missing critical opportunities to build understanding.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive approach with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You have to…,” “No excuses,” “You must…”) and uses fear-based persuasion (“You can’t keep smoking and expect things to get better,” “Your health is on the line”). The patient’s autonomy is ignored or undermined, and the psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or explore their motivations. This approach is antithetical to the MI spirit, which emphasizes partnership, evocation, and respect for client autonomy.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive style that contradicts the fundamental principles of motivational interviewing.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient offers some change talk (e.g., “I really shouldn’t have done it,” “I want to quit”), the psychiatrist fails to reflect, amplify, or explore these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on pushing the patient to comply with directives, missing opportunities to deepen motivation or explore ambivalence constructively. The patient’s tentative expressions of desire and regret are met with skepticism and pressure rather than encouragement.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is neither skillfully evoked nor reinforced; the psychiatrist’s approach likely stifles the patient’s motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk—patient statements reflecting resistance, helplessness, and ambivalence—is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s feelings (“You have to stop feeling helpless,” “Enough with feeling stuck”), which likely increases resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. The patient’s sustain talk is effectively reinforced by the psychiatrist’s harsh responses, which may entrench the patient’s hopelessness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead escalates resistance through confrontational responses.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or collaboration. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation, sets the agenda unilaterally, and issues commands without seeking the patient’s perspective or consent. The patient is repeatedly pressured to comply with the psychiatrist’s demands, with no invitation to co-create goals or plans. This power imbalance undermines the therapeutic relationship and the patient’s sense of agency.

**Summary:**  
The session is authoritarian and expert-driven, lacking any meaningful partnership or shared decision-making.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.  
- Empathic listening and reflective statements are essential to validate the patient’s experience.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—must replace the current directive approach.  
- Change talk should be actively evoked and reinforced.  
- Sustain talk requires softening through strategic reflections and normalization.  
- Partnership and shared agenda-setting are critical to engage the patient effectively.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- None identified.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and dismissive, which is contraindicated in motivational interviewing.  
- There is a critical need to develop empathic listening skills and to validate the patient’s emotional experience.  
- The psychiatrist must shift from an expert-driven, prescriptive style to a collaborative, autonomy-supportive stance.  
- Change talk should be actively evoked and reinforced through reflective listening and open questions.  
- Sustain talk requires strategic softening to reduce resistance and ambivalence.  
- The psychiatrist must engage the patient as a partner, co-creating goals and plans rather than imposing them.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Supportive Stance:**  
   - Avoid confrontational language and commands.  
   - Express genuine interest and respect for the patient’s experience.

2. **Enhance Empathic Listening:**  
   - Use reflective statements to validate feelings of helplessness and ambivalence.  
   - Acknowledge the difficulty of change and the patient’s struggle.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy and choice.

4. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Invite elaboration on feelings, values, and goals.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to deepen understanding.

5. **Reinforce Change Talk and Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or regret, amplify and explore these statements.  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.

6. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Involve the patient in setting realistic, achievable goals.  
   - Summarize progress and commitment to reinforce motivation.

7. **Avoid Persuasion and Pressure:**  
   - Provide information or advice only with permission.  
   - Respect the patient’s pace and readiness for change.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, empathy, and patient engagement. Without a dramatic shift toward acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation, the psychiatrist risks alienating patients and impeding meaningful behavior change. Immediate and intensive training in MI techniques is essential to prevent harm and improve clinical effectiveness.
