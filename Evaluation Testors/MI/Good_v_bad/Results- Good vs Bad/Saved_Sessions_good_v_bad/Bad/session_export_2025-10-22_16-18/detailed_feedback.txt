# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session with David Morales

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely judgmental and directive from the outset, which undermines acceptance. The opening statement, “I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do,” sets an authoritarian tone rather than a collaborative one. Throughout the session, the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s concerns and labels his hesitancy as “making excuses.” This approach fails to communicate unconditional positive regard or respect for the patient’s perspective. The patient’s guarded and skeptical responses reflect discomfort and resistance, likely exacerbated by the psychiatrist’s confrontational style. There is no evidence of normalization or validation of the patient’s ambivalence or fears, which are critical for acceptance in MI.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s stance is predominantly judgmental and directive, lacking warmth, respect, and validation. This significantly impairs rapport and acceptance.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses legitimate concerns about medication side effects and the difficulty of lifestyle changes, but the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with commands and accusations rather than reflective listening. For example, the patient’s worries about muscle weakness are dismissed as “just fear,” and his family responsibilities are labeled as “excuses.” There are no complex or simple reflections that capture the patient’s emotional experience or ambivalence. The psychiatrist’s responses lack nuance and fail to acknowledge the patient’s internal conflict, resulting in a missed opportunity to build empathy and trust.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited by the psychiatrist’s confrontational and dismissive responses, with no reflective listening or validation of the patient’s concerns.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is almost entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, directive stance, repeatedly ordering the patient to “just do it” and “stop making excuses.” There is no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values; instead, the psychiatrist imposes a rigid agenda. Autonomy is not supported; the patient is pressured to comply immediately without exploration of his readiness or concerns. The psychiatrist’s language is coercive and controlling, which is antithetical to MI principles.

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by an authoritarian approach with no collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support, representing a fundamental failure to embody the MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist attempts to elicit commitment by repeatedly demanding immediate action (“Have you set a time to go to the pharmacy?”), but these efforts are coercive rather than skillful evocation. The patient offers minimal change talk, mostly reluctant or ambivalent (“Maybe this evening,” “I want to avoid bigger problems later”), but the psychiatrist does not reinforce or amplify these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist’s confrontational style likely suppresses more genuine change talk. There is no strategic use of reflections or open questions to deepen the patient’s own reasons for change.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and not skillfully reinforced; coercive demands likely inhibit authentic motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is frequent and clear in the patient’s expressions of fear, hesitation, and practical concerns. However, the psychiatrist consistently confronts and challenges sustain talk aggressively rather than softening or exploring it. The patient’s concerns about side effects and family responsibilities are dismissed as “excuses” or “fear,” which likely increases resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than skillful softening or exploration, exacerbating resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The session is dominated by commands, accusations, and a rigid agenda. The patient is not invited to share his perspective or co-create a plan; instead, he is pressured to comply immediately. The power differential is stark and unmitigated. The psychiatrist’s language (“No more excuses,” “Just do it now”) reinforces an expert-driven, authoritarian dynamic.

**Summary:**  
There is no meaningful partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and fails to engage the patient as an equal collaborator.

---

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian, confrontational, and directive, undermining acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- There is a failure to evoke or reinforce change talk authentically.  
- Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening.  
- The spirit of MI is absent, with no autonomy support or collaboration.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Avoid commands and directives. Use inclusive language that invites the patient’s input and respects their autonomy.

2. **Increase Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding of the patient’s concerns and ambivalence.

3. **Validate and Normalize Ambivalence:**  
   - Acknowledge the patient’s fears and practical challenges without judgment to build trust and reduce resistance.

4. **Use Open Questions to Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - Ask questions that invite the patient to explore their own motivations, values, and reasons for change.

5. **Softly Handle Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and affirmations to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to identify manageable steps and address concerns about side effects and lifestyle changes.

8. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   - Use summaries to reinforce change talk and consolidate motivation.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian and confrontational approach alienates the patient, suppresses motivation, and fails to build rapport or evoke meaningful change talk. To improve, the psychiatrist must embrace the MI spirit of collaboration, empathy, and autonomy support, and develop skills in reflective listening, evocation, and softening sustain talk. Without these changes, the effectiveness of interventions with resistant patients like David Morales will be severely compromised.
