# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session with Milan Petrovic

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a largely judgmental and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the tone is authoritarian and lacks warmth or genuine acceptance. Phrases such as “I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do,” “You really need to cut down,” and “Stop making excuses” communicate disapproval and a lack of respect for the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s attempts to explain or contextualize his behavior, which likely increases defensiveness. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or normalization of ambivalence. The patient’s feelings and views are minimized or challenged rather than accepted.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is predominantly judgmental and directive, undermining acceptance and rapport. This severely limits the patient’s willingness to engage openly.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The responses are mostly confrontational and lack reflective listening. For example, the psychiatrist repeatedly accuses the patient of “making excuses” and “ignoring health risks” without acknowledging the patient’s expressed concerns about social belonging or his view that his drinking is moderate. The patient’s emotional experience—such as feeling pressured or controlled—is not validated. The psychiatrist does not paraphrase or summarize the patient’s statements to demonstrate understanding, nor do they explore the patient’s ambivalence with curiosity.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited; the psychiatrist fails to convey understanding or validate the patient’s perspective, which likely increases resistance.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with frequent persuasion and little collaboration. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You must stop,” “No exceptions,” “You have to take this seriously”) and challenges the patient’s resistance aggressively. There is no evidence of evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values, nor support for autonomy. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient to explore his own reasons for change or to set his own goals. Instead, the interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and insistence on immediate behavior change.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is absent; the session is coercive and directive, undermining collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. The patient offers minimal change talk, mostly in the form of acknowledging risks or expressing some awareness of consequences, but the psychiatrist does not build on these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on confronting sustain talk and issuing ultimatums. There are no strategic reflections or questions designed to elicit the patient’s own motivations, desires, or confidence for change. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore ambivalence or to highlight any positive intentions the patient expresses.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated; the psychiatrist’s approach suppresses rather than encourages the patient’s own motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly confronts and challenges sustain talk with admonishments and accusations (“stop making excuses,” “enough with the excuses,” “you’re ignoring the risks”). There is no use of double-sided reflections or normalization of ambivalence. The patient’s reasons for drinking, such as social belonging and feeling it is not problematic, are dismissed rather than explored or gently redirected. This confrontational style likely entrenches sustain talk and resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened or skillfully redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a clear expert-driven, authoritative stance with no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The patient is repeatedly told what to do, with no invitations to collaborate or negotiate goals. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in his own life or seek to co-create a plan. The power differential is emphasized rather than minimized.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s control and directives.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist is consistent in maintaining focus on health risks and the need for change.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The session is highly directive, judgmental, and confrontational.  
- There is a near-total absence of empathy, acceptance, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and instead escalates sustain talk and resistance.  
- The spirit of MI is not demonstrated at any point.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist consistently addresses the health risks of alcohol use and maintains focus on the target behavior.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The session is dominated by a confrontational, directive style that alienates the patient.  
- There is a critical lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to use core MI skills such as open questions, reflections, affirmations, and evocation.  
- Sustain talk is confronted harshly rather than softened or explored.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing occurs.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directing and commanding to partnering with the patient. Use inclusive language and invite the patient’s input.

2. **Increase Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Validate the patient’s feelings and perspective, even when resistant. Use reflective listening to demonstrate understanding.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Encourage the patient to explore his own reasons for drinking and potential motivations for change.

4. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses any desire or concern, reflect and amplify these statements to build motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence and reduce resistance.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize that change is the patient’s choice and control, reducing pressure and defensiveness.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to set realistic, personalized goals rather than issuing ultimatums.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Judgment:**  
   - Replace accusatory language with curiosity and respect to foster engagement.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational approach undermines rapport, engagement, and motivation. To improve, the psychiatrist must fundamentally shift to a collaborative, empathetic style that respects the patient’s autonomy and uses MI skills to evoke and reinforce change talk while softening sustain talk. Without these changes, the likelihood of meaningful patient engagement and behavior change is minimal.
