## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and authoritarian stance throughout the session. From the very first utterance—“I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do”—the tone is directive and lacks warmth or respect for the patient’s autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s perspective, labels his behavior as unacceptable, and uses confrontational language such as “You have to stop making excuses” and “You don’t get to negotiate here.” There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or acceptance of the patient’s ambivalence or feelings. The patient’s defensiveness and guarded responses reflect the lack of a safe, supportive environment. This is consistent with a score of 1, indicating a judgmental, disapproving, and labeling approach.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy or reflective listening. The patient’s expressions of ambivalence, discomfort, and explanations (e.g., “It’s part of my routine,” “I’m training for a marathon,” “I need to figure out how to make this work for me”) are ignored or dismissed. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or validate the patient’s perspective or feelings. Instead, the responses are confrontational and directive, showing little to no effort to accurately understand or convey the patient’s experience. The lack of any reflective statements or attempts to explore the patient’s ambivalence results in a score of 1.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding compliance. There is no collaboration or partnership; the psychiatrist explicitly refuses to negotiate or consider the patient’s input (“No middle ground here,” “You don’t get to negotiate”). Autonomy is not supported; rather, the patient is pressured to comply with a rigid plan. This is the antithesis of MI spirit and warrants the lowest score.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient offers some tentative change talk (e.g., “I’ll try to stick with twice a week,” “I want to do better”), the psychiatrist does not reflect, amplify, or explore these statements. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on confrontation and demands, which likely suppresses further change talk. There are no strategic efforts to elicit the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change. This results in a minimal presence of cultivated change talk.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s ambivalence as “making excuses” and “downplaying,” and repeatedly insists on immediate compliance. There are no double-sided reflections or validations of the patient’s struggle. This approach likely increases resistance and sustain talk rather than reducing it.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands and ultimatums, with no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s expertise in his own life. The patient’s attempts to negotiate or express concerns are shut down. The power differential is stark and unmitigated, resulting in a score of 1.

---

### **Global Ratings Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian, confrontational, and directive, lacking all core elements of motivational interviewing. There is no acceptance, empathy, collaboration, or skillful handling of ambivalence. The patient’s defensiveness and guardedness reflect the failure to create a supportive environment. This session is a clear example of poor MI practice.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (alcohol use).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and lacks all core MI skills.  
- There is no acceptance, empathy, or partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to use open questions, reflections, affirmations, or summaries.  
- Change talk is not evoked or reinforced; sustain talk is confronted rather than softened.  
- Autonomy is denied, and the patient is pressured to comply with a rigid plan.  
- Engagement is poor, leading to patient defensiveness and guardedness.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from authoritarian commands to a partnership approach that respects the patient’s autonomy and expertise.

2. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   Invite the patient to share his perspective, feelings, and motivations to foster engagement and exploration.

3. **Practice Reflective Listening:**  
   Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and validate the patient’s experience.

4. **Affirm Strengths and Efforts:**  
   Recognize the patient’s willingness to consider change and his efforts to manage training and stress.

5. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   Strategically elicit and reinforce the patient’s own reasons and desires for change rather than imposing directives.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and validate ambivalence to reduce resistance and facilitate movement toward change.

7. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Emphasize that the patient has control over decisions and change pace, which increases motivation and reduces defensiveness.

8. **Collaborative Planning:**  
   Work with the patient to develop realistic, achievable goals and plans that fit his life context.

9. **Avoid Confrontation and Ultimatums:**  
   Replace confrontational language with empathy and curiosity to build rapport and trust.
