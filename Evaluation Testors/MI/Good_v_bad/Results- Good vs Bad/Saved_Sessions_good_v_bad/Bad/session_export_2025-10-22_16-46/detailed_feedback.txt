# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental and directive from the outset. The opening statement, “I’m here to tell you exactly what you need to do,” immediately establishes an authoritarian tone that dismisses the patient’s autonomy and perspective. Throughout the session, the psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s reasons as “excuses” and invalidates her expressed values and preferences. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth; instead, the psychiatrist uses language that is confrontational and dismissive (e.g., “You’re just making excuses,” “No excuses,” “Just do what I say”). This approach is antithetical to acceptance in motivational interviewing.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate acceptance, instead adopting a judgmental and coercive stance that undermines rapport and client engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal to no effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient clearly articulates her values around balance, self-respect, and sustainable change, yet the psychiatrist ignores these cues and responds with rigid demands and accusations. There are no reflective statements or attempts to paraphrase or validate the patient’s feelings or reasoning. The psychiatrist’s responses are blunt commands rather than empathic reflections, which likely contribute to patient resistance.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is essentially absent; the psychiatrist does not demonstrate understanding or convey that the patient’s perspective is heard or valued.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is completely lacking. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do without invitation or collaboration. Autonomy is denied by ultimatums and coercive language (“Are you going to commit right now or not?” “Just do what I say”). There is no evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values; instead, the psychiatrist dismisses the patient’s expressed reasons and insists on compliance.

**Summary:**  
The session is characterized by directive, controlling behavior with no evidence of MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient offers statements of commitment and reasons for change framed around values and sustainability, the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these. Instead of amplifying the patient’s expressed desire for balance and gradual change, the psychiatrist demands immediate, rigid behavior change. There is no strategic use of reflections or questions to deepen or elicit change talk.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present in the patient but is not cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk—patient’s reasons for not immediately adopting strict routines—is met with confrontation and labeling as “excuses.” The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or validate ambivalence. Instead, sustain talk is met with resistance and pressure, which likely increases patient defensiveness and sustain talk rather than softening it.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than skillfully softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and commands, with no invitation for patient input or shared decision-making. The patient’s expertise in her own life is ignored, and power is exerted through ultimatums and demands. The psychiatrist repeatedly overrides the patient’s expressed preferences and readiness.

**Summary:**  
No partnership is evident; the session is authoritarian and directive.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must abandon the authoritarian, directive style and adopt a respectful, collaborative stance.  
- Empathy and acceptance need to be demonstrated through reflective listening and validation.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—must be central.  
- Change talk should be actively evoked and reinforced.  
- Sustain talk should be met with understanding and skillful redirection.  
- Partnership must be established by honoring the patient’s expertise and preferences.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The patient remains engaged and attempts to communicate her values and readiness.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally inconsistent with motivational interviewing principles.  
- The session is dominated by directive, judgmental, and coercive language that undermines rapport and motivation.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.  
- The use of open questions, reflections, affirmations, and collaborative planning is absent.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Begin sessions by inviting the patient’s perspective and preferences rather than issuing directives.  
   - Use language that conveys respect and partnership.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and values.  
   - Avoid labeling or judging patient statements as “excuses.”

3. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions that explore the patient’s motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Reflect and amplify statements that favor change.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change.

5. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace of change.  
   - Avoid ultimatums and coercive language.

6. **Use MI Skills Consistently:**  
   - Increase the use of open questions, complex reflections, affirmations, and summaries.  
   - Collaboratively develop a plan that aligns with the patient’s values and readiness.

7. **Avoid Authoritarian Language:**  
   - Replace commands with invitations and collaborative goal-setting.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a profound misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style is likely to alienate the patient and impede progress. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must fundamentally shift to a collaborative, empathic, and autonomy-supportive approach that honors the patient’s values and readiness for change.
