# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and authoritarian stance from the outset. The opening statement, “Today we’re going to get straight to the point... I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do,” immediately sets a confrontational tone that dismisses the patient’s autonomy and perspective. Throughout the session, the psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s expressed concerns and efforts, labeling her reasons as “excuses” and showing no warmth or positive regard. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or respect for the patient’s lived experience. The patient’s defensive posture and frustration are clear reactions to this lack of acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is disapproving, directive, and dismissive, which undermines rapport and trust. This is far from the respectful, supportive stance required in MI.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy or reflective listening. The patient expresses legitimate concerns about stress, life demands, and previous failed quit attempts, but the psychiatrist ignores these emotional and contextual cues. Instead of exploring or validating the patient’s feelings, the psychiatrist repeatedly challenges and confronts her, showing no attempt to understand her perspective. There are no reflections or paraphrases that capture the patient’s ambivalence or emotional state.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, resulting in a one-sided, confrontational interaction.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely directive and authoritarian. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues ultimatums (“no exceptions,” “non-negotiable,” “do it or else”) and demands immediate commitment without collaboration or evocation. There is no support for autonomy, no partnership, and no elicitation of the patient’s own motivations or values. The psychiatrist dismisses the patient’s readiness and capacity, instead imposing a rigid agenda.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is completely absent.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not evoke or reinforce any change talk. When the patient tentatively offers change talk (e.g., “I’m trying to cut back,” “I want to get healthier”), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses it, focusing instead on pushing a predetermined plan. There are no strategic reflections or questions to deepen or amplify the patient’s own reasons or desires for change.

**Summary:**  
No effort is made to cultivate or strengthen change talk; the patient’s motivation is stifled rather than supported.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s explanations as “excuses” and repeatedly demands cessation “now,” without acknowledging the legitimacy of the patient’s ambivalence or barriers. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize sustain talk or reduce its impact.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and escalated rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritarian stance throughout. The patient is not invited to participate in decision-making or planning. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands and demands compliance, ignoring the patient’s expressed needs and preferences. There is no evidence of shared power or collaboration.

**Summary:**  
No partnership or client-centeredness is demonstrated; the interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda.

---

None observed.

- Immediate need to adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.  
- Develop skills in reflective listening and empathy.  
- Embrace the MI spirit of collaboration and autonomy support.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is confrontational, directive, and dismissive, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The patient’s autonomy and readiness are ignored, leading to resistance and disengagement.  
- No effort is made to evoke or reinforce change talk or to soften sustain talk.  
- The session lacks collaboration, partnership, and client-centeredness.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Begin sessions with open-ended questions and affirm the patient’s efforts and struggles.  
   - Avoid labeling or dismissing patient statements as “excuses.”

2. **Develop Reflective Listening Skills:**  
   - Use reflections to demonstrate understanding and validate ambivalence.  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to deepen rapport.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing or commanding.  
   - Support autonomy by acknowledging the patient’s right to choose and pace of change.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and explore these statements.  
   - Use open questions to draw out motivations and confidence.

5. **Softly Address Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without reinforcing sustain talk.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change and validate the patient’s experience.

6. **Collaboratively Develop Plans:**  
   - Invite the patient to co-create achievable goals and next steps.  
   - Avoid imposing rigid plans or ultimatums.

7. **Build Engagement and Rapport:**  
   - Use empathy and acceptance to reduce defensiveness.  
   - Recognize and affirm patient strengths and efforts.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive style undermines rapport, stifles motivation, and alienates the patient. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a respectful, empathetic, and collaborative approach that honors the patient’s autonomy and readiness for change. Without these changes, the likelihood of meaningful behavior change is minimal, and patient harm through disengagement is a serious risk.
