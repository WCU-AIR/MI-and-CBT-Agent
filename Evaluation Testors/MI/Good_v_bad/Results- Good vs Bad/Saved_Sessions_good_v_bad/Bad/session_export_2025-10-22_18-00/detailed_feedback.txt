# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a largely judgmental and directive stance from the outset, stating, “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do to get better,” which immediately undermines acceptance and collaboration. The patient’s concerns and fears are met with dismissive language such as “You really need to stop worrying about cancer—that’s unlikely if tests haven’t shown anything,” and “Look, you’re overcomplicating this.” These statements convey a lack of unconditional positive regard and fail to validate the patient’s emotional experience. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s fears or ambivalence in a supportive way, instead pushing a rigid agenda. There is no evidence of warmth or empathy that would foster trust or safety.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is directive and dismissive, lacking respect for the patient’s perspective and emotional state. This significantly undermines acceptance.  

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. While the patient expresses fear about cancer and acknowledges some stress-related symptom fluctuation, the psychiatrist largely ignores these cues or responds with admonishments rather than reflective listening. For example, the patient’s admission that symptoms improve during her son’s wedding and worsen when her husband is away is met with “You have to stop making excuses about stress,” which invalidates the patient’s experience. The psychiatrist does not attempt to explore or validate the patient’s emotional distress or fears, nor do they use reflective statements to demonstrate understanding.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is limited and often replaced by confrontation and dismissal, which likely increases patient defensiveness and disengagement.  

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of motivational interviewing—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is almost entirely absent. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do with no invitation for input or exploration of the patient’s own motivations or concerns. Phrases like “You have to do this,” “No exceptions,” and “I expect you to follow this plan strictly” exemplify a directive, controlling approach. There is no evocation of the patient’s reasons for change, no support for autonomy, and no partnership. The psychiatrist does not explore ambivalence or elicit the patient’s values or goals.  

**Summary:**  
The session is dominated by a prescriptive, authoritarian style that contradicts the core principles of MI.  

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient expresses some tentative willingness to try changes (“I’ll try to be better about that,” “I want to feel better”), the psychiatrist does not capitalize on these statements to deepen motivation or commitment. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on compliance and adherence, missing opportunities to explore the patient’s own reasons for change or confidence in her ability to follow through. The patient’s expressions of ambivalence and fear are not used as openings to evoke change talk but are instead met with resistance.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and not strategically reinforced or amplified.  

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk—patient language favoring the status quo or expressing barriers—is met with confrontation rather than softening. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges the patient’s fears and doubts with statements like “You have to stop making excuses,” “No pain excuses,” and “No more hesitation—just do it.” There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s concerns while gently guiding toward change. This approach likely entrenches sustain talk rather than softening it.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal, lacking any skillful softening or strategic redirection.  

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist dominates the conversation, issuing commands and ultimatums without inviting the patient’s input or preferences. The patient is repeatedly told what to do, with no negotiation or collaboration. The power differential is stark and unmitigated. The patient’s tentative expressions of fear and ambivalence are overridden rather than explored collaboratively.  

**Summary:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with no meaningful partnership or client-centeredness.  

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary  

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on health-related behaviors and attempts to provide a clear plan.  

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, lacking acceptance, empathy, and collaboration.  
- The patient’s fears and ambivalence are dismissed rather than explored or validated.  
- Change talk is not effectively evoked or reinforced.  
- Sustain talk is confronted rather than softened.  
- The spirit of MI is absent, replaced by an authoritarian stance.  

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1.5/5** (rounded down to 1 for strictness)

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian and confrontational, undermining acceptance, empathy, and collaboration.  
- There is a failure to validate or explore the patient’s fears and ambivalence.  
- Change talk is not effectively evoked or reinforced.  
- Sustain talk is confronted rather than softened, likely increasing resistance.  
- The psychiatrist does not use reflective listening, affirmations, or summaries.  
- The patient’s autonomy is not supported or emphasized.  
- The session lacks the spirit of motivational interviewing.  

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Avoid directives and commands. Use language that invites partnership and respects the patient’s expertise in her own experience.  

2. **Increase Empathy and Acceptance:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s fears and ambivalence without judgment. Validate the emotional difficulty of her situation.  

3. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   - Paraphrase and reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and encourage deeper exploration.  

4. **Elicit and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses willingness or reasons for change, reflect and explore these statements to build motivation.  

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and affirmations to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right to choose and control the pace and nature of change. Ask permission before giving advice.  

7. **Use Open Questions:**  
   - Ask questions that invite elaboration and exploration rather than yes/no answers or leading questions.  

8. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Involve the patient in setting goals and deciding on next steps, ensuring buy-in and realistic commitments.  

9. **Summarize Regularly:**  
   - Use summaries to consolidate motivation, reinforce change talk, and clarify plans.  

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, directive, and confrontational style undermines acceptance, empathy, and collaboration. The patient’s fears and ambivalence are dismissed rather than explored, and change talk is not effectively evoked or reinforced. Sustain talk is confronted rather than softened, increasing resistance. To improve, the psychiatrist must adopt a fundamentally different approach grounded in MI principles, emphasizing partnership, empathy, and evocation.
