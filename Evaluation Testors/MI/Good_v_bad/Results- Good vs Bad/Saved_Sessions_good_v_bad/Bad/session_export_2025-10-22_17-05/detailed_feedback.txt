# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental, authoritarian, and directive stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do”—the tone is controlling and lacks warmth or respect for the patient’s autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s behavior as “laziness,” “excuses,” and “not disciplined enough,” which is stigmatizing and dismissive. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or acceptance of the patient’s perspective. The patient’s attempts to explain barriers are met with criticism rather than understanding. This is a clear violation of MI principles of acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is harsh, directive, and judgmental, severely undermining acceptance. This risks alienating the patient and reducing engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses feeling busy and finds dietary changes difficult, but the psychiatrist dismisses these concerns as “excuses” and “not making time.” There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s experience or ambivalence. The patient’s subtle emotional cues (e.g., shifting in seat, sighing) are ignored rather than explored. The psychiatrist’s repeated demands and accusations (“Are you just not disciplined enough?”) demonstrate a lack of empathy and perspective-taking.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is essentially absent; the psychiatrist fails to listen or respond to the patient’s feelings and barriers, instead focusing on control and compliance.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with no collaboration or evocation of the patient’s own motivations. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“You have to…,” “No excuses,” “You must commit”) and demands yes/no answers, leaving no room for patient autonomy or choice. The patient is positioned as passive and compliant rather than an active partner. There is no support for autonomy, no elicitation of the patient’s values or goals, and no exploration of ambivalence. This is the antithesis of the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive stance that undermines collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. When the patient tentatively offers “Maybe I could try some more walking,” the psychiatrist immediately shuts this down with “No, no, you don’t get to pick and choose here.” The patient’s tentative commitment is not amplified or explored. Instead, the psychiatrist demands rigid adherence to a plan without eliciting the patient’s own reasons or confidence. There is no strategic reinforcement or deepening of change talk, only pressure and commands.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is stifled rather than cultivated; the psychiatrist misses opportunities to build motivation and confidence.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk (patient’s statements about being busy, difficulty changing diet, uncertainty about commitment) is met with confrontation and dismissal. The psychiatrist labels these as “excuses” and “not making time,” rather than acknowledging the legitimacy of the patient’s struggles or ambivalence. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize sustain talk. Instead, sustain talk is met with increased pressure and demands, likely increasing resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and confronted rather than softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a partnership or minimize power differentials. The interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda, with repeated commands and demands for compliance. The patient is not invited to share their perspective or co-create a plan. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands yes/no answers and insists on strict adherence to a plan without negotiation. This creates a clear power imbalance and undermines client-centeredness.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must abandon the authoritarian, judgmental approach.  
- Develop genuine acceptance and empathy for the patient’s perspective.  
- Embrace the MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.  
- Learn to skillfully evoke and reinforce change talk while softening sustain talk.  
- Build a true partnership with the patient, sharing power and decision-making.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Maintains focus on relevant health behaviors (exercise, diet).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, judgmental, and confrontational, violating core MI principles.  
- There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and instead shuts it down.  
- Sustain talk is confronted rather than softened, increasing resistance.  
- No use of reflections, affirmations, or open questions to engage the patient.  
- Planning is imposed rather than collaboratively developed.  
- The overall style is likely to disengage the patient and reduce motivation.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling or blaming the patient.  
   - Express genuine respect and positive regard for the patient’s efforts and struggles.

2. **Develop Empathy Through Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion.  
   - Validate the patient’s experience and ambivalence.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than dictate.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy and choice.

4. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Invite the patient to elaborate on their thoughts and feelings.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen understanding.

5. **Skillfully Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses willingness or reasons for change, reflect and explore these statements.  
   - Avoid shutting down tentative commitments.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change.

7. **Collaboratively Develop Plans:**  
   - Involve the patient in setting realistic, achievable goals.  
   - Check for readiness and confidence.

8. **Avoid Confrontation and Demands:**  
   - Replace commands with invitations and suggestions.  
   - Use permission-seeking before offering advice.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, judgmental, and confrontational style is counterproductive and likely to increase patient resistance and disengagement. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation of the patient’s own motivations and autonomy. Without these changes, the session risks harming the therapeutic relationship and undermining patient motivation for health behavior change.
