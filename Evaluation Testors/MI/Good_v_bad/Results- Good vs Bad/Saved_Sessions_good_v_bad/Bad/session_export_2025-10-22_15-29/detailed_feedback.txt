# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently demonstrates a judgmental, confrontational, and directive stance throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist dismisses the patient’s perspective and ambivalence, labeling her reasons for smoking as “excuses” and “rationalizations.” There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s attempts to explain her stress and coping are met with criticism and demands rather than understanding. This approach likely increases patient defensiveness and disengagement. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s efforts or struggles, instead repeatedly invalidating her experience.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is harsh, directive, and lacking in respect or support, which is counterproductive in motivational interviewing.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist does not reflect or validate the patient’s feelings or perspective. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with accusations and commands, ignoring the patient’s expressions of stress, ambivalence, and difficulty. The patient explicitly states feeling unheard (“It feels like you’re telling me what to do without really listening”), yet the psychiatrist does not adjust or attempt to understand. The lack of reflective listening or perspective-taking is stark.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to convey understanding or emotional attunement, which undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands immediate behavior change, issues ultimatums, and dismisses the patient’s autonomy and readiness. There is no invitation for the patient to explore her own motivations or values. The psychiatrist’s stance is controlling and coercive, which is antithetical to the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is the opposite of MI spirit, relying on persuasion and pressure rather than partnership and evocation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient expresses some change talk (e.g., “I’ve thought about quitting,” “I want to quit when I’m ready”), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these statements. Instead of amplifying or exploring these motivations, the psychiatrist focuses on demanding immediate action and labeling the patient’s ambivalence as excuses. This approach likely suppresses further change talk.

**Summary:**  
There is no effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the psychiatrist’s responses likely inhibit motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly confronts and challenges sustain talk with accusations and demands rather than softening or exploring it. The patient’s sustain talk about stress, coping, and readiness is met with “You’re just making excuses” and “You’re being weak,” which escalates resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate the patient’s struggle while gently guiding toward change.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead exacerbates resistance through confrontational responses.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an expert, authoritarian role, issuing commands and ultimatums without collaboration or negotiation. The patient’s autonomy is ignored, and the psychiatrist does not invite input or share power. The repeated demands (“Are you going to do this or not?”) create a coercive dynamic rather than a partnership.

**Summary:**  
There is no evidence of partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and disregards the patient’s perspective.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling patient statements as “excuses” or “weakness.”  
   - Recognize the patient’s struggles and efforts.

2. **Practice Reflective Listening and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Validate the patient’s feelings of stress and ambivalence.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy and readiness.

4. **Elicit and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons to quit, explore and reinforce these statements.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change.

6. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Encourage elaboration and self-exploration.  
   - Affirm patient strengths and intentions.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Negotiate next steps based on patient readiness and preferences.  
   - Summarize key points and reinforce commitment.

8. **Avoid Coercion and Ultimatums:**  
   - Respect the patient’s pace and decision-making process.  
   - Build rapport and trust to facilitate change.
