# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental, confrontational, and directive stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do”—the tone is authoritarian and dismissive of the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist repeatedly accuses the patient of “making excuses” and “hesitating,” which invalidates the patient’s expressed ambivalence and efforts. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth; instead, the psychiatrist uses language that is likely to provoke resistance rather than engagement. The patient’s attempts to explain their experience and progress are met with dismissal and pressure rather than acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate respect or support, instead adopting a harsh, judgmental tone that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is virtually no demonstration of empathy or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or validate the patient’s feelings, fears, or ambivalence. The patient expresses concerns about readiness, fear of failure, and the importance of gradual change, but the psychiatrist ignores these cues and responds with repeated commands and accusations. The psychiatrist does not paraphrase or reflect the patient’s statements, nor do they acknowledge the patient’s efforts or emotional state. This lack of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s defensive posture and resistance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, missing critical opportunities to build rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive approach that contradicts the core MI principles of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly insists on immediate cessation and setting a quit date “tomorrow,” refusing to negotiate or explore the patient’s readiness or motivations. There is no evocation of the patient’s own reasons for change, no support for autonomy, and no partnership. Instead, the psychiatrist uses coercive language (“No ifs, ands, or buts,” “You don’t get to decide how it feels”), which is antithetical to MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally opposed to MI spirit, relying on control and persuasion rather than collaboration and evocation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient expresses several statements indicating desire, ability, and reasons for change (e.g., “I’ve cut down,” “My partner’s been helping,” “I want to get better”), the psychiatrist ignores these and instead focuses on pressuring the patient to commit to an immediate quit date. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify the patient’s change talk. The psychiatrist’s confrontational style likely suppresses further change talk rather than cultivating it.

**Summary:**  
No skillful evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the psychiatrist’s approach likely inhibits patient motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s ambivalence and caution as “making excuses” and “hesitating,” thereby reinforcing sustain talk rather than softening it. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. Instead, the psychiatrist confronts sustain talk with pressure and demands, which is likely to increase resistance and sustain talk. The patient’s legitimate concerns about readiness and fear of failure are dismissed rather than explored or reframed.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead exacerbates it through confrontational and dismissive responses.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish or maintain a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and directives, with no invitation for the patient to share their perspective or co-create a plan. The psychiatrist explicitly rejects negotiation or discussion (“No, we’re not negotiating here”), which reinforces a power imbalance and undermines client-centeredness. The patient is positioned as someone who must comply rather than an active participant in their own change process.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance that marginalizes the patient’s voice.

---

- There is an urgent need to develop skills in reflective listening, evoking change talk, softening sustain talk, and supporting autonomy.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (alcohol cessation).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally incompatible with motivational interviewing principles.  
- The session is dominated by confrontation, judgment, and coercion, which undermine patient engagement and motivation.  
- The psychiatrist must develop skills in acceptance, empathy, reflective listening, evocation, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist must learn to recognize and skillfully respond to ambivalence rather than dismiss it.  
- Collaborative planning and support for autonomy are essential to effective MI practice.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Replace accusatory language with respectful, supportive statements.  
   - Validate the patient’s experience and efforts, even if incomplete.

2. **Develop Reflective Listening Skills:**  
   - Use reflections to demonstrate understanding and encourage deeper exploration of ambivalence and motivation.

3. **Support Autonomy and Collaboration:**  
   - Invite the patient to share their perspective and co-create goals and plans.  
   - Avoid imposing timelines or decisions.

4. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions that explore the patient’s reasons, desires, and confidence for change.  
   - Amplify and reinforce any change talk expressed.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance and ambivalence.

6. **Avoid Confrontation and Coercion:**  
   - Replace commands and ultimatums with invitations and support.

7. **Practice Patience and Flexibility:**  
   - Recognize that readiness to change varies and support gradual progress.
