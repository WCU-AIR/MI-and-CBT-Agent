# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental, directive, and authoritarian stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“Today we’re going to get straight to the point about your health habits, and I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do”—the tone is controlling and lacks warmth or respect for the patient’s autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s feelings and concerns, labeling her reasons as “excuses” and demanding compliance without empathy. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or acceptance of the patient’s ambivalence or struggle. The patient’s anxiety and fear are met with blunt commands rather than supportive understanding. This is a clear violation of MI principles and warrants the lowest score.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is harsh, directive, and lacking in respect or warmth, which undermines rapport and client engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy or reflective listening. The patient expresses fear, guilt, and ambivalence multiple times, but the psychiatrist ignores these emotional cues and instead responds with blunt commands and yes/no questions. There are no reflections or attempts to understand the patient’s perspective or emotional state. For example, when the patient shares her fear of losing friends or her difficulty in quitting, the psychiatrist dismisses these concerns rather than exploring or validating them. The lack of empathy likely contributes to the patient’s discomfort and hesitancy.

**Summary:**  
No effort is made to understand or convey the patient’s perspective; emotional content is ignored or dismissed.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely authoritarian and directive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands compliance (“You have to stop drinking right now,” “No excuses,” “You must do it today”) and uses coercive language. The patient’s autonomy is not respected; instead, the psychiatrist insists on yes/no answers and issues ultimatums. There is no attempt to evoke the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change beyond superficial acknowledgment. This approach is antithetical to the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, expert-driven stance with no collaboration or evocation of patient motivation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**

Instead, the psychiatrist demands compliance and issues commands, which may suppress further elaboration of change talk. There are no reflective statements or open-ended questions designed to deepen the patient’s motivation or explore her ambivalence. The patient’s change talk emerges spontaneously but is not cultivated.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not effectively evoked or amplified by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The patient expresses sustain talk related to social identity and difficulty quitting (“I don’t want to lose my friends,” “Weekends are hard,” “I’m scared it won’t be enough”), but the psychiatrist dismisses these concerns harshly and repeatedly labels them as “excuses.” There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. Instead, sustain talk is met with confrontation and ultimatums, which likely increases resistance rather than softening it.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is ignored or confronted aggressively, with no skillful softening or validation.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by the psychiatrist’s agenda and commands, with no negotiation or shared decision-making. The patient is repeatedly pressured to give yes/no answers and comply with directives. There is no evidence of working as equals or minimizing power differentials. The psychiatrist’s stance is authoritarian and controlling.

**Summary:**  
No partnership or collaboration; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and disregards patient autonomy.

There is no evidence of acceptance, collaboration, or evocation of the patient’s own motivations. Change talk is present but not cultivated, and sustain talk is confronted rather than softened. ## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (alcohol use during pregnancy).  
- The patient expresses some change talk despite the challenging interaction.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian, confrontational, and lacks empathy.  
- No use of open-ended questions or reflective listening.  
- No affirmations or support for patient autonomy.  
- Sustain talk is confronted aggressively rather than softened.  
- No collaborative planning or summarizing.  
- The approach risks increasing patient resistance and disengagement.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from authoritarian commands to partnership. Use inclusive language and invite the patient’s perspective.

2. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   Replace yes/no questions with open questions that encourage elaboration and exploration of ambivalence.

3. **Practice Reflective Listening:**  
   Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding and validate the patient’s experience.

4. **Affirm Patient Strengths and Efforts:**  
   Recognize and reinforce the patient’s motivation and attempts to change, no matter how small.

5. **Evoke Change Talk Skillfully:**  
   Use strategic questions and reflections to deepen and amplify the patient’s own reasons and desire for change.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce resistance and support ambivalence resolution.

7. **Support Autonomy:**  
   Emphasize the patient’s right and ability to choose, avoiding ultimatums and coercion.

8. **Collaboratively Develop Plans:**  
   Work with the patient to create realistic, personalized plans and summarize progress to reinforce commitment.
