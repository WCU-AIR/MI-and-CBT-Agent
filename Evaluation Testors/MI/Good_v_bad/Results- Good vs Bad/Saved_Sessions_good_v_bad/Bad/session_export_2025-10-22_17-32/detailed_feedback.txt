# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and confrontational stance throughout the session. From the outset, the tone is directive and critical, exemplified by statements such as “you really need to stop avoiding these things,” “Was it just laziness or something else?” and “You have to get out more—it’s not optional.” These phrases communicate disapproval and blame rather than unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist repeatedly pressures the patient to commit, disregarding her hesitancy and emotional state. There is no evidence of warmth, support, or normalization of the patient’s struggle. This approach is likely to increase resistance and disengagement rather than foster collaboration.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is harsh, directive, and lacking in acceptance. This severely undermines rapport and the spirit of motivational interviewing.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy or reflective listening. The psychiatrist ignores the patient’s expressions of difficulty and fear, instead dismissing them as “overcomplicating it” or “making excuses.” The patient’s emotional hesitancy and ambivalence are met with impatience and demands rather than understanding. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge or validate the patient’s feelings of fear, overwhelm, or uncertainty. No reflective statements or paraphrasing are used to convey understanding of the patient’s perspective.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist does not demonstrate empathy or attempt to understand the patient’s experience, which is critical in motivational interviewing.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with repeated commands (“You have to push yourself,” “No more hesitation,” “It’s not optional”). The psychiatrist uses coercive language and insists on immediate commitment, disregarding the patient’s autonomy and readiness. There is no collaboration or evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values. The psychiatrist does not support autonomy but rather attempts to impose change through pressure and insistence. This is antithetical to the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The session lacks collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support, reflecting an authoritarian and confrontational style.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient offers minimal change talk (“Yes, I guess I get that it’s important”), the psychiatrist ignores these cues and instead focuses on pushing for commitment through pressure. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify the patient’s tentative openness. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands a yes/no commitment without exploring the patient’s own reasons or confidence for change.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is neither effectively evoked nor reinforced; the psychiatrist misses opportunities to build motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s explanations as “making excuses” and “overcomplicating it,” which likely increases resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to validate ambivalence. Instead, sustain talk is challenged aggressively, which is counterproductive in MI.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened or explored, undermining the patient’s engagement.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritative stance, repeatedly telling the patient what she “has to” do and demanding answers. There is no evidence of shared decision-making or respect for the patient’s expertise about her own experience. The psychiatrist does not invite collaboration or negotiate goals, instead imposing an agenda.

**Summary:**  
The session is characterized by a power imbalance and lack of partnership.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.  
- Empathy and reflective listening need to be integrated to understand the patient’s perspective.  
- The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—must replace authoritarian directives.  
- Change talk should be elicited and reinforced rather than ignored or challenged.  
- Sustain talk requires validation and gentle redirection, not confrontation.  
- Partnership and shared decision-making must be prioritized.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- None identified.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally inconsistent with motivational interviewing principles.  
- The session is dominated by confrontation, judgment, and pressure, which are contraindicated in MI.  
- The psychiatrist must develop skills in empathy, reflective listening, and autonomy support.  
- The use of open questions, affirmations, and strategic reflections is essential.  
- The psychiatrist should learn to evoke and reinforce change talk and gently soften sustain talk.  
- Partnership and collaboration must replace authoritarian directives.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Supportive Stance:**  
   Replace confrontational language with acceptance and warmth. Avoid blaming or labeling.

2. **Develop Empathic Listening Skills:**  
   Use reflective listening to understand and validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   Encourage elaboration and exploration rather than yes/no answers.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses even tentative motivation, reflect and reinforce it.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and validate ambivalence rather than confronting it.

6. **Support Autonomy and Collaboration:**  
   Invite the patient to set the agenda and make decisions at her own pace.

7. **Avoid Pressuring for Immediate Commitment:**  
   Recognize readiness varies and focus on small, manageable steps.

8. **Incorporate Affirmations and Summaries:**  
   Recognize patient strengths and summarize key points to reinforce motivation.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental style is likely to increase resistance and disengagement. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward empathy, acceptance, collaboration, and skillful evocation of the patient’s own motivations. Without these changes, the session risks causing harm rather than facilitating change.
