# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental, directive, and authoritarian stance throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist tells the patient what she “should do” and repeatedly dismisses the patient’s expressed feelings and ambivalence. For example, statements like “You really just have to stop,” “No more excuses,” and “You don’t get to ease into this” communicate a lack of unconditional positive regard. The patient’s fears, struggles, and motivations are minimized or ignored rather than accepted. There is no warmth or supportive tone; instead, the psychiatrist uses blunt commands and ultimatums. This approach is likely to alienate the patient rather than foster trust or openness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate respect or acceptance. The tone is harsh, directive, and dismissive, which is counterproductive in motivational interviewing.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses fear, shame, ambivalence, and a desire for support, but the psychiatrist ignores these emotional cues and instead pushes a rigid agenda. There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s feelings. For example, when the patient says, “I’m scared I’ll just fail again,” the psychiatrist responds with “You have to stop being scared,” which invalidates the patient’s experience. The lack of empathy is glaring and undermines rapport.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is essentially absent. The psychiatrist does not demonstrate understanding or convey that the patient’s perspective is heard or valued.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The spirit of MI—collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support—is completely missing. The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do and demanding compliance. There is no collaboration or invitation for the patient to explore her own motivations or values. Autonomy is denied through ultimatums and pressure (“No excuses,” “You don’t get to ease into this”). The psychiatrist does not evoke the patient’s own reasons for change but instead imposes a rigid plan.

**Summary:**  
The session is directive and controlling, lacking the fundamental MI spirit of partnership and autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient offers some change talk spontaneously (e.g., “I want to be better for her, for myself”), the psychiatrist does little to evoke, reinforce, or amplify this language. Instead, the psychiatrist pushes a predetermined agenda without exploring or deepening the patient’s own motivations. The patient’s change talk is overshadowed by the psychiatrist’s commands. There is no strategic use of reflections or questions to cultivate change talk.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not skillfully cultivated or reinforced by the psychiatrist.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk and ambivalence are met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s fears and struggles (“You have to stop being scared,” “No more excuses”), which likely increases resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that acknowledge the difficulty of change while gently guiding toward motivation.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than softened, increasing the risk of patient disengagement.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is one-sided, with the psychiatrist dictating the plan and expecting obedience. The patient’s input is ignored or overridden. The psychiatrist does not share power or invite the patient to co-create goals or plans. The repeated ultimatums and demands reinforce a hierarchical, expert-driven dynamic.

**Summary:**  
There is no partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the session and minimizes the patient’s role.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on smoking cessation and attempts to move toward a plan.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian, lacking acceptance, empathy, and collaboration.  
- The patient’s emotional experience and ambivalence are ignored or invalidated.  
- Change talk is not cultivated; sustain talk is confronted harshly.  
- The session lacks the MI spirit and is unlikely to foster lasting motivation or engagement.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Communicate unconditional positive regard and warmth. Avoid blaming or shaming language.

2. **Demonstrate Empathy Through Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings, fears, and ambivalence to show understanding and build trust.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient, evoke their own motivations, and support autonomy rather than imposing directives.

4. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Ask questions that invite elaboration and recognize the patient’s strengths and efforts.

5. **Cultivate Change Talk and Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Reflect and amplify change talk; use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk without confrontation.

6. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Involve the patient in setting goals and deciding on next steps, respecting their readiness and preferences.

7. **Avoid Ultimatums and Demands:**  
   - Replace “You must” and “No excuses” with supportive, invitational language.

8. **Summarize Regularly:**  
   - Use summaries to consolidate motivation and reinforce commitment.

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session falls far short of motivational interviewing standards. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines acceptance, empathy, and collaboration. The patient’s ambivalence and emotional experience are ignored or invalidated, and change talk is not cultivated. To improve, the psychiatrist must fundamentally shift to a patient-centered, empathetic, and collaborative approach that respects autonomy and skillfully navigates ambivalence. Without these changes, the likelihood of effective behavior change and sustained engagement is minimal.
