# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is largely directive, authoritarian, and judgmental rather than accepting. From the outset, the psychiatrist states, “I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do,” which immediately undermines the patient’s autonomy and sets a confrontational tone. Throughout the session, the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s perspective and concerns, using phrases like “you really need to stop making excuses,” “you don’t get to negotiate here,” and “there’s no room for ‘tough balance’ here.” These statements convey disapproval and a lack of respect for the patient’s autonomy and ambivalence. The psychiatrist fails to communicate unconditional positive regard or warmth, instead adopting a harsh, prescriptive stance that likely alienates the patient.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a judgmental and directive style, lacking the fundamental acceptance necessary for effective motivational interviewing. This severely limits rapport and patient engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient expresses ambivalence, cultural context, and a desire for autonomy, but the psychiatrist largely ignores these nuances. There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s feelings or struggles. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly challenges and confronts the patient’s statements without acknowledging the underlying concerns or emotions. For example, when the patient says, “I want to do what’s right for my baby…but I also need to feel like I’m making the decision,” the psychiatrist responds with ultimatums rather than empathy or reflection. This lack of empathic listening likely contributes to patient defensiveness and resistance.  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is minimal and inconsistent, with no reflective listening or validation of the patient’s ambivalence and autonomy needs.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist’s style is authoritarian and directive, directly opposing the spirit of MI, which emphasizes collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues commands (“you have to stop,” “no ifs or buts,” “you don’t get to negotiate”) and demands yes/no answers, which undermines partnership and client autonomy. There is no evidence of evoking the patient’s own motivations or exploring her values beyond superficial acknowledgment. The psychiatrist does not support the patient’s autonomy but instead attempts to impose change through pressure and coercion. This approach is antithetical to MI principles and likely to provoke resistance rather than facilitate change.  

**Summary:**  
The session lacks collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support, reflecting an authoritarian and confrontational approach.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
While the patient does express some change talk (e.g., willingness to take a brochure home, desire to protect the baby), the psychiatrist fails to skillfully evoke, reinforce, or amplify this language. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts or challenges the patient’s statements, focusing on compliance rather than exploration. There are no strategic reflections or open questions designed to deepen the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to explore the patient’s values around parenthood and health or to affirm her tentative steps toward change.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but poorly cultivated; the psychiatrist does not effectively evoke or reinforce the patient’s motivations.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften or skillfully respond to sustain talk. Instead, sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal (“stop making excuses,” “you don’t get to negotiate”). There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. The patient’s cultural context, skepticism, and autonomy needs are ignored or invalidated. This approach likely entrenches sustain talk and resistance rather than resolving ambivalence.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to establish a collaborative partnership. The session is dominated by commands, ultimatums, and demands for compliance. The patient is repeatedly told what to do without invitation or negotiation. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in her own life or invite her to co-create goals or plans. The power differential is stark and unmitigated, undermining trust and engagement.  

**Summary:**  
There is no meaningful partnership; the psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian, expert-driven stance.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Avoid commands and ultimatums. Invite the patient to share her perspective and co-create goals.

2. **Increase Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings, ambivalence, and cultural context.  
   - Affirm the patient’s strengths and efforts, even small steps.

3. **Use Open-Ended Questions:**  
   - Encourage elaboration and exploration of motivations, concerns, and values.

4. **Cultivate Change Talk:**  
   - Reflect and amplify statements favoring change. Explore the patient’s reasons, desires, and confidence.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections and normalize ambivalence rather than confronting or dismissing sustain talk.

6. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize that the patient is the expert in her own life and that change is her choice.

7. **Collaborative Planning and Summarizing:**  
   - Summarize key points, reinforce motivation, and develop a concrete, patient-driven plan.

8. **Avoid Yes/No Ultimatums:**  
   - Instead of demanding immediate commitment, explore readiness and support gradual steps.

---

**Summary:**  
This session reflects a fundamental misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s authoritarian, confrontational style undermines rapport, engagement, and motivation. To be effective, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward acceptance, empathy, collaboration, and evocation of the patient’s own motivations and autonomy. Without these changes, the risk of patient resistance and poor outcomes is high.
