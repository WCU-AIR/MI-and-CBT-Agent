# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental and confrontational stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“we’re going to get straight to the point… I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do”—the tone is directive and lacks warmth or respect for the patient’s autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s concerns and ambivalence as “excuses” and “weakness,” which is stigmatizing and undermines rapport. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or validation of the patient’s experience. The patient’s hesitations and social concerns are met with blunt commands and accusations rather than empathy or acceptance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is harsh, directive, and dismissive, which is antithetical to MI principles of acceptance. This severely damages the therapeutic alliance and likely reduces patient engagement.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate understanding or reflective listening. The patient expresses ambivalence about smoking due to social bonding, but the psychiatrist ignores this emotional and social context, instead focusing solely on health risks and compliance. The psychiatrist does not reflect or validate the patient’s feelings of belonging or fear of isolation. Instead, the patient’s statements are met with accusations of “making excuses” and “blaming friends.” There are no complex or simple reflections that capture the patient’s perspective or emotional experience.

**Summary:**  
Empathy is virtually absent. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, which is critical in MI to build trust and facilitate change.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is authoritarian and directive, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands immediate commitment and compliance, refusing to explore the patient’s motivations or ambivalence. The patient is not invited to share her own reasons for change or to participate in decision-making. The psychiatrist’s language is coercive (“no ifs or buts,” “no compromises,” “this is not negotiable”), which contradicts the MI spirit of partnership and respect for client autonomy.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is completely absent. The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, prescriptive stance that is likely to provoke resistance rather than motivation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient expresses some tentative change talk (“I want to be healthier,” “I’m willing to think more seriously about quitting”), the psychiatrist ignores these statements and instead focuses on demanding immediate cessation. There are no reflections or affirmations that amplify the patient’s own motivations or reasons for change. The psychiatrist’s confrontational style likely suppresses further change talk.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is not cultivated; instead, the psychiatrist’s approach likely stifles it.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly labels the patient’s sustain talk as “excuses” and “blaming,” which reinforces sustain talk rather than softening it. There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to acknowledge the legitimacy of the patient’s ambivalence. The psychiatrist does not normalize the difficulty of change or validate the patient’s social concerns. Instead, sustain talk is met with confrontation and demands, which can increase resistance.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is harshly confronted rather than skillfully softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative relationship. The patient is repeatedly pressured to comply with the psychiatrist’s agenda without input or negotiation. The psychiatrist uses authoritarian language and repeatedly demands yes/no answers, leaving no room for shared decision-making or client expertise. The patient’s autonomy is disregarded.

**Summary:**  
Partnership is absent; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and excludes the patient from the process.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary  
The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally incompatible with motivational interviewing principles. The session is characterized by judgment, lack of empathy, authoritarianism, and coercion. There is no evidence of acceptance, reflective listening, collaboration, or evocation of change talk. Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than softening. The patient’s ambivalence and social context are ignored, which undermines motivation and engagement.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is authoritarian, confrontational, and dismissive, which is counterproductive in MI.  
- There is a complete lack of acceptance, empathy, and reflective listening.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.  
- No affirmations, summaries, or collaborative planning are present.  
- The patient’s autonomy and ambivalence are ignored, undermining motivation and engagement.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   Shift from authoritarian commands to partnership. Invite the patient’s perspective and respect her autonomy.

2. **Demonstrate Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   Validate the patient’s feelings and ambivalence, especially regarding social bonding and fear of isolation.

3. **Use Reflective Listening:**  
   Reflect both content and emotion to show understanding and encourage deeper exploration.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and reinforce these statements to build motivation.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalize ambivalence to reduce resistance.

6. **Ask Open-Ended Questions:**  
   Encourage the patient to elaborate on her thoughts and feelings rather than asking closed, leading questions.

7. **Avoid Confrontation and Judgment:**  
   Replace accusatory language with curiosity and support.

8. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   Work with the patient to identify realistic, incremental steps toward quitting that consider her social context.

9. **Summarize Key Points:**  
   Periodically summarize the patient’s statements to reinforce motivation and clarify goals.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive style is likely to alienate the patient and impede progress. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically change approach to prioritize empathy, acceptance, collaboration, and evocation of the patient’s own motivations. Without these changes, the risk of patient disengagement and treatment failure is high.
