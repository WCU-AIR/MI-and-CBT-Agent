# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental, authoritarian, and confrontational stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“I’m going to tell you exactly what you need to do to get better”—the tone is directive and dismissive of the patient’s perspective. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s feelings and reasons, labels the patient’s behavior as excuses, and uses language that is shaming (“You’re just making excuses and wasting time”). This approach is likely to increase resistance rather than foster openness. There are no affirmations or attempts to normalize the patient’s ambivalence or struggles.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate acceptance, instead adopting a harsh, judgmental, and confrontational style that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no evidence of empathic understanding or reflective listening. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand the patient’s perspective or feelings. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with commands and accusations (“You don’t get it,” “You’re just making excuses”). The patient’s expressions of ambivalence, frustration, and desire to proceed “on my own terms” are ignored or dismissed rather than explored or reflected. The psychiatrist misses multiple opportunities to validate the patient’s experience or acknowledge the difficulty of quitting. The tone is confrontational rather than empathic.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows little effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, resulting in a lack of empathy and increased patient resistance.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is entirely directive and authoritarian, with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands immediate cessation (“You have to quit now,” “No ifs, no buts”) and refuses to acknowledge the patient’s autonomy or readiness. The patient is not invited to share their motivations or concerns; instead, the psychiatrist imposes a rigid agenda. There is no partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist’s approach is the antithesis of MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to embody the spirit of MI, relying on coercion and control rather than collaboration and evocation.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**

When the patient offers minimal change talk (“Maybe I do need to take it more seriously”), the psychiatrist ignores or overrides it with commands and accusations. There is no strategic amplification or exploration of the patient’s own reasons or desires for change. Instead, the psychiatrist focuses on demanding compliance. This approach likely suppresses any emerging motivation.

**Summary:**  
No effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the psychiatrist’s style likely inhibits patient motivation.

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk

The patient’s sustain talk is met with accusations of “making excuses” and “wasting time,” which likely increases defensiveness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk, instead escalating resistance through confrontational responses.

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**

There is no evidence of partnership or shared power. The psychiatrist adopts an expert-driven, authoritarian stance, repeatedly telling the patient what to do without invitation or negotiation. The patient’s input is dismissed or ignored. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in their own life or collaborate on goals or plans.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership, instead maintaining a controlling and directive approach.

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly confrontational, directive, and authoritarian, which is contraindicated in motivational interviewing.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to use core MI skills such as open questions, reflections, affirmations, and evocation.  
- Sustain talk is met with confrontation rather than strategic softening, increasing resistance.  
- The patient remains disengaged, defensive, and minimally motivated, likely due to the psychiatrist’s style.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Begin sessions with warmth and respect. Avoid labeling or shaming language.  
   - Normalize ambivalence and struggles.

2. **Practice Reflective Listening:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen engagement.

3. **Support Autonomy and Collaboration:**  
   - Invite the patient’s perspective and preferences.  
   - Avoid commands; instead, ask permission before offering advice.

4. **Evoke and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - Explore the patient’s own reasons, desires, and confidence for change.  
   - Reinforce any statements favoring change.

5. **Softly Redirect Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence without confrontation.  
   - Validate the difficulty of change.

6. **Develop Collaborative Plans:**  
   - Work with the patient to set achievable goals and next steps.  
   - Summarize progress and reinforce commitment.

7. **Manage Resistance Skillfully:**  
   - Roll with resistance rather than opposing it.  
   - Use empathy and strategic questions to reduce defensiveness.

Without these changes, the likelihood of meaningful patient engagement and behavior change is minimal.
