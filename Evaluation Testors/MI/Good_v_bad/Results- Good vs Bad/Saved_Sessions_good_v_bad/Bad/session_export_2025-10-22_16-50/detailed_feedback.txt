# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Diagnostic Session Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental, directive, and confrontational stance throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist states, “I’m here to tell you what you need to do,” which immediately establishes an authoritarian tone. The patient’s resistance and defensiveness are met with repeated challenges, accusations of “making excuses,” and dismissive language such as “that’s just nonsense” or “that’s just weak.” There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or respect for the patient’s autonomy or perspective. The psychiatrist fails to acknowledge the patient’s feelings or struggles and instead repeatedly invalidates them. This approach likely exacerbates the patient’s resistance rather than fostering engagement.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist’s approach is overtly judgmental and directive, lacking any warmth, respect, or acceptance. This is a critical failure in MI practice.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is minimal to no demonstration of empathy. The psychiatrist does not attempt to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective or emotional state. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly dismisses the patient’s explanations and feelings as “excuses” or “weakness.” The patient expresses stress, relational concerns, and ambivalence, but these are ignored or challenged rather than acknowledged or explored. The psychiatrist’s questions and statements are accusatory rather than reflective, showing no effort to convey understanding or validate the patient’s experience.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate empathy, missing opportunities to connect with the patient’s emotional state or perspective.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive style with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly commands the patient to “quit now,” “stop making excuses,” and “commit right now,” which contradicts the MI spirit. There is no invitation for the patient to share their motivations or values, nor is there any support for the patient’s autonomy. The psychiatrist’s approach is coercive and controlling, which is antithetical to MI principles.

**Summary:**  
The spirit of MI is entirely absent; the session is dominated by a confrontational, expert-driven approach.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. While the patient occasionally expresses relational motivation (“I don’t want to lose my wife”), the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these statements rather than exploring or amplifying them. The psychiatrist’s repeated demands and challenges likely suppress any emerging change talk. There is no strategic use of reflections or open questions to deepen the patient’s own reasons for change.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is neither effectively evoked nor reinforced; the psychiatrist’s style likely inhibits its emergence.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
Sustain talk and resistance are met with confrontation and dismissal rather than softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s explanations as “excuses” and “weakness,” which likely entrenches sustain talk and resistance. There are no double-sided reflections or affirmations that validate the patient’s ambivalence or struggles. The psychiatrist fails to normalize the difficulty of change or gently guide the patient toward considering alternatives.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is reinforced and intensified by the psychiatrist’s confrontational responses.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist assumes an authoritarian expert role, issuing commands and ultimatums without collaboration or negotiation. The patient is repeatedly told what to do, with no invitation to participate in decision-making or express preferences. The psychiatrist does not acknowledge the patient’s expertise in their own life or attempt to minimize the power differential. The interaction is hierarchical and coercive.

**Summary:**  
There is no partnership; the psychiatrist dominates the interaction and excludes the patient from the process.

---

- Develop skills in expressing acceptance, empathy, and respect for autonomy.  
- Learn to evoke and reinforce change talk rather than suppress it.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains focus on the target behavior (smoking cessation).

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally incompatible with motivational interviewing principles.  
- The session is dominated by confrontation, judgment, and coercion.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and instead intensifies sustain talk.  
- The patient’s autonomy and perspective are ignored, undermining engagement and motivation.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from telling and commanding to asking and listening.  
   - Invite the patient to share their perspective and values.

2. **Express Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Use reflective listening to validate the patient’s feelings and struggles.  
   - Avoid labeling or dismissing patient statements as “excuses.”

3. **Evoke Change Talk:**  
   - Explore the patient’s own reasons for change, especially relational motivations.  
   - Reflect and amplify any statements favoring change.

4. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change and validate the patient’s experience.

5. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize that change is the patient’s choice and control.  
   - Avoid ultimatums or demands.

6. **Use Open Questions and Affirmations:**  
   - Ask open-ended questions to explore motivation and barriers.  
   - Affirm patient strengths, efforts, and values.

7. **Collaboratively Develop a Plan:**  
   - Work with the patient to identify small, achievable steps.  
   - Summarize progress and reinforce commitment.

---

**Summary:**  
This session represents a profound misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, directive, and judgmental approach is likely to increase patient resistance and reduce motivation for change. Immediate and substantial retraining in MI principles and techniques is essential to prevent harm and improve patient outcomes.
