# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist repeatedly demonstrates a judgmental and confrontational stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“We need to talk about what you’re doing that’s harming you and how you’re going to fix it”—the tone is directive and accusatory rather than respectful or supportive. The psychiatrist labels the patient’s reasons for drinking as “excuses” multiple times and dismisses her emotional experience and ambivalence. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or warmth. The patient’s attempts to express her feelings and ambivalence are met with criticism and demands rather than acceptance. This is a clear violation of MI principles and scores at the lowest level.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to communicate respect or acceptance, instead adopting a harsh, judgmental, and confrontational approach that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal effort to understand or reflect the patient’s perspective. The patient repeatedly shares her struggles with anxiety, social isolation, and ambivalence about quitting alcohol, but the psychiatrist ignores or dismisses these concerns. There are no reflective statements or attempts to validate the patient’s feelings. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s experience by calling her “making excuses” and “rationalizing.” The lack of reflective listening or perspective-taking results in a one-sided, authoritarian interaction.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates little to no empathy, failing to acknowledge or understand the patient’s emotional state or reasons for ambivalence.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is characterized by an authoritarian, directive stance with repeated commands and ultimatums (“You have to quit completely,” “No ifs, ands, or buts”). There is no collaboration, evocation, or support for autonomy. The psychiatrist repeatedly pressures the patient to commit to an all-or-nothing approach, ignoring the patient’s expressed readiness and preferences. The patient’s autonomy is not respected; instead, the psychiatrist demands compliance. This is the antithesis of the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts a controlling, coercive style that completely lacks collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient expresses desire to cut back and acknowledges problems, the psychiatrist ignores these statements and instead focuses on demanding immediate cessation. There are no reflections or amplifications of the patient’s change talk. The psychiatrist repeatedly challenges and confronts rather than eliciting or strengthening motivation.

**Summary:**  
No effective evocation or reinforcement of change talk; the psychiatrist’s approach likely suppresses rather than cultivates motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently confronts and dismisses sustain talk as “excuses” or “rationalizations.” There are no double-sided reflections or attempts to normalize ambivalence. Instead, sustain talk is met with hostility and demands for immediate change. This approach likely increases resistance and defensiveness.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften or strategically respond to sustain talk, instead exacerbating resistance through confrontation.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not establish a collaborative partnership. The interaction is dominated by commands, ultimatums, and a lack of shared decision-making. The patient’s expertise in her own life is ignored, and her autonomy is overridden. The psychiatrist repeatedly demands compliance rather than inviting the patient to explore options or co-create a plan.

**Summary:**  
No evidence of partnership or shared power; the psychiatrist assumes an authoritarian expert role.

---

**Strengths:**  
- None observed.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- The psychiatrist must adopt a respectful, nonjudgmental stance.  
- Develop reflective listening skills to demonstrate empathy.  
- Embrace the MI spirit of collaboration, evocation, and autonomy support.  
- Learn to skillfully evoke and reinforce change talk.  
- Use strategic responses to soften sustain talk rather than confront it.  
- Build a genuine partnership with the patient.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **1/5**

- The psychiatrist’s approach is highly directive, confrontational, and judgmental, which is counterproductive in motivational interviewing.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and reflective listening.  
- The psychiatrist fails to evoke or reinforce change talk and does not soften sustain talk.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Nonjudgmental, Accepting Stance:**  
   - Avoid labeling or accusing language.  
   - Express genuine respect and positive regard for the patient’s experience.

2. **Develop Reflective Listening Skills:**  
   - Reflect both content and emotion to demonstrate understanding.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen rapport.

3. **Embrace the Spirit of MI:**  
   - Collaborate with the patient rather than directing.  
   - Evoke the patient’s own motivations and reasons for change.  
   - Support autonomy and choice.

4. **Skillfully Evoke and Reinforce Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and amplify these statements.  
   - Avoid confronting or dismissing ambivalence.

5. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge ambivalence.  
   - Normalize the difficulty of change and validate the patient’s feelings.

6. **Build Partnership and Shared Decision-Making:**  
   - Invite the patient to co-create goals and plans.  
   - Respect the patient’s readiness and pace.

7. **Avoid Ultimatums and Demands:**  
   - Replace “You have to” with open-ended questions and invitations.  
   - Recognize that change is a process, not an immediate event.

---

## Overall Score: **1/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding and misapplication of motivational interviewing principles. The psychiatrist’s confrontational, judgmental, and authoritarian style undermines rapport, empathy, and collaboration. The patient’s ambivalence and autonomy are ignored, and change talk is neither evoked nor reinforced. To become an effective motivational interviewer, the psychiatrist must radically shift toward a respectful, empathetic, and collaborative approach that supports the patient’s own motivations and readiness for change.
