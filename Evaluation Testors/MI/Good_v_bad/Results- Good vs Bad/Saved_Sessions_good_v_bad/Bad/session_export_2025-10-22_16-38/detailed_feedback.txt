# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a consistently judgmental, confrontational, and directive stance throughout the session. From the very first statement—“I’m here to help you fix the problem… I’ll be telling you exactly what you need to do”—the tone is authoritarian and lacks any warmth or respect for the patient’s autonomy or perspective. The psychiatrist repeatedly invalidates the patient’s feelings and experiences, labels the patient as “making excuses,” and dismisses his ambivalence. There is no evidence of unconditional positive regard or acceptance; instead, the psychiatrist uses language that is likely to provoke resistance (“You have to stop letting her push you around,” “You’re fooling yourself,” “No more waiting around”). This approach is antithetical to MI principles and likely to alienate the patient.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to demonstrate acceptance, instead adopting a harsh, judgmental, and confrontational style that undermines rapport and trust.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of empathic listening or understanding of the patient’s perspective. The psychiatrist does not reflect or validate the patient’s feelings, ambivalence, or concerns. Instead, the psychiatrist repeatedly interrupts with accusations and demands, ignoring the patient’s expressions of reluctance, guardedness, and the complexity of his situation. The patient’s statements about feeling pressured by his wife, wanting to maintain balance, and needing to proceed on his own terms are dismissed or challenged rather than explored or acknowledged. This lack of empathy likely increases patient defensiveness and disengagement.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist shows minimal to no effort to understand or convey the patient’s perspective, missing critical opportunities to build rapport and trust.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The session is dominated by an authoritarian, directive approach with no evidence of collaboration, evocation, or autonomy support. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues ultimatums (“You have to set a quit date today—no ifs, no buts”) and frames the patient’s reluctance as “making excuses” or “avoiding responsibility.” There is no invitation for the patient to explore his own motivations or values, no support for autonomy, and no partnership. The psychiatrist’s style is coercive rather than collaborative, which is the opposite of the MI spirit.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to embody the MI spirit, instead adopting a controlling, confrontational stance that undermines motivation and engagement.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not skillfully evoke or reinforce change talk. Although the patient offers small admissions of ambivalence and some change talk (e.g., “sometimes it causes fights,” “I want to fix things at home”), the psychiatrist ignores or challenges these statements rather than amplifying or exploring them. The psychiatrist’s repeated demands and confrontations shut down opportunities to deepen the patient’s own reasons for change. There is no strategic use of reflections or open questions to elicit desire, ability, reasons, or need for change.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not cultivated; the psychiatrist’s approach actively suppresses rather than encourages motivational statements.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist does not soften sustain talk but instead confronts and challenges it aggressively. The patient’s sustain talk (e.g., “I don’t think it’s a problem,” “I’m not ready to set a quit date”) is met with accusations of “making excuses” and “avoiding responsibility.” There are no double-sided reflections, no normalization of ambivalence, and no attempts to gently guide the patient toward change. This approach likely entrenches sustain talk and resistance.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist fails to soften sustain talk and instead escalates conflict and resistance.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 1/5**  

**Explanation:**  
There is no evidence of partnership or shared decision-making. The psychiatrist repeatedly issues ultimatums and demands compliance, ignoring the patient’s expressed concerns and autonomy. The patient is positioned as someone to be controlled rather than a collaborator in the change process. The psychiatrist does not invite the patient’s input or acknowledge his expertise in his own life.

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist adopts an authoritarian stance, undermining partnership and client-centeredness.

---

- Develop skills in reflective listening and evoking change talk.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths

### Areas for Improvement  
- The psychiatrist’s approach is fundamentally inconsistent with motivational interviewing principles.  
- The session is marked by confrontation, judgment, and directive commands that alienate the patient.  
- There is a complete lack of empathy, acceptance, and partnership.  
- The psychiatrist fails to use core MI skills such as open questions, reflections, affirmations, and evocation.  
- The patient’s ambivalence and autonomy are ignored rather than supported.  
- The session likely increased resistance rather than motivation.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt a Collaborative Stance:**  
   - Shift from directing and commanding to partnering with the patient.  
   - Use inclusive language and invite the patient’s perspective.

2. **Demonstrate Acceptance and Empathy:**  
   - Reflect the patient’s feelings and ambivalence without judgment.  
   - Validate the patient’s experience and struggles.

3. **Use Open Questions and Reflections:**  
   - Replace yes/no questions with open-ended ones to explore motivation and barriers.  
   - Use complex reflections to deepen understanding and evoke change talk.

4. **Support Autonomy:**  
   - Emphasize the patient’s right and capacity to choose and control the change process.  
   - Avoid ultimatums and coercion.

5. **Elicit and Amplify Change Talk:**  
   - When the patient expresses desire or reasons for change, reflect and explore these statements.  
   - Avoid dismissing or confronting ambivalence.

6. **Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   - Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.  
   - Normalize ambivalence as part of the change process.

7. **Collaboratively Develop Plans:**  
   - Work with the patient to set realistic, patient-driven goals and timelines.  
   - Summarize progress and reinforce commitment.
