# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session with Amina Diallo

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently maintains a respectful and nonjudgmental tone throughout the session. They explicitly acknowledge the patient’s guardedness and boundaries, for example: “I hear you—that sounds like you might be feeling a bit guarded or unsure,” and “I appreciate you sharing that with me—it sounds like keeping some things private is really important to you.” These statements demonstrate respect for the patient’s autonomy and privacy. The psychiatrist avoids any labeling or disapproval despite the patient’s repeated resistance. However, the acceptance feels somewhat procedural and lacks warmth or deeper positive regard that might help soften the patient’s defensiveness. There is no explicit normalization of the patient’s reluctance or validation of the difficulty of opening up, which would elevate this to a 5.

**Summary:**  
Respectful and supportive stance is maintained, but the acceptance is somewhat surface-level and could be warmer and more explicitly unconditional.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist attempts to understand and reflect the patient’s perspective, as seen in reflections like “It sounds like keeping some things private is really important to you,” and “That phrase ‘not really any of your business’ seems to carry a lot of weight for you.” These show some effort to grasp the patient’s emotional state and perspective. However, the reflections remain mostly factual or surface-level, lacking deeper emotional nuance or exploration of the patient’s underlying feelings such as fear, mistrust, or vulnerability. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to validate the patient’s emotional experience more fully or to explore the ambivalence beneath the resistance.

**Summary:**  
Adequate but limited perspective-taking; reflections are accurate but lack emotional depth and complexity.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a collaborative stance by inviting the patient’s input (“What would you like to focus on?” “What do you think would make this time feel more comfortable?”) and supports autonomy by respecting the patient’s boundaries. There is no evidence of persuasion or directive advice. However, the collaboration feels somewhat superficial and formulaic. The psychiatrist does not fully evoke the patient’s own motivations or values, nor do they explicitly acknowledge the patient’s expertise or co-create the agenda beyond initial questions. The session lacks a dynamic evocation of the patient’s internal motivations or a clear partnership in moving forward.

**Summary:**  
Moderate partnership and autonomy support, but limited evocation and collaboration; the session feels somewhat scripted rather than fluidly responsive.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 1/5**

**Explanation:**  
There is essentially no change talk evoked or present in the session. The patient remains minimally communicative and resistant, offering only brief, defensive responses (“It’s not really any of your business,” “Fine,” “Mm-hmm”). The psychiatrist does not successfully elicit any statements indicating desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment to change. While this is partly due to the patient’s presentation, the psychiatrist misses opportunities to gently evoke even small expressions of hope, curiosity, or ambivalence that could serve as seeds for change talk.

**Summary:**  
No effective evocation or cultivation of change talk; the session remains stuck in sustain talk and resistance.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist acknowledges the patient’s sustain talk and resistance without reinforcing it, which is appropriate. For example, they reflect the patient’s guardedness and respect boundaries. However, the responses are mostly neutral reflections rather than strategic softening or redirection. The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or other MI techniques to gently shift sustain talk toward ambivalence or openness. There is little evidence of active efforts to reduce the impact of sustain talk or to normalize the patient’s resistance as part of the change process.

**Summary:**  
Acknowledges sustain talk but rarely softens or redirects it; responses are neutral rather than strategically guiding.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist invites the patient’s input and respects autonomy, which supports a collaborative stance. However, the partnership is limited by the psychiatrist’s somewhat formulaic approach and lack of explicit sharing of power or co-creation of the session agenda. The psychiatrist does not explicitly acknowledge the patient’s expertise or negotiate the process beyond initial questions. The interaction feels more like a polite interview than a fully equal partnership.

**Summary:**  
Partial collaboration and respect for autonomy, but partnership is superficial and could be deepened.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental, and autonomy-supportive stance.  
- Uses open questions and reflections to attempt engagement.  
- Acknowledges and respects patient boundaries.

### Areas for Improvement  
- Reflections lack emotional depth and complexity; more nuanced understanding is needed.  
- No effective evocation or cultivation of change talk; missed opportunities to gently explore ambivalence or small openings.  
- Sustain talk is acknowledged but not skillfully softened or redirected; more strategic MI techniques needed.  
- Partnership is superficial and formulaic; needs deeper collaboration and co-creation.  
- Engagement is limited by lack of warmth, personalization, and dynamic responsiveness.  
- No planning or summarizing to consolidate progress or build momentum.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Deepen Reflections:**  
   Use more complex reflections that capture underlying emotions, fears, and ambivalence. Reflect not just content but also the emotional experience and meaning behind resistance.

2. **Gently Evoke Change Talk:**  
   Even with minimal engagement, look for small openings or expressions of hope, curiosity, or desire for safety to evoke change talk. Use strategic questions and reflections to amplify these.

3. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Employ double-sided reflections and normalization to acknowledge resistance while gently guiding toward openness. Validate the difficulty of sharing and the patient’s need for control.

4. **Enhance Partnership:**  
   Explicitly acknowledge the patient’s expertise and co-create the agenda and goals. Avoid formulaic phrasing and personalize the interaction to build trust.

5. **Increase Warmth and Emotional Attunement:**  
   Use tone, pacing, and language that convey genuine care and presence. Allow for silence and tolerate resistance without rushing.

6. **Use Summaries and Planning When Appropriate:**  
   Even in early engagement, brief summaries can reinforce understanding and tentative progress. When possible, collaboratively explore small next steps or options.

---

## Overall Score: **3/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a foundational respect for autonomy and nonjudgmental stance appropriate for a resistant adolescent client. However, the psychiatrist’s approach is somewhat formulaic and lacks emotional depth, warmth, and strategic MI techniques to evoke change talk or soften sustain talk. The session remains stuck in minimal engagement without progress toward motivation or planning. To improve, the psychiatrist must deepen reflections, skillfully handle resistance, and foster a more genuine, collaborative partnership that can gradually build trust and openness.
