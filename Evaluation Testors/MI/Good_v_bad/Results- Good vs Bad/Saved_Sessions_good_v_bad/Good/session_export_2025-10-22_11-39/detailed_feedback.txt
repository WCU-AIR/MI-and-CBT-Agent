# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a generally respectful and nonjudgmental tone throughout the session. Early on, the psychiatrist acknowledges the patient’s skepticism and frustration without criticism (“It sounds like you might be feeling a bit skeptical or unsure… which is completely okay”). The psychiatrist validates the patient’s feelings of monotony and obligation, showing understanding of the patient’s experience (“That sense of sameness can really weigh on you,” “That ‘no other choice’ feeling can be really tough to live with”). These affirmations demonstrate acceptance and positive regard.

However, the acceptance is somewhat formulaic and lacks deeper warmth or explicit unconditional positive regard. The psychiatrist does not explicitly normalize the patient’s ambivalence or disengagement, which would have strengthened the acceptance. The tone remains professional but somewhat distant, missing opportunities to convey genuine warmth or personalized empathy that might better engage a resistant patient like Kieran.

**Summary:**  
Respectful and supportive, but somewhat procedural and lacking in deeper warmth or explicit normalization of struggle.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates good reflective listening and perspective-taking. Reflections accurately capture the patient’s feelings of frustration, resignation, and desire for space (“It sounds like you’re carrying a heavy sense of obligation,” “That quiet, that relief from feeling watched or rushed, seems like it would mean a lot to you”). The psychiatrist’s reflections often paraphrase and validate the patient’s experience, showing understanding of the emotional undercurrents despite the patient’s flat affect.

However, the reflections tend to be surface-level and somewhat repetitive, mostly restating content rather than exploring deeper emotional nuance or underlying values. For example, the psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore the emotional toll of the patient’s resignation or the impact of social isolation beyond “space and peace.” The empathy is consistent but could be deepened by more complex reflections that capture subtle emotional layers.

**Summary:**  
Good reflective listening and understanding, but reflections lack emotional depth and nuance.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a collaborative stance, inviting the patient’s input and avoiding directive or authoritative language (“What would feel most helpful or comfortable for you to talk about today?” “What do you think might need to change?”). Autonomy is supported by acknowledging the patient’s right to feel skeptical and by asking permission implicitly through open questions.

However, the session lacks strong evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values. The psychiatrist’s questions and reflections are somewhat formulaic and do not actively draw out the patient’s intrinsic reasons for change or personal goals. The collaboration feels moderate rather than fully equal or co-created; the psychiatrist leads the agenda and does not explicitly negotiate or share power. The spirit of MI is present but not fully embodied.

**Summary:**  
Moderate collaboration and autonomy support, but limited evocation and partnership.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist attempts to evoke change talk by asking about what might be different or better (“If you could imagine a day that felt a little different… what might that look like?” “What small sign or change might catch your attention?”). The patient offers some tentative change talk, such as wanting “some quiet” or “someone actually listened.”

However, the psychiatrist does not consistently reinforce, amplify, or strategically respond to this change talk. There are missed opportunities to reflect and deepen the patient’s emerging motivations or to explore confidence and commitment language. For example, when the patient says “Maybe if someone actually listened… that might make me wanna give it a shot,” the psychiatrist does not follow up to amplify or explore this potential motivation. The evocation is weak and superficial.

**Summary:**  
Rare and weak efforts to evoke change talk; missed opportunities to deepen and reinforce motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist acknowledges sustain talk and ambivalence without judgment (“It sounds like you’re feeling pretty resigned,” “That makes a lot of sense”). There are attempts to validate the patient’s experience and normalize the difficulty of change.

However, the responses are mostly neutral reflections rather than active softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or explicitly highlight discrepancies between sustain talk and change talk to gently guide the patient toward change. Sustain talk is acknowledged but not skillfully minimized or shifted.

**Summary:**  
Some acknowledgment and validation of sustain talk, but lacks strategic softening or redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist invites the patient’s perspective and uses inclusive language, maintaining a collaborative tone. However, the partnership is limited by the psychiatrist’s control of the agenda and lack of explicit negotiation or co-creation of goals. The patient is invited to share but not empowered to direct the session or decision-making.

The psychiatrist does not explicitly acknowledge the patient’s expertise in his own life or explicitly share power. The collaboration is partial and somewhat one-sided, reflecting a moderate rather than strong partnership.

**Summary:**  
Partial collaboration with some client input, but limited sharing of power or co-creation.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Consistent respectful and nonjudgmental tone  
- Good use of open questions and reflective listening  
- Validates patient’s feelings and acknowledges ambivalence  
- Maintains a collaborative stance and supports autonomy implicitly

### Areas for Improvement  
- Reflections are mostly simple and repetitive; need more complexity and emotional depth  
- Change talk is weakly evoked and not amplified or reinforced  
- Sustain talk is acknowledged but not skillfully softened or redirected  
- Affirmations are sparse and generic; more specific recognition of strengths needed  
- No explicit planning or summarizing to consolidate motivation or next steps  
- Partnership is partial; more explicit sharing of power and co-creation needed  
- Engagement could be deepened with more personalized connection and warmth  
- No use of “persuade with permission” or explicit autonomy statements

---

## Recommendations

1. **Deepen Reflections:**  
   Use more complex and amplified reflections that capture underlying emotions, values, and ambivalence to move beyond surface content.

2. **Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When change talk emerges, reflect it back emphatically, explore reasons, confidence, and commitment, and strategically evoke more motivation.

3. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and gentle redirection to acknowledge sustain talk while guiding toward change.

4. **Increase Affirmations:**  
   Offer frequent, specific affirmations that recognize the patient’s resilience, strengths, and efforts to cope.

5. **Explicitly Support Autonomy:**  
   Reinforce the patient’s right to choose and control the process with clear statements.

6. **Enhance Partnership:**  
   Share power by explicitly inviting the patient to co-create goals and plans, and negotiate the agenda.

7. **Develop Planning and Summarizing Skills:**  
   Summarize key points, consolidate change talk, and collaboratively develop concrete next steps.

8. **Engage More Personally:**  
   Personalize responses to the patient’s unique experience and avoid formulaic language to build deeper rapport.

9. **Use Persuade with Permission:**  
   When offering information or suggestions, always ask permission first to maintain collaboration and respect.

---

## Overall Score: **3/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates foundational MI skills with consistent respect, empathy, and collaboration. However, it falls short of excellence due to formulaic reflections, weak evocation and reinforcement of change talk, limited handling of sustain talk, and absence of planning or summarizing. To improve, the psychiatrist must deepen emotional attunement, skillfully manage ambivalence, and more actively evoke and reinforce the patient’s own motivations and strengths while co-creating the change process.
