# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diabetes Management Session with Jonathan Reed

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a generally respectful and nonjudgmental tone throughout the session. Early on, the psychiatrist validates the patient’s feelings of overwhelm and burden (“That’s a really common experience,” “That’s a really understandable place to be”), which demonstrates acceptance. The psychiatrist avoids labeling or blaming and acknowledges the patient’s ambivalence without criticism. However, the acceptance sometimes feels somewhat formulaic and lacks deeper warmth or explicit unconditional positive regard. For example, the psychiatrist could have more explicitly normalized the patient’s resistance or frustration as a natural response to a difficult situation. There is no overt disapproval, but the acceptance could be more personalized and empathetic to reach a perfect score.

**Summary:**  
Respectful and supportive, but acceptance is somewhat surface-level and formulaic rather than deeply warm or individualized.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates good reflective listening and perspective-taking. Reflections accurately capture the patient’s feelings of overwhelm, burden, doubt, and ambivalence (e.g., “You’re carrying a lot of doubt,” “You’re weighing the effort against the payoff”). The psychiatrist consistently paraphrases the patient’s concerns and emotional state, showing understanding. However, the reflections tend to be somewhat repetitive and occasionally lack emotional depth or nuance. For example, the psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore the patient’s frustration or guarded affect more deeply, or to validate the emotional toll of feeling stuck. The reflections are mostly simple and surface-level rather than complex or evocative.

**Summary:**  
Good understanding and reflective listening, but reflections could be more nuanced and emotionally attuned.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 3/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist adopts a collaborative stance, inviting the patient’s perspective (“What would you like to focus on?” “What do you think might help?”) and supporting autonomy (“If you were to imagine…”). There is no evidence of persuasion or directive advice. However, the session feels somewhat formulaic and lacks strong evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values beyond surface-level statements. The psychiatrist does not explicitly reinforce the patient’s autonomy or agency beyond general invitations to share thoughts. The partnership is present but not fully equal or co-created; the psychiatrist leads most of the agenda and framing. The spirit of MI is moderate but not fully embodied.

**Summary:**  
Collaborative and autonomy-supportive but lacks deeper evocation and full partnership.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 3/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist attempts to evoke change talk by asking about small manageable steps and past successes (“What might that look like?” “Have there been times you stuck with something tough?”). The patient offers some tentative change talk (e.g., “Maybe just checking my blood sugar once in a while,” “I guess I could try it a little more if I have to”). However, the psychiatrist does not consistently reinforce, amplify, or strategically respond to change talk. There are missed opportunities to reflect and deepen the patient’s emerging motivation or to explore confidence and commitment more explicitly. The evocation is present but shallow and inconsistent.

**Summary:**  
Some evocation of change talk, but lacks consistent reinforcement and strategic amplification.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 3/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist acknowledges sustain talk and ambivalence without judgment (“It sounds like a lot of work for something that might not even change much,” “That makes a lot of sense”). However, responses are mostly neutral reflections rather than active softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or explicitly normalize sustain talk while gently guiding toward change. The sustain talk remains largely unchallenged or unresolved, which limits progress in resolving ambivalence.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is acknowledged but not skillfully softened or redirected.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 3/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist invites the patient’s input and perspective, maintaining a collaborative tone. However, the psychiatrist largely controls the agenda and framing, with limited explicit sharing of power or co-creation of the process. The patient’s role is mostly reactive rather than active in shaping the session. The partnership is partial and inconsistent rather than fully equal or client-centered.

**Summary:**  
Basic collaboration present but lacks full partnership and shared power.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, nonjudgmental, and empathetic stance.  
- Uses open questions and reflections to engage the patient and explore ambivalence.  
- Attempts to evoke small steps and past successes to build motivation.

### Areas for Improvement  
- Reflections are often simple and repetitive; need more complexity and emotional depth.  
- Affirmations are minimal; more explicit recognition of patient strengths and efforts needed.  
- Sustain talk is acknowledged but not skillfully softened or redirected.  
- Change talk evocation is shallow and not reinforced or amplified.  
- Partnership is partial; more explicit sharing of power and co-creation needed.  
- Planning and summarizing are weak; no clear consolidation of next steps or commitment.  
- No use of “persuade with permission” to offer information or advice.  
- The session feels formulaic and lacks the dynamic, evocative spirit of high-level MI.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Deepen Reflections:**  
   Use more complex reflections that capture underlying emotions, values, and ambivalence. Reflect not only content but also emotional nuance and meaning.

2. **Increase Affirmations:**  
   Explicitly recognize the patient’s strengths, efforts, and past successes. Affirm honesty and willingness to engage despite resistance.

3. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalization to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.

4. **Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When change talk emerges, reflect and reinforce it to deepen motivation. Explicitly elicit commitment and confidence language.

5. **Enhance Partnership:**  
   Share power more explicitly by inviting the patient to co-create the agenda and plan. Use inclusive language to minimize power differentials.

6. **Incorporate Persuade with Permission:**  
   When offering information or advice, ask permission first to respect autonomy and increase receptivity.

7. **Strengthen Planning and Summarizing:**  
   Collaboratively develop concrete, actionable next steps and summarize change talk to reinforce motivation and commitment.

8. **Personalize and Evoke:**  
   Avoid formulaic responses; tailor reflections and questions to the patient’s unique experience and values. Actively evoke the patient’s own reasons and ideas for change.

---

## Overall Score: **3/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a solid foundation in motivational interviewing with consistent respect, empathy, and collaboration. However, it falls short of excellence due to formulaic responses, lack of depth in reflections, insufficient amplification of change talk, minimal affirmations, and weak planning and summarizing. To improve, the psychiatrist must deepen emotional attunement, skillfully handle ambivalence, and more actively evoke and reinforce the patient’s own motivations and strengths while sharing power and co-creating the process.
