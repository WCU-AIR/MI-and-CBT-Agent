# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation – David Morales Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a generally respectful and nonjudgmental tone throughout the session. Early on, the psychiatrist explicitly acknowledges the patient’s concerns and skepticism without dismissing them (“Thank you for sharing that with me—it sounds like you’re feeling uncertain…”). The psychiatrist validates the patient’s feelings about side effects and the desire for control, which supports acceptance. However, the acceptance sometimes feels somewhat formulaic and lacks deeper warmth or explicit unconditional positive regard. For example, the psychiatrist does not explicitly normalize the patient’s ambivalence or skepticism as common and understandable, which would have enhanced acceptance to a 5. The patient’s guarded and skeptical demeanor is met with respect but not fully embraced with warmth or empathy that might have encouraged more openness.

**Summary:**  
Respectful and supportive stance is maintained, but the acceptance could be warmer and more explicitly unconditional to fully meet the patient’s guardedness.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates adequate reflective listening and perspective-taking, often paraphrasing the patient’s concerns about side effects and uncertainty (“It sounds like you really value feeling strong and healthy…”). These reflections capture content and some emotional undertones. However, the empathy is somewhat surface-level and lacks deeper emotional attunement or exploration of the patient’s underlying fears and ambivalence. For example, the psychiatrist misses opportunities to reflect the patient’s frustration, fear of losing independence, or emotional impact of feeling “stuck” between options. The reflections tend to restate content rather than delve into the emotional nuance or validate the patient’s internal conflict more fully.

**Summary:**  
Adequate understanding and paraphrasing are present, but empathy lacks depth and emotional nuance that would better engage the patient’s guarded affect.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist generally adopts a collaborative stance, inviting the patient’s perspective and supporting autonomy (“What do you think would help you feel more comfortable…?”). There is no evidence of persuasion or directive advice, which aligns with MI spirit. However, the session feels somewhat formulaic and lacks strong evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values beyond surface-level statements. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to more explicitly evoke the patient’s reasons for change or to reinforce the patient’s sense of agency and control. The partnership is present but not fully equal or co-created; the psychiatrist leads the agenda and does not explicitly negotiate or share power in decision-making.

**Summary:**  
Collaboration and autonomy support are evident but limited in depth and evocation; the session lacks the dynamic, evocative quality of high-level MI.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist elicits some change talk by asking about previous attempts at lifestyle change and confidence (“Thinking back to times when you’ve made changes… what has helped you?”). The patient offers some statements of desire and ability (“I stuck with jogging because I felt stronger…”). However, the psychiatrist does not consistently reinforce or amplify this change talk. There are missed opportunities to reflect and deepen the patient’s motivation or to strategically evoke commitment language. For example, when the patient expresses willingness to consider regular testing or follow-up, the psychiatrist does not capitalize on this to strengthen commitment or explore confidence further.

**Summary:**  
Change talk is present but not skillfully cultivated or amplified; missed opportunities to deepen motivation and elicit commitment.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist acknowledges the patient’s sustain talk (skepticism about medication, concerns about side effects, reluctance to commit). However, responses tend to be neutral reflections rather than active softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or explicitly normalize ambivalence to reduce resistance. For example, the patient’s repeated concerns about muscle weakness are acknowledged but not reframed or gently challenged to reduce sustain talk’s impact. The session lacks skillful handling of sustain talk that would help the patient resolve ambivalence more effectively.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is acknowledged but not actively softened or redirected; responses are mostly neutral rather than strategically guiding toward change.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a collaborative tone and invites the patient’s input throughout. However, the partnership is somewhat one-sided, with the psychiatrist guiding the agenda and not explicitly sharing power or co-creating the plan. The patient’s expertise and preferences are acknowledged but not fully integrated into decision-making. For example, the psychiatrist could have explicitly invited the patient to set the agenda or choose topics to explore more deeply. The session lacks explicit negotiation of roles or shared decision-making language that would demonstrate a fully equal partnership.

**Summary:**  
Collaboration is present but limited; partnership could be strengthened by more explicit sharing of power and co-creation of the process.

---

**Areas for Improvement:**

- Change talk is not consistently amplified or reinforced.  
- Sustain talk is acknowledged but not skillfully softened or redirected.  
- Partnership is present but not fully equal or co-created.  
- The session feels formulaic and lacks dynamic evocation and emotional engagement.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Respectful, nonjudgmental approach that validates patient concerns.  
- Use of open questions and reflective listening to engage patient.  
- Support for autonomy and collaborative tone.  
- Some elicitation of change talk and discussion of practical next steps.

### Areas for Improvement  
- Empathy needs to be deeper and more emotionally attuned to patient’s guardedness and ambivalence.  
- Reflections should be more complex and capture emotional nuance.  
- Change talk should be actively amplified and reinforced to build motivation.  
- Sustain talk requires more skillful softening and strategic redirection.  
- Affirmations should be more specific and personalized.  
- Planning and summarizing are weak; explicit consolidation of commitment and next steps is needed.  
- Partnership could be strengthened by more explicit sharing of power and co-creation of agenda.  
- Persuasion with permission is lacking; explicit permission should be sought before offering advice or information.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Deepen Empathy and Reflections:**  
   Use complex reflections that capture underlying emotions, fears, and ambivalence. Reflect not only content but also emotional experience and values.

2. **Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When change talk emerges, reflect and reinforce it to deepen motivation. Explicitly elicit commitment and confidence language.

3. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalization to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.

4. **Strengthen Affirmations:**  
   Offer specific, genuine affirmations that recognize patient strengths, past successes, and values.

5. **Enhance Planning and Summarizing:**  
   Provide explicit summaries that consolidate change talk and reinforce commitment. Collaboratively develop a concrete, actionable plan.

6. **Increase Partnership and Collaboration:**  
   Explicitly share power by inviting the patient to set the agenda and co-create the plan.

7. **Use Persuasion with Permission:**  
   Always ask permission before offering information or advice to respect autonomy and reduce resistance.

8. **Personalize and Evoke More Deeply:**  
   Avoid formulaic responses; tailor questions and reflections to the patient’s unique concerns and values to evoke intrinsic motivation.

---

## Overall Score: **3/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a foundational understanding of motivational interviewing principles with consistent respect, basic empathy, and collaborative stance. However, it falls short of excellence due to superficial empathy, limited evocation and amplification of change talk, weak handling of sustain talk, and insufficient planning and summarizing. To improve, the psychiatrist must deepen emotional attunement, skillfully manage ambivalence, and more actively evoke and reinforce the patient’s own motivations and strengths while fostering a fully equal partnership.
