# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a generally respectful and supportive tone throughout the session. Early affirmations such as “Thank you for sharing that with me” and “It sounds like you’re building a solid foundation for change” demonstrate positive regard. The psychiatrist avoids judgment or criticism and validates the patient’s ambivalence and current readiness level. However, the acceptance is somewhat formulaic and lacks deeper warmth or explicit unconditional positive regard. For example, the psychiatrist could have normalized the patient’s ambivalence more explicitly or acknowledged the difficulty of change in a more personalized way. There is no evidence of labeling or disapproval, but the interaction feels somewhat clinical rather than warmly supportive.

**Summary:**  
Respectful and supportive, but could deepen warmth and unconditional acceptance to fully meet the highest standard.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates good reflective listening and perspective-taking. Reflections such as “It sounds like there’s a real tension for you” and “You’re balancing curiosity about quitting with the comfort and routine smoking provides” accurately capture the patient’s ambivalence and emotional state. The psychiatrist listens carefully and paraphrases the patient’s feelings and thoughts well. However, reflections tend to be somewhat surface-level and occasionally repetitive, focusing mainly on content rather than deeper emotional nuance. For example, the emotional difficulty of breaking a routine or the patient’s possible fears about quitting are not deeply explored. The empathy is consistent but could be enriched with more complex reflections that capture underlying feelings or values.

**Summary:**  
Good reflective listening and understanding, but lacks consistent depth and emotional nuance.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a collaborative stance, frequently inviting the patient’s perspective (“What do you think would need to happen…?” “What do you imagine might help you stick with that plan?”). Autonomy is supported throughout, with no evidence of persuasion or directive advice. The psychiatrist evokes the patient’s own motivations and ideas for change, such as planning ahead and using gum as a substitute. However, the session feels somewhat formulaic and lacks a fully evocative style that deeply draws out the patient’s values or personal reasons for change. The partnership is present but somewhat superficial; the psychiatrist does not explicitly share power or co-create the agenda beyond inviting input.

**Summary:**  
Strong partnership and autonomy support, but could be more evocative and collaborative in drawing out deeper motivations and values.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist effectively evokes change talk by asking open questions about readiness, past successes, and next steps. The patient offers several statements of desire, ability, and reasons for change (“I’m kind of open to the idea,” “Sometimes I’ve tried cutting back,” “Maybe I could try planning ahead”). The psychiatrist reflects and reinforces these statements, amplifying the patient’s own motivations. However, there are missed opportunities to deepen or strategically amplify change talk further, such as exploring the patient’s values related to health or appearance, or eliciting stronger commitment language. The evocation is consistent but could be more skillful and intentional.

**Summary:**  
Frequent evocation of change talk, but lacks consistent amplification and deeper exploration of motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 3/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist acknowledges sustain talk indirectly by reflecting ambivalence and the patient’s current comfort with smoking (“the status quo feels more comfortable and familiar”). However, there is little active softening or strategic redirection of sustain talk. The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or explicitly validate the patient’s struggle with change while gently guiding toward change. Sustain talk is mostly neutrally acknowledged rather than skillfully minimized or reframed. This limits the potential to resolve ambivalence more effectively.

**Summary:**  
Some acknowledgment of sustain talk, but lacks active softening or strategic handling to reduce its impact.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a collaborative stance, inviting the patient’s input and ideas throughout the session. Language such as “What do you think…” and “How do you feel about…” supports partnership. The psychiatrist avoids an expert-driven approach and respects the patient’s autonomy. However, the partnership is somewhat superficial and formulaic. There is no explicit negotiation of agenda or shared decision-making beyond inviting responses. The psychiatrist could more explicitly acknowledge the patient’s expertise in their own life and co-create the plan with more equal power sharing.

**Summary:**  
Strong collaboration and client-centeredness, but could further minimize power differential and deepen co-creation.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- Consistently respectful, supportive, and nonjudgmental.  
- Good reflective listening and collaborative stance.  
- Effective evocation of change talk and support for autonomy.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- Reflections lack emotional depth and complexity.  
- Sustain talk is acknowledged but not skillfully softened or redirected.  
- Partnership is present but not fully equal or co-created.  
- Change talk could be amplified more strategically and deeply.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Deepen Reflections:**  
   Use more complex reflections that capture underlying emotions, values, and ambivalence. Reflect not just content but also emotional experience and meaning.

2. **Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When change talk emerges, reflect and reinforce it more strategically. Elicit stronger commitment and confidence language.

3. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and gentle redirection to acknowledge sustain talk while minimizing its impact. Normalize ambivalence and validate the difficulty of change.

4. **Strengthen Affirmations:**  
   Offer specific, genuine affirmations that recognize the patient’s unique strengths, efforts, and values. Avoid generic praise.

5. **Enhance Partnership:**  
   Explicitly share power by co-creating the agenda and plan. Acknowledge the patient’s expertise and invite collaboration in decision-making.

6. **Use Explicit Summaries:**  
   Provide structured summaries that pull together change talk and reinforce motivation and commitment.

7. **Increase Emotional Attunement:**  
   Personalize responses and avoid formulaic phrasing. Be more present and responsive to the patient’s emotional cues.

This session demonstrates a solid foundation in motivational interviewing with consistent respect, empathy, and collaboration. However, it falls short of excellence due to formulaic responses, lack of depth in reflections, insufficient handling of sustain talk, and weak summarizing. To improve, the psychiatrist should deepen emotional attunement, skillfully manage ambivalence, and more actively evoke and reinforce the patient’s own motivations and strengths.
