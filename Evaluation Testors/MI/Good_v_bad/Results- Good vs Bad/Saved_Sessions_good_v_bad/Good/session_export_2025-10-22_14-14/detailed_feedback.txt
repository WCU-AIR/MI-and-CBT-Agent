# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a generally respectful and supportive tone throughout the session. Early affirmations such as “Thank you for sharing that with me” and empathetic reflections (“It’s understandable that starting and stopping can feel frustrating”) demonstrate acceptance of the patient’s experience without judgment. The psychiatrist avoids labeling or disapproval and acknowledges the patient’s ambivalence and barriers. However, the acceptance sometimes feels somewhat formulaic and lacks deeper warmth or explicit unconditional positive regard that would elevate this to a 5. For example, while the psychiatrist acknowledges the patient’s struggle, there is no explicit normalization of difficulty or relapse, which would strengthen acceptance.

**Summary:**  
Respectful and supportive throughout, but could deepen the sense of unconditional positive regard and normalization of struggle.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates good reflective listening and perspective-taking. Reflections accurately capture the patient’s ambivalence and emotional state, such as “That push and pull between your goals and the social dynamics must feel pretty heavy at times.” The psychiatrist listens attentively and validates the patient’s feelings about family dynamics and personal challenges. However, some reflections remain surface-level and do not consistently explore the emotional nuance or underlying fears (e.g., the patient’s fear of causing tension or feeling isolated). There is room to deepen empathy by more explicitly naming emotions like fear, frustration, or discouragement.

**Summary:**  
Good reflective listening and understanding, but could deepen emotional nuance and attunement.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a collaborative stance, inviting the patient’s perspective and supporting autonomy (“What do you think would make it easier for you to introduce those kinds of changes?”). There is no evidence of persuasion or directive advice. The psychiatrist evokes the patient’s own motivations and ideas, such as exploring small manageable steps and family dynamics. However, the session occasionally feels somewhat formulaic and lacks a fully evocative style that draws out deeper values or personal meaning. The psychiatrist could more explicitly reinforce the patient’s autonomy and agency, and more actively evoke commitment language.

**Summary:**  
Strong partnership and autonomy support, but could be more evocative and collaborative in drawing out deeper motivations and commitment.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 3/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist elicits some change talk, such as the patient’s confidence rating (“six”) and ideas for small steps (“picking one meal a week,” “drinking more water”). However, the psychiatrist misses opportunities to amplify or reinforce this change talk. For example, when the patient expresses hope or practical ideas, the psychiatrist often reflects neutrally rather than strategically reinforcing or exploring these statements to deepen motivation. There is no explicit elicitation of commitment language or confidence beyond the scaling question. The evocation of change talk is present but limited in depth and frequency.

**Summary:**  
Some evocation of change talk, but lacks consistent amplification and reinforcement; missed opportunities to deepen motivation and commitment.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 3/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist acknowledges sustain talk, such as the patient’s concerns about family resistance and fear of causing tension. However, responses tend to be neutral reflections rather than active softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or explicitly normalize ambivalence to reduce the impact of sustain talk. For example, the patient’s fear of “messing things up at home” is acknowledged but not gently reframed or explored to reduce resistance. Sustain talk is recognized but not skillfully softened.

**Summary:**  
Occasional acknowledgment of sustain talk, but lacks skillful softening or strategic handling to reduce its impact.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a collaborative stance, frequently inviting the patient’s input and avoiding an expert-driven approach. Language such as “What do you think…” and “What would be a good first step…” supports partnership. The psychiatrist respects the patient’s autonomy and expertise in his own life. However, the partnership is somewhat superficial; there is limited explicit negotiation of agenda or co-creation of the plan beyond general invitations. The psychiatrist could more explicitly share power and co-create the process.

**Summary:**  
Strong collaboration and client-centeredness, but could further minimize power differential and explicitly co-create agenda and plan.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist maintains a respectful, supportive, and nonjudgmental stance.  
- Uses open questions and reflections to engage the patient.  
- Supports autonomy and invites patient’s perspective.  
- Elicits some change talk and explores barriers thoughtfully.

### Areas for Improvement  
- Reflections are often surface-level; more complex, emotionally attuned reflections are needed.  
- Change talk is not consistently amplified or reinforced; missed opportunities to deepen motivation and elicit commitment.  
- Sustain talk is acknowledged but not skillfully softened or reframed.  
- Affirmations are generic and limited; more specific recognition of strengths and resilience is needed.  
- Planning and summarizing are weak; no explicit consolidation of commitment or next steps.  
- Persuasion with permission is absent; explicit permission should be sought before offering advice or suggestions.  
- Partnership could be more explicitly co-created and egalitarian.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Deepen Reflections:**  
   Use more complex reflections that capture underlying emotions, values, and ambivalence. Reflect feelings explicitly to deepen empathy.

2. **Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When change talk arises, reflect and reinforce it strategically to deepen motivation. Explicitly elicit commitment and confidence language.

3. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce the impact of sustain talk and gently guide toward change.

4. **Strengthen Affirmations:**  
   Offer specific, genuine affirmations recognizing the patient’s strengths, past efforts, and resilience.

5. **Enhance Planning and Summarizing:**  
   Explicitly summarize key points, pull together change talk, and collaboratively develop a clear, actionable plan with patient buy-in.

6. **Use Persuade with Permission:**  
   Always ask permission before offering advice or suggestions to respect autonomy and enhance collaboration.

7. **Increase Evocation and Collaboration:**  
   More actively draw out the patient’s own motivations, values, and ideas for change. Share power by explicitly co-creating agenda and plan.

8. **Avoid Formulaic Responses:**  
   Personalize responses and avoid repetitive or scripted phrasing. Be present and responsive to the unique flow of the conversation.

---

## Overall Score: **3/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a solid foundation in motivational interviewing with consistent respect, empathy, and collaboration. However, it falls short of excellence due to formulaic responses, lack of depth in reflections, insufficient amplification of change talk, and weak planning and summarizing. To improve, the psychiatrist must deepen emotional attunement, skillfully handle ambivalence, and more actively evoke and reinforce the patient’s own motivations and strengths. Explicitly co-creating the plan and seeking permission before advice will further enhance the MI spirit and effectiveness.
