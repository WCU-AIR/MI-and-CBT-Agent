# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation – Asthma Management & Lifestyle Change Session

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a generally respectful and supportive tone throughout the session. Early affirmations such as “Thank you for sharing that with me” and recognition of the patient’s “strength and awareness” for cutting down smoking demonstrate respect and positive regard. The psychiatrist avoids judgment or criticism and validates the patient’s ambivalence and challenges. However, the acceptance occasionally feels somewhat procedural rather than deeply warm or personalized. For example, while the psychiatrist acknowledges the patient’s struggle, there is limited explicit normalization of the difficulty of change or unconditional acceptance of the patient’s current state. The acceptance is consistent but could be warmer and more individualized to reach a perfect score.

**Summary:**  
Respectful and supportive throughout, but lacks the deeper warmth and normalization that would elevate acceptance to the highest level.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates good reflective listening and perspective-taking. Reflections accurately capture the patient’s ambivalence, such as balancing “the weight of a long-standing habit with the hope that making this change could bring real relief.” The psychiatrist also reflects the patient’s concerns about stress and cravings and the difficulty of replacing smoking with other coping strategies. These reflections show understanding of both the content and emotional undertones. However, the empathy is somewhat surface-level and occasionally formulaic, lacking deeper emotional nuance or exploration of the patient’s feelings of frustration, fear, or resignation. For example, the psychiatrist could have explored more the emotional impact of feeling “not ready” or the patient’s sense of isolation in the change process.

**Summary:**  
Good reflective listening and perspective-taking, but could deepen emotional attunement and explore underlying feelings more fully.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a collaborative stance, inviting the patient’s perspective and supporting autonomy throughout (“What do you think would make the biggest difference…?”, “What do you imagine would need to happen…”). The session avoids directive or authoritarian language and encourages the patient to consider his own motivations and barriers. The psychiatrist also supports the patient’s right to experiment with a “test run” rather than pushing for immediate quitting. However, the spirit of MI is somewhat formulaic and lacks full evocation of the patient’s intrinsic motivations or values beyond the surface level. The psychiatrist could have more explicitly reinforced the patient’s agency and co-created the agenda or plan with more shared power.

**Summary:**  
Strong partnership and autonomy support, but could be more evocative and collaborative in drawing out deeper motivations and values.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist effectively elicits change talk by asking open-ended questions about what would help, confidence levels, and potential supports. The patient offers multiple statements of desire, ability, reasons, and need for change (e.g., “Maybe if I knew for sure it’d stop the attacks, I’d think about it more seriously,” “I’m halfway confident”). The psychiatrist reflects and reinforces these statements, helping to deepen the patient’s consideration of change. However, there are missed opportunities to amplify or strategically respond to change talk with more enthusiasm or to explicitly elicit commitment language beyond the patient’s tentative plans. The evocation is consistent but could be more skillful and dynamic.

**Summary:**  
Frequent evocation of change talk with good patient engagement, but lacks consistent amplification and explicit elicitation of commitment.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist acknowledges sustain talk, such as the patient’s ambivalence about quitting smoking completely and reliance on smoking for stress relief. However, responses tend to be neutral reflections rather than active softening or strategic redirection. For example, the psychiatrist reflects the patient’s concerns about cravings and stress but does not use double-sided reflections or reframe sustain talk to gently guide toward change. There is little evidence of normalizing ambivalence or minimizing the impact of sustain talk to reduce resistance. The sustain talk is acknowledged but not skillfully softened.

**Summary:**  
Some acknowledgment of sustain talk but lacks strategic handling or active softening to reduce its impact.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a collaborative stance, frequently inviting the patient’s input and avoiding an expert-driven approach. The patient is asked to identify what feels doable and to co-create next steps, such as involving his wife and setting small goals. The psychiatrist uses inclusive language and supports patient autonomy. However, the partnership is somewhat superficial and formulaic. There is limited explicit sharing of power or negotiation of agenda beyond standard MI phrasing. The psychiatrist could more explicitly acknowledge the patient’s expertise and invite him to lead the process more fully.

**Summary:**  
Strong collaboration and client-centeredness, but partnership could be deepened with more explicit power sharing and co-creation.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- Consistent respect and support for the patient’s ambivalence and partial progress.  
- Good reflective listening and perspective-taking.  
- Collaborative and autonomy-supportive stance.  
- Effective elicitation of change talk.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- Reflections and empathy could be deeper and more emotionally nuanced.  
- Sustain talk is acknowledged but not skillfully softened or redirected.  
- Change talk could be amplified more enthusiastically and commitment language explicitly elicited.  
- Partnership is present but could be more fully equal and co-created.  
- Responses sometimes feel formulaic rather than personalized.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **3.7/5** (rounded down to 3 for strictness)

- Good use of open questions and reflective listening.  
- Collaborative and autonomy-supportive stance.  
- Effective elicitation of change talk and patient engagement.

### Areas for Improvement  
- Reflections lack emotional depth and complexity; more complex reflections needed.  
- Sustain talk is acknowledged but not skillfully softened or redirected.  
- Affirmations are generic; more specific, personalized affirmations needed.  
- Planning and summarizing are weak; no explicit consolidation of commitment or next steps.  
- Partnership is present but could be more fully equal and co-created.  
- Responses sometimes feel formulaic rather than personalized and dynamic.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Deepen Reflections:**  
   Use more complex reflections that capture underlying emotions, ambivalence, and values. Reflect not just content but emotional experience.

2. **Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When change talk emerges, reflect and reinforce it enthusiastically. Explicitly elicit commitment and confidence language.

3. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and reframe sustain talk to gently guide toward change. Normalize ambivalence and validate difficulty.

4. **Strengthen Affirmations:**  
   Offer specific, genuine affirmations recognizing unique patient strengths, efforts, and values.

5. **Enhance Planning and Summarizing:**  
   Provide explicit summaries that pull together change talk and reinforce motivation. Collaboratively develop a concrete, actionable plan.

6. **Increase Evocation and Collaboration:**  
   More actively draw out patient’s own motivations and values. Share power by explicitly inviting patient to co-create agenda and plan.

7. **Avoid Formulaic Responses:**  
   Personalize responses and avoid repetitive phrasing. Be present and responsive to the unique flow of the conversation.

---

## Overall Score: **3.7/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a solid foundation in motivational interviewing with consistent respect, empathy, and collaboration. However, it falls short of excellence due to formulaic responses, lack of depth in reflections, insufficient amplification of change talk, and weak planning and summarizing. To reach a higher level of MI proficiency, the psychiatrist must deepen emotional attunement, skillfully handle ambivalence, and more actively evoke and reinforce the patient’s own motivations and strengths.
