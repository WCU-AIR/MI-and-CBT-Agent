# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session with Erik Johansson

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 5/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently demonstrates unconditional positive regard throughout the session. From the opening question inviting the patient to set the agenda (“what would you most like to focus on?”) to affirming the patient’s experiences and struggles, the tone is warm, respectful, and nonjudgmental. The psychiatrist explicitly acknowledges both progress and difficulties without criticism or blame (e.g., “That mix of pushing through discomfort while holding onto what matters to you really shows your resilience”). There is no evidence of labeling or judgment, and the patient’s ambivalence and self-criticism are met with acceptance and normalization.  

**Summary:**  
The psychiatrist excels in creating a safe, supportive environment that fosters openness and trust. This is a clear strength of the session.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates good reflective listening and perspective-taking, accurately capturing the patient’s ambivalence, emotional struggles, and motivations. For example, reflections such as “You’ve really captured something deep there—the desire not just to avoid pain, but to reclaim a sense of normalcy and freedom” show understanding beyond surface content. The psychiatrist also validates the patient’s discouragement and self-critical thoughts. However, while reflections are accurate and supportive, they tend to be somewhat formulaic and occasionally lack deeper emotional nuance or exploration of underlying feelings (e.g., the patient’s discouragement about younger gym-goers could have been explored more emotionally).  

**Summary:**  
Empathy is strong and consistent but could be deepened by more nuanced reflections that explore emotional subtext and unspoken feelings.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a collaborative stance, inviting the patient’s perspective and supporting autonomy (“What do you think has helped you get to the gym?” “What fits for you right now?”). The session avoids directive or authoritarian language and emphasizes partnership. The psychiatrist evokes the patient’s own motivations and values effectively, especially around health and family. However, the session occasionally feels somewhat structured and formulaic, with repeated phrasing (“It sounds like…”) that limits spontaneity and deeper evocation. There is room to more actively evoke the patient’s own language and to co-create the agenda and plan with more explicit negotiation.  

**Summary:**  
Strong collaboration and autonomy support are evident, but the session could be more evocative and less formulaic to fully embody the MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist skillfully elicits and reinforces change talk throughout the session. The patient offers multiple statements of desire, reasons, and ability (“My pants feel a little better,” “I want to be around for my family,” “I think I could aim for two consistent days”). The psychiatrist reflects and amplifies these statements, linking them to the patient’s values and goals. The use of scaling questions (“On a scale from 0 to 10…”) effectively elicits confidence talk. However, there are missed opportunities to deepen commitment language or to strategically amplify change talk with more evocative reflections or summaries that consolidate motivation.  

**Summary:**  
Change talk is well cultivated but could be strengthened by more strategic amplification and elicitation of commitment and taking steps language.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 3/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist acknowledges sustain talk and ambivalence (e.g., frustration with gym attendance, discouragement about younger gym-goers) without judgment. However, responses to sustain talk are mostly neutral reflections rather than active softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or explicitly normalize ambivalence to reduce the impact of sustain talk. There is little evidence of gently guiding the patient toward resolving ambivalence or reframing sustain talk in a way that lessens its influence.  

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is acknowledged but not skillfully softened or redirected. More strategic handling of sustain talk is needed to help the patient move toward change.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently invites the patient’s input and avoids an expert-driven stance. The patient is encouraged to set goals and identify manageable steps, and the psychiatrist offers suggestions only with permission (“Would it be okay if I shared a few easy ideas?”). This demonstrates respect for the patient’s autonomy and expertise in their own life. However, the partnership could be enhanced by more explicit co-creation of the agenda and plan, and by sharing power more overtly throughout the session.  

**Summary:**  
Strong partnership is evident, but the session could further minimize power differentials by explicitly negotiating goals and plans with the patient.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- Consistent, warm acceptance and respect.  
- Good reflective listening and empathy.  
- Collaborative, autonomy-supportive stance.  
- Effective elicitation of change talk and patient motivation.  

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- Reflections could be more emotionally nuanced and less formulaic.  
- Sustain talk is acknowledged but not skillfully softened or redirected.  
- More explicit co-creation of agenda and plan would strengthen partnership.  
- Amplification of commitment language and strategic evocation of change talk could be improved.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist creates a warm, accepting, and supportive environment.  
- Good use of open questions and affirmations to build motivation.  
- Consistent respect for autonomy and collaborative stance.  
- Effective elicitation of change talk and patient’s own motivations.  

### Areas for Improvement  
- Reflections are often formulaic and lack emotional depth; more complex reflections needed.  
- Sustain talk is acknowledged but not skillfully softened or reframed.  
- Partnership could be enhanced by more explicit co-creation of agenda and plan.  
- Summaries are implicit rather than explicit; more structured summarizing would reinforce motivation and commitment.  
- Amplification of commitment and taking steps language could be stronger.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Deepen Reflections:**  
   Use more complex and evocative reflections that capture emotional nuance and underlying meaning, not just content. Vary phrasing to avoid formulaic repetition.

2. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Employ double-sided reflections and normalization to reduce the impact of sustain talk and gently guide the patient toward change.

3. **Enhance Partnership:**  
   Explicitly negotiate agenda and co-create plans with the patient to minimize power differentials and increase collaboration.

4. **Strengthen Summarizing:**  
   Use explicit summaries that pull together change talk, reinforce motivation, and consolidate commitment and next steps.

5. **Amplify Commitment Language:**  
   When commitment or taking steps language emerges, reflect and reinforce it to deepen motivation and readiness.

6. **Increase Evocation:**  
   More actively evoke the patient’s own reasons, values, and confidence, using evocative questions and reflections.

---

## Overall Score: **4/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates strong foundational motivational interviewing skills, with excellent acceptance, empathy, and collaboration. The psychiatrist effectively elicits and supports change talk and respects patient autonomy. However, the session is somewhat formulaic, with reflections that lack emotional depth and sustain talk that is not skillfully softened. Partnership and planning could be more explicitly co-created, and summarizing could be more structured to consolidate motivation. With targeted improvements in these areas, the psychiatrist could elevate this work to a higher level of MI proficiency and better support the patient’s journey toward sustained behavior change.
