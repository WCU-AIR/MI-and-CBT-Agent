# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session with Andrei Petrov

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a generally respectful and nonjudgmental tone throughout the session. The patient’s brief and minimizing responses are met with patience and validation rather than confrontation or dismissal. For example, the psychiatrist acknowledges Andrei’s perspective on medication adherence and his feelings about the abstract nature of blood pressure management without judgment (“That makes a lot of sense, Andrei—you’re keeping an eye on how things are going…”). However, the acceptance is somewhat surface-level and formulaic. There is limited explicit expression of unconditional positive regard or normalization of ambivalence, which would elevate this to a 5. The psychiatrist could have more explicitly affirmed the patient’s autonomy and validated the difficulty of engaging with a largely asymptomatic condition.

**Summary:**  
Generally respectful and supportive, but lacks deeper warmth and explicit normalization of ambivalence or struggle.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates an adequate but somewhat inconsistent effort to understand Andrei’s perspective. There are attempts at reflective listening, such as summarizing the patient’s feelings about medication and the abstractness of blood pressure management (“It sounds like you’re doing your part... yet the blood pressure still feels a bit out of control”). However, reflections tend to be more content-focused and less emotionally nuanced. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore the patient’s emotional experience more deeply, such as the possible frustration or resignation underlying Andrei’s passive stance. The patient’s minimizing tone and brief responses are not fully explored or reflected back with emotional depth.

**Summary:**  
Adequate understanding and reflections, but lacks consistent emotional attunement and deeper perspective-taking.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a moderate level of collaboration and autonomy support. The session opens with an invitation to set the agenda (“What would you like to focus on?”), and the psychiatrist avoids directive or authoritarian language. There is some evocation of the patient’s own motivations and past successes (e.g., quitting smoking). However, the collaboration feels somewhat superficial and formulaic. The psychiatrist often leads with closed or narrowly focused questions and does not fully engage the patient in co-creating the plan or exploring his values and motivations in depth. The autonomy support is present but not strongly emphasized, and evocation of change talk is limited.

**Summary:**  
Moderate partnership and autonomy support, but lacks full collaboration and evocative engagement.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**  
Change talk is minimally evoked and not skillfully cultivated. The psychiatrist asks about readiness on a 0–10 scale and elicits a past success story (quitting smoking), but these moments are not fully leveraged to deepen motivation or commitment. The patient’s change talk remains tentative and low in intensity (“Probably a 3 or 4,” “Maybe just keeping an eye on it”). The psychiatrist misses opportunities to reflect and amplify these statements or to explore the patient’s confidence and reasons for change more thoroughly. There is no explicit elicitation of commitment language or exploration of ability and confidence beyond the initial scale question.

**Summary:**  
Limited and shallow evocation of change talk; missed opportunities to deepen and reinforce motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist acknowledges sustain talk and ambivalence without reinforcing it. For example, the patient’s minimization of stress and the abstractness of blood pressure management are met with reflective statements that neither challenge nor fully redirect sustain talk (“It sounds like you feel confident that the medications are doing their part…”). However, the responses are mostly neutral reflections rather than strategic softening or reframing. The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or explicitly normalize ambivalence to reduce resistance. There is some gentle redirection toward small steps (home monitoring), but this is limited.

**Summary:**  
Some acknowledgment and neutral handling of sustain talk, but lacks active softening or strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist invites the patient to set the agenda initially and asks for input about what might help with home monitoring. However, the overall interaction is somewhat one-sided, with the psychiatrist guiding the conversation and the patient responding briefly and passively. The psychiatrist does not explicitly share power or negotiate the plan in a way that fully engages the patient as an equal partner. The patient’s brief and minimal responses are not met with efforts to deepen collaboration or invite more active participation.

**Summary:**  
Partial collaboration with some invitation for input, but limited shared power and co-creation.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Deepen Reflective Listening:**  
   Use more complex reflections that capture underlying emotions, ambivalence, and values rather than just content. Reflect the patient’s minimizing tone and explore possible feelings of frustration or resignation.

2. **Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When change talk emerges, reflect it back with emphasis, ask follow-up questions to deepen motivation, and explicitly elicit commitment and confidence language.

3. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change. Normalize ambivalence and validate the difficulty of engaging with asymptomatic conditions.

4. **Enhance Partnership and Collaboration:**  
   Actively invite the patient to co-create the agenda and plan. Share power by explicitly asking for preferences and checking in about next steps.

5. **Explicitly Seek Permission:**  
   Before offering advice or suggestions (e.g., about home monitoring), ask permission to maintain collaboration and respect autonomy.

6. **Strengthen Planning and Summarizing:**  
   Develop a concrete, actionable plan with the patient and summarize key points, especially change talk, to reinforce motivation and commitment.

7. **Increase Engagement:**  
   Use personalized, evocative language to deepen rapport and encourage more active patient participation, especially given the patient’s brief and minimizing style.

---

## Overall Score: **3/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a foundational understanding of motivational interviewing principles, with respectful tone and some reflective listening. However, it falls short of excellence due to formulaic and surface-level reflections, minimal evocation and amplification of change talk, and limited partnership and planning. To improve, the psychiatrist must deepen emotional attunement, skillfully handle sustain talk, and more actively engage the patient in co-creating motivation and plans for change.
