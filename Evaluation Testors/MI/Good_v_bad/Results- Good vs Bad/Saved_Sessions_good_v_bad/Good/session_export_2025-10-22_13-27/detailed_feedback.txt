# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a generally respectful and supportive tone throughout the session. The patient’s guarded and somewhat defensive posture is met with nonjudgmental curiosity and validation (e.g., “I appreciate you sharing that,” “That makes a lot of sense”). The psychiatrist avoids any labeling or confrontation, which is appropriate given the patient’s precontemplative stance. However, the acceptance feels somewhat procedural and lacks warmth or explicit expressions of unconditional positive regard that would elevate this to a 5. For example, there is no explicit normalization of the patient’s ambivalence or defensiveness, which could have deepened rapport.

**Summary:**  
Respectful and supportive, but somewhat formulaic and lacking deeper warmth or explicit normalization of patient’s stance.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 3/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates adequate reflective listening and perspective-taking, accurately summarizing the patient’s expressed concerns and motivations (e.g., “It sounds like simplicity and prevention are really important to you”). However, reflections tend to be surface-level and largely paraphrasing content rather than capturing emotional nuance or underlying meaning. The patient’s defensive posture and minimization of risk are not deeply explored or empathized with. There is a missed opportunity to reflect the patient’s possible fears, ambivalence, or rationalizations more empathically, which would have demonstrated a higher level of understanding.

**Summary:**  
Adequate but somewhat superficial empathy; reflections do not consistently capture emotional depth or underlying ambivalence.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 3/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist adopts a collaborative stance, inviting the patient to set the agenda (“What would you like to focus on?”) and supporting autonomy throughout. There is no evidence of directive or confrontational behavior, which is appropriate. However, the session lacks strong evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values beyond surface-level statements. The psychiatrist does not actively draw out deeper reasons for change or explore ambivalence in a way that would fully embody the MI spirit. The interaction feels somewhat formulaic and procedural rather than dynamically collaborative and evocative.

**Summary:**  
Moderate partnership and autonomy support, but limited evocation and collaboration; lacks dynamic MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist makes some attempts to elicit change talk by asking about importance and motivation (e.g., “On a scale from 0 to 10… how important is it?”). The patient offers some change talk related to prevention and avoiding pain. However, the psychiatrist does not consistently reinforce, reflect, or amplify this change talk. There are missed opportunities to deepen or strategically respond to change talk statements (e.g., “What makes that a 7 and not a lower number?” or “What would it mean for you to catch those signs earlier?”). The evocation of change talk is minimal and lacks skillful reinforcement.

**Summary:**  
Rare and weak efforts to evoke and cultivate change talk; missed opportunities to deepen motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 2/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The patient expresses sustain talk indirectly through minimization and rationalization (e.g., “I don’t have any pain,” “It’s just maintenance stuff”). The psychiatrist acknowledges these statements neutrally but does not actively soften or redirect sustain talk. There are no double-sided reflections or strategic reframing to gently shift the patient toward change. The sustain talk remains largely unchallenged or unsoftened, which limits progress in resolving ambivalence.

**Summary:**  
Rare and weak attempts to soften sustain talk; mostly neutral acknowledgment without strategic redirection.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently invites the patient’s input and respects his autonomy, allowing the patient to set the agenda and express his views. The language is inclusive and collaborative. However, the partnership is somewhat superficial and procedural rather than deeply co-created. There is little explicit sharing of power or negotiation of goals beyond initial agenda setting. The psychiatrist could do more to explicitly acknowledge the patient’s expertise and co-create the plan.

**Summary:**  
Strong collaborative stance but lacks deeper co-creation and explicit power sharing.

---

## Overall Global Ratings Summary

**Strengths:**  
- Consistently respectful, nonjudgmental, and autonomy-supportive.  
- Collaborative stance with agenda-setting and patient input.  
- Some elicitation of motivation and patient’s own reasons for care.

**Areas for Improvement:**  
- Reflections lack emotional depth and fail to explore ambivalence or defensiveness.  
- Minimal and weak evocation and reinforcement of change talk.  
- Sustain talk is acknowledged but not softened or redirected.  
- Partnership is present but superficial and procedural.  
- The session feels formulaic and lacks the dynamic, evocative spirit of MI.

**Overall Global Ratings Score:** **3/5**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Respectful, nonjudgmental, and autonomy-supportive approach.  
- Collaborative agenda-setting and patient engagement.  
- Some elicitation of motivation and patient’s own reasons for care.

### Areas for Improvement  
- Reflections lack emotional depth and complexity.  
- Minimal and weak evocation and reinforcement of change talk.  
- Sustain talk is not skillfully softened or redirected.  
- Affirmations are sparse and generic.  
- Planning and summarizing are weak and lack explicit commitment.  
- The session feels formulaic and lacks dynamic MI spirit.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Deepen Reflections:**  
   Use more complex reflections that capture underlying emotions, ambivalence, and patient’s rationalizations. Reflect not just content but emotional subtext.

2. **Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When change talk emerges, reflect and reinforce it to deepen motivation. Ask evocative questions to explore reasons, desire, ability, and commitment.

3. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and gentle reframing to acknowledge sustain talk while guiding toward change. Normalize ambivalence and validate patient’s perspective.

4. **Increase Affirmations:**  
   Explicitly recognize patient strengths, resilience, and support systems. Use specific affirmations to build confidence and rapport.

5. **Enhance Planning and Summarizing:**  
   Collaboratively develop concrete, manageable next steps with explicit patient commitment. Use structured summaries to consolidate motivation and plan.

6. **Explicitly Emphasize Autonomy:**  
   Reinforce patient’s control over decisions and pace of change throughout the session.

7. **Avoid Formulaic Responses:**  
   Personalize responses and avoid repetitive phrasing. Be more present and responsive to the unique flow of the conversation.

8. **Incorporate Permission-Based Information Sharing:**  
   When appropriate, ask permission before offering advice or information to maintain collaboration and respect autonomy.

---

## Overall Score: **3/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a foundational understanding of motivational interviewing principles, with consistent respect, collaboration, and autonomy support. However, it falls short of excellence due to superficial reflections, weak evocation and reinforcement of change talk, minimal handling of sustain talk, and lack of explicit planning and summarizing. To improve, the psychiatrist must deepen emotional attunement, skillfully manage ambivalence, and more actively evoke and reinforce the patient’s own motivations and strengths.
