# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation of Diagnostic Session with Matteo Ricci

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a generally respectful and supportive tone throughout the session. Early on, the psychiatrist invites the patient’s agenda (“what’s on your mind or what would you most like to focus on?”), which demonstrates respect for the patient’s autonomy. Affirmations such as “That shows a lot of resilience” and “You’ve already got a thoughtful approach” reinforce acceptance. The psychiatrist avoids judgment or labeling and normalizes ambivalence by acknowledging the patient’s uncertainty about the drinking issue.

However, the acceptance is somewhat surface-level and formulaic. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to deepen unconditional positive regard by explicitly normalizing the patient’s experience or validating the difficulty of ambivalence more warmly. For example, the patient’s minimizing language (“I don’t think it’s a huge deal”) could have been met with stronger affirmations of the patient’s honesty and openness rather than neutral reflections alone.

**Summary:**  
Generally respectful and supportive, but lacks the warmth and depth of unconditional positive regard that would elevate this to a 5.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates good reflective listening and perspective-taking. Reflections accurately capture both content and some emotional undertones, e.g., “It sounds like your ankle is definitely getting in the way of some things that matter to you,” and “there’s a bit of surprise or maybe some curiosity about what it all means” regarding drinking. These reflections show an understanding of the patient’s ambivalence and mild surprise.

However, the reflections tend to be somewhat surface-level and repetitive, often restating content without deeper emotional exploration. For example, the psychiatrist repeatedly reflects the patient’s ambivalence about drinking but does not probe the underlying feelings such as possible anxiety, fear, or shame that might be present. The emotional nuance is acknowledged but not deeply explored.

**Summary:**  
Good reflective listening and perspective-taking, but reflections could be more complex and emotionally nuanced.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates a collaborative stance, inviting the patient’s input and supporting autonomy (“what would you most like to focus on,” “What do you think would help you get clearer on that?”). The conversation avoids directive or authoritarian language and respects the patient’s pace.

The psychiatrist evokes some of the patient’s own motivations and ambivalence, particularly around drinking, and supports autonomy by asking about confidence and possible first steps. However, the session feels somewhat formulaic and lacks a fully evocative style that would draw out deeper personal values or emotional drivers. The collaboration is present but could be more explicitly co-constructed, with more explicit invitations to the patient to shape the agenda and plan.

**Summary:**  
Strong partnership and autonomy support, but could be more evocative and collaborative in drawing out deeper motivations and co-creating the process.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist elicits some change talk, particularly when asking about what signs might indicate drinking is a problem and what a first step might be. The patient offers tentative commitment language (“maybe I could just keep track,” “I think I can do it”). However, the psychiatrist misses opportunities to amplify and reinforce this change talk more consistently. For example, when the patient expresses mild surprise about drinking frequency, the psychiatrist reflects but does not strategically deepen or explore the implications.

There is no explicit elicitation of commitment language beyond a general question about confidence, and the psychiatrist does not capitalize on moments of expressed desire or ability to strengthen motivation.

**Summary:**  
Some evocation of change talk, but inconsistent and lacking strategic amplification or reinforcement.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist acknowledges sustain talk (e.g., “it’s just part of hanging out,” “I don’t see it messing up my school or work”), but responses are mostly neutral reflections rather than active softening or reframing. The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or explicitly normalize ambivalence to reduce resistance. There is little attempt to gently redirect sustain talk toward change or to explore the legitimacy of the patient’s reasons for maintaining current behavior while softly guiding toward consideration of change.

**Summary:**  
Sustain talk is acknowledged but not skillfully softened or redirected; responses remain neutral rather than strategically guiding.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a collaborative stance, frequently inviting the patient’s perspective and avoiding an expert-driven approach. The patient is asked to prioritize topics and suggest next steps, which supports partnership. The language is inclusive and autonomy-supportive.

However, the partnership is somewhat superficial and formulaic. The psychiatrist does not explicitly share power or negotiate the agenda beyond initial questions, nor does the psychiatrist explicitly invite the patient to co-create the plan or express expertise in their own life. The session could benefit from more explicit invitations to the patient to lead or direct the conversation.

**Summary:**  
Strong collaboration and client-centeredness, but partnership could be deepened with more explicit co-creation and power-sharing.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Deepen Reflections:**  
   Use more complex reflections that capture underlying emotions, values, and ambivalence. Reflect not just content but also feelings and meaning.

2. **Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When change talk arises, reflect and reinforce it strategically to deepen motivation. Explicitly elicit commitment and confidence language.

3. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalization to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change.

4. **Strengthen Affirmations:**  
   Offer specific, genuine affirmations recognizing patient strengths, efforts, and values. Avoid generic praise.

5. **Enhance Planning and Summarizing:**  
   Provide explicit summaries that consolidate change talk and clarify next steps. Collaboratively develop a concrete plan and check for commitment.

6. **Increase Evocation and Collaboration:**  
   More actively draw out patient’s own motivations, values, and ideas for change. Explicitly invite patient to co-create agenda and plan.

7. **Use Persuasion with Permission:**  
   When offering suggestions or information, ask permission first to maintain autonomy and collaboration.

8. **Avoid Formulaic Responses:**  
   Personalize responses and avoid repetitive phrasing. Be present and responsive to the unique flow of the conversation.

---

## Overall Score: **3/5**

**Summary:**  
This diagnostic session demonstrates a solid foundation in motivational interviewing with consistent respect, empathy, and collaboration. However, it falls short of excellence due to formulaic reflections, insufficient amplification of change talk, weak handling of sustain talk, and lack of explicit planning and summarizing. To improve, the psychiatrist should deepen emotional attunement, skillfully manage ambivalence, and more actively evoke and reinforce the patient’s own motivations and strengths.
