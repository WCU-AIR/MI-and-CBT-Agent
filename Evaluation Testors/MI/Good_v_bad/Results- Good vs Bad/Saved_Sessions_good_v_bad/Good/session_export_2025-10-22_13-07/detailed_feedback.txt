# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 5/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently demonstrates unconditional positive regard throughout the session. From the outset, the psychiatrist validates the patient’s experience without judgment, acknowledging the complexity of smoking as “a long and complicated part of your life” and recognizing the patient’s ambivalence and fears. The tone remains warm, supportive, and respectful, with explicit affirmations of the patient’s openness and struggle (e.g., “It’s completely understandable that you’d feel unsure,” “I really appreciate how you’re setting yourself up with manageable steps”). There is no evidence of labeling, criticism, or pressure. The psychiatrist normalizes ambivalence and frames setbacks as part of the process, which is essential for acceptance.

**Summary:**  
Exemplary unconditional positive regard and respect, creating a safe and supportive environment for the patient.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates good reflective listening and perspective-taking, accurately capturing the patient’s ambivalence, fears, and hopes. Reflections such as “smoking has woven itself into your sense of self, making the idea of change feel both hopeful and scary” and “holding onto hope and willingness, while still feeling the weight of past disappointments” show an understanding of the patient’s emotional experience. The psychiatrist also validates the patient’s desire for connection and realistic planning. However, while reflections are accurate and empathetic, they tend to be somewhat formulaic and occasionally repetitive (e.g., multiple reflections about “supportive connection” and “manageable steps” without deeper exploration of underlying emotions such as frustration, shame, or fear). There is room to deepen emotional nuance and explore the patient’s feelings more richly.

**Summary:**  
Strong empathy and understanding, but reflections could be more varied and emotionally nuanced to deepen connection.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a collaborative stance, inviting the patient’s input and emphasizing autonomy (“What do you think would be a manageable first step?” “How confident do you feel?”). The session avoids directive or authoritative language and supports the patient’s self-efficacy. The psychiatrist skillfully evokes the patient’s own motivations and ideas for change, reinforcing the patient’s control over the process. However, the session occasionally feels somewhat scripted or formulaic, with repeated phrasing around “manageable steps” and “supportive connection” that limits the dynamic flow of collaboration. The psychiatrist could more explicitly co-create the agenda or invite the patient to lead the direction more fully.

**Summary:**  
Strong collaboration and autonomy support, but the interaction could be more fluid and less formulaic to fully embody MI spirit.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist effectively elicits and reinforces change talk throughout the session. The patient articulates desire, reasons, need, and some commitment language (e.g., “I want to believe I can quit,” “Maybe starting with tracking,” “I’m willing to give this a try”). The psychiatrist reflects and amplifies these statements, helping the patient articulate a realistic plan and build confidence. The use of scaling questions (“On a scale from 0 to 10…”) helps quantify confidence and opens discussion about barriers and supports. However, while change talk is consistently evoked, the psychiatrist misses some opportunities to deepen or amplify commitment language more explicitly (e.g., asking “What would it take to move from a 5 to a 7?” or “What would it look like if you really succeeded?”). The evocation is solid but could be more strategic and intense.

**Summary:**  
Good evocation and reinforcement of change talk, but missed opportunities to deepen commitment and amplify motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 3/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist acknowledges the patient’s ambivalence and sustain talk indirectly by validating fears and uncertainty (“It’s scary to think about quitting completely,” “You’re nervous, and that’s natural”). However, sustain talk is not explicitly named or reflected in a way that softens or redirects it. The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or strategic reframing to gently shift sustain talk toward change. For example, the patient’s statements about fear of failure or “one cigarette won’t hurt” are not directly addressed or reframed. The approach is mostly neutral and supportive but lacks active softening or redirection of sustain talk.

**Summary:**  
Acknowledges ambivalence but does not skillfully soften or redirect sustain talk; more strategic handling needed.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently invites the patient’s perspective and choices, using inclusive language and avoiding an expert-driven stance. The patient is encouraged to identify goals and steps, and the psychiatrist offers options rather than directives. However, the partnership could be strengthened by more explicit sharing of power and co-creation of the agenda and plan. The psychiatrist often leads with questions and reflections but does not explicitly invite the patient to set the session’s direction or negotiate the plan in a way that fully equalizes the relationship.

**Summary:**  
Strong collaborative stance with good client-centeredness, but partnership could be deepened by more explicit co-creation and power sharing.

---

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- The psychiatrist creates a warm, accepting, and nonjudgmental environment.  
- Good use of open questions and reflective listening to explore ambivalence.  
- Supports autonomy and collaboration effectively.  
- Elicits meaningful change talk and develops a realistic, patient-centered plan.

### Areas for Improvement  
- Reflections are often formulaic and lack emotional depth; more complex reflections needed.  
- Sustain talk is not actively softened or redirected; missed opportunities to use double-sided reflections or reframing.  
- Partnership could be strengthened by more explicit co-creation and power sharing.  
- Summaries are implicit; explicit summaries could consolidate motivation and commitment.  
- Change talk evocation could be more strategic and amplify commitment language.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Deepen Reflections:**  
   Use more complex reflections that capture underlying emotions, ambivalence, and values. Avoid repetitive phrasing and explore emotional nuances.

2. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and gentle reframing to acknowledge sustain talk while guiding toward change. Address sustain talk explicitly rather than only indirectly.

3. **Enhance Partnership:**  
   Explicitly invite the patient to co-create the agenda and plan. Share power by asking how the patient wants to proceed and negotiate next steps collaboratively.

4. **Strengthen Summarizing:**  
   Use explicit, structured summaries to pull together change talk, reinforce motivation, and consolidate commitment before ending the session.

5. **Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When change talk emerges, reflect and amplify it strategically. Use scaling questions to explore what would increase confidence or commitment.

6. **Personalize and Vary Language:**  
   Avoid formulaic or repetitive phrasing. Tailor reflections and questions to the unique flow and emotional content of the conversation.
