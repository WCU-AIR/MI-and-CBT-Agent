# Motivational Interviewing Feedback: Psychiatrist Evaluation

---

## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a generally respectful and nonjudgmental tone throughout the session. They acknowledge the patient’s perspective without overt criticism or confrontation, for example, “It’s completely fine to keep it casual,” and “Thanks for being open to hearing more.” The psychiatrist avoids labeling or disapproval and validates the patient’s current stance and feelings. However, the acceptance feels somewhat procedural and lacks warmth or deeper positive regard that might foster greater trust or openness. There is no explicit normalization of ambivalence or struggle, which would be expected for a perfect score.

**Summary:**  
Respectful and supportive, but somewhat formulaic and lacking in genuine warmth or explicit unconditional positive regard.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates adequate reflective listening and attempts to understand the patient’s perspective, as seen in reflections like “It sounds like you’re managing a lot with work stress,” and “That makes a lot of sense—you’re saying that without a clear, personal reason… it’s hard to justify the effort.” However, these reflections are mostly surface-level and paraphrasing content rather than deeply exploring the patient’s emotions or underlying motivations. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore the patient’s subtle ambivalence or emotional nuances, such as the patient’s slight defensiveness or dismissiveness. The empathy is present but inconsistent and lacks depth.

**Summary:**  
Basic understanding and paraphrasing are present, but emotional nuance and deeper perspective-taking are limited.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a collaborative stance, inviting the patient’s input and supporting autonomy (“If it’s okay with you, I’m curious…”; “Would it be okay if I shared…”). There is no overt persuasion or directive approach, which is positive. However, the session feels somewhat formulaic and lacks strong evocation of the patient’s own motivations or values. The psychiatrist does not actively explore or amplify change talk beyond surface-level curiosity. The autonomy support is present but not fully evocative or empowering. The partnership is polite but not deeply co-constructed.

**Summary:**  
Moderate collaboration and autonomy support, but limited evocation and partnership depth.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 2/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist makes some attempts to evoke change talk by asking about readiness (“On a scale from 0 to 10…”) and exploring potential reasons for change (“What would need to happen for that number to move higher?”). However, these efforts are minimal and not consistently reinforced or amplified. The patient’s change talk remains weak and is not strategically deepened or reflected to build momentum. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore the patient’s strengths or past successes as motivators for change in a more evocative way. Overall, change talk is rare and shallow.

**Summary:**  
Rare and weak evocation of change talk; missed opportunities to deepen and amplify motivation.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 3/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist acknowledges sustain talk without confrontation and reflects the patient’s ambivalence (“It sounds like you’re comfortable sticking with what you’ve got,” “I hear you… it’s hard to justify the effort right now”). There are attempts to validate the patient’s perspective and normalize the difficulty of change. However, the responses are mostly neutral reflections rather than active softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or explicitly reframe sustain talk to gently guide toward change. Sustain talk is acknowledged but not skillfully minimized.

**Summary:**  
Some acknowledgment and validation of sustain talk, but lacks strategic softening or reframing.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 4/5**

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently invites the patient’s input and permission before sharing information (“Would it be okay if I shared…”), demonstrating respect for the patient’s autonomy. The tone is collaborative and avoids an expert-driven stance. However, the partnership remains somewhat superficial and polite rather than deeply co-created. The psychiatrist does not explicitly negotiate agenda or power sharing beyond basic permission-seeking. The patient remains somewhat passive, and the psychiatrist does not actively foster a sense of equal partnership.

**Summary:**  
Strong collaborative tone and respect for autonomy, but partnership could be deepened with more explicit co-creation.

**Areas for Improvement:**

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Maintains respectful, nonjudgmental stance throughout.  
- Uses permission-seeking before providing information.  
- Basic reflective listening and paraphrasing present.  
- Supports patient autonomy and avoids confrontation.

### Areas for Improvement  
- Reflections are surface-level; need more emotional depth and complexity.  
- Change talk evocation is minimal and not reinforced or amplified.  
- Sustain talk is acknowledged but not skillfully softened or redirected.  
- Affirmations are sparse and generic; should highlight patient strengths and values more.  
- No concrete planning or commitment elicited; session ends without clear next steps.  
- Engagement is polite but lacks emotional attunement and personalization.  
- The session feels formulaic and lacks dynamic evocation of motivation or ambivalence.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Deepen Reflections:**  
   Use complex reflections that capture underlying emotions, ambivalence, and values rather than just paraphrasing content.

2. **Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When change talk emerges, reflect and reinforce it to build momentum. Ask evocative questions to draw out reasons, desires, and confidence for change.

3. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and gentle reframing to acknowledge sustain talk while guiding toward change.

4. **Strengthen Affirmations:**  
   Offer specific, genuine affirmations recognizing the patient’s strengths, past successes, and values to build confidence.

5. **Enhance Planning and Summarizing:**  
   Explicitly summarize change talk and collaboratively develop a concrete plan or next steps to consolidate motivation.

6. **Increase Emotional Engagement:**  
   Personalize responses and attune to subtle emotional cues to deepen rapport and patient reflection.

7. **Avoid Formulaic Responses:**  
   Be more spontaneous and responsive to the patient’s unique expressions rather than relying on scripted or generic phrases.

---

## Overall Score: **3/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a foundational understanding of motivational interviewing principles, with respectful tone, autonomy support, and permission-seeking. However, it falls short of proficiency due to superficial reflections, minimal evocation of change talk, weak handling of sustain talk, and lack of concrete planning. To improve, the psychiatrist must deepen emotional attunement, actively evoke and reinforce motivation, skillfully manage ambivalence, and collaboratively develop actionable plans. Without these improvements, the session risks remaining a polite but ineffective conversation that fails to move the patient toward meaningful change.
