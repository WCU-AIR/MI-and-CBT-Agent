## Global Ratings (Counselor Behaviors)

### 1. Acceptance  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist consistently demonstrates respect and validation of the patient’s frustration and emotional state. For example, early reflections such as “I can hear how frustrated and pressed for time you feel” and “It’s completely understandable to feel that way” show a supportive stance. The psychiatrist avoids judgment or blame and acknowledges the patient’s experience as legitimate. However, the acceptance is somewhat formulaic and lacks deeper warmth or explicit unconditional positive regard that might fully normalize the patient’s anger and ambivalence. There is no explicit affirmation that the patient’s feelings are understandable given the systemic issues, which would elevate this to a 5.

**Summary:**  
Generally respectful and supportive, but could deepen the sense of unconditional acceptance and warmth, especially given the patient’s emotional state.

---

### 2. Empathy  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist demonstrates good reflective listening and perspective-taking, accurately capturing the patient’s frustration, sense of powerlessness, and desire for respect. Reflections such as “You’re carrying a lot of pressure” and “It sounds like you’re feeling stuck” show understanding of both content and emotional undertones. However, the reflections tend to be surface-level and occasionally repetitive, without fully exploring the emotional complexity or validating the patient’s anger beyond acknowledging frustration. The psychiatrist misses opportunities to explore the emotional impact of feeling disrespected or the toll of chronic systemic failures.

**Summary:**  
Good empathic reflections and perspective-taking, but lacks deeper emotional attunement and nuance.

---

### 3. Spirit of MI  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist maintains a collaborative stance, inviting the patient’s input (“What would feel most helpful for you?” “What do you think you might be able to do?”) and supporting autonomy (“What might help you follow through?”). There is no evidence of persuasion or directive advice. The psychiatrist evokes the patient’s own ideas and respects his ambivalence. However, the session feels somewhat formulaic and lacks a fully evocative style that draws out the patient’s intrinsic motivations or values beyond the immediate problem. The partnership is present but not fully equal or co-created, with the psychiatrist leading the agenda-setting.

**Summary:**  
Strong collaboration and autonomy support, but could be more evocative and less formulaic.

---

### 4. Cultivating Change Talk  
**Score: 3/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist attempts to evoke change talk by asking about possible strategies and confidence levels (“What kinds of changes or strategies come to mind?” “On a scale from 0 to 10, how confident do you feel?”). The patient offers some tentative change talk about blocking out time and asking for updates. However, the psychiatrist does not consistently amplify or reinforce this change talk. There are missed opportunities to reflect and deepen the patient’s emerging commitment or to explore the meaning behind his tentative steps. The evocation is present but limited in depth and frequency.

**Summary:**  
Some evocation of change talk, but lacks consistent reinforcement and amplification.

---

### 5. Softening Sustain Talk  
**Score: 3/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist acknowledges sustain talk and resistance (“It feels like you don’t have much control,” “You’re carrying the frustration alone”), but responses are mostly neutral reflections rather than active softening or strategic redirection. The psychiatrist does not use double-sided reflections or explicitly normalize ambivalence. There is little evidence of attempts to minimize the impact of sustain talk or to gently guide the patient toward change despite resistance.

**Summary:**  
Occasional acknowledgment of sustain talk, but lacks skillful softening or strategic handling.

---

### 6. Partnership  
**Score: 4/5**  

**Explanation:**  
The psychiatrist invites the patient’s perspective and choices throughout, maintaining a collaborative tone and avoiding an expert-driven approach. The patient is asked for input on goals and strategies, and the psychiatrist offers support rather than directives. However, the partnership is somewhat one-sided, with the psychiatrist guiding the agenda and framing the conversation. There is limited explicit sharing of power or co-creation of the plan beyond the psychiatrist’s questions.

**Summary:**  
Strong collaboration and client-centeredness, but could further minimize power differential and explicitly co-create the process.

## Summary of Feedback

### Strengths  
- Respectful, validating, and nonjudgmental stance.  
- Good reflective listening and collaborative approach.  
- Patient remains engaged despite frustration.  
- Some evocation of change talk and support for small, manageable steps.

### Areas for Improvement  
- Reflections are often simple and lack emotional depth or complexity.  
- Change talk is not consistently amplified or reinforced.  
- Sustain talk is acknowledged but not skillfully softened or redirected.  
- Affirmations are limited and generic; more specific recognition of strengths needed.  
- Planning and summarizing are weak; no explicit consolidation of commitment or next steps.  
- The session feels somewhat formulaic and lacks dynamic evocation and emotional attunement.  
- Partnership could be more equal and co-created.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Deepen Reflections:**  
   Use more complex reflections that capture underlying emotions, ambivalence, and values. Reflect not just content but emotional experience and meaning.

2. **Amplify Change Talk:**  
   When change talk emerges, reflect, reinforce, and strategically evoke more. Explore the patient’s motivations and reasons for change in greater depth.

3. **Skillfully Soften Sustain Talk:**  
   Use double-sided reflections and normalization to acknowledge sustain talk while gently guiding toward change. Validate ambivalence and reduce resistance.

4. **Strengthen Affirmations:**  
   Offer specific, genuine affirmations recognizing the patient’s strengths, efforts, and values. Avoid generic praise.

5. **Enhance Planning and Summarizing:**  
   Explicitly summarize change talk and reinforce commitment to next steps. Collaboratively develop a concrete, actionable plan and check for patient buy-in.

6. **Increase Evocation and Collaboration:**  
   More actively draw out the patient’s own motivations, values, and ideas for change. Share power by explicitly inviting co-creation of agenda and plan.

7. **Avoid Formulaic Responses:**  
   Personalize responses and avoid repetitive phrasing. Be present and responsive to the unique flow of the conversation.

---

## Overall Score: **3/5**

**Summary:**  
This session demonstrates a solid foundation in motivational interviewing with consistent respect, empathy, and collaboration. However, it falls short of excellence due to formulaic responses, lack of depth in reflections, insufficient amplification of change talk, and weak planning and summarizing. To reach a higher level of MI proficiency, the psychiatrist must deepen emotional attunement, skillfully handle ambivalence, and more actively evoke and reinforce the patient’s own motivations and strengths.
